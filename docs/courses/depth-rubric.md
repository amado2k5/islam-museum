# The depth rubric: what a rich lesson contains

This is the standard for the **depth layer** of a lesson in the Education Explorer (`/[lang]/learn/courses/`). An agent with no other briefing can apply it to any course. It adds to the ingestion runbook (`docs/courses-mission/PIPELINE.md`) and changes none of it: the course tree, the URLs, the expectation codes and texts, the connections and the draft labelling stay exactly as the pipeline left them.

## 1. Why it exists

A practising Ontario educator, who runs an Islamic school from Junior Kindergarten to Grade 8, reviewed the Explorer in November 2026. His verdict was that the content is "very surface level", and he said where the depth belongs:

1. **Social studies: the concepts.** "When it comes to social studies, Islam becomes relevant with the objectivity around the concepts of justice and morality."
2. **Science: the metaphysical layer.** "When it comes to science, it gives a metaphysical understanding of the universe around me."
3. **Motivation.** "What was the motivation for the Muslim scientist, and how were they driving this area of knowledge from the Qur'an?"

The pipeline's lessons were built to be honest and short: a Brief, one or two method questions, an activity, and one to three cards. A card says what a record is. The depth layer says what a student can *think with*: a scene or a source, a reason, a disagreement, a question that needs judgement, and doors into the graph.

## 2. The lenses

Every depth layer is written through the lens of its subject. A lens is a question the writer asks of the linked records. It is never a licence to add what the records do not say.

### 2.1 Social studies and geography: justice and morality as concepts

Treat justice (*ʿadl*) and morality (*akhlaq*) as ideas a student can reason with, described objectively and never devotionally. Ask of the records:

- **The ideal.** What did the idea require in theory, and who said so? (A jurist's definition, a clause of a covenant, a founder's deed.)
- **The practice.** Which institution or case carried it? Courts and judges (`job/qadi`), the grievance court (`concept/mazalim`), market inspection (`invention/hisba`, `job/muhtasib`), endowments that paid for public goods (`invention/waqf`, `concept/waqf`, `concept/sabil`), covenants and plural societies (`event/constitution-of-medina`, `concept/dhimma`, `concept/aman`), the rights of neighbours (`concept/jiwar`), shared land and water (`concept/hima`, `concept/shirb-water-rights`).
- **The shortfall.** Where does a record say practice fell short of the ideal: an officer dismissed for abusing his post, a rule that was evaded, an office that decayed?
- **The weighing.** How do historians judge it, and where do they disagree?

Give the evidence and let the student reason. The museum states no verdict.

### 2.2 Science and technology: the universe as its scientists understood it

Show the frame in which the science was done, **as the scientists' own worldview, attributed and sourced**: a cosmos that is ordered and can be understood, nature read as signs (*ayat*), observation and reasoning as acts that carried religious meaning. Ask of the records:

- How does the scientist open the book? (Records often describe a preface: praise of God, the purpose stated, the patron named.)
- What does the scientist say knowledge is for?
- Which problem of religious life did the work serve: prayer times, the direction of prayer, the calendar, inheritance shares, care of the sick, water for a town?

Write "al-Khazini's preface says…", "Nallino notes that the preface of al-Battani's tables cites verses of the Qur'an…". Never write, in the museum's voice, that the universe is a sign, that a verse means something, or that faith caused a discovery.

### 2.3 Motivation: why the scientist did it

For every major scientist a lesson features, give the motive **the sources preserve**, in this order of preference:

1. The scientist's own stated purpose, as a record reports it from a preface, an introduction or a letter. Quote only words the record itself prints inside quotation marks, at most 40 words, with the record's citation.
2. What a named historian or editor says moved the work, attributed to that historian.
3. If the sources preserve no motive, say so in one plain sentence, then say what the sources do show: who paid for the work, which practical problem it solved, where the scientist was employed (a mosque timekeeper, a court physician). Leave it there.

Never invent a quotation or a motive. Never infer piety from a name, a patron or an employer. A record's own statement of what it does not know ("the sources read say nothing about…") is good material: use it.

### 2.4 World religions: beliefs and practices as their adherents hold them

Approved by the founder on 13 November 2026 for the World Religions courses (`hrt3m`, `hrf3o`). A belief or practice is described as its adherents hold it, attributed to named sources, in the order of `AGENTS.md`: the Sunni position or positions first, then the academic view, then any other view, each labelled before it is stated, with no verdict. Ask of the records:

- **What is held or done, and on whose word?** The creed text, the jurists' encyclopaedia, the hadith collection the record names. "Muslims believe…" is never the museum's sentence: "the creed text of al-Tahawi states…", "the jurists hold…", "the Encyclopaedia of Islam describes…".
- **Where do adherents differ?** Between the schools of law, between Sunni and other traditions where the record gives them, each named. This is the natural "Two views".
- **What does an outside scholar say?** The academic account, attributed, set beside the adherents' own, neither correcting the other.
- **How does the text or practice reach us?** Who transmitted it, when it was written down, what the record says is disputed or unknown ("How do we know?").

The page teaches about a religion and never teaches the religion: no sentence instructs, recommends or says what a reader should believe or do, and no sentence judges a belief true or false. "Think about it" asks the student to compare, to explain how a practice expresses a belief its adherents state, or to weigh sources; it never asks the student to judge the faith or to take a religious position. "Why they did it" is not used in this lens, except for a named historian or scholar whose purpose a record reports. The Qur'an and hadith rules of section 5 apply in full: a verse is never typed, translated, paraphrased or explained; a hadith is given only as the record reports it, with its collection.

### 2.5 The arts: what the maker and the patron meant the work to do

Approved by the founder on 13 November 2026 for the Arts, Visual Arts and Music courses. An object, a building, a script or a piece of music is described through what its maker and its patron meant it to do, what its inscription says, and how it was made, each on the word of a named source. Ask of the records:

- **Who made it, who paid for it, and for what use?** A signature, a foundation inscription, a patron named by a chronicler or an art historian. Where the records name no maker, the lesson says so.
- **How was it made?** Materials, tools, steps and training, as the record gives them from a treatise, a conservator's study or a named scholar.
- **What does it say?** The words of an inscription only as the record reports them.
- **How do historians read it, and where do they differ?** Each reading attributed by name.

No sentence in the museum's voice says what a form "means" (that a pattern stands for infinity, that a dome stands for heaven): a meaning is stated only as a named author's reading. The avoidance of figures is reported as named jurists and named art historians describe it, with no verdict, and never as a rule in the museum's voice. Superlatives ("the finest", "the most honoured art") appear only where a record attributes them to a source that can carry them.

**Music.** The jurists' disagreement about music is always given as "Two views", by school and scholar as the record names them, beside the historical practice the record documents (who played, where, for whom, on what instrument). The lesson never says that music is permitted or forbidden. Where the records a lesson links carry no juristic view at all, the lesson does not supply one and does not raise the question.

"Why they did it" is used for a named maker, patron or musician whose purpose a record reports; otherwise the lesson says the records preserve none and gives what they show. The grade-fit rule of section 5 holds: an elementary lesson draws nothing from a record's passages on slavery, and a secondary lesson that links a record on enslaved performers keeps to what its expectation asks.

## 3. The sections

The depth layer is a set of sections in the lesson's prose files (`content/ar/course/<slug>.md` first, then `content/en/course/<slug>.md`), placed after the lesson's existing question sections and before "Try this". The headings are fixed, because the page finds the sections by them (`DEPTH` in `site/src/lib/courses.ts`) and shows them after the lesson's cards.

| Section | English heading | Arabic heading | What it holds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Go deeper | `## Go deeper` | `## تعمّق` | One short primary-source excerpt (translated, cited) or one concrete scene from a linked record. Not a longer summary: a single thing seen closely. |
| Why they did it | `## Why they did it` | `## لماذا فعلوا ذلك؟` | Science lessons that feature a named scientist: the sourced motive and the worldview (2.2, 2.3). |
| Two views | `## Two views` | `## رأيان` | Where the record carries a real disagreement between named scholars: each view, attributed, no verdict. |
| Often misunderstood | `## Often misunderstood` | `## خطأ شائع` | Where the record corrects a common error (a "first", a legend, a wrong date, a claim the source does not support). |
| How do we know? | `## How do we know?` | `## كيف نعرف؟` | Source criticism: who reports this, how far from the event, what the record says was not read or is not known. |
| Think about it | `## Think about it` | `## فكّر في هذا` | One question that needs reasoning or judgement. |
| Into the museum | `## Into the museum` | `## إلى المتحف` | Two to five links into the graph, each with a clause saying what the student will find there. |

Write the sections in the order of the table. Use only these headings, spelled exactly.

## 4. Grade bands

| | Grades 1–5 | Grades 6–8 | Grades 9–12 |
|---|---|---|---|
| The aim | Concrete, story-led, **one idea done well** | Sources, motives and differing views are introduced | Full source criticism, historiography and counter-arguments |
| Go deeper | Required. 40–90 words. One scene a child can picture: who, where, what they did. Short sentences. Name the source once, simply ("a book about the market inspector says…"). | Required. 70–130 words. A scene or an excerpt, with the author of the source named and what kind of source it is. | Required. 90–160 words. An excerpt or a closely read passage, with the source, its date or distance from the event, and its limits. |
| Why they did it | Not a separate section. If the record preserves a motive a child can understand, give it in one sentence inside Go deeper. | Required when the lesson features a named scientist. 40–90 words. | Required when the lesson features a named scientist. 60–120 words, including the worldview where a record carries it. |
| Two views / Often misunderstood | Omit. | One of them, where the record genuinely has one. 40–90 words. Omit otherwise. | One or both, where the record genuinely has them. 60–120 words each. Include the strongest counter-argument the record gives. |
| How do we know? | Omit. | Optional: one or two sentences inside Go deeper on where the information comes from. | Required where the record gives the material (most do). 50–110 words. |
| Think about it | Required. One question a child answers from the scene and their own life, and must give a reason. | Required. Compare two things, or weigh a piece of evidence. | Required. Take a position and defend it against a named counter-argument, or judge which source deserves more trust and why. |
| Into the museum | Required. 2–3 links. | Required. 3–4 links (2 if the graph holds no more honest doors). | Required. 3–5 links (2 if the graph holds no more honest doors). |

A link in "Into the museum" is a claim that the record belongs to the lesson. Never pad the list: a person is not linked beside a lesson on a religious community unless the person's record states that faith with its basis.

The question in "Think about it" is never recall-only. It fails the rubric if a student can answer it by copying a sentence from the page. Test it: the answer must need a *because*.

In the social-studies lens, at Grades 1–5 the idea is one moral notion in a concrete act (the inspector checks the scales so that no buyer is cheated); at Grades 6–8 the ideal is set beside the practice; at Grades 9–12 the shortfall and the historians' weighing are required where the records carry them.

## 5. Sourcing: the rules that never bend

1. **Records only.** Every factual sentence of the depth layer restates a sentence in the prose of a museum record, in the same language (the Arabic section follows the Arabic record, the English the English), and ends with that record's own citation marker for that sentence, copied exactly: `{cite:<slug> <locator>}`. Nothing comes from general knowledge, however true.
2. **Which records.** The records the lesson's connections already link; and records found by search that bear directly on those (the maker of an invention, the book an instrument is described in, the concept an institution rests on). Read the whole record first (`python3 scripts/courses/entity_brief.py <id> --full`). A stub is not a record.
3. **Link what you restate.** Each record a section draws on is linked in that section with `[[class/slug|label]]`, so the checker and the reader can follow the claim to its record.
4. **New sources.** A fact the graph does not hold may be added only by first adding it to the record it belongs to, from a source you have actually read, under `docs/agents/publication-standard.md`. That is a separate change to that record, made before the lesson cites it. Never cite a source in a lesson that no linked record cites.
5. **Quotations.** Only words a record itself prints inside quotation marks, at most 40 words, attributed as the record attributes them (speaker, and translator or editor where given). Otherwise paraphrase and say "he writes", "the editor says". Do not use block quotes (`>`); quote inline.
6. **Attribution.** Every interpretive, legal or theological statement names who holds it ("Ibn Khaldun says…", "the TDV encyclopaedia finds no evidence that…"). Nothing interpretive is stated as settled fact. Keep the record's hedges ("it is said", "in one report", "about"). No superlative ("the first", "the greatest") unless the record states it and attributes it.
7. **Qur'an.** Never type, translate, paraphrase or explain a verse. A verse may be referred to only as `[[passage/quran-<sura>-<aya>|Qur'an <sura>:<aya>]]`, only when a linked record itself cites that verse, and only to say who cites it and for what ("the record says al-Battani's preface cites verses of the Qur'an"). What a verse means is stated only as a named commentator's reading that a record reports.
8. **Hadith and rulings.** Reported as the record reports them, with the collection or the jurist named. A ruling is never health, safety or technical guidance, and is never given in the museum's voice.
9. **Records that cannot carry depth.** A record that reports a preacher's talk, a television programme or a poll is evidence of what was said there, not of the subject. Do not use it for the ideal, the practice or a motive. A record citing only Wikipedia or Wikidata supports names, dates and places only.
10. **Fit the grade** (PIPELINE.md step 4). In an elementary lesson nothing on slavery, killing, torture, the detail of battle, sexual matters or graphic disease. Medicine never reads as advice.
11. **The museum's standing rules** (`AGENTS.md`): no typed honorifics; no mention of any blocked organisation; a neutral docent's voice; a parenthetical containing Arabic script is wrapped in U+2066 … U+2069; raw HTML is not allowed in prose.
12. **If the records hold nothing for a section, omit the section.** A lesson whose linked records are thin gets "Go deeper" from what there is, "Think about it" and "Into the museum", and a note in the evidence file. Thin and honest beats rich and invented.

## 6. Language

Arabic is written first, then English. Each says the same thing in its own words; neither is a copy of the other. Urdu: the Urdu lesson files were not changed in the first run of this rubric (the instruction for that run was not to touch `/ur/`), so the Urdu lesson pages show the lesson without its depth layer. Adding the Urdu sections (`## مزید گہرائی`-style headings would have to be added to `DEPTH`) is a separate, later change.

## 7. Evidence

For every lesson, append rows to `docs/courses/evidence/<subject-slug>.md` under a heading `## Depth layer`, in this table:

```
| Lesson | Section | Claim made | Record | Record sentence it restates |
```

"Record sentence it restates" quotes the record's English prose exactly (at most 40 words; join two separate fragments with ` ... `), followed by the record's citation marker. The checker compares each quoted fragment with the record's prose, so a paraphrase in this column fails.

## 8. The check

```bash
python3 scripts/courses/check_depth.py course/<subject-slug>          # one course
python3 scripts/courses/check_depth.py --all-enriched                 # every course that has a depth layer
```

When several writers work on lessons of one course at once, each keeps its evidence rows in a file of its own and names it with `DEPTH_EVIDENCE_EXTRA=<file>`; the coordinator moves the rows into the course's evidence file before shipping.

It prints `DEPTH: GREEN` or the failing lines. It checks, for each lesson and each of the two languages: the required sections for the grade band are present and no band-forbidden section is; the headings are the fixed ones and Arabic and English carry the same sections; every `[[link]]` is a real record with prose; every `{cite:…}` in the depth layer is a source cited by a record linked in the same file or by one of the lesson's connections; no quotation runs over 40 words; "Think about it" ends in a question; no block quote, honorific or Qur'an bracket; and every evidence fragment is found in the prose of the record it names. The checker cannot judge whether a question needs reasoning or whether an attribution is fair: a reader does that (section 9).

Then the repository's gates, as always: `npm run validate` must print `0 errors` and `npx tsx scripts/cite-check.ts <ids>` must print `0 fail`.

## 9. The reader's check

Before a course is shipped, read every depth layer once against these questions:

1. Could the "Think about it" question be answered by copying a sentence? Then rewrite it.
2. Is any interpretive sentence unattributed? Then attribute it or cut it.
3. Does "Go deeper" show one thing closely, or is it a second summary? Cut to the one thing.
4. Would a teacher in a public school and a teacher in an Islamic school both be able to use this page as written?
5. Does the lesson still say only what its expectations ask for?
