**Attachment: concept note (one page)**

> **Mapping islam.museum to a national school curriculum: a pilot with the International Islamic Youth League**
> Concept note, draft, `[date]`
>
> **Aim.** To test, in one country and one subject, whether the museum's records can be set beside a national curriculum so that a teacher finds, for each topic, the scholars, places, inventions and texts that belong with it.
>
> **What exists.** The museum has done this once, for the public curriculum of Ontario, Canada: 65 courses from Grade 1 to Grade 12 and 361 lessons. Those lessons are drafts, written with AI help from cited sources, and have not yet been read by a human editor. The pilot is a chance to have educators read them.
>
> **Steps.**
> 1. The League chooses one country, one subject and two or three grades, and shares the official syllabus.
> 2. The museum maps each topic to its records and drafts a first set of about twenty lessons, in English and Arabic.
> 3. Educators named by the League read them and mark each for accuracy, fit to the syllabus, language level and usefulness in class.
> 4. The museum corrects what they find and publishes the corrections with credit.
> 5. Both sides read the result and decide whether to go further.
>
> **Time.** About eight weeks: two to choose, four to map and draft, two to review.
>
> **Who does what.** The League chooses the curriculum, names the reviewers and decides what is fit for its classrooms. The museum does the mapping, drafting, correcting and hosting, and keeps editorial responsibility for what it publishes.
>
> **Cost and data.** No cost to the League and no payment either way. The museum has no accounts and tracks no reader; nothing is collected from pupils or teachers.
>
> **Limits, said plainly.** The museum publishes in Arabic, English and Urdu only. It is one person's project, five weeks old in public. Its lessons are a starting text for teachers to judge, not a finished course.
