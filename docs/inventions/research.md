# Muslim Inventions Browser: research method

This plan follows [`docs/sources-policy.md`](../sources-policy.md), [`docs/agents/publication-standard.md`](../agents/publication-standard.md), [`docs/agents/content-standards.md`](../agents/content-standards.md) and the approved research brief. Any source may be read; facts are rewritten in the museum's own words with a defined citation. Copyrighted text, PDFs, OCR, page images, dumps, claim tables and unwhitelisted media stay outside Git.

## 1. Order for every lead

1. **Resolve the museum graph first.** Read the person's Arabic, English and Urdu records/prose; inspect their sources, faith block, tradition/perspectives, roles and occupations; inspect linked concepts, works, events, objects, places and existing media. Reuse records and aliases; do not duplicate them. Read every census prose hit as a lead.
2. **Check the maker.** Require a source-backed Muslim identity. Apply the Sunni default only when the cited source and Sunni milieu name no contrary tradition; record the explicit default basis. An attested other tradition is excluded; disputed tradition requires a Sunni-first perspectives block. Apply the modern-figures lane's two-independent-source faith requirement to modern figures.
3. **Establish the claim and contribution.** Identify whether it is a device, process, method, institution, discovery or transmission; identify the earliest source that supports the exact claim; name non-Muslim and earlier precursors. Popular claims are leads only.
4. **Read reference literature and primary sources.** Prioritize Hill and al-Hassan, Rashed, Sezgin, King, Saliba, Djebbar, Savage-Smith, Pormann, Watson and Decker, Bloom, Hillenbrand, and relevant EI2/EI3, DİA and Iranica entries. Read primary texts in Arabic where available and cite edition/page. Register each source entity before citing.
5. **Verify objects and media.** Search open museum catalogues for surviving examples, technique, accession number, creator, date and licence. Check the museum's existing image records first. If no suitable image exists, use only an asset that passes the illustrations pipeline; diagrams/maps are sourced/generated at build time and receive localized alt text.
6. **Modern records.** Prefer patent text and grant date, first paper/DOI, Nobel/IEEE citation, inventor's own account and a reliable biography for the identity basis. Do not count a product or company without a cited technical novelty.
7. **Write, cite, and log.** Every factual sentence has a source; write Arabic first, with English and Urdu in the same batch. Record uncertainty as status/perspectives, never as a verdict. Each record has provenance. Run validation and citation checks on every batch.

## 6. Scholarly-source reading and Home chapter pass (5 November 2026)

Five independent scholarly references have been opened and relevant sections read before any invention record is written. They are research guides, not automatic support for a published claim; the extent and limits of each pass are recorded so later work can resume without overstating what was checked.

- **Willem M. Floor, “PAPER ii. Paper and Papermaking,” Encyclopaedia Iranica.** [Article](https://iranicaonline.org/articles/paper-and-papermaking/). Read the historical overview, the account of early paper centers and production in Iran, the process section, and later industry sections. Floor presents the Talas-captives account as disputed: papermaking was already present in eighth-century Central Asia, while documented diffusion into Islamic lands is later; he does not establish a single inventor. The entry's focus on Iran and its historical industry makes it unsuitable as sole support for the early Samarkand claim.
- **David A. King, “Islamic Astronomical Instruments and Some Examples of Transmission to Europe.”** [Accessible copy](https://www.academia.edu/34703713/248_KING_2008_Islamic_astronomical_instruments_and_transmission_to_to_Europe_Millas_Vallicrosa_mem_vol_pdf). Read the introduction and sections on celestial globes, astrolabes and variants, quadrants, sundials, and selected transmission examples. King distinguishes Greek inheritance (including the planispheric astrolabe) from later developments in Islamic instrument-making, and cautions that not every instrument influenced Europe. Use specific instrument histories and manuscript/object evidence rather than generalizing from the overview.
- **Sami Hamarneh, “Drawings and Pharmacy in al-Zahrāwī’s 10th-Century Surgical Treatise,”** *Contributions from the Museum of History and Technology*, Paper 22 (1962). [Full text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26038/pg26038-images.html). Read the article's printed pages 83-94 and notes. Hamarneh compares Arabic manuscript illustrations with later Latin versions and describes al-Zahrāwī's surgical treatise as the thirtieth and final part of *al-Taṣrīf*. His wording differentiates instruments he used, devised, or recommended; verify any particular design against the manuscript or a critical edition before assigning invention.
- **Hend Mahgoub, Tiphaine Bardon, Dirk Lichtblau, Tom Fearn and Matija Strlič, “Material properties of Islamic paper,”** *Heritage Science* 4, article 34 (2016), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40494-016-0103-4). The full 14-page open-access article was read from the UCL Discovery copy ([PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1527462/1/2016%20Mahgoub%20HS.pdf)); its CC BY 4.0 licence and download were recorded in `$HARVEST/sources.yaml`, and the PDF/text remain outside Git. The 228-paper sample is dated approximately to the 18th-20th centuries; 88% of the studied papers were polished, starch-sized, or both, and the authors explicitly reject a single defining characteristic or unequivocal test for “Islamic paper.” This material study cannot establish early papermaking origins.
- **Gunalan Nadarajan, “Islamic Automation: A Reading of al-Jazari’s The Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices (1206),”** in *MediaArtHistories*, ed. Oliver Grau (MIT Press, 2007), pp. 163-178. [Accessible copy](https://www.academia.edu/861335/Islamic_Automation_A_Reading_of_al_Jazaris_The_Book_of_Knowledge_of_Ingenious_Mechanical_Devices_1206_). Read the introduction, cited al-Jazari/Hill examples of automata, and sections on “Islamic Programming” and “Untoward Automation”; the page later returned 403, so the essay was not read end to end. Treat it as a historiographical interpretation, not as evidence for al-Jazari's engineering details or for a general Islamic theology of technology.

The Home chapter was read as claims leads from EPUB FTS rows 12-40. The row numbers are not printed pagination: the contents page locates Coffee at p. 12, Clocks at p. 14, Chess at p. 18, Cleanliness at p. 20, Trick Devices at p. 24, Vision and Camera at p. 26, Fine Dining at p. 30, Three-course Meal at p. 32, Sound System at p. 34, Fashion and Style at p. 38, and Carpets at p. 40. A camera-obscura lead tied to Ibn al-Haytham's account was added at p. 29. ### Ammar al-Mawsili hollow-needle lead (5 November 2026)

The Wellcome Library scan on Internet Archive, item [b31363337_0002](https://archive.org/details/b31363337_0002), identifies Max Meyerhof's 1937 edition, *Las operaciones de catarata de ʻAmmār ibn ʻAlī al-Mausilī: The cataract operations of ʻAmmār ibn ʻAlī al-Mawsilī*, with Arabic facsimile and translations. ### Barid postal-intelligence system (5 November 2026)

The full TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi “Berîd” entry (`source/tdv-berid`, pp. 498-501) says that official courier activity began earlier, including under the Prophet and the Rightly Guided caliphs, and identifies Mu'awiya as the first Umayyad caliph to establish a systematic postal organization; it says that he added an intelligence function and that Abd al-Malik developed it further. This identifies the specific change as an attributed systematization/improvement, not invention of postal relays.

Clifford Edmund Bosworth's Encyclopaedia Iranica [“BARĪD” entry](https://iranicaonline.org/articles/barid-the-official-postal-and-intelligence-service-of-the-early-islamic-caliphate-and-its-successor-states/) traces courier networks to Achaemenid and Sasanian Iran and describes Byzantine influence on the system's terminology. These precedents rule out an original-invention claim, but the screened sources do not compare their intelligence functions with the change attributed to Mu'awiya. The Cambridge repository record for Adam J. Silverstein's thesis, “The origins and early development of the Islamic postal system (al-Barid), until ca. 846 CE,” reports that the thesis file is padlocked for research requests; it was not read or used as evidence.

The existing Mu'awiya person record has a `faith` entry within the museum's Sunni frame, but that entry has no source list.
