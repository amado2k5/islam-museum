## What the records say they did

1. Al-Hasan ibn al-Haytham, an Arab scholar of mathematics and physics, is known for his book Kitab al-Manazir on optics. The editor of the book's edition describes him as having lived in the fifth century AH, and holds that his book is the most valuable thing Arab scholars produced in physics . — source location `content/en/person/ibn-al-haytham.md — Overview`; citation `{cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.9}`; flag: **stated**.
2. The Encyclopaedia of Islam gives his full name as Abu Ali al-Hasan, or Husayn, ibn al-Haytham al-Basri al-Misri. Towards the end of the nineteenth century he was identified with the Alhazen, Avennathan or Avenetan of medieval Latin texts. The article counts him among the principal Arab mathematicians and calls him, without any doubt, the best physicist . — source location `content/en/person/ibn-al-haytham.md — Overview`; citation `{cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 820}`; flag: **stated**.
3. The article stresses that his biography has numerous gaps. He was born at Basra around 354 AH/965 . During the reign of the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim, 386 to 411 AH/996 to 1021, he went to Egypt and tried to regulate the flow of the Nile. He gave up the task when he realised it was impossible, despite his fear of the caliph's anger . — source location `content/en/person/ibn-al-haytham.md — Life`; citation `{cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 820} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 820}`; flag: **stated**.
4. After the caliph's death he returned to Cairo, where he earned his living by copying scientific manuscripts, particularly mathematical ones. The article gives his death as 430 AH/1039 . — source location `content/en/person/ibn-al-haytham.md — Life`; citation `{cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 820}`; flag: **stated**.
5. TDV gives birth around 354 AH/965 and discusses differing death reports, including an autograph dated 432 AH/1040; the reports are not collapsed into a single certain date . Approximate data dates remain; an activity century does not precisely date every treatise. The Encyclopaedia of Islam gives 430 AH/1039 for his death, a year that differs from the autograph date TDV reports . — source location `content/en/person/ibn-al-haytham.md — Dates of his life`; citation `{cite:tdv-ibnul-heysem pp.82–87} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 820}`; flag: **stated**.
6. His writings number more than a hundred titles, which Ibn Abi Usaybi'a listed. Most, some of them very short, deal with mathematics and physics, though he also wrote on philosophical and medical subjects. Throughout them the article sees his deep knowledge of the Greek authors, notably Ptolemy, whom he edited, studied and criticised . — source location `content/en/person/ibn-al-haytham.md — Writings`; citation `{cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 820}`; flag: **stated**.
7. Among the works the article names as best known is a treatise on finding the direction of the qibla, in which he is said to have established a cotangent theorem . It also lists a treatise on the light of the moon, important for its ideas on light, colours and the movements of the heavens; treatises on burning mirrors, including parabolic ones; a treatise on the form of eclipses; and a work on the shadows. 8. One short treatise shows that, of two regular polygons inscribed in the same circle, the one with more sides has both the larger surface and the larger perimeter . Another, on the form of an eclipse, is described as the first to set out the use of the camera obscura for observing solar eclipses .
