His given name was Muhammad, his kunya Abu Abd Allah, his lineage Muhammad son of Jabir son of Sinan, and he carried several nisbas: al-Harrani for his origin in Harran, al-Raqqi for his residence in al-Raqqa, al-Battani, and al-Sabi. 5. Harran was an ancient city long devoted to the cult of the Moon; its people kept their pagan worship through the first four centuries after the Hijra and, from 215 AH, called themselves Sabians so as to be treated as a people of the book paying the jizya, as Nallino relates. 6. Al-Battani spent most of his life in al-Raqqa and observed the sky there for many years to set the elements of his tables for that city's meridian and latitude. He measured the Sun's course over successive years, fixed the solstices and equinoxes above all in 882 and 883 CE, determined the longitudes of certain fixed stars in 879–880, and observed solar and lunar eclipses in 883 and 891. 7. Nallino rejects the story current among some Europeans that he was a prince or king of Syria, saying he found nothing of the kind in the Arabic authors. He infers al-Battani's devotion to study and his indifference to praise from the advice he gives to observers and from his great modesty. — source location `content/en/person/al-battani.md — Observations at al-Raqqa and Antioch`; citation `{cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 26} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 26}`; flag: **stated**.

Signs of the esteem in which the learned of his day held him are the epistle the vizier Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Muhammad ibn al-Furat asked of him, his conversations with Ja'far ibn al-Muktafi, and the mention of his observations by Thabit ibn Qurra.
