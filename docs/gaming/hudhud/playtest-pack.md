# Hudhud playtest pack

_26 November 2026. Documents only; sent to nobody. This pack adapts `docs/gaming/playtest-protocol.md` for Hudhud. No playtest has been held. A script, AI review or developer playthrough is regression evidence only and never counts as a child or parent playtest._

## Purpose

Hudhud is for children and families, so the test must measure understanding, comfort, agency and stopping points—not only whether a quest can be finished. The first test subject is the golden scene/Atlas slice unless the founder chooses otherwise.

The founder decides who is invited and how they are compensated. Nobody is contacted from this document.

## Age bands

- **A:** 6–9
- **B:** 10–13
- **C:** 14–17
- **P:** parent or guardian, observing or playing separately

Record only the band. Never record exact age, birthdate, name, school, address, face, voice, contact or other identifying detail in the repository. Testers are `A1`, `B1`, `C1`, `P1` and so on. The founder alone keeps any invitation list outside the repository.

## Before anyone agrees: guardian consent text

Read or give this text exactly, in the family's language:

> We are testing an unfinished children's game made for islam.museum. We are not testing your child; we are testing the game. Your child may stop at any time, for any reason or no reason, and nothing bad will happen. We will watch what they do in the game and write down things like where they tapped, what they said about it, how long they paused, and whether they finished. We will not record their name, exact age, school, face, voice or contact details in the project notes. We will not record video or audio unless you separately agree to that, and the default is not to record. The notes will use a code such as A1 for a child aged 6–9. You may stay with your child. You may also withdraw them at any time during the session. Do you agree to this session being written down in that limited way?

For a child old enough to understand, also ask:

> You can stop whenever you want. You do not have to answer any question. Is it okay if we watch how the game works while you play?

If either answer is not a clear yes, do not run the session.

## Session setup

- Length: 20 minutes maximum for ages 6–9; 25 minutes for ages 10–17; a parent interview may add 10 minutes.
- Address: the current UAT Hudhud page with `fresh=1`, in the player's chosen language.
- Device: the player's own device where possible; otherwise write down the test device in generic terms (`laptop`, `tablet`, `keyboard-only laptop`) without serials or identifying details.
- Language: record the interface language, not the child's ethnicity or background.
- Input: keyboard, mouse, touch, gamepad or keyboard-only/assistive setup, as applicable.
- One adult facilitates; one may observe. Do not crowd the child.

## What the facilitator says

Say only:

> This is an unfinished game. Please play it as you would if you had found it yourself. You can say aloud what you are thinking if you want. I will not help while you play, and there is nothing you can do wrong. You can stop whenever you want.

If the child asks what to do, answer once:

> What would you like to try?

Write the question down. Do not point, nod, smile approvingly, correct them or explain.

## Core tasks to observe

Do not read these as instructions. They are what the facilitator watches for while the child plays naturally.

### 1. Opening and Atlas

- Does the player understand that the large button starts the first quest?
- Do they notice or understand the working-title/prototype labels?
- Can they find the settings, pause and music controls without being taught?
- Do they understand the Atlas as a collection of worlds, or is it just decoration?
- In Arabic/Urdu, do they use the right-to-left interface comfortably?

### 2. First movement and flight

- Time from scene opening to first deliberate movement.
- Time to first hop/flight.
- Do they discover the controls from prompts, motion, curiosity or trial?
- Do they understand perching/landing rather than fighting the controls?
- Is anyone frightened, frustrated or made motion-uncomfortable by movement, flashes or sounds?

### 3. Talk and characters

- Do they listen/read or skip? Why, in their own words?
- Can they say who Hudhud, the Storykeeper and the scholar are within the fiction?
- Do they understand that the people are imaginative game figures rather than portraits?
- Do expressions and gestures help or confuse?

### 4. Light puzzle

- Do they understand the beam's purpose?
- Can they find the first mirror interaction?
- Do they understand what changed after a mirror moved?
- Do they use the words/list/accessibility alternative if needed?
- Do they become stuck because of reading, control, visual logic or puzzle logic?

### 5. Secret/curiosity layer

- Do they explore beyond the required path?
- Do they notice the feather, cord, curtain or dark-room effect without being told?
- Is the secret delightful, scary, confusing or invisible?

### 6. Results and return to Atlas

- Do they understand that the quest ended?
- Do they read the museum note or understand “historical review pending”?
- Can they return to the Atlas without help?
- Do they choose to continue, replay, change settings or stop?

## Age-specific observation

### Ages 6–9

- Use shorter sessions; stop at signs of fatigue or distress.
- Watch whether reading is a barrier even when captions/interface are correct.
- Watch whether the child expects every glowing object to be interactive.
- Watch whether flight and mirrors are understood through action rather than text.
- Ask simple questions with no pressure. Silence is an acceptable answer.

### Ages 10–13

- Watch whether they read the museum note and connect the game to real history.
- Ask whether the puzzle felt too easy, too hard or fair, and why.
- Watch whether they experiment with settings, controls and exploration.

### Ages 14–17

- Watch for whether the tone feels childish, respectful or interesting.
- Ask about the line between story invention and history.
- Watch whether interface polish, performance and repetition affect trust.

### Parents/guardians

A parent does not replace a child test. Ask separately:

- What did you think the game was teaching?
- Was there anything you would not want your child to imitate or misunderstand?
- Did the history/invention distinction feel clear?
- Was the session length comfortable?
- Would you want to be present, nearby or not needed for this kind of game?
- Did anything in language, imagery, sound or theme concern you?

## What to time and count

| measure | how recorded |
|---|---|
| time to first deliberate action | from page loaded to movement/click that is not accidental |
| time to first flight | from scene control to first airborne hop/flight |
| time to first meaningful interaction | mirror, object or talk choice that changes the game |
| time to first mirror success | first mirror correctly turned |
| time to finish | if finished; otherwise furthest point reached |
| stalls | each stop over 20 seconds, with what was on screen |
| help requests | exact question asked; facilitator answer remains limited |
| errors/faults | anything broken, misleading, uncomfortable or unreadable |
| voluntary stop | exact words where the player explains stopping, if they offer them |
| return choice | continue, replay, settings, Atlas, stop, unsure |

## Questions afterward

Ask after play, never during. Use simple versions of these, in the player's language:

1. What was the game asking you to do?
2. What did you do first? Why?
3. Tell me about one thing you discovered yourself.
4. Was there a moment you did not know what to do? What were you looking at?
5. Did anything feel too fast, too slow, too loud, too bright or uncomfortable?
6. Did anything go wrong? Could you get out of it?
7. Who was Hudhud? Who was the scholar? What was real and what was made up?
8. What would you change first?
9. Would you open it again tomorrow? Why or why not?
10. Is there anything you did not like that adults might not notice?

Do not ask leading questions such as “Was it fun?” or “Was it clear?” Do not defend the game.

## What this pack does not claim

- It does not claim the game is safe, fun, educational or accessible.
- It does not replace assistive-technology testing.
- It does not replace native-language review.
- It does not turn parent approval into child assent; both matter.
- It does not permit recording, publication or recruitment.
