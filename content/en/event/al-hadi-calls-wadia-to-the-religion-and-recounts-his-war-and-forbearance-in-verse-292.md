## Overview
The continuation of the poem on p. 327, in which the poet addresses the clan of Wadi'a and calls them to make ready for the religion, speaks of the Commander of the Believers and of their forefathers with him, vows to come on his opponents with horses and heroes, and says he forbade them and called them to the soundness of the religion, that they cast the truth aside, that he forgave first and then threatened them with punishment; it ends with a saying of his people's poet.

## Detail
After the line on the neighbourhood of Muhammad at the top of p. 327 come the lines "O clan of Wadi'a the generous, make ready for the religion; upon you is my pride" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.327}, then "I mean the Commander of the Believers, brother of intellect, who wipes out the unbelievers" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.327}. The poet says he rises over the enemy through them because they are "my right hand in battle and my left" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.327}, and that their forefathers were so with "my father", whom he calls "kin of the Messenger, the pure, the excellent" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.327}; the book names neither the Commander of the Believers nor the father, so no name is linked. In lines before these he speaks of a trial that lasted nights and of people he charges with drinking wine and eating the orphan's wealth, and the lines do not make clear who they are {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.327}. He says he will visit them with scowling horses and heroes, that he is not "Ibn Ahmad, of generosity and eminence" unless he stirs dust in the plain of Azal, and that he sets the cutting swords to judge among them until he straightens the swaying of the base {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.327}. He says his forgiving them has been long and that they banded together against him, like the deed of Ad in the time long gone {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.327}. He says he forbade them vice and ruin and called them to the soundness of the religion of Muhammad and to the duties of works, and they grew insolent, inclined to error and desire and cast the truth aside in exchange {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.327}, and that he then said that if they accept it they take their own share and if they bolt he will destroy them with a punishment {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.327}. He ends with the line of his people's poet "O bearer of burdens, tomorrow you are led" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.327}. A footnote of p. 327 says the manuscript S reads "I attack" where the text has "I rise over" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.327}. The name "Ibn Ahmad" in a line is not explained by the book, and the earlier poems of this run are ascribed to al-Hadi, Yahya ibn al-Husayn, so the museum does not settle here whose lines these are and records the question in the ledger. It follows [[event/al-hadi-sends-word-to-banu-rabia-and-banu-sarim-and-promises-his-sword-in-verse-292|his word to Banu Rabi'a and Banu Sarim]]; Wadi'a here is read as [[group/wadia-tribe-yemen|the tribe of Wadi'a]]. The poem stands in a Zaydi book in the voice of the imam's followers; its claims, descriptions and judgments on opponents are the poet's or the narrator's, and the museum adopts none of them, repeats no word of abuse and gives no verdict.
