## Overview
Khaybar is a famous oasis, and the name of its principal settlement, about 150 km from [[place/medina|Medina]]. According to the Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose account this page follows alongside the traditional narrative, the district owes its renown to events there in the years 7 and 20 of the Hijra rather than to natural features that set it apart from other oases. In the year 7 the Prophet [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] led an expedition against the [[group/jews-of-khaybar|Jewish inhabitants]] of the oasis, which ended with their capitulation and an agreement under which they stayed on their land and handed over half its produce. In the year 20 the caliph [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] revised that arrangement. The article gives its date as Muharram 7, May to June 628, following [[person/ibn-hisham|Ibn Hisham]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1189} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1191}

## The oasis and its people
Because the oasis was famous for events and not for its terrain, the early Arab geographers say little about it beyond praising its abundant palms. Only al-Bakri and Yaqut give it as much as two pages. Yaqut says that the name covered a whole district of seven fortified strongholds, among them Na'im, al-Qamus, al-Natat, al-Sulalim, al-Watih and al-Katiba, and that Khaybar meant a fortress in the dialect of the Jews, which is why the plural Khayabir was also used. Modern travellers describe a lava field with swamp, salt-crusted valleys and black basalt; the traveller Doughty, who stayed there in 1877 and 1878, found it oppressive despite the fertile soil. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1189} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1190}

The earliest mention of the place, the article says, is a bilingual inscription at Harran dated one year after a Khaybar expedition, which it takes to mean 568 CE. In the Prophet's time the population consisted of Jewish and Judaised tribes. The author states that the date of their arrival is unknown and offers his own reconstruction: a movement of Jews towards northern Arabia after the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE, drawn by trade and by openings for craftsmen, bankers and farmers, who worked in partnership with Arab intermediaries. He also reads the goods the Muslims later found in one stronghold, a siege engine, twenty bales of Yemeni cloth and five hundred cloaks, as stock held for sale. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1190}

## The account in al-Raheeq al-Makhtum
In Muharram of the seventh year of the Hijra the Prophet marched out with fourteen hundred fighters and two hundred horses — the people of the Pledge of al-Ridwan at al-Hudaybiyya and no others — heading for the fortified oasis of Khaybar, the chief stronghold of intrigue and warmongering against Medina. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}

The Prophet took a road that cut Khaybar off from its Ghatafan allies, then surprised them at dawn; its mighty forts were besieged one by one: the fort of Na'im, then the fort of al-Sa'b ibn Mu'adh, then the castle of al-Zubayr, the fort of Abi and the fort of al-Nizar, then the forts of the second quarter such as al-Katiba, al-Watih and al-Salalim. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}

When some forts held out, the Prophet said: "tomorrow I will give the banner to a man who loves God and His Messenger and whom God and His Messenger love; God will grant victory at his hands." He called [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]], touched his eyes with his spittle and handed him the banner; Marhab came out to him declaiming verses and Ali struck him and split his crown — and the forts fell in succession. {cite:sahih-al-bukhari}

When the Jews were certain of ruin they asked for peace on the sparing of their blood and their evacuation, then agreed to remain on their land working it for half its produce; the spoils of Khaybar were divided among the people of al-Hudaybiyya; and the Prophet freed the lady [[person/safiyya-bint-huyayy|Safiyya bint Huyayy ibn Akhtab]] and married her after her conversion. {cite:sahih-al-bukhari}

## Motives, as the article reports them
The article sets out several explanations of why the Prophet marched on Khaybar and attributes each to its author. It reports Caetani's view that the Prophet acted from political opportunism: the [[event/treaty-of-hudaybiyya|pact of al-Hudaybiyya]] secured him against the Quraysh during the expedition, and booty from Khaybar could satisfy companions who had hoped to take Mecca. It reports [[person/montgomery-watt|Montgomery Watt]]'s view that the [[group/banu-nadir|Banu al-Nadir]], driven out of Medina, had taken refuge at Khaybar, where their chiefs and others were plotting revenge with neighbouring Arab tribes; the article agrees that the sources support this, and notes the earlier coalition of the year 5 that had besieged Medina. It also records Haykal's opinion that the Prophet feared the northern oases would betray him to Heraclius and Chosroes, and the author rejects this as reading later developments into the event. Finally the author adds that the Prophet needed arms and money and could expect to find them there. The article's own economic reading of hostility to the Jews is a suggestion by its author, not an established finding. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1190} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1191}

## The march and the siege
Sources differ on numbers: the article gives 1,800 or 1,600 men with 100 horses, and, citing al-Mas'udi, 1,400 men with 200 horses, the figure also given in the traditional account above. The march was secret and quick, and the people of Khaybar learned of it only when they went out to their fields. They withdrew into their strongholds, each family or group holding its own, since the oasis had no single authority able to organise a common defence and the article reports discord among them. Their main policy was to seek the help of neighbouring tribes with promises of reward. Several thousand men of [[group/ghatafan|Ghatafan]] came but withdrew when their help was needed; the sources say they feared for the families and livestock they had left in the desert, and another version speaks of a mysterious warning voice. Ghatafan accepted Islam soon after the conquest, and the Prophet granted them a hill in the oasis, which the article suggests may have been the price of their staying away. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1191}

The article cautions that the course of operations is not clearly described, that the topography and local tradition cannot be used to reconstruct it, and that the place names Doughty collected differ from those in the sources; only the site of Ali's deed can be identified, because a mosque bears his name. It describes three regions of the oasis, al-Natat, al-Shikk and al-Katiba, each with several fortified farmhouses on hills or basalt crags, surrounded by fields and palm groves. The Muslims, with only primitive means, besieged the strongholds one after another and waited for thirst and hunger to force surrender, and on one occasion the Prophet ordered four hundred palms cut down around a stronghold. The besieged moved women, children and valuables between strongholds by night, and there were duels, sorties, exchanges of arrows and informers among the Jews. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1191}

## Episodes in the traditions
When the defenders of one stronghold held out, the article says that [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] and then Umar each took the standard and failed. The Prophet then declared that he would give it next day to a man loved by God and His Prophet, and called Ali, whose eye ailment was cured when the Prophet applied spittle. The article adds that this tradition, as Ibn Hisham reports it, shows a Shi'i tendency, which is the author's judgement and not the traditional account's. In it Ali killed a Jewish champion with one stroke. Having lost his shield, he is said to have torn a gate from its hinges and used it as protection and then as a bridge into the stronghold; the article adds that eight men were needed to put it back. The author notes that Ali had shown valour at [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]] and [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]], that this feat astonished the Muslims, and that it was greatly exaggerated and later made him a prototype of heroes in popular storytelling. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1192}

At the start the Muslims lacked food and killed and cooked about twenty donkeys that had escaped from a stronghold. The Prophet then declared the meat of horses, mules and donkeys forbidden and the pots were overturned, though the article notes that necessity relaxes such rules. Among the captives was [[person/safiyya-bint-huyayy|Safiyya]], wife of a Jewish chief; the Prophet threw his mantle over her, and her husband was put to death for hiding the treasure of the Banu al-Nadir. The Prophet freed her and married her, which later raised the juristic question whether her emancipation could stand in for the bridal gift or was a special privilege of the Prophet. A Jewish woman served him a roast sheep; he spat out the shoulder after noticing a strange taste, and a companion who ate some fell ill and died later. The sources do not say whether she was executed, and [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]] states that he did not punish her. The Prophet also ordered the Jews' scriptures returned and gave instructions on selling and exchanging precious metals. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1192}

## The agreement and its legal character
After about six weeks of fighting, with very few Muslim casualties, about twenty dead according to Caetani, the Jews asked for an agreement and capitulated on his terms. The article prefers Ibn Khaldun's version of the details. They would stay on their land and cultivate it but hand over half the produce to the Muslims. The article judges that the pact was legally defective because it did not say whether they remained owners of the soil, and that the Prophet's concern was only that the oasis stay under cultivation; it calls the result a perpetual servitude of land-serfs, a characterisation that is the author's own. Later jurists classed the arrangement as muzara'a or musaqat, a form of sharecropping. The article contrasts it with the Prophet's other settlements: in the year 4 he gave part of al-Nadir's land to emigrants and kept part, in the year 5 he divided four fifths of the land of the [[group/banu-qurayza|Banu Qurayza]], and at [[place/fadak|Fadak]] he later decided differently. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1192}

## The division of Khaybar
The article treats the distribution of Khaybar as a matter on which the sources are unclear. Movable goods were pooled and divided. For the land, it reports Caetani's view that only the produce was divided, with Montgomery Watt in agreement, whereas von Tornauw and van Berchem had held that the Prophet divided the land itself. In the account the article describes, the Prophet first set aside his fifth, the region of al-Katiba, and divided the rest into eighteen major parts of one hundred equal plots each, assigned by lot, with each beneficiary entitled to take half the yield of his plot directly from the Jewish cultivator. A variant account gives thirty-six parts, half for the troops and half for the Prophet's needs and visitors. The article regards these variant traditions as authentic though possibly shaped by the debate over Umar's later policy. It also describes how traditionists distinguished spoils taken by force from property gained by treaty, and disagreed on which category Khaybar belonged to. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1193}

The article notes that the Prophet assigned shares of the produce of al-Katiba to his wives and some relatives, and calls Sprenger's idea that the revenues could support an army of four or five thousand an exaggeration. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1194}

## Umar and the year 20
The article links the dispute to Umar's treatment of the Sawad in Iraq, which he did not divide among the conquerors but kept as a source of revenue for the whole community, and which it says stirred discontent that later fed opposition to [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]]. Both his defenders and his critics appealed to the Prophet's conduct at Khaybar. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1192} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1193}

Abu Bakr changed nothing at Khaybar, and Umar changed nothing in his early years. In the year 20 he announced in the mosque of Medina that he would expel the Jews from Arabia, saying he had learned that the Prophet had said before his death that two religions could not coexist there. The article, following Caetani, doubts the traditions reporting this saying and those accusing the Jews of crimes, and regards them as devices to clear Umar of acting against the Prophet's will. Caetani's own view is that the motive was local and practical, since the oases could now be worked by slaves brought by the conquests; [[person/al-baladhuri|al-Baladhuri]] is said partly to confirm this. After the removal of the Jews, Umar redivided Khaybar and gave the beneficiaries ownership of the land from which they drew rents. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1194}

## Consequences and later history
The article lists immediate effects: Muhammad's power grew, several formerly hostile tribes accepted Islam and recognised Medina's leadership, and he and the Muslims, especially the emigrants, gained fixed revenues; the article quotes [[person/aisha|Aisha]]'s remark that dates could now be eaten to the full. It nevertheless locates the event's historical importance in the pact itself, which set more or less the style for relations between the Muslims and the non-Muslim peoples they later ruled. The later history of the oasis is tied to that of the Hijaz and Najd and the article finds nothing of interest in it, noting only that some Jews stayed or returned. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1194}

## Ali at Khaybar

Ibn Ishaq, as the book reports him, dates the expedition to Muharram of the year 7 AH, while al-Zuhri and Malik give other dates. In it the valour of [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] was displayed, and the fortress was opened by his hand {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.104}. Khaybar was the last stronghold of the Jews in the Arabian Peninsula and a base from which they watched for the Muslims {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.104}.

The Prophet said: 'Tomorrow I will give this banner to a man through whom God will grant victory, who loves God and His Messenger, and whom God and His Messenger love.' In the morning people asked where Ali was, and were told that he was suffering in his eyes. The Prophet called for him and spat into his eyes, and he was healed {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.104-105}. Ali asked: 'Messenger of God, shall I fight them until they become like us?' The Prophet said: 'Proceed at your own pace until you reach their courtyard, then call them to Islam...' {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.105}

In the duel [[person/marhab-al-yahudi|Marhab]], the champion of Khaybar, came out against him. Ali said: 'I am the one my mother named Haydara', struck his head and killed him, and the victory came by his hand {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.105}. The book draws from the episode several virtues: the Prophet's testimony of his love and the blessing of his prayer. It also holds that the hadith contains no proof of Ali's imamate, as the Rafidah claim, and it presents this refutation as attributed to its own proponents {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.105-106}.

## Notes for researchers

- The verses of the duel are garbled in the scan, so they are not quoted word for word; the original should be consulted.
- The book says Ali was given the banner while he was ill. It draws from the fact that the banner came to one who had not sought it the need to believe in divine decree; this inference is presented as the book's own.

## For Scholars
In Muharram 7 AH — al-Zuhri and Malik date it otherwise — the Prophet marched with fourteen hundred men and two hundred horses, the very people of the Ridwan pledge at Hudaybiyya, against the oasis of Khaybar, the Jews' last stronghold in Arabia and their base of plotting against Medina. He took a road cutting it off from Ghatafan and surprised them at dawn; the forts were besieged one by one: Na'im, al-Sa'b ibn Mu'adh, al-Zubayr's castle, Abi, al-Nizar, then al-Katiba, al-Watih and al-Sulalim in the second quarter. When some resisted he said 'tomorrow I will give the flag to a man who loves God and His messenger and whom God and His messenger love — God will open at his hands'; he called Ali, sore-eyed, and healed his eyes, telling him to go at his own pace and call them to Islam first; Marhab, Khaybar's horseman, duelled him and was brought down, and the opening was at Ali's hands. The Jews settled on sparing lives, then on remaining in their land for half the produce; the spoils were divided among the people of Hudaybiyya, and the Prophet freed Safiyya bint Huyayy and married her after her Islam. Al-Sallabi draws from it the Prophet's attesting of Ali's love and the blessing of his prayer, and answers — attributed to his sources — the Rafidi use of the hadith for the imamate.

## In Abla al-Kahlawi's programme 'In the Love of al-Mustafa'
[[person/abla-al-kahlawi|Abla al-Kahlawi]] relates in her programme [[work/fi-hub-al-mustapha|'In the Love of al-Mustafa']] that one of the fortresses of Khaybar resisted everyone who went to take it, and the Prophet said that the next day he would give the banner to a man who loved God and His Messenger and was loved by them, through whom God would grant victory. {cite:assabile-abla-al-kahlawi fi-hub-al-mustapha/ep15 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-abla-al-kahlawi fi-hub-al-mustapha/ep15 00:07:30} She reports that Umar ibn al-Khattab wished he would be the one meant and said he had never wished for the banner as he did that day. {cite:assabile-abla-al-kahlawi fi-hub-al-mustapha/ep15 00:07:30} The Prophet called Ali, she relates, whose eyes were inflamed, laid his hand on them and they were healed, and gave him the banner; Ali mounted his horse and the troops followed him. {cite:assabile-abla-al-kahlawi fi-hub-al-mustapha/ep15 00:08:00} She reports him saying that his armour fell from him and he met them with his bare chest, and that he saw the horses cross, the gate open and the troops enter with him, until the fortress was taken and he returned victorious. {cite:assabile-abla-al-kahlawi fi-hub-al-mustapha/ep15 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-abla-al-kahlawi fi-hub-al-mustapha/ep15 00:09:00}
