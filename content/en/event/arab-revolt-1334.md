## Overview
The Arab Revolt was the rising proclaimed in 1334/1916 by [[person/hussein-bin-ali-al-hashimi|Sharif Husayn b. Ali]] of Mecca against Ottoman rule in the [[place/hijaz|Hijaz]], during the First World War. The Encyclopaedia of Islam has no single article on it; this page gathers what its entries on the Hashimites, Faysal, Abd Allah, the Arabian peninsula and related subjects say. They agree on the outline: a Sharif who had long served as the sultan's representative broke with the [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottoman state]] in June 1916 after negotiating with the British, and his sons led his forces. Different entries describe the revolt differently, as an Arab movement, as a dynastic and separatist rising, and as one promoted by Britain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 283} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 820}

## The Sharif and his sons
The royal houses of the Hijaz, Iraq and Jordan descend from the Dhawu Awn branch of the Hashimite Sharifs of [[place/mecca|Mecca]]. Their rise began with Husayn b. Ali and his sons Ali, Abd Allah and Faysal, whom the Ottoman government brought to [[place/istanbul|Istanbul]]; the youngest son, Zayd, was born there. Husayn obtained appointment as Sharif and Amir of Mecca, became supreme in the Hijaz, and tried to limit the influence of [[person/ibn-saud|Abd al-Aziz Al Saud]] and of the Idrisi in [[place/asir|Asir]]. In this phase the article describes him as the loyal representative of the sultan, ideologically conservative and never close to the Young Turks, relying instead on older Ottoman statesmen. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 283}

## The road to revolt
In 1914 the Unionist government tried to apply centralising measures in the Hijaz, which brought a crisis with the Amir that was settled to his satisfaction. In the early war Husayn looked cautiously to his own interests. He sought an Ottoman guarantee of the Hijaz as a hereditary autonomous amirate, while negotiating with the British in Egypt and with certain secret Arab nationalist societies. His agreement with the British, set out in letters exchanged with Sir Henry McMahon, was not entirely satisfactory to him. The Ottoman government, deaf to his requests, became threatening in the spring of 1916. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 283}

His second son [[person/abdullah-i-of-jordan|Abd Allah]] had joined the Arab Union, an association founded in Cairo by [[person/rashid-rida|Rashid Rida]], and in April 1914 met Lord Kitchener and Ronald Storrs in Egypt, taking part in the talks that led to the revolt. The best known of the Arab nationalist societies, al-Jam'iyya al-Arabiyya al-Fatat, founded in Paris in 1911 by seven Arab students and later centred in Damascus, played an important part in the secret negotiations between Husayn and the British in Cairo. Faysal was a member. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 65} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 454}

Faysal's own route is traced in his entry. Born at Ta'if in 1301/1883, he spent years in Istanbul, returned to Mecca with his father in 1327/1909, took part in expeditions against the Idrisi of Asir and sat in the Ottoman parliament. Resentful of Ottoman severity toward Arab dissidents in Syria in 1915, and admitted to the secret societies, he joined the revolt in 1916. In Damascus, [[person/cemal-pasha|Jamal Pasha]] had hanged eleven prominent Arabs in August 1915 and twenty-one more, including a member of the Ottoman senate, were dealt with without formal trial; the article on him says some of these men had links to the Hijaz movement. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 910} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 558}

## The proclamation
Abd Allah's entry gives the announcement in Mecca as 9 Sha'ban 1334/10 June 1916. The Hashimite article adds that Husayn issued a proclamation in November as malik al-bilad al-arabiyya, king of the Arab countries, while the Allies addressed him only as King of the Hijaz. Another entry mentions a proclamation of June 1916 by the Sharif renouncing allegiance to the Ottoman sultan and denouncing the heretical measures of the Committee of Union and Progress; copies reached as far as the East African coast, where the Muslim population did not respond with a large movement. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 65} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 283} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 928}

## The fighting
The sources give little campaign detail. Faysal commanded the armies of the Sharifian revolt for two years. At the end of the war, as commander of the Northern Arab Army, he was the commander of Allied forces in Syria and Transjordan. Abd Allah, the entry says, played only a minor role in the hostilities. The revolt cut the Hijaz railway; [[person/enver-pasha|Enver Pasha]] nevertheless refused to withdraw the army corps stationed in [[place/medina|Medina]], whose commander learned of the armistice only two and a half months late and surrendered on 10 January 1919. The revolt greatly weakened the Ottoman Fourth Army in Syria, and in early 1917 the British began their attack on Palestine. Faysal was allowed by the British to enter [[place/damascus|Damascus]] on 1 October 1918. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 910} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 283} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 65} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 729} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 558} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 945}

## Contemporary reactions
Responses among Ottoman Muslims differed. The Iraqi poet al-Rusafi, loyal to the Ottoman framework, attacked the Sharif for the revolt, and he was given a cold reception in Damascus when Faysal's government was formed there. In 1917 the Ottoman Committee of Union and Progress sent the poet Mehmed Akif on a mission to the pro-Ottoman Al Rashid at [[place/hail|Hail]] after the outbreak of the Sharif's revolt. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 642} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 1012}

## How the revolt is interpreted
The entries do not agree. One describes the revolt as the culmination of Arab separatism provoked by Ottoman reform, with British promotion of an Arab Revolt against the sultan-caliph. Another calls it a dynastic, separatist rising and says that Faysal's short-lived Arab kingdom in Damascus became the basis of the myth of modern Arab nationalism. A third says the revolt began after the Ottoman government grew threatening and that Britain encouraged it. These are the encyclopaedia's own judgements, and this page does not choose between them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 820} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 580} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 283}

## Aftermath
Faysal represented the Hijaz at the Peace Conference and accepted the crown of Syria from the Syrian General Congress in March 1920; the French defeated his forces at Maysalun on 24 July 1920 and he fled. He became king of Iraq on 23 August 1921. Abd Allah moved to Transjordan in late 1920 and became its amir under the British mandate. Husayn, king of the Hijaz, adopted the title of Caliph in 1924, fled in October 1924 leaving Ali as king, and died in Amman on 4 June 1931; Ali followed him when Ibn Saud completed the conquest of the Hijaz in December 1925. The Hijaz was thus an independent polity for less than ten years, from 1334/1916 to 1343/1924, and has belonged to the House of Saud since 1344/1925. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 283} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 945} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 383}
