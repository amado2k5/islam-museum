## Overview
The battle of al-Qadisiyya was the Muslims' resounding victory over the army of the Sasanian king [[person/yazdegerd-iii|Yazdagird III]], fought at a date that the sources place between the years 14 and 16 AH, that is 635 to 637 CE. The Encyclopaedia of Islam says it opened the route to Ctesiphon-Seleucia, the Sasanians' winter capital, which the Arabs call [[place/al-madain|al-Mada'in]]. It also put the Muslims in a position to win another great success at [[event/battle-of-jalula|Jalula']], and finally, once all of Iraq had fallen to them, to cross the Zagros and begin the conquest of the Iranian plateau. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 405}

The older sources of the museum describe the affair as follows. It began in Muharram 14 AH, when [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar ibn al-Khattab]] consulted his Companions about marching out himself. Then the great battle took place, in which there were the day of Armath, the day of Aghwath, the day of 'Amas, and the night of al-Qadisiyya. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.3 p.480}

## The place
Al-Qadisiyya lay south-west of [[place/al-hira|al-Hira]] and south-south-west of the plain where Kufa was later founded. It stood in the western part of the Taff, the steppe region rising above the cultivated land of the Sawad and marked by springs, such as that of [[place/udhayb|al-Udhayb]], on the way up to the high plateau of the Arabian desert. In Sasanian times the Taff was guarded against Arab raids by a line of watch-houses and a great fortified ditch, and al-Udhayb was the last village before the desert. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 405}

The article reports that the exact site was long unknown. Some scholars had identified it with the early Islamic ruins at al-Ukhaydir, but the article calls that identification erroneous, since the ruins there are dated to the Abbasid period. A. Musil found the real site on an exploring journey in 1912: on the left bank of the valley where the spring of al-Udhayb rises, at the edge of a swampy depression, he was shown ruins that he took to be al-Qadisiyya. The article places them about nineteen miles from Kufa, directly south of Najaf. The place name also occurs in the shorter form Qadis, and the poets called the whole district al-Kawadis. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 405}

Geographers of the fourth century AH, the tenth century CE, describe al-Qadisiyya as a small town with two gates and a mud fortress, set among fields and date-palm groves watered by a canal from the Euphrates. Al-Mas'udi notes that the main arm of the Euphrates once flowed toward al-Hira between al-Qadisiyya and al-Udhayb, in a bed called al-Atiq, the old river, and that a bridge crossed it at al-Qadisiyya. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 405} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 406}

The same article also lists an unrelated al-Qadisiyya on the east bank of the Tigris, south-east of Samarra, an octagonal walled town of the Abbasid period; this page concerns only the battlefield in southern Iraq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 405}

## Sources for the battle
Al-Tabari gave the single battle about a hundred pages, and nearly as many again to the events before and after it. The article adds that most of his account rests on traditions of Sayf ibn Umar, whom some Islamists accuse of falsification. It does not examine those accusations, but notes that Sayf's version also underlies most Arab and Persian historians, so that any account has to use him, correcting him where possible. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 406}

## Background
After the Muslims' heavy defeat at [[event/battle-of-the-bridge|the Bridge]], the Iraq campaign suffered a setback, despite reinforcements from Medina, the victory of [[event/battle-of-al-buwayb|al-Buwayb]] and several raids. [[person/al-muthanna-ibn-haritha|Al-Muthanna]] feared that Yazdagird III was preparing a vigorous reaction. He therefore withdrew to the edge of the desert, scattered his men to watering places, limited himself to raiding, and appealed to Medina for help. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 406}

Umar made a general levy among the Bedouin and assembled and equipped an army at Sirar, near Medina, under Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas, whom he ordered to leave for Iraq. The article gives the dates of the levy and of the muster only with question marks, between 13 and 15 AH. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 406}

## The march
On the way, other contingents sent by Umar joined Sa'd. At the beginning of winter he halted in the Najd at Zarud, or at al-Tha'labiyya near it, to call out the tribes of the area. He then went to Sharaf, at the western end of the Arab plateau, which was well supplied with waterholes. There he waited in an organised camp for several thousand more Bedouin, among them some Christian bands, divided his men into groups of ten, set up a chain of command, and sent [[person/al-mughira-ibn-shuba|al-Mughira ibn Shu'ba]] to occupy various places and protect his forces. Al-Muthanna was to meet him at Sharaf, but died that winter of the wounds he had received at the Bridge. Sa'd then advanced to Udhayb al-Hijanat, left there the women who had followed the expedition, and camped on the plain of al-Qadisiyya. While waiting for the enemy, for about a month according to the article, he carried out raids to secure supplies. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 406}

## The battle
Meanwhile Yazdagird had assembled a large army and compelled his marshal [[person/rustam-farrukhzad|Rustam]] to advance, although Rustam was reluctant to meet the Arabs on ground he thought unfavourable. The Persians camped across the channel called al-Atiq from the Muslims. Negotiations followed and came to nothing. In Muharram Rustam built a kind of dyke of reeds, earth, straw and pack-saddles, which let his army cross the channel, and battle began the next day. The article puts this on a Monday in Muharram 15 or 16 AH, and calls the date of 14 given by Sayf an error. The Persians had the channel behind them, and the Muslims the moat of a fortress. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 406}

Sa'd suffered from abscesses on his thighs and hips and could neither ride nor stand. He lay on the fortress and sent his orders by notes to his lieutenant [[person/khalid-ibn-urfuta|Khalid ibn Urfuta]]. According to Sayf, the fighting lasted three days and one night; as the night drew to a close the Persian retreat began, and by the fourth day they were wholly routed. Rustam fell in the melee, killed by an unknown Arab warrior. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 406}

The article lists the names of the days and nights. The first day was Yawm Armath, which it glosses as the Day of the Rafts and suggests may refer to the dyke over the channel. The second was Yawm Aghwath, the Day of Help, perhaps alluding to reinforcements from Syria. The third was Yawm 'Amas, the Day of the Hard War, followed by Laylat al-Harir, the Night of the Cries of Sorrow. The end of that night was Laylat al-Qadisiyya, and the fourth day was Yawm al-Qadisiyya. The glosses are the article's own tentative suggestions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 406}

The article summarises further details from al-Tabari, as presented by Caetani. The Muslims were very poorly equipped. The Persians used a number of elephants, but their opponents found a way out of that difficulty. Syrian reinforcements arrived on the second and third days. The poet Abu Mihjan, who had been put in irons, nevertheless played a valiant part. [[person/tulayha-ibn-khuwaylid|Tulayha ibn Khuwaylid]], the false prophet whom Khalid ibn al-Walid had defeated in the wars of apostasy, and who later joined the Muslims, proved a brave defender of Islam. In the first two days the fighting was by day only, and the nights went to burying the dead, giving water to the dying and carrying off the wounded Muslims, while wounded enemies were finished off with blows of a staff. The article reports, with a question mark over the figure, that thirty thousand Persians who had chained themselves together to resist to the end fled when defeat was certain and drowned in the channel, which the pursuing Muslims then crossed using their bodies as a bridge. The Muslims also seized the great Persian royal banner, Dirafsh-i Kaviyan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 406}

## Aftermath
Sa'd sent part of his force after the fugitives and stayed at al-Qadisiyya for another month. One of the Persian officers, [[person/jalinus|Jalinus]], was killed while covering the retreat. Later attempts to regroup, for example in the district of Babil, came to only a few battles that did not change the situation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 406} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 407}

Sa'd then marched on al-Mada'in and, after besieging and taking Bahurasir, one of the seven or ten towns that made up the group, was able to ford the Tigris, an event regarded as the result of divine favour. On the east bank the Muslims occupied the other towns of al-Mada'in, which Yazdagird had abandoned without a fight. A further important battle on Iraqi soil followed at Jalula', which the article says marked the definitive end of Sasanian rule in Iraq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 407}

## Problems of numbers and date
The article states two problems that in all likelihood must stay unsolved. The first is the total strength of the forces: the figures in the sources differ too much for any evaluation to rest on solid ground. The second is chronology: the sources date the battle between 14 and 16 AH. The article's author rejects the earlier date because of too much circumstantial evidence against it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 407}

Scholars who worked on the question took different routes. Wellhausen, Caetani and S. M. Yusuf judged it impossible for the Muslims to recover so soon after the Bridge, dated al-Buwayb to 14 AH, and so put al-Qadisiyya at the beginning of 16. Caetani and Yusuf also held that Umar could not turn to Iraq until the Syrian campaign ended at [[event/battle-of-yarmuk|Yarmuk]], which the article dates to 12 Rajab 15 AH, 20 August 636 CE. Yusuf placed al-Qadisiyya a little after Rajab 15, a month after Yarmuk. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 407}

The article's author, after re-examining the sources, prefers Muharram 15 AH, February to March 636 CE. His reasons are as follows. Al-Buwayb soon after the Bridge is not impossible, since troubles at al-Mada'in kept the Persians from exploiting their win and Muslim reinforcements arrived at once. Placing Umar's decision after Yarmuk would leave too little time between the two battles for Sa'd's march and wait, which the sources describe in detail. Placing it in the month before Yarmuk would have Umar sending forces to Iraq just when he was preoccupied by the Byzantine emperor's preparations. If Sayf is right about the muster at Sirar in Muharram 14 and the long march, then Umar decided at the end of 13 to renew the Iraq campaign, while the Syrian victories were following one another. The author says that other circumstances supporting this will be set out in another work. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 407}

## The date

al-Tabari reports that al-Waqidi fixed the year as 14 AH, Ibn Ishaq said 15, and some people of Kufa said 16. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.3 p.590} The year is therefore disputed.

## Before the battle
al-Baladhuri relates that the Muslims wrote to Umar about the large number of the Persians and asked him for reinforcements. He wanted to march out himself, but al-Abbas and a group of the elder Companions advised him to stay and to dispatch the armies, so he sent Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas. The account says that the Persian army numbered about a hundred and twenty thousand, with thirty elephants, and that the Muslims numbered nine to ten thousand. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

## The duels and the fighting
The account mentions the dispatch of al-Mughira ibn Shu'ba to Rustam, and then of Amr ibn Ma'dikarib and al-Ash'ath ibn Qays. It tells the story of Abu Mihjan al-Thaqafi and the permission that Sa'd's wife gave him to fight. It says that Rustam was killed, that his killer was not known, and that his death was attributed to a number of men, and that the Persians fled to al-Mada'in. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

al-Mas'udi named its days and nights: the day of Aghwath, the night of al-Harir, the night of al-Sawad, the day of 'Amas, and the night of al-Qadisiyya. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.609-613}

It needs an additional source for the numbers of the two armies and the names of the slayers.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lecture series Al Dinu Al Qayem

The speaker recounts Umar's letter to Sa'd at Qadisiyya: the Muslims are given victory through their obedience to God and their enemy's disobedience, and if both sides are equal in disobedience, the enemy prevails by numbers and arms {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep02 00:25:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep13 01:13:00}.

He says that the sins of an army are more to be feared for it than its enemy {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep02 00:25:30}. In the speaker's own opinion, religion does not separate from politics, religion covers everything, and Umar's letter to Sa'd expresses this principle {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep02 00:26:00}.

He recounts Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas praying before Qadisiyya, and the story of the companion Sawad ibn Abdullah, whom the Prophet struck on the back with a stick while straightening the rows, and who spoke to him about it {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep10 00:08:00}.

In the judge's sermon as the speaker recites it, Qadisiyya is listed among the battles, under Sa'd's command {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep07 00:29:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep07 00:30:30}.

He recalls that Abu Lu'lu'a passed Persian captives in the days of Qadisiyya, in the context of his account of Umar's death, in a passage that is partly unclear {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep10 00:44:30}.

## In al-Dinawari
[[person/abu-hanifa-al-dinawari|Al-Dinawari]] reports that about twenty thousand men gathered for Sa'd, and that Yazdagird was crowned as a boy of sixteen and gave the command to Rustam ibn Hurmuz. {cite:akhbar-tiwal-amir p. 119} Rustam camped at Dayr al-A'war for four months to wear the Arabs down. {cite:akhbar-tiwal-amir p. 120} An abscess on his thigh kept Sa'd from riding, so he gave command to Khalid ibn Urfuta and stayed in the palace of al-Qadisiyya. {cite:akhbar-tiwal-amir p. 121} The Persians drew up in thirteen ranks and the Arabs in three. {cite:akhbar-tiwal-amir p. 122} Rustam was found with a hundred wounds, and no one knew who had killed him; some say he drowned in the river of al-Qadisiyya. {cite:akhbar-tiwal-amir p. 123}

## In Khalifa ibn Khayyat
[[person/khalifa-ibn-khayyat|Khalifa ibn Khayyat]] records Qadisiyya among the events of the year 15, with Sa'd ibn Malik, that is Ibn Abi Waqqas, commanding the Muslims and Rustam, with al-Jalinus and Dhu al-Hajib, commanding the Persians. He gives several reports on the numbers: from Abu Wa'il, that the Muslims were between seven and eight thousand against Rustam's sixty thousand; from Khalid ibn Umayr, that the Persians were forty thousand, with seventy elephants in one report; and from Ibn Ishaq, that Rustam had sixty thousand of his picked troops against six or seven thousand Muslims. {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 131}
He then reports from Abu al-Hasan al-Mada'ini that Sa'd followed the defeated army from Qadisiyya: the people of al-Hira and the lord of Nahr Bistam made terms with him, he routed forces at Nars, Kutha and Dayr Ka'b, and at last camped before al-Mada'in and took it. {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 133}

## From Omar Abdelkafy's series on the Hikam of Ibn Ata Allah
Omar Abdelkafy relates that when news of the Muslims' victory at al-Qadisiyya reached Umar ibn al-Khattab, he was told of the commanders who had been killed, and then that others had been killed whom he did not know, and Umar wept for them. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep05 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep05 00:02:00}
