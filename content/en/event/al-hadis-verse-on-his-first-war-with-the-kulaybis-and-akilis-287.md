## Overview
Under the words that this was the end of al-Hadi's first war with the Kulaybis and the Akilis at [[place/allaf-yemen|Allaf]] and [[place/saada|Sa'da]], the narrator sets a poem he says [[person/al-hadi-yahya-al-rassi|al-Hadi]] spoke on it on one rhyme; the editor's footnotes give the other manuscript's variant readings. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.198} {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.199} {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.200} By sense, the poem says that war's calm has come after wakefulness and that life is sweet after longing, that the enemy saw death and left their houses, crops, many vines, slaves, mail, weapons, goods, clothes and families behind, threw down their arms and scattered into hard mountains, and that his men followed and killed those they reached. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.198} {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.199}

## Detail
It describes the camp of those he calls the wrongdoers as left with nothing new or old, takes satisfaction in their humbling, blames the Akili leader [[person/ahmad-ibn-abbad-al-akili|Ahmad ibn Abbad]] and his followers for opposing the right, says the fortification of the Allaf road did not hold and that he broke through, and warns that no mountain would save the man and that he will pursue him soon. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.199} The closing lines say the imam stood for the truth and those who stood with him, with brave men for whom he trusts in war, and that they are God's soldiers on earth whose banner of truth flies, as its thunder and lightning are felt. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.199} {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.200} The poem's abuse of its opponents is not repeated here, and its claims are the imam's own and his follower's, to which the museum adds no verdict. It follows [[event/ahmad-ibn-abbad-asks-safety-is-refused-and-goes-to-iraq-287]].
