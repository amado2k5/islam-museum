## Overview
Al-Baladhuri relates in "Futuh al-Buldan" that groups of the Arabs withheld the alms after [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] became caliph, and some of them said that they perform the prayer but do not pay the zakat. Abu Bakr resolved to fight them. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.131-133}

## The position on those who withheld alms
Al-Baladhuri says that Abu Bakr said he would fight them even if they withheld a cord (iqal), and in another account "a kid goat" ('anaq). For him the iqal was the alms of the year. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.131-133}

He quotes Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud that after the death of the Prophet the Muslims were on the brink of destruction, had it not been for God's grace through Abu Bakr, since the opinion of many of them had leaned toward abandoning the fight over the zakat. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.131-133}

In the report of Tariq ibn Shihab, Abu Bakr gave them the choice between "the war that clears" and "the humiliating peace", and the report mentions the terms of the peace. The report quotes Aisha on what came upon her father when the Arabs apostatised and hypocrisy appeared in Medina. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.131-133}

## The campaigns
Abu Bakr set out to al-Qassa and sent armies against the apostates. Kharija ibn Hisn and Mandhur ibn Zaban al-Fazariyyan held them back, and Talha ibn Ubayd Allah followed them. Then Abu Bakr appointed [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] over the people, and [[person/thabit-ibn-qays|Thabit ibn Qays]] over the Ansar. He began with Tulayha ibn Khuwaylid at Buzakha, then battles took place at al-Yamama, Bahrain, Oman, Yemen, Hadramawt, Mecca, Tihama and Iraq. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.131-133}

The details of the dates and places need an additional source.

## The academic reading: what the term covers
The pages above give the account of the early Sunni sources, in which the central issue is the refusal of the alms and the caliph's decision to fight. What follows is a separate reading, that of the article on al-Ridda in the supplement to the second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam. The museum reports both and gives no verdict between them.

The article explains that ridda, literally apostasy, is the name Islamic historiography gives to a series of battles against tribes, both nomadic and settled. It says they began shortly before the death of [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] and went on throughout the caliphate of Abu Bakr. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 765}

The article judges that in many cases the term is a misnomer, because numerous tribes and communities had had no contact with the Muslim state or had no formal agreements with it. It adds that most of the tribes that had been under Medinan domination merely stopped paying taxes while stating that they were ready to go on practising Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 765}

## Leaders who posed as prophets
According to the article, several tribes were led by chieftains who presented themselves as prophets. It names Abhala al-Ansi in Yemen, nicknamed al-Aswad, the black one, and also Dhu al-Khimar, the veiled one; [[person/musaylima|Musaylima]] of the [[group/banu-hanifa|Hanifa]] in Yamama; [[person/tulayha-ibn-khuwaylid|Tulayha]] of the [[group/banu-asad|Asad]] and [[person/sajah|Sajah]] of the [[group/banu-tamim|Tamim]], both in Najd; and Dhu al-Taj Lakit ibn Malik of the [[group/al-azd|Azd]] in Oman. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 765}

## Muhammad's arrangements and the Muslim enclaves
The article gives its own view that, had Muhammad not died prematurely, his effective methods would have given Islam a firmer footing in tribal Arabia. It describes how he would give a tribal representative authority over both the Muslims and the pagans of that tribe, with the instruction to fight those who turn away together with those who come forward. As a result, in many tribes Muslims and pagans neutralised each other. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 765}

Its example is the [[group/madhhij|Madhhij]]. There Farwa ibn Musayk, the Prophet's representative to the Murad, the Zubayd and the rest of the Madhhij, faced a frustrated rival, [[person/amr-ibn-madikarib|Amr ibn Ma'dikarib]] al-Zubaydi, who rebelled when Muhammad died. Because there were Muslim enclaves in many tribes, the article says, the Prophet and Abu Bakr could confront the rebels by means of messengers and letters. Many Muslims were nevertheless killed by the rebels, and the Muslim representatives were driven out. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 765} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 766}

## The difficulty of the sources
The article cautions that, beyond the general outline, reconstructing the course of events is complicated by many contradictory accounts, often apologetic or polemical in nature. It explains that this chapter of tribal history mattered greatly to the tribal informants who handed the accounts down, and that they often improved the place of a tribal leader in history. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 766}

## Oman, Mahra and Hadramawt
The article reports that Lakit was besieged at Daba, and that the besiegers were joined by other tribal forces under [[person/ikrima-ibn-abi-jahl|Ikrima ibn Abi Jahl]]; troops of the Najiya and of the [[group/abd-al-qays|Abd al-Qays]] also supported the Muslims. See also the record of the [[event/oman-campaign-of-the-ridda|campaign in Oman]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 767}

Ikrima then marched into the land of the [[place/mahra|Mahra]], with tribal units that included a subdivision of the Azd and a branch of the Tamim. Among the Mahra there was internal strife, and the weaker party allied itself with the Muslims, which the article glosses as converting to Islam. After the stronger party was subdued, the leader of the weaker one brought a fifth of the spoils to Medina. When Ikrima went on to [[place/hadramawt|Hadramawt]], his army also included Mahra warriors. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 767}

## Bahrayn
Shortly after the Prophet's death his governor in Bahrayn, [[person/al-mundhir-ibn-sawa|al-Mundhir ibn Sawa]] of the Tamim, also died. The article says that the Qays ibn Tha'laba of the [[group/bakr-ibn-wail|Bakr ibn Wa'il]], and the whole of the Rabi'a, rebelled under al-Hutam, while al-Jarud of the Abd al-Qays remained steadfastly loyal to Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 767}

Al-Ala ibn al-Hadrami, a client of the Quraysh who had replaced al-Mundhir as governor, fought the rebels together with the Arabs and Persians who joined him, and he had a large Tamim force behind him. Al-Hutam held [[place/al-qatif|al-Qatif]], Hajar, Darin and al-Khatt, and the Muslims were besieged in their stronghold at Juwatha until al-Ala rescued them. The rebels were beaten on the mainland and fled to the island of Darin, which the Muslims took after, as the article puts it, crossing the sea miraculously. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 767}

## A prelude to the conquests
The article sees the ridda as a prelude to the wider conquests. In its view the Qurashi generals gained valuable experience in mobilising large multi-tribal armies over long distances, and they benefited from the close acquaintance of the [[group/quraysh|Quraysh]] with tribal politics throughout Arabia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 767}

It gives Khalid ibn al-Walid a crucial role and notes that his mother was a nomad. The Bedouin way of life was not foreign to him: he is said to have eaten a lizard that Muhammad, watching, found repellent. Already at the [[event/conquest-of-mecca|conquest of Mecca]] in 8/630 he led a troop of nomads, and at the [[event/battle-of-hunayn|battle of Hunayn]] soon after he led the nomadic [[group/banu-sulaym|Sulaym]] in the vanguard. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 767}

## Effects on the relation between the centre and the tribes
The article holds that the ridda changed for ever the relationship between the central government and the strong tribes of Arabia. The tribes were trying to undo whatever ascendancy the Muslim state had gained in Muhammad's lifetime, but they were overpowered by large expeditionary forces under capable Muslim generals. The Muslims' successes secured the cooperation of the tribes living between Medina and the rebellious ones. Medina restored its prestige and inflicted the severest punishments on those who had killed Muslims earlier in the fighting. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 767}

## Settlements in Yamama
The article describes new realities on the ground. Khalid's treaty with the Hanifa required that he receive one orchard and one field of his choice in every village the treaty covered, while the villages of [[place/al-yamama|Yamama]] outside it bore the full consequences of defeat. The people of the village of Mar'at were enslaved and a group of the Tamim settled there. Al-Haddar, Musaylima's home town, was also outside the treaty; Khalid enslaved its people and settled there a Tamimi clan. The article reads these changes as showing how local Tamim groups gained by cooperating with Khalid in Yamama. For the fighting there, see the record of the [[event/battle-of-yamama|battle of Yamama]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 767}

It lists several other villages left outside the treaty, and says that the village of al-Majaza was inhabited by the Hizzan of the Anaza and by people of mixed descent, including clients of the Quraysh, who settled there after the ridda. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 767}

## State grazing grounds
The article links the new balance of power to the state's takeover of tribal protected pastures, the [[concept/hima-protected-pasture|hima]]. It says that the thousands of camels and other animals taken as booty in the last years of Muhammad's life, together with those collected as taxes, needed large grazing lands, and that influential Qurashis such as [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman ibn Affan]], [[person/zubayr-ibn-al-awwam|al-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam]] and [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-awf|Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf]] competed with the state as owners of large herds. Abd al-Rahman is said to have left his heirs 1,000 camels, 3,000 ewes and 100 horses. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 767}

According to the article, Muhammad declared the pasture of al-Naki, about 120 km south of Medina, a state hima for the Muslims' horses, probably at the expense of the Sulaym, and put a man of the Muzayna in charge of it. He is also reported to have abolished tribal pastures by declaring that the only legitimate hima belongs to God and His messenger. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 767}

Under Abu Bakr, the article says, the state pastures kept growing. After the fighting against the Abs and the Dhubyan at al-Abrak, in the area of [[place/al-rabadha|al-Rabadha]] some 200 km east of Medina, Abu Bakr took the territory of the Dhubyan and expelled its owners, made al-Abrak a hima for the Muslims' horses, and allowed everyone to graze the rest of al-Rabadha at the expense of the Tha'laba. Later he declared the whole of al-Rabadha a hima for camels collected as alms. The article notes that one report credits Umar ibn al-Khattab with expelling the Tha'laba, and another says Muhammad had already created the state hima there, in which case Abu Bakr was only re-establishing state authority. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 767}

## For Explorers
Al-Baladhuri's Futuh al-Buldan tells that groups among the Arabs withheld the sadaqa after Abu Bakr took the caliphate, some saying they would keep the prayer but not pay the zakat, and Abu Bakr resolved to fight them — for even a hobbling cord, 'or a she-kid' in another telling, the cord meaning the year's sadaqa. Ibn Mas'ud is quoted that the Muslims were near ruin but for God's favor through Abu Bakr, many leaning toward not fighting over the zakat; and Abu Bakr offered the choice of 'open war' or 'shameful peace'. He marched to al-Qassa and sent the armies — Kharija ibn Hisn and Manzur ibn Zaban turned them back, Talha ibn Ubaydullah following — then gave the standard to Khalid ibn al-Walid over the people and Thabit ibn Qays over the Ansar, beginning with Tulayha at Buzakha, then Yamama, Bahrain, Oman, Yemen, Hadramawt, Mecca, Tihama and Iraq.

## For Scholars
Al-Baladhuri's account in Futuh al-Buldan tells that groups among the Arabs withheld the sadaqa after Abu Bakr took the caliphate, some saying they would keep the prayer but not pay the zakat, and Abu Bakr resolved to fight them. Al-Baladhuri records Abu Bakr saying he would fight them for even a hobbling cord — 'or a she-kid' in another telling — the cord meaning the year's sadaqa. He quotes Abdullah ibn Mas'ud that the Muslims after the Prophet's death were near ruin but for God's favor through Abu Bakr, as many of them leaned toward not fighting over the zakat. In Tariq ibn Shihab's telling Abu Bakr gave them the choice of 'open war' or 'shameful peace' and set out the peace terms; and the account relays Aisha's description of what befell her father when the Arabs apostatized and hypocrisy appeared in Medina. Abu Bakr marched to al-Qassa and sent the armies to the apostates — Kharija ibn Hisn and Manzur ibn Zaban al-Fazari turned them back with Talha ibn Ubaydullah following. Then Abu Bakr gave the standard to Khalid ibn al-Walid over the people and Thabit ibn Qays over the Ansar, beginning with Tulayha ibn Khuwaylid at Buzakha, then the events of Yamama, Bahrain, Oman, Yemen, Hadramawt, Mecca, Tihama and Iraq.
