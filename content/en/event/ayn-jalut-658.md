## Overview
In 658 AH the Tatars crossed the Euphrates and reached Aleppo and then Damascus. In Sha'ban the Egyptians went out under the command of al-Muzaffar [[person/qutuz|Qutuz]], with [[person/baybars|Baybars]] al-Bunduqdari leading the vanguard. They met at 'Ayn Jalut on Friday, the fifteenth of Ramadan, and the Tatars were routed. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p. 720} The Encyclopaedia of Islam knows 'Ayn Jalut chiefly as the site of this famous battle and dates it to Friday 25 Ramadan 658, which it equates with 3 September 1260. The two sources thus differ over the day of the month. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 836} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 835}

## The place
'Ayn Jalut means the spring of Goliath. The medieval geographers mention it as a village between [[place/baysan|Baysan]] and [[place/nablus|Nablus]], in the military district of Filastin, standing at the head of the Wadi Jalut. It is said to owe its name to a tradition that [[person/dawud|David]] killed Goliath beside it. Crusader chronicles call the neighbourhood Tubania or Tubanie. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 835}

The place first appears in the sources in Jumada II 578, September 1183, when the armies of [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Saladin]] and of the Franks camped there face to face and then parted without fighting. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 835}

## The battle
According to the article, the Mamluk army was commanded by Baybars, and the Mongol general was Kitbuga, the [[person/kitbugha|Kitbugha]] of the museum's records. Estimates of the numbers differ. The Syriac and Arabic texts of Bar-Hebraeus put the Mamluk force at 120,000 and the Mongols at 10,000 horsemen, while Rashid al-Din speaks of only a few thousand. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 835} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 836}

The Mongol force and its Christian auxiliaries at first drove the Mamluk left wing back, or the vanguard, according to other accounts. They were then overcome and destroyed by the main body of the Mamluk army. The article says that Kitbuga was captured and put to death. The record of Kitbugha elsewhere in the museum, following Ibn al-Imad, says he fell in the fighting, so the sources differ on how he died. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 836}

The article also touches on the part played by the sultan and by Baybars. In its entry on Baybars it says that [[person/qutuz|Sultan Qutuz]] distinguished himself at 'Ayn Jalut with great valour, that the Mongol general was killed, and that Baybars fought in the vanguard. It describes the Egyptian success as decisive and credits the tenacity of a sultan who had managed to field an army against the odds. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1185} The article on 'Ayn Jalut itself adds that the chronicler Ibn 'Abd al-Zahir takes pains to stress Baybars' vital contribution to the victory. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 836}

## How the sources judge it
The Arabic chroniclers, above all the Egyptian ones, regard the battle as a decisive victory that saved the Syro-Egyptian realm, and indeed Islam itself, from the Mongol menace. For the first time a Mongol army had been beaten in a pitched battle. The article adds that the Mamluk army was largely Turkish and defeated the Mongols with their own methods of fighting. In the chroniclers' view this meant that the energies of the steppe peoples were now serving Islam. The article cites remarks of [[person/abu-shama-al-dimashqi|Abu Shama]] and of Yunini in this sense, and points to comments of [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]] on the role of the steppe peoples in renewing Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 836}

Sources sympathetic to the Mongols tell it differently. They present the battle as an inconclusive clash in which a small Mongol force was overwhelmed by far greater numbers and was spared revenge only because Hulegu was busy with more important matters. The museum reports both readings and gives no verdict. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 836}

## Consequences
[[person/hulagu|Hulegu]] was furious at the defeat and prepared a punitive expedition to Syria. According to the article he was prevented from sending it by the struggle inside the [[polity/mongol-empire|Mongol empire]] that followed the death of Mongke Khan in September 1259. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 836}

The victory did not end the danger. The Mongols still held Mesopotamia and Iraq and threatened Syria from the north and the east. The article nevertheless calls 'Ayn Jalut the high water mark of the Mongol advance, and judges it likely that the ebbing of the tide was due at least as much to events in the East as to Mamluk resistance. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 836}

## After the battle
al-Muzaffar entered Damascus, and Baybars pursued the Tatars into the lands of Aleppo. The sultan promised him Aleppo and then went back on it, and according to al-Suyuti this was the beginning of estrangement between them. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p. 720}

## Ibn al-Imad's account
He reports that the Christians rose up in Damascus and raised the cross, and that the Tatar commander Katbugha was killed in the battle west of Baysan. Plunder and killing of Christians in Damascus followed. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 299}

## Sources named in the article
The article names as the contemporary Egyptian accounts the two biographers of Baybars, Ibn Shaddad and [[person/ibn-abd-al-zahir|Ibn 'Abd al-Zahir]]. Ibn Shaddad's account of the battle is lost from the surviving fragment of his work. Ibn 'Abd al-Zahir's narrative seems to underlie most later Egyptian historians. Among the Persian sources the chief one is Rashid al-Din. The article also lists Syrian and Iraqi accounts and brief allusions in Frankish and Eastern Christian sources. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 836}
