## Overview
A major battle between the Muslims and the Byzantines on the river [[place/yarmuk|Yarmuk]], part of the [[event/conquest-of-syria|conquest of Syria]]. The commander of the Muslims was [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah]]. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407} The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, treats it as the battle that decided the fate of [[polity/byzantine-empire|Byzantine]] Syria and dates its end to 20 August 636. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 301} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302}

## Disagreement over its date
al-Waqidi says it was in 15 AH, and al-Tabari gives the report of Sayf that it was in 13 AH. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.3 p.441} al-Baladhuri says that it was in Rajab 15 AH. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam gives 12 Rajab 15 AH, that is 20 August 636, as the day the battle ended, and 13 Jumada II 15 AH, that is 23 July 636, for the first clash. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302} Khalifa ibn Khayyat, quoting Ibn al-Kalbi, gives a different day, as set out below.

## The Yarmuk river
The Yarmuk is the main left-bank tributary of the [[place/jordan-river|Jordan]]. The Encyclopaedia of Islam article says it joins the Jordan about 9 km south of Lake Tiberias, with its headwaters on the south-western slopes of the Hawran in southern Syria. It runs through a deeply cut valley, which is nevertheless the main way through the eastern wall of the rift valley, the [[place/ghawr|Ghawr]], to the north-south routes along the western edge of the Syrian Desert. For that reason a narrow-gauge railway from Haifa by way of Samakh to Dar'a followed it, where it met the Hijaz railway. Pliny the Elder called the river Hieromix or Hieromices, and part of its lower course now forms the border between Syria and Jordan. The article also mentions the idea of diverting its lowest stretch into Lake Tiberias as a reservoir to irrigate the Jordan valley. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 301}

## Background and the site
The article says the battle was fought near the meeting of the Yarmuk and the Wadi al-Rukkad, which now marks part of the border between Jordan and Syria, and that it decided the fate of Byzantine Syria. [[person/heraclius|Heraclius]], who was not present in person, gathered in early 636 a very large force of Byzantines and Christian Arabs, in order to reverse the recent Muslim victories in Syria, Transjordan and Palestine and to drive the Muslims out. The site offered high ground, water, pasture and command of the important routes between [[place/damascus|Damascus]] and Galilee. It was an important settled base and a key pasture land of the [[group/ghassan|Ghassan]], and it lay near the meeting point of four Byzantine provincial boundaries. A major battle had been fought near it in 614, when the Persian general [[person/shahrbaraz|Shahrbaraz]] defeated the Byzantines and opened the way into Palestine for the Persians. The article notes that both sides understood the strategic value of the ground, and that on the eve of the battle the Byzantines had not devised any effective new tactics or strategy to check the Muslims. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 301}

The article describes the Byzantine side as troubled by supply problems. Its commanders had little experience of handling and feeding such large numbers in this region, and they found it hard to obtain supplies from the local people; Mansur son of Sergius, the fiscal official at Damascus, refused to supply the unprecedentedly large force. The army also contained mixed ethnic groups, many of whom did not know the country, and there was probably mistrust and friction among Greeks, Armenians and Christian Arabs. The article finds no sign that the local inhabitants fought on either side, and says the Byzantines were still shaken by a series of recent defeats. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302}

## Forces and manoeuvres
According to the article, the battle, which it also calls the battle of al-Jabiya, lasted more than a month if the preliminary manoeuvres are counted. It began near al-Jabiya, the traditional base of the Ghassan, and ended on 12 Rajab 15 AH, that is 20 August 636. The Byzantine forces came from [[place/homs|Homs]] under the general Vahan, probably the commander of the eastern field army, and under Theodore Trithurios, the treasurer. [[person/jabala-ibn-al-ayham|Jabala ibn al-Ayham]], king of the Ghassan, led the Ghassanid troops, and other Christian Arabs whom Heraclius had recruited in upper Mesopotamia and elsewhere also took part. Reports conflict on whether Theodore, the brother of Heraclius, was present; the article thinks that he took part in planning some of the campaign but had probably been recalled to Constantinople in disgrace before the final stage. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302}

The Muslims under [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah]] withdrew from Homs and Damascus when stronger Byzantine armies approached from the north. They fell back to a line between Dayr Ayyub and [[place/adhriat|Adhri'at]] and waited there for more than a month in a naturally strong position, to deter any Byzantine advance further south. On 13 Jumada II 15 AH, that is 23 July 636, the Muslims won an early clash near al-Jabiya. The Byzantines used the waiting time to accustom their troops to the Muslims and tried, without success, to stir up desertion and discord in the Muslim ranks. Both sides received reinforcements, but when the decisive clash came the Muslims were still gaining more. The article says the Byzantines and their Christian Arab allies probably had the larger army; it adds that all figures for the battle, of combatants and of casualties alike, should be treated with suspicion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302}

## The course of the battle
By a feigned retreat the Muslims drew the Byzantines into attacking them and their camp near Dayr Ayyub. The Muslims broke into the exposed Byzantine left flank and then used the gaps that opened between the Byzantine infantry and cavalry. The article says the infantry apparently tried a risky and complicated manoeuvre, the so-called mixed formation, in which the foot opened their ranks to let horsemen through and then locked their shields again; poor coordination let the Muslims exploit the gap and kill many exposed foot soldiers. The Byzantines then withdrew into the ground between the Wadi al-Rukkad and the Wadi al-Allan, west of the Wadi al-Harir, which they took to be a safe camp protected by the high bluffs of the wadis. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302}

In a night raid the Muslims under [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] seized the bridge over the Wadi al-Rukkad, the only workable line of retreat for the encircled men and animals. The Byzantines found themselves blocked: they could not retreat in order, fight their way out, or negotiate acceptable terms, and they panicked. The Muslims stormed their camps between the wadis and at the village of Yaqusa, at the edge of the Jawlan. The Byzantines lost cohesion and most were killed, though a few may have escaped down the steep wadi walls; the Muslims took few or no prisoners. The article reports that some Christian Arabs allegedly wavered in their loyalty and fled, which helped the Muslims, and that one Muslim tradition has some downcast Byzantine soldiers, aware of their position, awaiting death with resignation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302}

## Aftermath
The article says the battle destroyed the one viable Byzantine army in Syria, and a rout followed. For the Muslims the victory ruled out any Byzantine advance further south, confirmed their hold on Palestine and Transjordan, and opened the way to the conquest of the valley of the [[place/biqa|Biqa]], of Damascus and of all Syria beyond. They followed up with a fast, far-reaching and relentless pursuit that gave the Byzantines no respite. The psychological effect was great as well as the material one: the article says the battle broke the Byzantine will to fight further open battles, so that from then on they avoided open battle with the Muslims in Syria and upper Mesopotamia and gave up trying to recover or hold Syria. Heraclius was then staying first at Homs and then at [[place/antioch|Antioch]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302}

## Sources and causes of the result
The article says that no documents or eyewitness accounts survive and that memory of the real course of the battle soon faded and was embellished with legend, while the scale of the Muslim victory was recognised at once. It judges that the account preserved by [[person/ibn-asakir|Ibn Asakir]] in his history of Damascus is probably the best. Muslim losses were considerable, but far smaller than the human and material losses of the [[polity/byzantine-empire|Byzantines]]. The article adds that the Strategikon of Maurice, a military manual written about 600, gives reliable insight into the fighting methods and supply arrangements the Byzantines probably used. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302}

On the causes of the outcome the article reports that the ruling Heraclian dynasty seems to have tried to move the blame elsewhere: to an alleged failed rebellion by the Armenian general Vahan, to the failure to heed the emperor's warnings about Arab ambushes, or to the climate. It finds no evidence that Christian religious division affected the result. It notes that Muslim writers later stressed religious zeal, but its author judges that better Muslim leadership and higher morale, including the confidence that came from their recent successes, were important factors. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302}

## al-Baladhuri's account
[[person/heraclius|Heraclius]] gathered hosts of Byzantines, the people of Syria, al-Jazira and Armenia, numbering about two hundred thousand, and he put at their head [[person/jabala-ibn-al-ayham|Jabala ibn al-Ayham]] al-Ghassani. The Muslims numbered twenty-four thousand. They fought very fiercely, and about seventy thousand Byzantines were killed. Women of the Muslims also fought that day, among them [[person/hind-bint-utba|Hind bint Utba]]. The eye of [[person/al-ash-ath-ibn-qays|al-Ash'ath ibn Qays]] and the eye of [[person/hashim-ibn-utba|Hashim ibn Utba]] were lost. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

## After the defeat
In al-Tabari's account, Abu Ubayda called for the march after the defeat, and the Muslims halted at Marj al-Saffar, and then at Damascus. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.3 p.404}

The page number in al-Baladhuri's account is not given in the data file, and it needs review.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lecture series Al Dinu Al Qayem

The speaker recounts that Khalid ibn al-Walid's cap, in which he kept some of the Prophet's hair, fell in the thick of the battle of Yarmuk. Khalid left the fighting and the command, and people began searching for it {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep12 00:15:30}.

In the judge's sermon that the speaker recites, "the epics of Yarmuk" are named among the army's deeds {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep07 00:29:30}.

## In Khalifa ibn Khayyat
[[person/khalifa-ibn-khayyat|Khalifa ibn Khayyat]] places Yarmuk in the year 15. He reports from Ibn Ishaq that the Byzantines camped at the Yarmuk a hundred thousand strong, with tribes of Quda'a, under a eunuch of Heraclius, and from Ibn al-Kalbi that they were three hundred thousand under Bahan, a man of Persian descent who had become a Christian and joined the Byzantines. [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda]] drew in the commanders of the military districts, and Umar reinforced him with Sa'id ibn Amir ibn Hidhyam; the Byzantines were routed after hard fighting. Ibn al-Kalbi, as Khalifa quotes him, dates the battle to Monday, 5 Rajab 15 AH. {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 130}
Among those killed at Yarmuk Khalifa lists, after Ibn Ishaq, Amr ibn Sa'id ibn al-As, Aban ibn Sa'id, Ikrima ibn Abi Jahl, Abd Allah ibn Sufyan ibn Abd al-Asad and Sa'id ibn al-Harith ibn Qays; he notes from Abu al-Hasan al-Mada'ini that some placed the deaths of Aban and Ikrima at Ajnadayn or Marj al-Suffar instead, and that Suhayl ibn Amr and al-Harith ibn Hisham fell at Yarmuk. {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 130} {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 131}
