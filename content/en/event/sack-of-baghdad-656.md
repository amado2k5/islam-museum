## Overview
The sources say the Tatars under [[person/hulagu|Hulagu]] entered [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] in 656 AH, and that the caliph [[person/al-mustasim-billah|al-Musta'sim]], the last Abbasid caliph in Baghdad, was killed {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.715-716} {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.900} {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.901}. [[person/al-suyuti|Al-Suyuti]] relates that they were two hundred thousand, that the caliph's army went out and was routed, and that they entered Baghdad on the day of Ashura {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.715-716}. [[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] dates the great assault to Tuesday 22 Muharram, 2 February 1258 CE, not the day al-Suyuti gives {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:17:30} {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.716}. In al-Suyuti the vizier advised the caliph to placate them, so the caliph went out to Hulagu with a group of notables, and the jurists and leading men, summoned "to attend the contract", were beheaded {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.716}. Then the sword was loosed on Baghdad, the killing lasted about forty days, and the caliph was trampled to death {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.716}.

In [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]], Hulagu advanced on Iraq in 652 AH, and in 655 AH a letter reached him with the counsel of the vizier [[person/al-alqami|Ibn al-Alqami]], urging him on to Baghdad {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.900}. Ibn Khaldun says the city was given over to plunder for days, and that the books of learning kept in the treasuries were thrown into the Tigris {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.901}. Al-Suyuti puts the dead at more than a million, while al-Suwaidan holds the figures are not free of exaggeration, the lowest being four hundred thousand {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.716} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:14:00}. Al-Suyuti calls it a calamity unlike any that had struck Islam, and says the world had no caliph until the caliphate was set up in Egypt in Rajab 659 AH, a break of three and a half years {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.716} {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.719} {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.721}.

## Al-Suyuti's account
Al-Suyuti says that the Tatars reached Baghdad two hundred thousand strong, the caliph's army was routed, and they entered on the day of Ashura. The vizier advised the caliph to placate them, saying he would go out to settle a peace. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.715-721}

The vizier then reported from Hulagu that he wished to marry his daughter to the caliph's son. The caliph went out with a group of notables, and the jurists and the men of learning were summoned "to attend the contract", and their necks were struck. The caliph was killed by kicking, and al-Dhahabi says he does not think he was buried. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.715-721}

## Ibn Khaldun's account
In Ibn Khaldun, Hulagu advanced toward Iraq in 652 AH after taking al-Rayy, Isfahan and Hamadhan, and went for the fortress of Alamut in 655 AH. A letter from [[person/al-alqami|Ibn al-Alqami]] reached him urging him to make for Baghdad. The Muslims were routed before Baghdad, the chief chamberlain was killed, and then al-Musta'sim and those with him came out and were killed. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.900-901}

## Baghdad on the eve of the siege
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, in its history of Baghdad, describes the city in the last decades of the caliphate as weakened by fire, flood and quarrels between its quarters. It says the soldiers sent to restore order in 654/1256 joined crowds in pillaging the quarter of Karkh, and that the worst flood of those years came in the same year. It sums up the situation by saying that nature and man together eclipsed the city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 961}

The article also notes that the caliphate's last century had been a time of great religious building. It counts the colleges known as madrasas, among them the Nizamiyya and the Mustansiriyya, and the many Sufi lodges founded by caliphs and their relatives, and says the traveller Ibn Jubayr saw thirty madrasas in the eastern half of the city. The [[building/al-madrasa-al-nizamiyya-baghdad|Nizamiyya]] was founded in 459/1066, and the [[building/mustansiriyya-madrasa|Mustansiriyya]] in 631/1233; the Mustansiriyya taught the law of all four schools. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 961}

## The surrender and the killing
The article's account is brief. It says that two years after those events the Mongols invaded, and that on 4 Safar 656, which it converts to 10 February 1258, the caliph al-Musta'sim made an unconditional surrender. The people of the city were then put to the sword without distinction for over a week, and many country people who had crowded into Baghdad before the siege shared their fate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 961} In its article on the Abbasids the encyclopaedia puts the same event in a wider frame: the Mongol general, having already conquered Persia, appeared before Baghdad in that year, and the caliph, whom it calls the last of the line, could offer no serious resistance because the successors of al-Nasir were weak and incompetent. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 40}

## How many died
The encyclopaedia reports that the estimates of the dead range between 800,000 and two million, and observes that the figure grows with the passing of time. It names the Fakhri, Ibn al-Fuwati, al-Dhahabi's Duwal al-Islam and Ibn Kathir's Bidaya as sources of these larger figures. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 961} It sets against them the Chinese traveller Ch'ang Te, who wrote in 1259 that several tens of thousands were killed and whose information the article judges to have come from Mongol sources. The author concludes that it is hard to give any figure but that the toll probably exceeded a hundred thousand. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 961}

## Damage and first repairs
Many quarters were ruined by the siege, by looting or by fire, according to the article, and the mosque of the caliphs, [[building/al-khulafa-mosque-baghdad|Jami' al-Khulafa']], and the shrine of Kazimayn, the [[building/al-kadhimiya-mosque|Kadhimiya shrine]], were burnt down. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 961} Before leaving, it says, Hulagu ordered the restoration of some public buildings. The supervisor of endowments rebuilt the Jami' al-Khulafa' and saw to it that the schools and Sufi lodges were reopened. The author adds that culture suffered much but was not uprooted, and that Baghdad became a provincial city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 961} The text of the article is damaged at the point where it speaks of the devastation and of a legal opinion obtained from the scholars, so this page does not repeat that passage.

## Under the Ilkhans
According to the article, Baghdad stayed under the Ilkhanids, the line of the [[polity/ilkhanate|Ilkhanate]], until 740/1339-40, governed by a governor with a shihna, a police and garrison officer, and a military garrison. The Mongols registered the inhabitants in tens, hundreds and thousands for taxation and imposed a poll tax on everyone except the aged and children; it was levied for about two years. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 961}

The city began to recover slowly. The article credits this to an administration entrusted mainly to Persians and in large part to the policy of [[person/ata-malik-al-juwayni|Ata Malik al-Juwayni]], governor for about twenty-three years, from 657/1258 to 681/1282. Under him the minaret of the Jami' al-Khulafa' and the market of the Nizamiyya were rebuilt, and the Mustansiriyya was repaired and given a new water system. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 961} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 962} The mosques of Shaykh Ma'ruf and of the Qamariyya were repaired. Some older schools resumed teaching, especially the Nizamiyya and the Mustansiriyya, the Bashiriyya, the Tatashiyya and the Madrasat al-Ashab, and the 'Ismatiyya school for the four schools of law, with a ribat near it, was founded. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 962} The article regards as probably a pious wish a message in which the Ilkhan [[person/tekuder-ahmad|Tekuder]] asked Baghdad for the return of endowments to schools and mosques as under the Abbasids. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 962}

## Non-Muslims under Ilkhan policy
The article says that the Ilkhans favoured Christians, exempted them from the poll tax, rebuilt churches and opened schools, and that this led to an outbreak against them in 665. Jews rose to prominence under [[person/arghun-khan|Arghun]] through his Jewish finance minister Sa'd al-Dawla, who made his brother governor of Baghdad; when Sa'd al-Dawla was killed in 690/1291, the populace in Baghdad fell on the Jews. Under [[person/ghazan-khan|Ghazan]], non-Muslims suffered through distinctions of dress, the return of the poll tax and the attitude of the mob, and many adopted Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 962} The article adds that Uljaytu caused trouble by wavering between Shi'ism and Sunnism, and that the paper money the Ilkhans tried to introduce was so unpopular in Baghdad that Ghazan abolished it in 697/1297. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 962}

## Travellers and geographers
The article draws on three writers of the Mongol period. The author of the Marasid says that most of eastern Baghdad was ruined when the Tatars came, that they killed its people so that few were left, and that people from outside then came in. Ibn Battuta, visiting in 727/1327, says that the mosques and schools were very numerous but in ruins. Mustawfi, writing in 740/1339, describes the eastern wall with four gates and a circuit of 18,000 paces and the western side, Karkh, with a circuit of 12,000 paces; he found the Shafi'is and Hanbalis dominant, called the Nizamiyya the greatest of the colleges and the Mustansiriyya the most beautiful building. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 962}

## Later centuries in brief
The article says that Hasan Buzurg established himself in Baghdad in 740/1339 and founded the Jalayirid dynasty, which lasted until 813/1410. [[person/timur-lang|Timur]] took the city twice: in 795/1392-3 it escaped with little damage, but in 803/1401 its population was put to the sword and many Abbasid buildings and quarters were ruined, which the author calls the devastating blow to culture in Baghdad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 962}

## How the encyclopaedia judges the end of the caliphate
The article on the Abbasids gives its own view, and it is the author's, not the museum's. It says the Mongol conquest of Baghdad and the destruction of the caliphate are usually described as a major catastrophe, and that they do mark the end of an epoch, in the forms of government and in Islamic civilisation itself, which after the Tatar invasions flows in new channels. But it holds that the immediate moral effects have been overrated, since the caliphate had long ceased to be an effective institution and the invasions changed little in the real organs of temporal power, except that the sultanate began to gain recognition in law. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 40} It also records that [[person/baybars|Baybars]] set up an Abbasid shadow caliphate in Cairo in 659/1261, the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate-cairo|Abbasid caliphate of Cairo]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 40}

## The results
Al-Suyuti says that the last sermon given in Baghdad began with the praise of God "who has destroyed with death the lofty buildings". He says the caliphate was set up in Egypt in 659 AH. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.715-721}

The accounts attribute a role in these events to Ibn al-Alqami, and sources assess it differently. The academic views on this are not yet included here.

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "History Taught Me"
[[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] treats the fall of Baghdad in five episodes (thirteen to seventeen) of his programme [[work/allamani-al-tarikh-program|"History Taught Me"]]. He says he presents it from two sides: the Tatar side, how the Tatars took up the causes of victory, which for him are "neutral", so that whoever takes them up wins, Muslim or not; and the Abbasid side, how it practised the obstacles to victory. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:03:30} He cites [[passage/quran-11-15|Hud 15]] on those who want the life of this world being paid in full for their deeds in it. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:05:30} In episode sixteen he states a rule he calls a law: if one side is believing and the other unbelieving, victory goes to the believer if he takes up the causes of victory; but if both sides have left God's way, victory goes to the one with more planning and material preparation. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:03:30}

### The Tatar preparation
Al-Suwaidan relates that [[person/hulagu|Hulagu]], brother of the Tatar ruler Möngke, began his preparation in 649 AH and kept at it for five full years until 654 AH: he repaired the roads from China to Baghdad and bridged the rivers, built huge carriers for the siege engines, and cleared the route of herds so that the grass would be left as fodder for the army's animals. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:08:00} He says the Tatars made alliances with the king of Armenia, the Georgian kingdom and the prince of Antioch, and with some Muslim princes, among them [[person/badr-al-din-lulu|Badr al-Din Lu'lu']] of Mosul and [[person/al-nasir-yusuf|al-Nasir Yusuf]] of Aleppo and Damascus, so that Baghdad was hemmed in on every side. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep13 00:18:00} In his view these alliances, made by princes afraid for their little states, left the peoples deeply dispirited and without trust in their leaders, so that resolve weakened. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:03:00}

### Psychological war
Al-Suwaidan says the Tatars launched raids whose purpose was psychological: in 650 AH units of theirs attacked the districts of al-Jazira, Saruj and Sinjar, killing and plundering, and seized the goods of a trading caravan worth more than six hundred thousand gold dinars. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:11:30} He says the Tatars' followers in Muslim lands waged a "media war": storytellers, men of letters, poets and historians spoke of the Tatars' superhuman powers until people lost heart, and sayings spread that news of the nations reached them while news of them reached no one, that their women fought, and that their horses needed no barley. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:12:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:12:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:14:00} He adds that Tatar letters claimed they were Muslims who believed in the Qur'an and had come to lift the rulers' oppression from the peoples, a claim that found its way into frightened hearts. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:15:30}

### Weakening the army
Al-Suwaidan says the vizier [[person/al-alqami|Ibn al-Alqami]] persuaded the caliph to cut the army's budget, reduce the number of soldiers and turn them to civilian work so as not to provoke the Tatars; the regular army fell from a hundred thousand in the last days of [[person/al-mustansir-billah|al-Mustansir]] to ten thousand horsemen in 654 AH, soldiers went unpaid and begged in the markets, and military training was neglected. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:06:00}

### The advance
He says Hulagu gathered for Baghdad the largest Tatar army since the founding of Genghis Khan's state, and that the troops assigned to the direct attack on the city numbered more than two hundred thousand fighters, besides the supply, reconnaissance and road-guard forces. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:10:00} He began with the Ismailis and rooted them out of their strongholds in Persia in 655 AH, so that the road to Baghdad lay safe and open. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:19:30}

### The diary of the siege
In what he calls "the diary of the siege of Baghdad", al-Suwaidan relates that on 9 Dhu al-Hijja 655 AH Hulagu reached the Hulwan river and camped there until the 22nd of the month. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:12:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:12:30} The caliph's army under the Dawatdar and Fath al-Din camped near Ba'quba, about fifty kilometres east of Baghdad, then crossed the Tigris to face the Mongols on the west bank north of the city, leaving the eastern front empty of troops. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:12:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:13:00} The caliph tried to send out the remaining army commanders under one leader, who excused himself and did not obey; al-Suwaidan thinks such insubordination makes the enemy's task easy. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:13:30}

He says the caliph's army met the Mongol vanguard at al-Anbar on 9 Muharram 656 AH, and that historians differ on the vanguard's size, between ten and thirty thousand. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:14:00} The Mongols feigned flight; the Dawatdar charged after them, ignoring Fath al-Din's advice to hold firm, while a Mongol unit broke the dams on a canal branching from the Tigris and flooded the ground behind the Baghdad army; then they turned and attacked, and most of the army perished, except those who threw themselves into the water or fled into the open country; the Dawatdar escaped and entered Baghdad with a few companions. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:14:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:15:30}

He relates that on 14 Muharram the vizier Ibn al-Alqami went out with a body of his mamluks and followers telling people not to shoot arrows and saying that a peace was coming. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:16:00} On 21 Muharram Hulagu came down on the eastern side of Baghdad and besieged it, built a wall around it, set up mangonels to batter its walls from every side and stopped all supplies; the defenders' weapons were so weak that not one stone from their mangonels reached the enemy, and the caliph's offer of money rewards to those who stood firm availed nothing. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:16:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:17:00} On Tuesday 22 Muharram, which he equates with 2 February 1258, the Mongols began their great assault, with Hulagu at the heart of his camp opposite the Ajami tower at Bab al-Halba; they took this part of the city, while other forces faced the gate of Suq al-Sultan. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:18:00}

He goes on that the caliph sent a delegation to ask for peace, but Hulagu refused, saying it was too late, and shot arrows into the city carrying leaflets that promised safety to all who did not fight, addressed to judges, scholars, shaykhs, notables and merchants; al-Suwaidan sees this as moral warfare alongside the material war. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:05:00} On Monday 28 Muharram 656 AH the Mongols climbed the wall at Bab al-Ajami and by that evening held all the eastern walls, sparing none of the fleeing garrison they captured. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:05:30}

On 1 Safar the Dawatdar and Sulayman Shah, the two leaders of the resistance, went out to meet Hulagu, who asked them to order the defenders to surrender and join the Mongol army bound for Egypt and Syria; a great number of soldiers came out, and all were killed, the two commanders with them. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:07:30} On Sunday 4 Safar the caliph went out with his sons and three thousand of the notables, imams, judges and leading men of Baghdad; Hulagu asked him to order the people of Baghdad to lay down their arms, he had it proclaimed and they did so, and Hulagu then ordered those who had come with him killed. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:08:00}

### What happened in the city
Al-Suwaidan relates that the invaders entered the city, plundered and burned it and killed whoever they met; that they threw the libraries' books into the Tigris and crossed over them on horseback, turned mosques into stables and used books as fodder. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:09:00} He describes women and children coming out holding copies of the Qur'an, pleading with the soldiers in the belief that they were Muslims, and being trampled by the horses; he says the Tigris ran red for three days, mosques were wrecked for the gold of their domes, the sick in the hospitals were killed, and libraries, treasuries and learned academies were burned or thrown into the Tigris. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:10:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:11:00} He quotes the description in "al-Fakhri fi al-Adab al-Sultaniyya" by [[person/ibn-al-tiqtaqa|Ibn al-Tiqtaqa]], that the Mongols slaughtered most of the people as sheep are slaughtered, as though the Hour had come. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:11:30}

He says that on 10 Safar 656 AH Hulagu ordered the killing of the caliph [[person/al-mustasim-billah|al-Musta'sim bi'llah]] with his sons, uncles, relatives and the notables of Baghdad, and declared the rest slaves. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:11:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:12:00} He says most historians put the dead at a million, that he thinks the figures not free of exaggeration, and that the lowest figure given is four hundred thousand killed in the week the Mongols occupied Baghdad. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:14:00} He tells of a Mongol who came upon forty Muslim men, ordered them to line up, went and fetched a knife, and killed them while people watched, as an example of the depth of humiliation reached. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:14:30} Hulagu then stopped the killing and ordered the dead buried, because the air had grown foul and plague had spread and he feared for his army; he left two Mongol commanders in Baghdad with only three thousand soldiers and left for [[place/hamadan|Hamadan]] in Jumada al-Ula 656 AH. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:14:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:15:00} Al-Suwaidan says that the capital of the caliphate for more than five hundred years fell with an ease that not even the cities of al-Andalus had fallen with. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:15:30}

### The causes of the fall, in al-Suwaidan's account
Al-Suwaidan lists causes of the fall: the enemy's clear aim and five years of planning; poor leadership, since the caliph was not equal to the event; and the distraction of the caliph and his court with amusement. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:16:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:19:00} As an instance of the broken home front he cites strife between Shia and Sunnis, saying the caliph's son was charged with ending it but instead raided al-Karkh, the Shia quarter, committing atrocities there, and that each side treated the other in the same way. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:19:30} He concludes that Baghdad fell only "because of ourselves": the outside attack was a cause, but had the community been strong within, holding to God's way and taking up planning and material means, it would not have fallen; he cites [[passage/quran-47-7|Muhammad 7]]. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:20:00}

## For Sprouts
The city of Baghdad faced difficult days when the Tatars arrived, but its people persevered with patience and knowledge to rebuild.

## For Explorers
In the year 656 AH, the Tatar army led by Hulagu arrived at Baghdad, the Abbasid capital. The event brought an end to the caliphate in Baghdad and harmed its historic libraries, yet other Islamic centers like Cairo and Damascus continued preserving heritage and advancing knowledge.

## For Scholars
The Mongol capture of Baghdad under Hulagu in Safar 656 AH (February 1258 CE), resulting in the execution of Caliph al-Musta'sim and the termination of the Abbasid Caliphate in Iraq. A watershed moment in Islamic historiography, it caused profound urban disruption and loss of manuscripts, after which geopolitical and intellectual leadership shifted to Mamluk Cairo, where a symbolic Abbasid caliphate was re-established and the Mongol advance halted at Ayn Jalut.
