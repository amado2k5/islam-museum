## Overview
In Ramadan of the third year of his retreat in [[place/ghar-hira|the cave of Hira]], when [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] had completed forty years, the angel came to him, pressed him three times, and then brought down to him the opening of [[passage/quran-96-1|Surat al-'Alaq]] {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.66} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.67} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/2-3}. The author of al-Raheeq al-Makhtum judges that this was the night of Monday 21 Ramadan, corresponding to 10 August 610 CE, when he was forty lunar years, six months and twelve days old {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.66}. Historians differ on the month of the mission, naming Rabi' al-Awwal, Ramadan or Rajab, and those who say Ramadan differ on the day {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.66} {cite:mukhtasar-sirat-al-rasul-al-najdi p.75} {cite:rahma-lil-alamin 1/49} {cite:muhadarat-tarikh-al-umam-al-islamiyya 1/69}.

[[person/aisha|Aisha]] relates in the Sahih that revelation began with the good dream; then solitude was made dear to him and he would devote himself to worship in the cave of Hira, until the truth came to him there {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.67} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/2-3}. The angel told him to read and he answered that he was not a reader, so the angel pressed him to exhaustion and released him, three times {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.67} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/2-3}. He went back with his heart trembling to [[person/khadija|Khadija]] and told her he feared for himself; she reassured him that God would not disgrace him, since he kept the ties of kinship and honoured the guest {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.67} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/2-3}. She took him to her cousin [[person/waraqa-ibn-nawfal|Waraqa ibn Nawfal]], an old man who had become a Christian in the Jahiliyya, and he said that this was the Namus that God had sent down to Moses {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.67} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.68} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/2-3}. Soon afterwards Waraqa died and the revelation paused ([[event/fatrat-al-wahy|the pause in revelation]]) {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.67} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.68} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/2-3}.

## Before revelation: the true dream
In Ramadan of the third year of his retreat in [[place/ghar-hira|the cave of Hira]], the angel came to [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] when he had completed forty years. He pressed him three times, and then sent down upon him the opening verses of [[passage/quran-96-1|Surat al-'Alaq]]. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.66} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.67} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/2-3}. The author judges that this was the night of Monday, the twenty-first of Ramadan, corresponding to 10 August 610 CE, when he was forty lunar years, six months and twelve days old. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.66}

When he had completed forty years, the signs of prophethood began to appear to him, namely the true dream. He would see no dream but that it came about like the break of dawn, and this continued for six months. The author reckons the duration of prophethood at twenty-three years, so this dream is one part of forty-six parts of prophethood. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.66} {cite:fath-al-bari 1/27} The author relates from Ibn Hajar, from al-Bayhaqi, that the beginning of prophethood with the dream occurred in Rabi' al-Awwal, the month of his birth, and the beginning of waking revelation in Ramadan. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.66} {cite:fath-al-bari 1/27}

## Aisha's account
The author quotes the hadith of [[person/aisha|Aisha]] in the Sahih: the first thing with which revelation began was the good dream. Then solitude became beloved to him, and he would seclude himself in the cave of Hira for many nights, taking provisions for it, then return to [[person/khadija|Khadija]] to take provisions for another such retreat, until the truth came to him while he was in the cave. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.67} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/2-3} The angel came to him and commanded him to read. He answered that he was not a reader. The angel pressed him until he reached the limit of effort, then released him, three times. Then he recited to him the opening of Surat al-'Alaq, from [[passage/quran-96-1|al-'Alaq 1]] to [[passage/quran-96-5|al-'Alaq 5]]. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.67} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/2-3}

## Returning to Khadija and Waraqa
He came back trembling, his heart pounding, and entered upon Khadija and asked her to wrap him up until the fear left him. He told her that he feared for himself. She reassured him that God would not disgrace him, given that he maintains ties of kinship, bears the burdens of others, earns for the destitute, hosts guests and helps in the calamities of truth. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.67} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/2-3} Then she took him to her cousin [[person/waraqa-ibn-nawfal|Waraqa ibn Nawfal]], who had become a Christian in the Jahiliyya and used to write from the Gospel in Hebrew. He was a very old man who had gone blind. When he heard the report, he said that this is the Namus that God sent down upon Moses, and he wished that he would be alive when his people drove him out. He told him that no man had ever come with what he had brought without being opposed. Waraqa did not tarry long before he died, and revelation paused ([[event/fatrat-al-wahy|the pause of revelation]]). {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.67} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.68} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/2-3}

## The account of al-Tabari and Ibn Hisham on his leaving the cave
The author relates from al-Tabari and Ibn Hisham that he went out of the cave after being startled by the revelation, then returned, completed his sojourn, and then went back to Mecca. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.68} {cite:tarikh-al-tabari 2/207} {cite:ibn-hisham-sira 1/237-238} In al-Tabari's text, nothing was more hateful to him than to be called a poet or a madman. He feared that Quraysh would say this of him, so he went out intending to throw himself from the mountain. Halfway up, he heard a voice from the sky calling him that he was the Messenger of God and that the caller was Gabriel. He raised his head and saw him in the form of a man standing with his feet on the horizon of the sky. He stood gazing at him, neither advancing nor retreating, until Khadija sent her messengers to look for him. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.68} {cite:tarikh-al-tabari 2/207} He then returned to Khadija and told her. She gave him good tidings and said she hoped he would be the prophet of this community. Then she went to Waraqa, who said that the great Namus that used to come to Moses had come to him, and that he was the prophet of this community. When his sojourn ended, Waraqa met him and said the same. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.68} {cite:ibn-hisham-sira 1/237-238}

## Fixing the day
The author notes in a footnote that historians differ about the month of the mission. Rabi' al-Awwal is held by a large group, Ramadan by others, and Rajab is also mentioned. He prefers Ramadan on the basis of the verses [[passage/quran-2-185|al-Baqara 185]], [[passage/quran-97-1|al-Qadr 1]] and [[passage/quran-44-3|al-Dukhan 3]], and because his sojourn at Hira was in Ramadan. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.66} {cite:mukhtasar-sirat-al-rasul-al-najdi p.75} Those who hold Ramadan differ about the day: the seventh, the seventeenth or the eighteenth. Al-Khudari insisted on the seventeenth. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.66} {cite:rahma-lil-alamin 1/49} {cite:muhadarat-tarikh-al-umam-al-islamiyya 1/69} The author prefers the twenty-first, although he has not come across anyone who holds it. The people of the sira agree that the mission took place on a Monday, based on the hadith of Abu Qatada about fasting on Monday. In Ramadan of that year, by his calculation, a Monday falls only on the seventh, fourteenth, twenty-first or twenty-eighth. And the Night of Power falls on an odd night of the last ten nights. So the twenty-first is settled for him. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.66} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.67} {cite:sahih-muslim 1/368} {cite:musnad-ahmad 5/297-299}

## The Encyclopaedia of Islam on wahy and the first revelation
The entry on [[concept/wahy|wahy]] in the Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, explains the word as a Qur'anic term for revelation given as communication without speech, and as an exceptional way in which God addresses His creatures. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 61} The article adds that the Qur'an uses the same word for the inspiration of bees and of heaven and earth, and for the whispering of satans to their friends, but it holds that it is wahy that inspires prophets. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 62}

For the way revelation came, the article turns to the sira and hadith literature, whose picture of revelation, it says, is bound up with the biography of Muhammad. It summarises that material as follows. Revelation began with dreams that anticipated real events. The first revelation in which [[person/jibril|Jibril]] appeared took place on Mount Hira', where the angel named himself; Muhammad then hurried to Khadija and asked to be wrapped up, which the article links to an allusion in [[passage/quran-73-1|Surat al-Muzzammil]] or [[passage/quran-74-1|Surat al-Muddaththir]]. The first portion of the Qur'an to be revealed was [[passage/quran-96-1|Surat al-'Alaq]]: in Ramadan, during the retreat, the angel showed him a piece of cloth and told him to recite; Muhammad protested that he could not read; the angel pressed him so hard that he nearly suffocated, and at the third repetition pronounced the verses, which Muhammad retained. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 62}

After this, the article reports, came a pause in revelation, during which Muhammad was so downcast that the thought of ending his life came upon him; it cites [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]], [[person/ibn-hisham|Ibn Hisham]] and Ibn Sa'd for this. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 62} This agrees in outline with the accounts given earlier on this page. The OCR of the sentence that names what ended the pause is unreadable, so it is not reported here.

## How the experience of revelation is described
The same article says that the angel who carried revelation was visible to Muhammad and to others, and that the ascension and the night journey may to some extent also be counted as revelations. It notes that the Qur'an speaks of visions, with a passage in [[passage/quran-53-1|Surat al-Najm]] as the central one and the figure seen usually identified as Jibril, while other passages speak of revelation by hearing. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 62}

The article collects reports from the hadith literature on how it felt. In one, Muhammad says that revelation sometimes comes like the ringing of a bell, which is the most painful kind, after which he retains what was said, and sometimes an angel speaks to him in the form of a man. In another form of the report the angel comes as a young man who hands it down. A sound like the humming of bees is said to have come near his face before the opening verses of [[passage/quran-23-1|Surat al-Mu'minun]] were revealed. He used to move his lips in pain when revelation began. Asked whether he perceived it, he is reported to have said that he heard sounds like metal being beaten and often thought he would die. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 62} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 63}

The article also lists how others saw him in those moments. Sweat appeared on his forehead even on cold days; he covered his head, his colour reddened, and he snored or rattled like a young camel before recovering; his colour could turn livid; he could fall into a lethargy or a trance. Zayd ibn Thabit is quoted as saying that the Prophet's thigh fell on his so heavily that he feared it would break, and a report says that a sura was revealed while he rode his camel, which could no longer bear his weight. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 63} As to the circumstances, the article says revelation often came when he was asked for an opinion or a decision, and also while he was riding, while his head was being washed, at a meal and on the pulpit. It adds that the content of such answers is not always communicated and is not necessarily part of the Qur'an. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 63}

On theology, the article quotes a scholar who says that classical Muslim theology had no consistent theory of revelation as such until the time of the philosophers. It then reports the reply of al-Iji and his commentator al-Jurjani to the philosophers' view that prophets see angels in bodily form and hear their speech; al-Iji argues that this view reduces revelation to imagining without basis in reality. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 63} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 64}

## Gabriel, the Spirit and the traditional dating in the Qur'an article
The article on the Qur'an says that the orthodox view of its dramatic form is that God speaks throughout, Muhammad receives, and Gabriel is the intermediary of revelation, whoever may appear to speak. It judges that an analysis of the text shows the situation to be more complex: in what it takes to be the oldest parts the speaker and the source are not indicated, and later passages speak of a spirit as the agent of revelation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 440} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 441} Gabriel is named as the agent only once, in [[passage/quran-2-97|Surat al-Baqara, verse 97]], and the commentators, on the basis of that verse and of hadith, identified the spirit of the earlier passages with Gabriel and placed him at the very beginning of Muhammad's ministry. The author adds that the Qur'an never numbers Gabriel among the angels and never says that angels deliver revelation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 441} These are the article author's readings; they are set here beside the traditional Muslim account above and are not offered as a ruling between them.

The same article records that in the early Islamic centuries certain passages were tied to stories about the Prophet's life in Mecca: two were read as call visions, and Surat al-'Alaq and Surat al-Muddaththir were connected with his call to public prophethood. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 453} The standard Egyptian edition of the Qur'an lists Surat al-'Alaq first in its order of revelation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 454}

## Scholarly views on the call in the article on Muhammad
The article on Muhammad sets out the sources first. It names the sira of Ibn Ishaq, preserved in the recension of Ibn Hisham, as the most respected, followed by al-Tabari's history, which keeps further material from Ibn Ishaq and reports from Urwa ibn al-Zubayr. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 413} Muhammad's biographers, it says, usually make him forty, or sometimes forty-three, at the time of his call. The Qur'an itself offers only an indefinite word for the period of his life before it, which the article says could mean thirty-five as well as forty or forty-three. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 413} The sources, it observes, tell almost nothing of his life from his marriage to Khadija until shortly before the visions and voices began. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 414}

The article judges that his emergence as a religious reformer is among the hardest questions to answer. It suggests that Waraqa ibn Nawfal, named in the sira among those seeking a monotheist faith, probably played a larger part in the rise of Islam than the sources admit. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 415} It then sets two pictures side by side: one scholar's view that the emergence was gradual, with long periods of reflection called tahannuth in the sira, and the Islamic tradition, whose stories, some involving Gabriel, give the impression of a sudden call at a precise moment. The article notes that verses such as [[passage/quran-44-1|Surat al-Dukhan, verses 1 to 4]], [[passage/quran-97-1|Surat al-Qadr, verse 1]] and [[passage/quran-2-185|al-Baqara, verse 185]] have been read in support of the second view, but it judges them ambiguous and without a clear reference to a first revelation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 415}

The author also judges the traditional stories that make Surat al-'Alaq or Surat al-Muddaththir the first revelation to be highly suspect. His reason is that the same tradition places a pause of three years between those first revelations and the start of public preaching, which would mean that Muhammad waited three years before acting on a divine command. He finds this pause unexplained and suggests that it may have come from biographers building an exact chronology, and that the very earliest revelations may not have been written down or memorised and so may have been lost. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 415} He regards the vivid descriptions of Muhammad's state at moments of inspiration as probably genuine, since later writers are unlikely to have invented them, and he says that the accounts in which Muhammad at first did not recognise these experiences as a call may compress a much longer period. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 415} These are the views of the article's author, an academic reading; the Muslim accounts earlier on this page rest on the hadith and sira sources they cite.

## Notes for researchers
- The date of 21 Ramadan (10 August 610 CE) is the author's preference alone, by his own declaration. The event card carries it with the status "disputed," alongside the other views.
- The reckoning of "one part in forty-six" rests on a prophethood of twenty-three years, with six months of dreams, as the author explains the hadith.
- For the first verses revealed, up to [[passage/quran-96-5|al-'Alaq 5]], see the author's footnote. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.67}

## For young visitors
The Prophet used to go to a small cave on a mountain near Mecca to worship. On one of the nights of Ramadan the angel Gabriel came to him and told him to read. These were the first verses of the Qur'an. The Prophet returned to his home afraid, and his wife Khadija reassured him.

## For Sprouts
At the cave of Hira, Jibril came to the Prophet with the first verses of surat al-Alaq, and revelation began.

## For Explorers
In the cave of Hira, near Mecca, Jibril came to the Prophet Muhammad with the first words of surat al-Alaq. This was the beginning of revelation. The Prophet used to spend time in this cave in quiet reflection, about thirteen years before the Hijra.

## For Scholars
In the cave of Hira on a mountain near Mecca, Jibril came to the Prophet Muhammad with the opening verses of surat al-Alaq, and this was the beginning of revelation. The sources say he used to withdraw to the cave for reflection, and that the event took place in Ramadan. The reports differ on the exact day, and the book gives its preferred reckoning with the calculation. Historians place the event about thirteen years before the Hijra. The Gregorian date in this entry is derived by calculation, not taken from a source.

## In Abu Ishaq al-Heweny's «Difaf al-Kawthar»
[[person/abu-ishaq-al-heweny|Abu Ishaq al-Heweny]] says the hadith of the beginning of revelation was narrated by al-Bukhari and Muslim: by al-Bukhari in several places, among them the Book of the Beginning of Revelation, the Book of Tafsir and the Book of Dream Interpretation, and by Muslim in one place, the Book of Faith; and that the hadith turns on al-Zuhri from [[person/urwa-ibn-al-zubayr|Urwa]] from [[person/aisha|A'isha]]. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep04 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep04 00:07:30} He explains its words: the first revelation was the righteous, or true, dream, both readings being established, coming like the break of dawn; then seclusion was made dear to him, and he would practise tahannuth in the cave of Hira', tahannuth meaning devotion, and return to [[person/khadija|Khadija]] for provisions. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep04 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep04 00:08:30} He explains that the angel pressed him hard three times, {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep04 00:08:30} and that the bawadir are the flesh between the neck and the shoulder. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep04 00:09:30}

He says scholars explained the Prophet's saying that he feared for himself in twelve opinions, among them madness, inability, illness and not being able to bear the burden of prophethood, and that the view he prefers is that he feared illness from being pressed so hard three times. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep04 00:11:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep04 00:12:00} He says the namus in the words of [[person/waraqa-ibn-nawfal|Waraqa ibn Nawfal]] is [[person/jibril|Jibril]], and that some scholars distinguished the namus, keeper of secrets of good, from the jasus, keeper of secrets of evil; {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep04 00:11:00} and that Waraqa died after this and the revelation paused. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep04 00:11:30}
