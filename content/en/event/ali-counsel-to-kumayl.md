## Summary
al-Sallabi narrates a counsel that Ali ibn Abi Talib gave to Kumayl ibn Ziyad al-Nakha'i, taking him out into the desert and speaking about knowledge and its people. The counsel combines moral upbringing with a classification of people according to their knowledge and conduct {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 201}.

## Context
The book cites this counsel in the chapter dealing with Ali's knowledge, jurisprudence, and upbringing, beginning it with Ali's saying: "Hearts are vessels, and the best of them is the one most retentive of knowledge," before dividing people into three classes {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 201}. The book assigns an educational meaning to this classification: that the community can only be guided by those who combine jurisprudence and wisdom {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 201}.

## The Three Classes
- Godly scholars, who are religious scholars combining jurisprudence and wisdom; the book explains "godly" (rabbaniyyun) using what is narrated from Ibn Abbas and al-Bukhari {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 201}.
- Seekers of knowledge who are sincere in their intentions, whom Ali described as "and a learner on the path of salvation" {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 201}.
- The common rabble who have forsaken religious knowledge and have no connection to scholars; they are "followers of every caller, swaying with every wind" {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 202}.

## Knowledge and Wealth
Ali draws a comparison between knowledge and wealth, noting that knowledge guards its possessor while the possessor of wealth must guard it, that knowledge grows through action while wealth diminishes through spending, that knowledge is a ruler while wealth is ruled over, and that relationships built on wealth vanish when it disappears {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 202-203}. The book adds that "hoarders of wealth are dead even while alive, whereas scholars endure as long as time endures" {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 202}.

## From His Sayings on Scholars and Their Students
- The right of a scholar is that you do not overwhelm him with questions, nor press him hard for an answer, nor divulge his secret {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 205}.
- A scholar is better than one who fasts, prays at night, and strives in battle, because his death leaves a breach in Islam {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 205}.
- People only lost interest in seeking knowledge because they saw how little the scholar benefited from his own knowledge; this is a warning to corrupt scholars and a call to scholars to act upon what they know {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 205}.

## The Book's Analysis
al-Sallabi views the ranking presented by Ali—in which action whose benefit extends to others is placed ahead of worship confined to the individual—as reflecting Ali's understanding of prioritizing duties {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 205}.

## Notes for Researchers
- The book does not date this counsel or specify its exact location; the date in the museum record is derived rather than explicitly stated.
- Most of the sayings here are quoted with "Ali said" from works like al-Hilyah and literary collections, and the book does not rule on the authenticity of every chain of transmission, indicating this by noting the grade of the hadith in the footnotes when it mentions it.
- Some sentences on page 205 are garbled in the available text, so only the legible portions have been included here.
