## Overview
Al-Hadi closes the poem (pp. 318-319), saying he is pleased as they are pleased, guards them from every harm and gives his life before them, and calls Banu Hasan and Banu Ali not to show weakness but to gather their purpose, citing a line of Abu Tammam that the sword is truer in tidings than books; then he says he set himself for God's command and went among Hamdan, Khawlan, Hashid and al-Ahlaf, and he praises Banu Hasan and prays for them; one line has a blank in the original and in manuscript S. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.318}

## Detail
On p. 318 al-Hadi says: "I am pleased when you are pleased, may I never lose you, and if you are angry then in your anger is my anger", that if good reaches him it reaches them and if evil they stand aside from it, that he guards them from every harm and misfortune and gives his life to "the Indian blades" before them lest they be struck, "O Banu Hasan, people of religion, grace and manners" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.318}. He calls "Banu Ali" not to show weakness before disaster or hang back, because "earnest is not play", and not to stay in humiliation while their right stands up with the spears in the horizons, and he asks how they can accept that their rulers are described in a way that guidance is invoked for them in the sermons; one word of that line is doubtful between two readings and is not carried over {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.318}. On p. 319 he says "so gather, for you have might and honour, and you are the lions on the day of terror", and that they have heard "Habib" in his saying "the sword is truer in tidings than books"; the footnote says this is [[person/abu-tammam|Abu Tammam, Habib ibn Aws al-Ta'i]], whose line is also recorded in his own entry {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.318}. He says this is worthier than blame in vain and than the long speech of the wealthy, and that even if the one who blames him sleeps, the remembrance of God does not frighten him, and that he "set himself for God's command, seeking reward", hoping for the highest rank {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.318}. He says he went among [[group/banu-hamdan|Hamdan]], joined by [[group/khawlan-tribe|Khawlan]], "the people of sense", in a roaring host, with [[group/hashid|Hashid]] and the peaks of [[group/al-ahlaf-najran|al-Ahlaf]] all together, and "the lords of Thaqif in the hour of anger"; the word "al-sayd" here is read as lords from the context, and Thaqif is not linked to a record {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.319}. He then says "the party of the Prophet and my party after him ... will not guard them one day" and a blank follows that the note says is in the original and in manuscript S {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.319}. He prays that God reward them for him with every good deed and shield them from the misery of fetters and flame, says "this is my praise on you, Banu Hasan", that through them the peaks of Islam return flourishing, and ends with the peace of his Lord upon them as long as the sun rises and a dove coos in the west {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.319}. It follows [[event/al-hadi-reproaches-the-people-of-the-prophets-house-for-ease-and-unproved-claims-in-verse-292|his reproach over their ease]]. The tribes are named in the poet's own voice only, and the book names none of them a commander. The poem stands in a Zaydi book in the voice of the imam's followers; its claims, descriptions and judgments on opponents are the poet's or the narrator's, and the museum adopts none of them, repeats no word of abuse and gives no verdict.
