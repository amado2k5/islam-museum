## Overview
The battle of Uhud, the second great battle between the Muslims of Medina and Quraysh, took place on Saturday 7 Shawwal 3 AH at the foot of [[place/uhud|Mount Uhud]], north of [[place/medina|Medina]] {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.166}. It began as a complete Muslim victory and ended in heavy losses after most of the archers left their positions. Seventy Companions were martyred, foremost among them [[person/hamza|Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib]]. The Prophet was wounded in the face, and Quraysh withdrew without occupying the Muslim camp or entering Medina {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.284}.

## Context
Quraysh was consumed with rage over the defeat at Badr. It forbade weeping for its dead, delayed the ransoming of captives, and held back the goods of the caravan that had escaped with [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-harb|Abu Sufyan]] to finance revenge {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.164}. It gathered three thousand fighters, including two hundred cavalry and seven hundred in armour. Overall command went to Abu Sufyan, and command of the cavalry to [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]], who was then still a polytheist {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.249}. In Medina the Prophet held a consultative council, in which most of the Companions chose to march out to the battlefield, despite his preference for staying.

## Plan and deployment
The Prophet camped with seven hundred fighters in the gorge of Uhud, facing Medina with his back to the mountain, which gave him the best position on the field. He selected fifty skilled archers and placed them under [[person/abd-allah-ibn-jubayr|Abdullah ibn Jubayr ibn al-Nu'man]] al-Ansari. He stationed them on a small hill south-east of the camp, known as [[place/jabal-al-rumma|the Archers' Hill]], to protect the left flank from the polytheist cavalry. He charged them: "Protect our backs. If you see us being killed, do not come to our aid; and if you see us taking spoils, do not share them with us" {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.255; sahih-al-bukhari}. He placed al-Mundhir ibn Amr over the right wing and [[person/zubayr-ibn-al-awwam|al-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam]] over the left.

## Before the battle: the withdrawal of Abdullah ibn Ubayy
On the road to Uhud, close to the enemy, [[person/abdullah-ibn-ubayy|Abdullah ibn Ubayy ibn Salul]] mutinied and withdrew with about three hundred fighters, exposing a hypocrisy that had been hidden {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.168}. The clans of Banu Haritha of the Aws and Banu Salama of the Khazraj were about to turn back, but they held firm. [[person/abdullah-ibn-amr-ibn-haram|Abdullah ibn Haram]], the father of Jabir, tried to bring the hypocrites back, but they refused. Seven hundred Companions remained with the Prophet.

## The first round: Muslim victory
At the start, the weight of the battle centred on the polytheists' standard, which was held by Banu Abd al-Dar. [[person/zubayr-ibn-al-awwam|Al-Zubayr]] leapt upon its first bearer, [[person/talha-ibn-abi-talha|Talha ibn Abi Talha]], threw him from his camel and slaughtered him. The men of Banu Abd al-Dar took the standard in turn and were wiped out to the last, ten men, until an Abyssinian slave named Sawab carried it. He knelt upon it with his chest and neck until he was killed {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq pp.258-260}. [[person/abu-dujana|Abu Dujana]], with his red headband, went forward carrying the Prophet's sword and cut through the ranks of the polytheists {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.256}. The polytheists fled, until al-Zubayr saw the women hitching up their skirts as they ran up the mountain {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.263; sahih-al-bukhari}.

## The martyrdom of Hamza
[[person/hamza|Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib]] fought in the thick of the people like a grey camel, cutting down the ranks. [[person/wahshi-ibn-harb|Wahshi ibn Harb]], an Abyssinian slave of Jubayr ibn Mut'im, lay in wait for him. Jubayr had promised him his freedom if he killed Hamza in revenge for Jubayr's uncle Tu'ayma, who had been killed at Badr. Wahshi said: "I shook my spear until I was satisfied with it, then I threw it at him. It struck him in the belly and came out between his legs" {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.261; sahih-al-bukhari}. Then [[person/hind-bint-utba|Hind bint Utba]] came and slit open Hamza's liver {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.276}.

## The archers leave their posts and the reversal
When most of the archers saw the Muslims gathering the spoils, they left their position, all except Ibn Jubayr and nine others. [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] seized the opening, swung his cavalry around behind the Muslim army and destroyed Ibn Jubayr and his companions. The routed polytheists turned back on the Muslims {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.264}. The Muslims found themselves between two millstones. In the confusion the news spread that the Prophet had been killed, and some of them collapsed, until [[person/anas-ibn-al-nadr|Anas ibn al-Nadr]] reminded them: "What will you do with life after him? Stand up and die as he died" {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.265}.

## Defending the Prophet
At the most desperate moment, only [[person/talha-ibn-ubaydullah|Talha ibn Ubayd Allah]] and Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas remained with the Prophet at his rear. Utba ibn Abi Waqqas pelted him with stones, breaking his front tooth and wounding his lip. Abd Allah ibn Qami'a struck his cheek, and two rings of his helmet sank into it {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.178}. Talha fought like eleven men until his fingers were cut off, and he received thirty-nine wounds {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.178; sahih-al-bukhari}. [[person/abu-dujana|Abu Dujana]] sheltered the Prophet with his back while the arrows fell on him, and he did not move {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.178}. [[person/nusayba-bint-kab|Nusayba bint Ka'b]] fought until she received twelve wounds {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.178}. [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah]] removed the two rings of the helmet from the Prophet's cheek with his incisors {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.178}.

## The withdrawal to the gorge and the end of the battle
About thirty Companions gathered around the Prophet and forced a way to the mountain gorge. During the withdrawal, the Prophet himself stabbed [[person/ubayy-ibn-khalaf|Ubayy ibn Khalaf]] in the collarbone, and he died of it at Sarif {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.183}. Once he had settled in the gorge, [[person/fatima|Fatima]] washed his wound and stopped the bleeding with a piece of burnt mat {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.278}. Abu Sufyan then called out to the Muslims. [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] answered him and made clear that the Prophet was alive {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.278}.

## Losses and burial of the martyrs
Seventy were martyred: sixty-five Ansar, four Muhajirun and a Jew. The Prophet ordered them buried in their resting places without washing, and he placed first in the grave whoever knew the most Qur'an {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.187}. [[person/sad-ibn-al-rabi|Sa'd ibn al-Rabi']] was found on the verge of death with seventy blows on him, and he sent his greeting to the Prophet {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.280}. When they searched for [[person/hanzala-ibn-abi-amir|Hanzala]], they found him dripping water. The Prophet told them that the angels had washed him, so he was named "the one washed by the angels" {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.187}.

## The expedition to Hamra' al-Asad
The next day the Prophet called on the people to pursue Quraysh. The Muslims marched on their wounds until they reached [[place/hamra-al-asad]], eight miles from Medina {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.190}. He sent Ma'bad al-Khuza'i to Abu Sufyan, who waged a war of nerves against him until the Meccan army's resolve collapsed, and they continued their withdrawal to Mecca. The Prophet executed [[person/abu-azza-al-jumahi|Abu Azza al-Jumahi]], who had broken his pledge {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.286}.

## The Qur'an and the battle
About sixty verses of Surat Al 'Imran came down on the matter of Uhud, beginning with the verse [[passage/quran-3-121|آل عمران 121]] {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.193}. The verses address the position of the hypocrites, the cause of the reversal in disobeying the order, and the wisdom of the trial in distinguishing the truthful from the liar.

## The academic synopsis in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The sections above follow the traditional Muslim narrative as retold in modern biographies. The entry on Uhud in the Encyclopaedia of Islam, by C. F. Robinson, gives a shorter synopsis in the register of academic history, and the points below are its account, labelled as such. It describes the mountain as rocky and plateau-topped, about five kilometres north of [[place/medina|Medina]], and the site of an important battle between [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] and the Meccans {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 837}.

Traditional accounts date the battle to Shawwal of year 3, and trace its origins to the aftermath of [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]]; variant traditions date it to year 4 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 837}. Under the leadership of [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-harb|Abu Sufyan ibn Harb]], the Meccans resolved to avenge their defeat. They were incited by tribesmen and tribeswomen who had lost relatives at Badr, such as his wife [[person/hind-bint-utba|Hind bint Utba]], and by those whose goods had been plundered. The large force they assembled is often put at three thousand men {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 837}.

The article notes that the sequence and chronology of the Meccans' movements around Uhud are somewhat confused, but that most authorities have them encamping north of the city and letting their mounts graze on the ripening fields. It suggests that this provocation, at least in part, moved the Prophet to muster a large force of his own, against the advice of several Companions, and to leave Medina. Ibn Ishaq's sources put his force at one thousand {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 837}. Although outnumbered, the Muslims are said to have carried the early part of the battle, perhaps because the topography favoured them {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 837}.

According to the synopsis, the turn came when the Muslim archers, numbered at fifty in some accounts and at a hundred in others, left the position from which they protected the flank or rear, apparently to join their fellows in despoiling the Meccan camp. [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]], commanding the right flank of the Meccan cavalry, exploited the gap and overran the Muslims. In the confused rout a large number were killed, perhaps as many as sixty-five to seventy, among them the Prophet's uncle [[person/hamza|Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib]], whose death is said to have grieved Muhammad greatly {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 837}.

Rumours spread that Muhammad had been killed; the article says he was apparently only wounded, and that the news of his survival raised Muslim spirits enough for them to regroup higher up the mountain. Abu Sufyan did not press his advantage and withdrew towards Mecca towards the end of the day. The number of Meccans killed is usually put in the low twenties {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 837}.

## How the accounts took shape: an academic view
The same article stresses that this synopsis is drawn from the material deposited in the sira and maghazi tradition of the second and early third centuries after the Hijra, which came to dominate the classical tradition without quite monopolising it. By the time of [[person/ibn-ishaq|Ibn Ishaq]], who died around 150 AH, a general consensus on the sequence of events had emerged, even though [[person/al-waqidi|al-Waqidi]], writing two generations later, was still piecing his account together from a variety of sources, as Ibn Ishaq himself had done {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 837}.

On reliability, the author judges the material difficult to assess. He regards it as clear that the Muslims suffered a disheartening defeat, provided that several verses of Surat Al Imran are taken to allude to real events, a point he supports with references to Noldeke and Schwally and to Watt. He also considers the date fairly certain, since the tradition's basic chronology of the Medinan period inspires confidence and the battle is occasionally used to date other events, for example in a report by al-Zuhri cited by [[person/al-baladhuri|al-Baladhuri]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 837}. Because the Qur'an alludes to Uhud rather than describing it, he adds, any full reconstruction of the battle has to presume that the sira is essentially historical in its details {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 837}.

The article says that how the tradition arose is not yet clear and that Uhud was probably not exceptional. It suggests that early, probably fragmentary accounts were passed on mainly by the descendants of participants and of those who fell, and that these produced plain lists on one side and well-finished legends on the other, such as the story of the Ethiopian slave [[person/wahshi-ibn-harb|Wahshi]], who killed Hamza. It adds that much of the poetry embedded in the accounts reflects tribesmen's experience of the melee; its authenticity can be doubted, and some of it is clearly exegetical, but much may be authentic. Meanwhile, Qur'anic exegetes were concerned to place the Qur'anic echoes of Uhud in their historical setting {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 837}.

## Positions compared
The traditional narrative above and the Encyclopaedia's synopsis agree on the main outline: a Meccan force under Abu Sufyan, an early Muslim advantage, the archers leaving their post, Khalid's cavalry turning the battle, the death of Hamza and the withdrawal of the Meccans. They differ in framing. The traditional sections give a detailed narrative with named individuals and report the Prophet's instructions to the archers, while the academic entry gives round or alternative figures such as fifty or a hundred archers and a Muslim force of one thousand in Ibn Ishaq's sources, and discusses how the accounts were transmitted. The museum records both and does not decide between them {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 837}.

## Ali at Uhud: al-Sallabi's account
The fighting at Uhud opened with a single combat between [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] and the standard-bearer of the polytheists, who is called "Talha ibn Uthman" in the book's text. The man repeatedly demanded single combat until Ali went out to him, struck him and cut off his leg. He fell, his nakedness was exposed, and he cried out: "O my cousin, I adjure you by God and by kinship." Ali turned away from him and did not finish him off. The Prophet said Allahu akbar, and when some of the Companions asked Ali why, he said: "My cousin appealed to me by kinship when his nakedness was exposed, so I was ashamed before him" {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.93}.

After the melee was joined, Ali took up the standard after [[person/musab-ibn-umayr|Mus'ab ibn Umayr]] was killed, and he distinguished himself in defending the Prophet. It was he who took the Prophet's hand when the Prophet fell into the pit {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.93}. When the news of the Prophet's death spread and Ali could not find him, he broke the scabbard of his sword and charged the enemy until they opened a way for him. He found the Prophet and stood firm with him. He received sixteen blows that day {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.94}.

After the polytheists withdrew, the Prophet sent Ali after them to learn their intention. If they had turned their horses aside and mounted camels, they meant Mecca; if they had mounted horses and driven the camels before them, they meant Medina. Ali saw that they had set out for Mecca and informed the Prophet {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.94}. The book counts among the lessons of this episode the Prophet's courage and his trust in Ali, Ali's generosity in leaving his opponent once the man's nakedness was exposed, and the abandonment of the hill by some archers as one of the causes of the defeat {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.94}.

- The Bukhari numbers 3712 and 4075 in the book's margin notes need checking before they are quoted.

## For Sprouts
The battle of Uhud was fought near the mountain. The Muslims learned a lesson there about staying faithful to what they were commanded.

## For Explorers
In the battle of Uhud, near Mount Uhud, Quraysh had the upper hand at first. Children learn from it that the Prophet told the archers to stay in their places, that patience and obedience matter, and that the Prophet loved his companions and prayed for them.

## For Scholars
A battle near Mount Uhud in which Quraysh had the upper hand at first, after some Muslims disobeyed the Prophet's order to the archers on the hill and lost what they had been guarding. The sources relate its events through al-Bukhari and others, including the Prophet's charge to the archers, Abu Sufyan's words about the day of Badr, and the Prophet's climb of the mountain with his companions. Accounts of the dead and the missing need exact sources; this entry gives the battle as far as is needed to understand its lessons, without the details of wounds and killing.

## Al-Sallabi's analysis: the lessons of Uhud
In his [[work/al-sira-al-nabawiyya-sallabi|al-Sira al-Nabawiyya]], [[person/ali-muhammad-al-sallabi|Ali Muhammad al-Sallabi]] devotes a section to "some lessons, morals and benefits" of Uhud — his own reading of the events. {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.512} He sees the Qur'anic verses on Uhud as reminding the believers of God's patterns in history, consoling them and explaining God's wisdom in what happened, correcting mistakes and holding up earlier fighters as examples. {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.513} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.514} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.515} Among the chief lessons he counts that the archers' disobedience to their commander caused the setback and that preferring this world to the next is dangerous, and he dwells on how the Prophet treated the archers who erred and the hypocrites who withdrew. {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.516} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.517} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.520} He gives paragraphs to the hadith "Uhud is a mountain that loves us and we love it" and to the angels at Uhud, then sums up the laws of victory as he understands them: victory is in God's hand and follows fixed patterns — unity of ranks, obedience to God and His Messenger, not scrambling for worldly gain; small numbers are not the cause of defeat, though preparation is required; and steadfastness, patience and much remembrance of God. {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.522} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.523} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.524}
