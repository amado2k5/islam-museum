## Overview
Al-Hadi also wrote at the foot of his letter another poem, which says in sense that nothing restrains the people of error and ruin but swords, that the threat is near and the hour of war come {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.280}. He follows it with two lines in the tawil metre in which he names himself the son of the Messenger of God and of his legatee {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.281}.

## Detail
The poet describes the imam, that is himself, as one who trusts in God and is resolute like a lion, who forgave again and again and then cleared the air, acting out of kindness and care for Islam, until their breaking of faith lasted long and they went to excess in rebellion, so that he gave them notice of war, sure of God's help; he bids them strive and take shelter, says he will charge with his sword called Dhu al-Fiqar through the surges of war until he lifts the darkness, that arms and hands will fly and heads fall, and describes his standing as set in the midst of prophethood, one raised among lance-points {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.280} {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.281}. He closes that the letter has reached its time, removing every ignorance and wrong, and that God does what He wills {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.281}. In the two following lines he calls himself the son of the Messenger of God and of his legatee, whose merit and battles cannot be counted, long among the lions of war in thrust and blow {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.281}. The notes give manuscript S's readings ("and his also" in the base text, two single words, and "the days") and explain a plural word as a variant of "shin". The poem is ascribed to al-Hadi in a Zaydi book; the claim of descent and merit is his own, which the museum neither affirms nor denies, and no word of abuse is repeated.
