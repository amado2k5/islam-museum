## Overview
The rest of the poem on p. 329 and the top of p. 330, in which the poet says of a praised man that he was guidance and light in peace and a sword in battle, tells what the people of the sons of al-Zahra did in defence and vengeance, and boasts that his father is al-Hadi, his grandfather the Messenger of God, and that the first of them was a prophet and the last an imam; on p. 330 he says they filled the earth with Islam and justice, ruled the peoples of Yemen and Sham, and that without them people would not have prayed, made pilgrimage, paid zakat or fasted.

## Detail
P. 329 opens with the praise of a man who was "in peace guidance and light, and a sword in battle" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.329}, and says some people copied the deeds of Banu Ziyad, the enemies of al-Taff, and followed Hisham, led their horses and sought from the sons of al-Zahra force and wrong {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.329}. The poet says they found them noble lions and found those others base cowards, and gave them the cup of poison to drink {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.329}; he says "O sons of al-Zahra, defend your lineage or die noble" and calls on "Sa'd of the defenders" and "Ra'al" to take vengeance on their enemy, the footnote glossing ra'al as the young of the ostrich {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.329}. He says the sword of vengeance of Banu Ali broke its fast after a long fast, that the cutting swords gave judgment on their foreheads, that they seized their horses and widened the captives' lot {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.329}, and that they saw killing them as better than humiliation {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.329}. Then he says his father is al-Hadi, who subdued creatures and defended guidance, that his grandfather is the Messenger of God, that his people originated every noble act, that the first of them was a prophet and the last an imam, and that people owe obedience to their wet-nurse {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.329}. The footnotes note that S reads "we judged" and "he moved" in two places {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.329}. On p. 330 he says they "filled the earth with Islam and justice" and ruled the peoples of Yemen and Sham, guided them to a straight path and stood firm for their religion, and made what was forbidden to them allowed and what they followed as allowed forbidden {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.330}; that without them people would not have bowed down in prostration nor stood in voluntary prayer, nor made pilgrimage, nor undertaken jihad, nor paid zakat, nor imposed fasting {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.330}; that every grown person prays blessings on them when he prays and follows with greetings of peace {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.330}; and that it is enough of a boast that they were made the completion of every guidance and obligation {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.330}. These are the poet's claims in a Zaydi book; the museum adopts none and gives no verdict, and notes that the poem comes under a heading that ascribes it to Muhammad ibn al-Mukhtar, as in [[event/a-poem-after-the-killing-of-hamdan-at-saada-that-the-heading-gives-to-muhammad-ibn-al-mukhtar|the previous event]]. After the last line on p. 330 come a formula of praise and the basmala, then the heading "and of what happened of the reports of al-Hadi ila al-Haqq" and the year 294, whose reports begin the next batch. The poem stands in a Zaydi book in the voice of the imam's followers; its claims, descriptions and judgments on opponents are the poet's or the narrator's, and the museum adopts none of them, repeats no word of abuse and gives no verdict.
