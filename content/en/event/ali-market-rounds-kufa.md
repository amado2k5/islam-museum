## Summary
Al-Sallabi describes Ali ibn Abi Talib's rounds in the market of Kufa, in which he personally inspected those conducting transactions and ordered them to deal in accordance with religious law in buying and selling {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 265}.

## Facts
The book narrates from Abu Matar that he left the mosque and a man called out from behind him, "Raise your lower garment, for it is longer-lasting for your clothing and more pious before your Lord," and then he realized it was Ali, who was carrying the dirra {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 265}. In the market, Ali passed by camel sellers and said, "Sell and do not swear oaths, for swearing moves the goods but blots out the blessing" {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 265}. Then he came to the date sellers and found a slave girl weeping because her master had rejected dates she had bought for a dirham; he said to the seller, "Take your dates and give her a dirham, for she has no authority of her own," and told the date seller that he wished to be pleased with him, to which the man replied that he would not be pleased with him unless he gave people their full rights {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 266}.

## Rulings and Prohibitions
The book enumerates what Ali used to command in the market: prohibiting swearing oaths in selling, urging the feeding of the poor as an increase in earnings, prohibiting the sale of floating dead fish, and prohibiting inflating meat (blowing air into it) {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 266}. Among his prohibitions was his disapproval of women crowding men in the markets {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 267}, and his saying to merchants, "Do not turn away a small profit lest you be deprived of a large one" {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 267}. When he saw a man telling stories, he asked him about what preserves religion and what destroys it, and the man replied: what preserves it is scrupulousness, and what destroys it is greed {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 267}.

## The Issue of Market Stalls
The book states that Ali ruled in the market of Kufa that whoever reached a spot first had the greater right to it as long as he remained there that day, and once he left it, it belonged to whoever took his place, and that this rule remained in effect until Ziyad's governorship over Kufa {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 268}.

## The Book's Reading
Al-Sallabi sees these episodes as showing that governance in Islam was based on regulating markets and teaching merchants the rules of sale, and that today this requires lessons for merchants in mosques located in the heart of markets {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 268}.

## Notes for Researchers
- The book transmits these rounds from scattered reports (Abu Matar, al-Asbagh, and Abu Sa'id al-Khallal), some of them in late sources; they should be verified in their original sources.
- The text of the book places some of these reports in Kufa and some in Basra (a man told Abu Matar that he was from Basra); the spatial setting of these rounds requires review.
