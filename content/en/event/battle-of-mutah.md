## Summary
In Jumada al-Ula of 8 AH the Prophet sent an army of three thousand fighters to al-Sham to answer the killing of his messenger al-Harith ibn Umayr; it met Heraclius's forces and their Arab allies at [[place/mutah|Mu'ta]] in the land of al-Balqa. The three commanders the Prophet had named in order were martyred — [[person/zayd-ibn-haritha|Zayd ibn Haritha]], then [[person/jafar-ibn-abi-talib|Ja'far ibn Abi Talib]], then [[person/abd-allah-ibn-rawaha|Abdullah ibn Rawaha]] — and [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] took the banner and brought the army back safely {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/15-25}.

## Preparations and the farewell
The Prophet named the commanders in order: if Zayd falls, then Ja'far; if Ja'far falls, then Abdullah ibn Rawaha. At the farewell Ibn Rawaha wept, explaining that he feared returning from a place he could not know, and excused himself with rajaz verses voicing his desire for martyrdom {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/15}.

## The halt at Ma'an and Ibn Rawaha's address
The army halted at [[place/maan|Ma'an]] in the land of al-Sham, where word reached them that Heraclius had halted at Ma'ab in al-Balqa with a hundred thousand Romans, joined by as many again of Lakhm, Judham, al-Qayn, Bahra' and Bali under the command of Malik ibn Zafila. Some men wavered and spoke of asking for reinforcements, but Abdullah ibn Rawaha stood and said: "you do not fight people by numbers, strength or multitude — you fight them only with this religion by which God has honoured us. Go forward: it is one of two fair outcomes, victory or martyrdom" — and the people agreed to march on {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/16-17}.

## The battle
When the army reached [[place/masharif|Masharif]] in al-Balqa, Heraclius's army met it, and the Muslims drew off to the village of Mu'ta and formed for battle. The right wing was led by Qutba ibn Qatada al-Udhri and the left by Ubaya ibn Malik al-Ansari {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/19}.

Zayd ibn Haritha carried the banner until he "died amid the spears of the host" {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/19}; Ja'far ibn Abi Talib took it, charged forward on his chestnut mare and hamstrung her — the first hamstringing in Islam — then fought until he was killed {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/20}. Ibn Hisham adds that Ja'far held the banner in his right hand until it was cut off, then in his left until it too was cut off, then clasped it to his chest with his upper arms until he was killed, aged thirty-three {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/20}.

Abdullah ibn Rawaha took the banner after him and hesitated a moment; he rebuked himself in rajaz — "I have sworn, soul, that you shall dismount" — then dismounted his horse and went on fighting until he was killed {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/21}.

## The withdrawal with Khalid
Thabit ibn Aqram raised the banner and declined the command, so the Muslims agreed upon Khalid ibn al-Walid. Khalid covered the army, then withdrew with it safely {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/21}.

## The prophetic announcement and the grief
In Medina the Prophet announced the martyrdom of the three commanders by revelation before the news arrived, reporting that he had seen them on couches of gold in Paradise; he noted that Abdullah ibn Rawaha's couch tilted somewhat from his two companions' because he had hesitated briefly before going on {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/22}. He visited Asma bint Umays, Ja'far's wife, kissed his children's heads while his eyes welled, then ordered the people to prepare food for Ja'far's household, "for they are occupied with the affair of their man" {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/22}.

## The martyrs of Mu'ta
Ibn Ishaq gives the list of martyrs: of the Muhajirun, [[person/jafar-ibn-abi-talib|Ja'far ibn Abi Talib]] and [[person/zayd-ibn-haritha|Zayd ibn Haritha]], Mas'ud ibn al-Aswad (Banu Adi) and Wahb ibn Sa'd (Banu Malik); of the Ansar, [[person/abd-allah-ibn-rawaha|Abdullah ibn Rawaha]] and Abbad ibn Qays (Banu al-Harith ibn al-Khazraj), and al-Harith ibn al-Nu'man and Suraqa ibn Amr (Banu Mazin). Ibn Hisham adds, from Ibn Shihab al-Zuhri, Abu Kulayb and Jabir the sons of Amr, and Amr and Amir the sons of Sa'd {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/30}.

## The Encyclopaedia of Islam on Mu'ta

The second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam treats Mu'ta in an article on the place, describing it as a town in the centre of a fertile plain east of the Jordan, east of the southern end of the Dead Sea and about two hours' journey south of Karak. It is known, the article says, for the defeat of the Muslims there in Jumada I of the year 8 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 810}. The paragraphs below set out what that article reports and how it judges the sources. They are the academic reading of the event, kept apart from the Muslim accounts above.

### Cause and aim

According to the Arabic account, [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] sent three thousand men because an envoy he had sent to the ruler of the region was murdered. The article's author judges that the real reason seems to have been a wish to bring the Christian or pagan Arabs living there under his control {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 810}.

### Leaders and the size of the enemy

The article notes that if the story of the three appointed leaders is correct, the choice of [[person/zayd-ibn-haritha|Zayd ibn Haritha]], then [[person/jafar-ibn-abi-talib|Ja'far ibn Abi Talib]], then the poet [[person/abd-allah-ibn-rawaha|Abdullah ibn Rawaha]] shows that the danger of the expedition was fully recognised. It also observes that the stories tend to stress the dangers and the overwhelming size of the opposing force, in the author's view to put the unhappy outcome in a better light. In the diwan of [[person/hassan-ibn-thabit|Hassan ibn Thabit]], the article adds, we are told only that the three leaders fell in succession {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 810}.

At [[place/maan|Ma'an]] the Muslims learned that 100,000 Byzantine soldiers and Bedouin had gathered at Ma'ab. The article calls this figure much exaggerated and notes that Ibn Hisham doubles it. It records two proposals for the site of Ma'ab, Ladjdjun and al-Rabba, and says that al-Tabari describes it as a camp rather than a town {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 810}. The article states that the Arab story, which makes [[person/heraclius|Heraclius]] himself the gatherer of this army, is of course not true {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 810}.

### The battle and the command

The article reports that the Muslims lost courage and wished to wait for reinforcements until Abdullah ibn Rawaha filled them with enthusiasm for martyrdom. It says the Ibn Hisham placing of the meeting at the village of Masharif must be a misunderstanding, since the term denotes Syrian fortresses at the desert's edge. Seeing the enemy host, the Muslims withdrew southward, and fighting began at the village of Mu'ta {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 810}.

When the three named leaders had fallen in the order given, the men wanted Thabit to take command, but he gave it to [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]], who saved the rest of the force. The article calls this the first occasion on which Khalid's military talents served the Muslims, says it is not known how he did it, and judges that the stratagem told by [[person/al-waqidi|al-Waqidi]] is not to be taken seriously {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 810}.

### The Byzantine account

The article points to a Byzantine report, the earliest in the history of the Prophet, by the historian Theophanes, whose version the author says bears the stamp of veracity. In it Muhammad sent four chiefs to the land east of the Jordan against the Christian Arabs, who were to fall on them on a feast day at a village named Mucheon. Scholars read this name differently: one takes it as a copyist's error for Ma'ab, another identifies it with Khirbet al-Mahna {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 810}. The imperial deputy Theodorus learned of the plan, gathered the garrisons of the fortresses and attacked the Muslims at Mu'ta, defeating them. Three of the leaders and most of the force were killed, and one man called Chaledos, named the sword of God, alone escaped {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 810} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 811}.

### The tombs

The article adds that the tombs of the martyrs who fell there used to be pointed out at Mu'ta, where a mausoleum was built over them {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 811}.

### How the accounts differ

Read together, the Muslim sources above, which report the withdrawal under Khalid as a rescue of the army, and the Byzantine report, which speaks of a defeat of the Muslims, are different views of the same event. The museum records both as attributed positions and gives no verdict between them.

## For young visitors
A small army went out from Medina to distant al-Sham and found an army far larger than itself. The commanders did not flee; each fought knowing he might not return, one after another, until the fighting ended and those who remained came home.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lecture series Al Dinu Al Qayem

The speaker says Khalid ibn al-Walid entered a hundred battles in jihad and Islam without being defeated in one. At Mu'ta, he says, the Muslims numbered about thirty thousand against about two hundred and forty thousand Romans (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep11 00:46:30}.

He recounts that after the three senior commanders fell, Ja'far ibn Abi Talib, Abdullah ibn Rawaha and Zayd ibn Haritha, the army chose Khalid to lead, and that Khalid described himself as recently converted to Islam {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep11 00:47:00}.

He says Khalid reorganised the army by night, moving the rear to the front and the front to the rear to deceive the enemy {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep11 00:47:30}. He describes Khalid's withdrawal as a planned retreat that saved the rest of the army {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep11 00:48:00}.

The speaker's view (opinion): the decision to withdraw was right, and Khalid was a commander, not a hired soldier {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep11 00:48:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep11 00:50:00}. He recounts that Abu Bakr called Khalid "the drawn sword of God", and he urges that Khalid's battles be studied strategically {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep11 00:50:00}.

He says Khalid was not defeated in a battle before Islam or after it, and asks what Islam added to him {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep11 00:49:00}. He says Khalid was about thirty, and that the Companions were young, and he urges the study of their lives (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep11 00:50:30}.

## For Sprouts
In the eighth year after the Hijra the Prophet sent a small army of three thousand north, because his messenger had been harmed and slain. They fought at Mu'ta, near today's Jordan, and their three leaders fell one after another. Then Khalid ibn al-Walid took the flag and brought the army home to Medina safely.

## For Explorers
In Jumada al-Ula of year 8 AH (629 CE) Sharahbil ibn Amr al-Ghassani took the life of the Prophet's envoy al-Harith ibn Umayr, so the Prophet sent three thousand men to Syria — the first clash between the Muslims and the Byzantines and their Arab allies. The Prophet named three commanders in order: Zayd ibn Haritha, then Ja'far ibn Abi Talib, then Abd Allah ibn Rawaha, and all three were martyred at Mu'ta in the Balqa region. The soldiers then asked Khalid ibn al-Walid to lead, and he managed a careful withdrawal that brought the army back to Medina. The battle is also framed as a test after the Hudaybiyya truce; al-Bukhari records that nine swords broke in Khalid's hand.

## For Scholars
The Battle of Mu'ta (Jumada al-Ula 8 AH, c. September 629 CE) was fought on the edge of Syria at Mu'ta in the Balqa. Its cause was the killing of the Prophet's envoy al-Harith ibn Umayr al-Azdi by Sharahbil ibn Amr, the Ghassanid governor of Busra. The Prophet sent three thousand men under Zayd ibn Haritha, ordering that Ja'far ibn Abi Talib succeed him if he fell, then Abd Allah ibn Rawaha — the first large engagement between the Muslims and Byzantine power with its Arab allies the Ghassanids, Lakhm and Judham. The three commanders were martyred in the named order; Thabit ibn Arqam raised the flag, and the troops called on Khalid ibn al-Walid, who led a disciplined withdrawal back to Medina. Some Medinans called them 'the runners-away'; the Prophet answered they were 'the returners-to-attack'. In the sira Mu'ta foreshadows the confrontation with Byzantium at Tabuk; al-Bukhari preserves the Prophet naming Khalid 'a sword among the swords of God' and his vision of the three martyrs in Paradise.

## Al-Sallabi's analysis: Mu'ta
[[person/ali-muhammad-al-sallabi|Ali Muhammad al-Sallabi]] relates the Muslims' farewell to the army, Abd Allah ibn Rawaha's weeping for fear of passing over the Fire, and his stand when the army hesitated before the Byzantine host: "We do not fight people by number, strength or multitude; we fight them only by this religion with which God has honoured us… it is one of the two good things: victory or martyrdom." {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.734} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.735} He describes the martyrdom of the three commanders, Zayd, Ja'far and Ibn Rawaha, and then Khalid ibn al-Walid's withdrawal plan: holding the two armies apart, switching right and left wings and van and rear by night with loud noise, and rapid attacks at dawn to make the enemy think reinforcements had arrived. {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.735} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.736} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.737} He dwells on the people of Medina meeting the army by throwing dust at it and crying "Runaways!", and the Prophet's reply, "They are not runaways but those who will return to the attack, God willing", seeing in it the effect of the Prophet's education even on children. {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.738}
