## Overview
al-Baladhuri relates that the Byzantines gathered about a hundred thousand for the battle of Ajnadayn, and that Heraclius was staying at Homs that day. The Muslims fought very fiercely, [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] distinguished himself greatly, and the battle ended in the defeat of the Byzantines. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.157} It is part of the [[event/conquest-of-syria|conquest of Syria]].

## The martyrs
al-Baladhuri names among those who were martyred there [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr-ibn-abd-al-muttalib|Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr ibn Abd al-Muttalib]] and [[person/tulayb-ibn-umayr|Tulayb ibn Umayr]], whose right hand was struck in a duel before he was killed. He also names others, such as [[person/salama-ibn-hisham|Salama ibn Hisham]] and [[person/ikrima-ibn-abi-jahl|Ikrima ibn Abi Jahl]], and he relates that some were said to have been killed at Mu'ta or at the Yarmuk or Marj al-Saffar. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.157}

## Heraclius and the date
al-Baladhuri says that when the news reached [[person/heraclius|Heraclius]] he was alarmed and moved from Homs to Antioch, and it was said that his flight came when the Muslims arrived in Syria. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.157}

Most sources place the battle on Monday, twelve nights before the end of Jumada al-Ula 13 AH, and two other reports place it in Jumada al-Akhira. The Gregorian conversion is derived. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.157} It took place at [[place/ajnadayn|Ajnadayn]].

## Encyclopaedia of Islam account
The Encyclopaedia of Islam (2nd ed.), unsigned, describes Adjnadayn as the traditional name of the site of a battle fought in Jumada I or II 13 (July–August 634) between the Muslim Arab invaders and the Greek defenders of Palestine. Topographical reasoning places the battle on the Wadi al-Samt near the two villages of al-Dabbana, and the traditional name seems to have arisen from the dual al-Dabbanatayn, conflated with the Arabic plural adnad, "armies". The Greek forces were commanded by Theodorus, brother of the emperor Heraclius. The Arab forces, three contingents that had been operating in Palestine and Transjordan, were united under the command (most probably) of Khalid b. al-Walid, who had reached Syria from the Euphrates three months earlier; a less probable version names 'Amr b. al-'As. The Arabic sources greatly exaggerate the numbers, and the forces on each side probably scarcely reached 10,000 men. The Greeks were severely defeated and withdrew to Damascus, leaving Palestine open to the invaders. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 227}
