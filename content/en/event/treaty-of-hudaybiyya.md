## Overview
Al-Hudaybiya, also spelled al-Hudaybiyya, is a medium-sized village on the edge of the sacred territory of Mecca, one stage of travel from the city itself. The Encyclopaedia of Islam notes that neither the village nor the Mosque of the Tree, which it presumes stood where the pledge described below was taken, was known in the time of al-Fasi, who died in 832/1429. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 571}

The village gave its name to an expedition led by [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] in Dhu al-Qa'da of the year 6 of the Hijra, March 628, and to the ten-year treaty that ended it. The article is by W. Montgomery Watt, and where it offers an interpretation the text below says so. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 571}

## Background and the march
According to the article, Muhammad had a dream, compare [[passage/quran-48-27|Sura al-Fath verse 27]], in which he saw himself performing the rites of the lesser pilgrimage, the [[concept/umra-rite|umra]], at [[place/mecca|Mecca]], and he decided to set out for that purpose. The author adds his own reading that the journey would also show the pagans of Mecca that Islam was an Arabian religion and posed no threat to the standing of their sanctuary. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 571}

Muhammad hoped the nomadic tribes near [[place/medina|Medina]] would join him, but the article says they saw little prospect of booty and feared the venture might end in disaster. He set out with only about 1,400 followers, mostly from Medina itself, taking camels for the sacrifice. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 571}

The author judges that the Meccans, after the failure of their attempt to besiege Medina, would have been seen as weaklings had they let Muhammad enter even as a pilgrim, and so they sent out 200 cavalry to bar his way. The party took an unusual and very difficult route and reached al-Hudaybiya, on the edge of the sacred territory, where Muhammad decided to halt and negotiate. Several emissaries passed between him and the Meccans. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 571}

## The terms
A treaty to last ten years was agreed. The article lists its chief provisions. For the present Muhammad and his followers were to withdraw, but in the following year Mecca would be vacated for three days so that they could perform the umra. There was to be no raiding between the two parties. Muhammad was to send back to Mecca anyone of [[group/quraysh|Quraysh]] who came to him without the permission of his or her protector, and the author presumes this meant minors or women. Parties other than Quraysh were free to enter an alliance with either side. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 571}

On the conclusion of the treaty Muhammad and his followers sacrificed their animals. The article says some thought Muhammad's policy mistaken. The OCR of the next lines is cut off, so the text on how this disappointment was answered is not used here. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 571}

## The Pledge under the Tree
The article reports that during the negotiations a belief arose among the Muslims that one of their envoys, [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman ibn Affan]], had been killed. Muhammad then called on them to take an oath of support, known as the Pledge of Good Pleasure, bay'at al-ridwan, or the Pledge under the Tree, see [[event/bayat-al-ridwan|the Pledge of the Tree]]. The name recalls the wording of [[passage/quran-48-18|Sura al-Fath verse 18]], which says God was well pleased with the believers when they pledged under the tree. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 571}

The oath is usually described as a vow not to flee. The author considers it more likely, following a statement attributed to one authority in al-Waqidi, that it was an oath to follow Muhammad in whatever he decided, and he adds that if so the oath marked an increase in Muhammad's constitutional powers. This is his scholarly assessment, not the report of the early sources. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 571}

## What followed
A year after the original expedition Muhammad performed the pilgrimage with a party of about 2,000, see [[event/umrah-of-qada|the Fulfilled Umrah]]. The article says the treaty remained in force for only about ten months more, because a quarrel between allies of the two contracting parties led to Muhammad's victorious entry into Mecca in Ramadan 8/March 630, see [[event/conquest-of-mecca|the Conquest of Mecca]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 571}

## The account in the Sira and Hadith sources
In Dhu al-Qa'da of the sixth year after the hijra, the Prophet set out from Medina with fourteen hundred of his Companions as umra pilgrims, driving the sacrificial animals and entering ihram at Dhu al-Hulayfa so people would know they sought no fighting; when they reached Usfan word came that Quraysh had mustered its Ahabish to bar the Muslims from the Sacred Mosque. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}

The Prophet took a rough road through the ravines until he halted at al-Hudaybiyya at the sanctuary's edge, where its dry well welled up for them by the blessing of the Prophet's arrow; negotiation envoys came in succession from Quraysh — Budayl ibn Warqa', Urwa ibn Mas'ud al-Thaqafi and al-Hulays ibn Alqama — until Quraysh sent [[person/suhayl-ibn-amr|Suhayl ibn Amr]] to seal the peace. {cite:sahih-al-bukhari}

The treaty's terms set down war between the two parties for ten years; the Muslims were to return home this year and come back the following year as umra pilgrims, staying three days with a rider's arms only; whoever came to the Prophet from Quraysh as a Muslim without his guardian's leave was to be returned to them, while whoever came to Quraysh from the Muslims was not returned; and whoever wished to enter the Prophet's pact entered it, and whoever wished to enter Quraysh's pact entered it. Khuza'a entered the Prophet's alliance and Banu Bakr entered Quraysh's. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}

When the peace was sealed [[person/abu-jandal-ibn-suhayl|Abu Jandal ibn Suhayl]] came dragging his chains, and the Prophet returned him to his father in keeping faith with the treaty, counselling patience; the Muslims slaughtered their sacrificial animals and shaved their heads following the Prophet's lead after [[person/umm-salama|Umm Salama]]'s counsel, and on their return road God sent down Sura al-Fath, giving tidings that it was "a manifest victory", as in [[passage/quran-48-1|Sura al-Fath verse 1]]. {cite:sahih-al-bukhari}

## Ali in the treaty
[[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] wrote the treaty between the Muslims and the polytheists. He wrote "Muhammad, Messenger of God." The polytheists objected: "Do not write 'Muhammad, Messenger of God.' If you were the Messenger of God we would not fight you." The Prophet told Ali, "Erase it." Ali replied, "I will not erase it," so the Prophet erased it himself with his hand. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.100} The peace also allowed him and his Companions to enter Mecca for three days, but only with their weapons sheathed. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.100}

The treaty also dealt with the case of runaway slaves. The Prophet said to Quraysh, "Either you stop, or God will send against you someone who will strike your necks with the sword for the sake of the religion." He then said of Ali, "He is the one who mends the sandal," because the Prophet had given Ali his own sandal to mend. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.100} See [[event/hudaybiyya-refusal-to-return-slaves|Refusal to return the slaves]].

The book reports that some extremist Rafidites criticised the stance taken by Umar and by some Companions during the treaty. It answers them, holding that Umar's questioning of the Prophet was an effort to reach the truth and not doubt, and that it shows the Prophet consulted his Companions and excused those who questioned him. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.100-102} The book states that the Prophet ended his ihram after the Companions did not follow his command, and it records the scholars' explanations for their hesitation; the book takes this as evidence of freedom of opinion in the Muslim community. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.102}

The book says that the verse about the people of the pledge under the tree was revealed concerning them, and that the verse is referred here to [[passage/quran-48-18|Sura al-Fath verse 18]] without quoting its text. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.103}

## Notes for researchers
- What the book attributes to "the extremist Rafidites" is a view attributed to them as al-Sallabi presents it, and it is not the view of the museum.
- The book states that the report of "the sandal-mender" is sound in the sum of its chains, and it refers to two sources; those references have not yet been verified.

## For Sprouts
The Prophet set out for Mecca to perform Umrah, and a peace was made with Quraysh at al-Hudaybiyya, which God called a clear victory.

## For Explorers
The Prophet and his companions set out for Mecca to perform Umrah, and they did not want war. The people of Mecca made peace with them at al-Hudaybiyya, a truce for ten years. The Qur'an calls this treaty a clear victory, although many people did not understand it at first.

## For Scholars
In Dhu al-Qa'da of the sixth year after the Hijra the Prophet set out with about fourteen hundred companions to perform Umrah, seeking no war. When Quraysh blocked them at al-Hudaybiyya, talks were held through Suhayl ibn Amr and ended in a ten-year truce, with a clause that people coming from Mecca as Muslims without their guardian's permission would be returned. The Qur'an, in surat al-Fath, calls it a clear victory. The reports say some companions objected to its terms at first. This entry presents the treaty as the sources give it, and makes no judgement on what the negotiators felt.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lecture series Al Dinu Al Qayem

The speaker recounts Suhayl ibn Amr, the orator of the Arabs, who was writing down the treaty's terms. He objected to the word "the Merciful" in the basmala, said he did not know it, and asked that it be written "in your name, O God" {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep05 00:36:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep05 00:37:00}.

He recounts a clause on returning people who passed from one side to the other, which he calls an unjust condition. Umar objected to it and left the tent of negotiation, while Abu Bakr stood guard at the tent {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep05 00:39:30}.

He recounts that the Prophet said to Umar, "Calm down, O Umar," and Umar, not at peace, went to Abu Bakr for support {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep05 00:40:30}.

He says Suhayl ibn Amr later became Muslim. He recounts that Umar had proposed, when Suhayl was a captive, that his front tooth be knocked out so that he could not stand up as an orator against the Prophet; Umar then said the Arabs need an eloquent orator, and that Suhayl might one day have a stand that would matter {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep05 00:45:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep05 00:45:30}. After the Prophet's death, he says, Suhayl preached in Mecca and brought back those who had wavered into apostasy {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep05 00:46:00}.

## Al-Sallabi's analysis: the lessons of al-Hudaybiya
[[person/ali-muhammad-al-sallabi|Ali Muhammad al-Sallabi]] holds that al-Hudaybiya was "rich in lessons of creed, law, legal theory and education", and gives examples. {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.687} In creed: al-Mughira ibn Shu'ba standing over the Prophet with a sword when the enemy's envoys came; the difference between good omen and evil augury; the hadith of Zayd ibn Khalid al-Juhani on attributing rain to the stars; and al-Shatibi's remark that seeking blessing from relics was never practised by the Companions with anyone after the Prophet. {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.687} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.688} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.689} In law: the expiation for an ailment of the head, from the hadith of Ka'b ibn Ujra ([[passage/quran-2-196|al-Baqara 2:196]]); making up a prayer one slept through; and the length of a truce — ten years, longer if in the public interest according to Abu Hanifa, or renewal of the contract according to al-Shafi'i — preferring the first. {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.690} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.691} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.692} He states his view that the idea that jihad was legislated only for defence is "an alien idea", citing Sayyid Qutb — a position in a modern debate, attributed to him. {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.693} In legal theory, the rule that "the unqualified stays unqualified", from the Prophet's answer to one who asked about the promise of entering Mecca; in education, the story of Umar and the good deeds he did out of fear for what he had said that day. {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.693} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.694} He calls the treaty "the herald of the beginning of Islam's expansion" beyond Arabia through the Prophet's letters to rulers. {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.714}
