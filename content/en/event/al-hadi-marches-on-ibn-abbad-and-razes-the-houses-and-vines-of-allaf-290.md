## Overview
Al-Hadi stayed so for a time until in Muharram of the year 290 [[person/al-hadi-yahya-al-rassi|al-Hadi]] resolved to march on the men and sent for the armies; Ibn al-Hakami had sent [[person/ahmad-ibn-abbad-al-akili|Ibn Abbad al-Akili]] an army of horse and foot, and when they learned of al-Hadi's resolve to fight them they dispersed the army of [[group/banu-tarif|Al Tarif]] and the army of al-Hakami, and Ibn Abbad and his clan stayed in their place {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.243}. When al-Hadi's armies had gathered he went out with them in the year 290, on Thursday, to a place called al-Hadaiq, where he pitched his tents and ordered his army to halt, and stayed two days {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.243}.

## Detail
On Saturday he went out against the men, drew up his army on the edges of the land and the fight broke out from the sides of the land; his companions charged and drove them into the mountains, horse and foot following them, and they entered the fort of [[place/allaf-yemen|Allaf]] and razed the houses and cut the vines {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.244}. Then [[person/al-zubayr-al-kulaybi|al-Zubayr ibn Muhammad al-Kulaybi]] came, asked safety for himself and his household and received it, and al-Hadi went back to his camp and spent the night there; on Sunday he drew up his army and went on until he reached Allaf, and while he was razing the houses and burning, al-Zubayr came and asked safety for others of [[group/banu-kulayb-saada|Banu Kulayb]]; al-Hadi said he would give no safety to them unless al-Zubayr brought him [[person/sulayman-ibn-hajar-al-kulaybi|Sulayman ibn Hajar]]; he came, still diligently cutting the vines, and al-Hadi stopped cutting, gave safety to all Banu Kulayb, went back to his camp and spent the night of Monday there, and resolved to return to [[place/saada|Sa'da]] {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.244}. The margin of the manuscript reads Alaq (Allaf) and the print and the previous batches read Allaf, so the museum keeps Allaf. The museum describes these acts of war as the narrator tells them and gives no judgement. The Ibn Abbad here is probably the Ahmad ibn Abbad al-Akili of his record, on the strength of the name, the clan and the book's own context, and is linked as probable only.
