## Summary
The treaty concluded in 941/1535 between [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Süleyman]] and [[person/francis-i-france|Francis I]] through his secretary [[person/jean-de-la-foret|Jean de la Forêt]], later known as the Franco-Ottoman Capitulations {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p. 203}.

## Its chief articles in al-Salabi
Free navigation by armed and unarmed ships; trade rights for French subjects in every part of the state; customs and taxes paid once; consular representation with immunity for the consul, his relatives and staff; the French consul judging civil and criminal cases of the king's subjects with local enforcement of his rulings; a consular interpreter present when sultan's subjects were parties; French testimony admitted; freedom of worship; and a ban on enslaving the king's subjects {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p. 203-204}.

## Consequences and the author's verdict
The two fleets cooperated, together attacking Naples' coasts and Nice in 1543 {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p. 204}. Al-Salabi judges the treaty critically — 'the first wedge driven into the Ottoman state's coffin,' whose own subjects gained nothing, as if concluded only to serve Western demands; this is the author's judgement, presented as such {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p. 204-205}.
