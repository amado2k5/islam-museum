## Overview
The battle of al-Yamama is part of the [[event/ridda-wars|wars of the Ridda]]. Abu Bakr sent [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] to [[place/al-yamama|al-Yamama]] to fight [[person/musaylima|Musaylima]], and that was in 12 AH according to al-Baladhuri. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.120-127}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam, which this page also follows, describes the Ridda as the name given in Islamic historiography to a series of battles against nomadic and settled tribes, from shortly before the Prophet's death through the caliphate of Abu Bakr. The article adds that the term is often a misnomer, because many of the tribes had no formal tie to the Muslim state, and that the tribe of the Hanifa in al-Yamama was led by Musaylima, one of several chieftains who posed as prophets. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 765}

## The classical Muslim account
This section gives the account of the Muslim writers, as the Encyclopaedia of Islam summarises it. A deputation of the [[group/banu-hanifa|Banu Hanifa]] of al-Yamama visited the Prophet in Medina during his lifetime, and among its members was Musaylima ibn Habib al-Hanafi, whom Muslim authors later called al-Kadhdhab, the Great Liar. On returning to al-Yamama he is said to have renounced Islam and declared himself a prophet, and the tribe was allowed to drink wine and to do without prayer, as [[person/ibn-hisham|Ibn Hisham]] reports. The article names [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]] and Ibn al-Athir as the chroniclers of the long struggle against him, led by Khalid. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 280} The separate record of the [[event/delegation-of-banu-hanifa|deputation of the Banu Hanifa]] sets out the differing reports about Musaylima's own conduct there.

After the Prophet's death, the article says, Musaylima's following among the Banu Hanifa increased greatly, and he was felt to be a serious threat to the young Islamic state. [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] therefore sent a large army against him under Khalid. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 719} The fighting took place at al-Akraba', with its centre in a walled garden that came to be called the garden of death because of the numbers killed on both sides. The Muslims won, but lost many of their Qur'an reciters. Several men claimed responsibility for the death of Musaylima, among them Wahshi, the Abyssinian slave who had killed [[person/hamza|Hamza]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 719}

## The land and the place of the battle
The article on al-Yamama explains that in early Islamic times it was a region of Najd whose centre was [[place/hajr-yamama|Hajr]]. Today the name belongs to a small town in Saudi Arabia, about 70 km south-east of Riyadh, and the article suggests that the name may come from the Arabic word for wild pigeons. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 280}

The article on al-Akraba' calls it a place on the frontier of al-Yamama, famous for the bloody battle in which Khalid defeated Musaylima and the Banu Hanifa. Near it stood a grove surrounded by a wall, which before the battle was known as "Rahman's garden". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 366}

## The year of the battle
The sources do not agree on the year. Al-Baladhuri, whose account is set out below, places the conquest of al-Yamama in 12 AH. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.120-127} The article on al-Yamama states that Musaylima was killed there in 11/632. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 280} This page records both and gives no verdict.

## The battle
al-Baladhuri relates that al-Rajjal ibn Unfuwa was the first to meet the Muslims, and he was killed. Notable men and reciters of the Qur'an were martyred, then the Muslims rallied and God sent down His help. The Muslims drove their enemies into a garden called that day [[place/hadiqat-al-mawt|the Garden of Death]], and Musaylima was killed there. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.120-127}

Reports differ over who killed Musaylima, and those named include Khidash ibn Bashir, Abd Allah ibn Zayd ibn Tha'laba, Abu Dujana, and [[person/wahshi-ibn-harb|Wahshi ibn Harb]]. Some say they all took part in killing him. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.120-127}

## Among the martyrs
al-Baladhuri names among the martyrs [[person/zayd-ibn-al-khattab|Zayd ibn al-Khattab]], [[person/thabit-ibn-qays|Thabit ibn Qays]], Abu Hudhayfa ibn Utba, and Salim, client of Abu Hudhayfa. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.120-127}

## The settlement
[[person/mujjaa-ibn-murara|Mujja'a ibn Murara]] told Khalid that most of the people of al-Yamama had not come out to fight him, and he made peace with him. al-Baladhuri's account ends with Khalid appointing a deputy over al-Yamama. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.120-127}

The Gregorian date is derived. The details of the settlement need an additional source.

## The academic reading of Musaylima's movement
The article on Musaylima, written by W. Montgomery Watt, offers a reading that differs in places from the classical account above, and it is set out here under its author's name. The article calls Musaylima a man of the Banu Hanifa who led a large part of his tribe in revolt, and says he is often called al-Kadhdhab, the liar or false prophet, because he claimed prophethood. It doubts the report that he was also called al-Rahman, since that was a name of God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 718}

The author argues that Musaylima did not become a person of importance until after the death, in 630, of [[person/hawdha-ibn-ali-al-hanafi|Hawdha ibn Ali]], ruler of the Banu Hanifa. Hawdha was possibly the strongest man in central Arabia, allied with the Persian empire and guaranteeing the security of its caravans over part of the route from Yemen to Iraq. He is said to have answered the Prophet's envoy favourably but to have asked for a share in the political control of Arabia, and he did not become a Muslim. After his death the nomadic section of the tribe probably recognised [[person/thumama-ibn-uthal|Thumama ibn Uthal]] as leader, while a large settled farming section also existed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 718}

On this reading Musaylima made a bid for leadership, possibly in rivalry with Thumama, on the basis of his claim to prophethood. He had strong support from al-Rajjal ibn Unfuwa, who had been one of the deputation and may have encouraged him to build a political order centred on a prophet, on the model of Medina. His aim seems to have been a strong principality in al-Yamama, in view of the decline of Persia and the likelihood that trade between Yemen and Iraq would fall. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 718} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 719}

The author adds that the movement had a genuine religious basis, which the hostile Muslim propaganda does not entirely conceal. Many of the Banu Hanifa were Christians, and Musaylima appears to have been influenced by Christian ideas: he spoke of the kingdom of heaven and taught resurrection and a Last Day on which people are judged by their deeds. For his followers he prescribed formal prayer three times a day, fasting and abstinence from wine, and the author rejects the contrary report in Ibn Hisham as propaganda. He also used rhythmic rhymed prose, like the early suras of the Qur'an. Near the end of the year 10, the article says, Musaylima is said to have written to the Prophet proposing a division of authority, and the proposal was rebuffed. Shortly before the battle he is said to have married [[person/sajah|Sajah]], the prophetess of the [[group/banu-tamim|Tamim]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 719}

The article also notes that a traveller, W. G. Palgrave, found Musaylima still regarded as a prophet in Najd in 1862. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 719}

## The treaty with the Hanifa, according to the article on the Ridda
The article on the Ridda warns that its course is hard to reconstruct, because the accounts contradict one another and are often apologetic or polemical, written to raise the standing of a tribal leader or to blacken an opponent. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 766} It says that Khalid's treaty with the Hanifa required that he receive one orchard and one field of his choice in each village the treaty covered. Villages left out of the treaty bore the full consequences of defeat: the people of Mar'at were enslaved and a group of the Tamim settled there, and Musaylima's home town of al-Haddar was likewise outside the treaty, so Khalid enslaved its people and settled a Tamim group in it. The article reads these changes as showing how local Tamim groups gained from cooperating with Khalid in al-Yamama. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 767}

These terms differ from those reported by al-Baladhuri in the section on the settlement above, and this page does not decide between them. The article also sees the Ridda as a prelude to the wider conquests, in which Khalid, whose mother was a nomad, played a crucial part. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 767}

## In Khalifa ibn Khayyat
[[person/khalifa-ibn-khayyat|Khalifa ibn Khayyat]] reports from Abu Ma'shar, from Zayd ibn Aslam, that 140 of the Muhajirun and Ansar were killed at Yamama and 450 in all; Ibn al-Musayyab says 500, among them fifty, or thirty, bearers of the Qur'an. {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 111} He then gives the roll of those martyred. {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 111}
