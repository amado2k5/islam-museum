## Overview
Al-Hadi goes on with the poem over pp. 314-315, saying they made for the addressee's house trusting his brotherhood and he excused himself as busy, that they asked him for people up to twenty and he did not, saying they had disobeyed him, and claimed every lion was with him, then saying they had thought he would not hold back, and that his horse went off to the chase and fled from meeting the lions when they rose for the thrust. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.314}

## Detail
On p. 314 al-Hadi says "we made for your house and held to a brotherhood from you that was not doubtful", that they did not think there would be a barrier at his door, that he told them his house had work and they kept saying the answer would come, that they were excusing and did not press him, and he swears "by the right of the grandfather of Abu Turab", words printed so and calling on [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Abu Turab]], a name of Ali {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.314}. He says they asked him "for people up to twenty" when the opponent was dull, and he did not, saying they had disobeyed him, which is the oddest of wonders, and that he claims every lion is with him going to death like wolves, and that if a dog saw prey they would obey {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.314}. On p. 315 he says this obedience "came about by my guess", that the addressee claimed that if they had stayed in his house they would have been strong in the fighting, that they had not hurried to leave nor been anxious to go, that they sent to him at dawn and thought him not one to turn away, but he stammered the talk and spoke like a timid man {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}. He says the addressee's horses "went astray" when they sought to fight their enemy, that they wanted war, and that when they rose for thrust and blow they "went off to the chase seeking every gazelle and fled from meeting the lions of the thicket" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}. The footnotes record that the first line of p. 315 and the one after it are missing from manuscript S, that "al-ghalt and al-ghalat are the same", and that S reads another word in the line with "bin-nab" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}. The book names neither the addressee nor the house nor the place or time of the meeting, so it is unknown who he is or which war is meant; the lines remain a verse complaint the Zaydi book reports from the poet, with intentions and sayings ascribed to the addressee in the poet's voice alone, and the museum does not judge them. They continue [[event/al-hadi-abandons-the-abodes-of-love-and-names-his-arms-in-verse-292|the poem that begins with leaving the abodes of love]] and are followed by [[event/al-hadi-reflects-on-death-the-reckoning-and-the-stranger-in-the-world-292|his reflections on death and the reckoning]]. The poem is given in a Zaydi book in the voice of the imam's followers; its claims, descriptions and judgments on opponents are the poet's or the narrator's, and the museum adopts none of them, repeats no word of abuse and gives no verdict.
