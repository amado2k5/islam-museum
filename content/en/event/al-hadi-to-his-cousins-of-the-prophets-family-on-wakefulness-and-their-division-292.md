## Overview
"And also of his, to his cousins of the family of the Messenger": "Sleep is driven from my eyes by cares that lie down beside me, and a grave affair that bars sleep" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.300}.

## Detail
He says he is kept awake because he has no friend or brother who shares with him what he bears, and he thinks about the world and the paltriness of its affairs {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.300}. On p. 301 he speaks of people each of whom is fond of his desires, who gathers for the heir, grudges good to himself and puts off the door of right guidance, and then asks whether it is not grave that a man should make peace with a wrongdoer who is unjust to the people of the right; he describes the right as having few followers whom their helpers and defenders have lost, its precinct empty and its signs and ordinances effaced, the family of the Messenger busied with rancour and the reviving of grudges while they have many springs, properties and fields, the Talibid lions having failed one another, not joined in it and cooperated little, not seeking the inheritance of prophecy with the lance, their right lying deposited with others {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.301}. He calls them to what brings pride, saying that if a Talibid band supported him it would have praiseworthy qualities and chieftains, he turns on those among them who come to terms with the enemy, and says it is grave that they should become abased when they are lions in times of fear; a footnote glosses "dasa'i'" as virtues or noble deeds {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.301}. This is the poet's claim ascribed to al-Hadi in a Zaydi book and includes a political description of the family of Abu Talib that the museum neither endorses nor rejects. The poem goes on in p. 302 in [[event/al-hadi-calls-the-family-of-ahmad-to-unite-like-a-torrent-292|its call to unite]]; [[event/al-hadi-to-abu-al-qasim-patience-the-forefathers-and-counsel-290|the counsel to his son]] came before. The account is in a Zaydi book in the voice of the imam's followers; its descriptions and its judgments on opponents are the narrator's or the poet's, and the museum adopts none of them and gives no verdict.
