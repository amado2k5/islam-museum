## Summary
The call went out among the people that the prayer was gathering, and when he had finished praying the Prophet sat on the pulpit, smiling, and said that he had not gathered them out of hope or fear but because Tamim al-Dari, who had been a Christian and had come, pledged allegiance and become Muslim, had told him an account that agreed with what he had been telling them about the Messiah, the Dajjal. This is what Fatima bint Qays relates in Sahih Muslim, number 2942; she was in the rows of women behind the men. {cite:sharh-nawawi-minhaj-risala on Muslim 2942, pp. 529-534}

## The gathering
Fatima says she heard the call that the prayer was gathering, went to the mosque and prayed with the Prophet; he then sat on the pulpit and said that each person should stay in his place of prayer, and told them why he had gathered them. In another narration from al-Sha'bi she says she heard him preaching from the pulpit and saying that some cousins of Tamim al-Dari had gone to sea. {cite:sharh-nawawi-minhaj-risala on Muslim 2942, pp. 529-534} The hadith gives no date for the gathering. Ibn Hajar says in al-Isaba, in the entry on Tamim, that the Prophet told the story of the Jassasa and the Dajjal and related it from him on the pulpit, and that this was counted among his merits. {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba entry of Tamim al-Dari}

## The account
Tamim said he had sailed in a sea-going ship with thirty men of Lakhm and Judham, and the waves played with them for a month. They put in at an island at sunset and sat in the ship's small boats, and a creature with thick hair, so thick they could not tell its front from its back, met them. Asked what it was, it said it was the Jassasa and told them to go to a man in a monastery who longed for news of them. They were frightened it might be a she-devil; they went into the monastery and found the largest man they had ever seen, bound tight, his hands gathered to his neck and iron between his knees and ankles. He asked about the date palms of Baysan, Lake Tiberias, the spring of Zughar and the prophet of the unlettered people, and said he was the Messiah, that he was about to be allowed out and would set foot in every town within forty nights except Mecca and Tayba, which were forbidden to him, angels guarding every pass into them. The Prophet then struck the pulpit with his staff, said "this is Tayba" three times, and said that the man was in the Sea of Syria or the Sea of Yemen, no, from the east, and pointed his hand toward the east. {cite:sharh-nawawi-minhaj-risala on Muslim 2942, pp. 529-534} The museum has also recorded al-Mas'udi's version of the same account, taken from Muruj al-Dhahab, in [[concept/al-jassasa]]. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 530-531} See also [[concept/dajjal]], [[concept/dabbat-al-ard]], [[place/buhayrat-tabariyya]], [[person/tamim-al-dari]] and [[person/fatima-bint-qays]].

## How the scholars received it
Al-Nawawi says the name is pronounced with a fathah on the jim and a doubled sin and that it was said to be so named for spying out news for the Dajjal, and that a report from Abdullah ibn Amr ibn al-As makes it the beast of the earth named in the Qur'an. Of the Prophet's saying that Tamim told him this, he says it is counted among Tamim's merits since the Prophet related the story from him, and that it shows a more excellent person narrating from a lesser one, a leader from his follower, and the acceptance of a single narrator's report. {cite:sharh-nawawi-minhaj-risala on Muslim 2942, pp. 529-531} In Tadrib al-Rawi al-Suyuti makes the Jassasa hadith the root example of type forty-one, the narration of seniors from juniors, saying the basis is the Prophet's narrating from Tamim al-Dari the hadith of the Jassasa, which is in Muslim. {cite:tadrib-rawi-awwama-v5 p. 266} Ibn Hajar says Fatima bint Qays is the one who narrated the Jassasa story at length and was alone in it, and that al-Sha'bi took it from her when she came to Kufa to her brother, who was its governor. {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba entry of Fatima bint Qays}

:::perspectives
**Sunni: Abu al-Abbas al-Qurtubi in al-Mufhim.** He found that Fatima's hadith in this narration differs from her better-known one in several places: for example, her words that her husband was struck down in the first jihad suggest he was killed, whereas the well-known account is that she was finally divorced from him; he relays Qadi Iyad's reading that she meant to list his merits. He also notes that Umm Sharik was a Qurashi, not an Ansari, and that there is disagreement over the genealogy of Ibn Umm Maktum. {cite:mufhim-qurtubi-v7 pp. 294-296}

**Sunni: al-Nawawi defends the wording of the narration.** He answers Qadi Iyad on Ibn Umm Maktum by saying the narration is sound, that "branch" here means tribe, and that "cousin" is meant loosely since he belonged to her tribe. He reads her words about being proposed to as coming after the end of her waiting period, not during it. {cite:sharh-nawawi-minhaj-risala on Muslim 2942, pp. 530-531}

**Sunni: al-Qurtubi on the Prophet's hesitation over the direction.** He says the words that he is in the Sea of Syria or the Sea of Yemen, no, from the east began as conjecture, then doubt appeared, and then he turned from them to the truth; that conjecture and hesitation occur in the Prophet as a human being, as forgetfulness does, but that he is not left in them and is guided to the truth, which he then confirmed with the extra "ma" and by repeating it. Al-Nawawi relays Qadi Iyad's view that "ma" here is a filler, not a negation. {cite:mufhim-qurtubi-v7 p. 300} {cite:sharh-nawawi-minhaj-risala on Muslim 2942, pp. 533-534}
:::

## What the sources do not specify
The hadith places the island no more exactly than on the eastern side, gives no date for the gathering and names none of the thirty men beyond their tribes, Lakhm and Judham. In al-Nawawi's commentary on the Ibn Sayyad reports there is a pointer that those who held Ibn Sayyad to be someone other than the Dajjal relied on Tamim's hadith, but that page was read from a two-column OCR scan whose columns are mixed, so the speaker could not be confirmed. Ibn Hajar's own commentary on this hadith in Fath al-Bari was not read in this pass, because the volume on the trials is not in the scans available.
