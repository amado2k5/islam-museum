## Overview
The surrender of [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]] to the Muslims, in the time of the caliph [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar ibn al-Khattab]], is the opening event of the city's Islamic history. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, article al-Kuds, describes it as a bloodless and poorly recorded handing over of the town to an inconspicuous tribal commander, and says the period that follows was inaugurated by the building of the [[place/dome-of-the-rock|Dome of the Rock]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 360} The sources differ on who received the surrender, on the text of any treaty, on the year, and on what Umar did in the city. This page sets out each account and its source without choosing between them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 361} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362}

## Names of the city
In early Islam the full name was Iliya, the city of the Temple, in practice shortened to Iliya or, more often, Bayt al-Maqdis. The article explains Iliya as the Roman name Aelia, which Muslim scholars did not recognise as such and so explained in other ways, for example as the sanctuary of Elijah. Bayt al-Maqdis is the Aramaic word for Temple, and it was soon transferred from the sanctuary to the city. The name al-Quds is not found in al-Tabari, Ibn Sa'd, al-Baladhuri and the other early classics, and it appears frequently only from al-Muqaddasi, writing around 375 AH. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 360} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 361}

## The road to the surrender
The article takes the battle of [[place/ajnadayn|Ajnadayn]], in the summer of 13 AH/634, as the event that opened southern Palestine to the Muslims. It reports that no siege was laid on Jerusalem, and quotes the sermons of the aged patriarch Sophronius, who in the winter of 634 mourned the raids that made the usual journey to Bethlehem impossible. Four years passed, it says, between the invasion of Palestine and the fall of the city, which it places early in 638, at the end of 16 or the beginning of 17 AH, after the decisive battle of the [[place/al-yarmuk|Yarmuk]] in Rajab 15/August 636. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 361}

## The date: two positions
The date given in the article, early 638, corresponds to 16 to 17 AH. The record on this page also carries 15 AH, because the index to the third volume of al-Tabari's history lists the account under that year. The two dates are reported side by side here, with no verdict. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 361}

## The accounts of the surrender
The article divides the stories of the fall into groups. The oldest and, in its judgement, most trustworthy tradition, found in [[person/al-baladhuri|al-Baladhuri]], says only that the capitulation was arranged with Khalid ibn Thabit al-Fahmi, a little-known tribal commander. The terms were that the open country belonged to the Muslims, while the city would be left alone as long as its people paid the tribute laid on them. No treaty text is mentioned in this version. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 361}

A second type, represented by [[person/al-yaqubi|al-Yaqubi]] and by the Christian chronicler Eutychius, reproduces a treaty. According to the article, it is very short and differs little from al-Baladhuri's version. Later tradition added conditions like those made with the Byzantine authorities in Egypt, and some Christian authors added that no Jew should live in the city. The article traces this last condition in al-Tabari to his source Sayf ibn Umar, whom it calls thoroughly unreliable, citing Wellhausen, and it judges the treaty Sayf offers worthless because of its wrong date and unlikely witnesses. This is the author's assessment and other readers may weigh the transmission differently. The article adds that the stipulation, in its view, could not have been in the Muslims' interest, and that later practice shows it never existed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 361} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362}

A later version, found in Mujir al-Din's [[work/al-uns-al-jalil|al-Uns al-jalil]], adds several conditions of the legendary Covenant of Umar, among them that the Christians would not speak Arabic. A still later one in [[person/ibn-asakir|Ibn Asakir]], attributed to pseudo-al-Waqidi, has the treaty made with twenty Jews led by Yusuf ibn Nun, which the article calls a harmonising legend. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362}

## The covenant of Umar
The article treats the Covenant of Umar as a later development rather than a document of the conquest, and the entry on [[concept/ahl-al-dhimma|the dhimmis]] in the same encyclopaedia says the allegedly ancient Pact of Umar is not attested in its complete form before the end of the 5th/11th century. It places the pact among the texts that Muslims who read the early pledges to the dhimma restrictively sought out as authority. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 242} The museum keeps a separate record of the covenant, [[event/umar-covenant-jerusalem|Umar's covenant for Jerusalem]].

## Umar's visit
Al-Tabari and many later Muslim and Christian sources tell of a visit by the caliph. The article regards everything reported about it as legend, and judges that the tendencies of the stories are easy to see. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362}

In one school of accounts, Umar was accompanied by Jews who showed him the true site of the Temple, buried under rubble that Christians had heaped there. When it was cleared, [[person/kab-al-ahbar|Kab al-Ahbar]] suggested that Umar pray behind the Rock so that both qiblas would be before him. The caliph refused, since Muslims should face the Kaba alone. The article reads this as one of the traditions against over-valuing the sanctity of Jerusalem. According to Christian sources, the caliph visited the churches but declined to pray in one of them, so as to prevent later claims on it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362}

The article reasons that since the terms of surrender protected the Christians' use of their churches, the largely empty Temple area probably served as a place of prayer for the Muslims from the start, and that there is no reason to doubt that this was done on the order of the ruling caliph Umar. This is the author's inference. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362}

## Futuh al-Sham's telling
The [[work/futuh-al-sham-waqidi|Futuh al-Sham]] ascribed to al-Waqidi gives a long account of the conquest of Jerusalem, in volume 1, pages 219 to 236. Abu Ubayda's commanders besiege the city, its people say they find in their books that only the companion of the Muslims' prophet named Umar will take it and that they know his description, and [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda]] writes to Umar accordingly. {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.225} {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.226} Umar consults the Companions, Ali advises him to go, and he sets out on a red camel. {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.227} The book records judgments he gave among the Bedouin on the way, {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.228} his sermon at al-Jabiya and Bilal's call to prayer that brought the people to tears, {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.230} and how he put on white clothes and mounted an ambling Byzantine pack-horse, then dismounted in haste and returned to his patched cloak for fear of vanity. {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.231}

When Umar camped where Abu Ubayda was, the patriarch asked that Umar stand alone facing the wall so that he might verify his description. Umar rode out on his camel in his patched cloak with only Abu Ubayda, and on seeing him the patriarch said: this is the one whose description we find in our books, and told the people of the city to go down to him. They came out asking for covenant and protection and accepting the jizya. {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.232} {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.233} In this account Umar entered on a Monday and stayed until Friday, marked out a prayer niche on the eastern side, the site of his mosque, led his companions in the Friday prayer, and stayed ten days in all. {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.233} It includes the conversion of Ka'b al-Ahbar at Umar's hands after the dawn prayer at the Rock. {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.234} {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.235} Umar then wrote a covenant for the people of Jerusalem, confirmed them on the jizya, went to al-Jabiya where he set up the registers, and divided Syria between Abu Ubayda, from Hawran to Aleppo, and Yazid ibn Abi Sufyan, Palestine and the coast. {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.235}

This account gives no year for the conquest, and the book's ascription to al-Waqidi is open to question; see [[question/attribution-of-futuh-al-sham-waqidi|the attribution of Futuh al-Sham]].

## The Tabari report
al-Tabari relates that Umar came to al-Jabiya, and the people of Iliya went out to him seeking safe-conduct. They made peace with him for the jizya and opened the city to him. A Jew who used to ask him about the Antichrist advised him not to return until God had opened Iliya. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.3 p.607} The index of the third volume of al-Tabari places the account in 15 AH, with its pages running from 607 to 613. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.3 p.632} The details of the treaty need an additional source.

## Aftermath
The article says the conquest threw the Christian community into disarray. Sophronius died soon afterwards and no new patriarch was appointed until 706, yet the city kept much of its Christian character: al-Muqaddasi reports that Christian festivals set the rhythm of the year for Muslims too. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362}

Among the early Muslim settlers, the article names people from [[place/medina|Medina]], such as Aws, nephew of [[person/hassan-ibn-thabit|Hassan ibn Thabit]], and the Companion [[person/ubada-ibn-al-samit|Ubada ibn al-Samit]], whom it calls the first Muslim judge in the city. It also mentions the later report that Uthman gave the revenue of the gardens of Siloam, which under the settlement belonged to the Muslims, to the city's poor. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362}

About two years after the fall, [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Muawiya]] was appointed to command the army in Palestine and Syria. The article states that during his long rule the Muslim place of worship on the Temple area must have taken shape, citing the report that he built the sanctuary after Umar. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 363} The Dome of the Rock itself dates from the reign of [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik]]; its inscription bears the year 72/691-2. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 363}
