When the Prophet was thirty-five, a raging flood had cracked the Ka'ba's walls and locusts had weathered its timber; Quraysh resolved to demolish and rebuild it, and swore to put into its building only lawful earnings — no harlot's fee, no usurious sale and no wronged person's due — so it was that, for want of funds, they left the Hijr and al-Hatim out of the structure. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}

[[person/al-walid-ibn-al-mughira|al-Walid ibn al-Mughira]] began the demolition, and the Prophet helped carry stones alongside his uncle [[person/abbas-ibn-abd-al-muttalib|al-Abbas]]; the mason was a Roman named Baqum. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}

When the building reached the Black Stone's spot, the tribes disputed the honour of raising it until some nearly fought, whereupon [[person/abu-umayya-ibn-al-mughira|Abu Umayya ibn al-Mughira]] stood and proposed they let the first man to enter the mosque's gate judge — the first was the Prophet. They accepted him as arbiter, saying: "this is al-Amin — we are content with him." {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}

He called for his cloak, placed the stone in its middle, and ordered each tribe to hold an edge — so they raised it all together, and he set it in its place with his own hand. God thereby preserved the sanctity of blood and achieved every tribe's honour at once; it was among the first signs of the arbitration he would come to in disputes. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}
