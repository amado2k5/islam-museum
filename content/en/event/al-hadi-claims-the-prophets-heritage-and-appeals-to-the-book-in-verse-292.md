## Overview
Lines on p. 328 that complete the poem begun on p. 326, in which the poet says his father the Messenger of God founded his call and that his guidance bequeathed him guidance, that he set himself up as an adviser to a people, calls on them to ask the Book of God between them, asks whether he or "the servants of Al Hawwal" is more entitled to their affair, says the Prophet will stand tomorrow with his proof, and says his wish is for the Creator and not for what is in their hands, and that by Him they are mighty.

## Detail
On p. 328 the poet says "and my father the Messenger of God founded my call" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.328}, and that his guidance bequeathed him guidance, which he followed model by model {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.328}. He says he set himself up in his place as an adviser to a people given to every falsehood {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.328}. He calls on the people to ask the Book of God, which bears witness between them and speaks at every question {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.328}, then asks "am I more entitled to your command and your forbidding, O people, or the servants of Al Hawwal?" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.328}; the book does not explain the last name, so it is given as printed and nothing is linked. He says the Prophet will stand tomorrow with his proof, so they should give the answer after a delay {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.328}, that his wish is not in what their hands hold but in the Exalted Creator {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.328}, and that by the might of God, glorified, they are mighty {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.328}. The page then ends with the next heading, which leads to [[event/a-poem-after-the-killing-of-hamdan-at-saada-that-the-heading-gives-to-muhammad-ibn-al-mukhtar|a poem after the killing of Hamdan at Sa'da]]. It follows [[event/al-hadi-calls-wadia-to-the-religion-and-recounts-his-war-and-forbearance-in-verse-292|the call to Wadi'a]]. The book does not date the lines. The poem stands in a Zaydi book in the voice of the imam's followers; its claims, descriptions and judgments on opponents are the poet's or the narrator's, and the museum adopts none of them, repeats no word of abuse and gives no verdict.
