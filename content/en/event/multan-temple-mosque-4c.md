## Overview
In the second half of the 4th/10th century the Ismaili call reached Sind, and one of its da'is, Shayban, destroyed Multan's temple — its famous budd — and raised a congregational mosque in its place {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 603}.

## The budd of Multan
The budd of Multan was the best-known Indian temple to the Arabs: treasure was offered to it, vows were paid to it, and the people of Sind made pilgrimage to it, shaving their heads and beards before it. When the city was conquered the temple was left standing and the amir received a large share of its income — the earliest documented case in which Hindus were implicitly granted the standing of ahl al-dhimma {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 602}.

## The date
The source notes that the historians disagree on the year of the temple's destruction, so no single year is certain; what is sure is that the Ismaili amirate at Multan survived the destruction until Mahmud of Ghazna's campaigns {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 603}.
