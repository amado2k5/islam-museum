## Overview
For the conquest of [[place/mecca|Mecca]] (January 630 CE) the Prophet [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] left [[place/medina|Medina]] on the tenth of Ramadan, 8 AH, with ten thousand Companions {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.380}. Its cause was that [[group/quraysh|Quraysh]] broke the [[event/treaty-of-hudaybiyya|peace of al-Hudaybiyya]] by helping [[group/banu-bakr|Banu Bakr]] with weapons and men against [[group/khuzaa|Khuza'a]], the Prophet's allies, at al-Watir {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.377} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.378}. When the army camped at [[place/marr-al-zahran|Marr al-Zahran]], [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-harb|Abu Sufyan]] came out seeking news; [[person/abbas-ibn-abd-al-muttalib|al-Abbas]] met him and brought him to the Prophet, he accepted Islam, and the Prophet declared safe whoever entered Abu Sufyan's house {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.381} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.382}.

The Prophet went on to [[place/dhu-tuwa|Dhu Tuwa]], his head bowed in humility before God, the hair of his beard almost touching the middle of the saddle, and there divided his army: [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] to enter from lower Mecca, [[person/zubayr-ibn-al-awwam|al-Zubayr]] from upper Mecca and [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda]] along the valley floor {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.384}; the banner of the Ansar had been handed to [[person/qays-ibn-sad|Qays ibn Sa'd ibn Ubada]] or, it is said, to al-Zubayr {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.383}. The Prophet and his army entered Mecca, each column by its road, and at [[place/al-khandama|al-Khandama]] Khalid skirmished with men of Quraysh gathered with [[person/ikrima-ibn-abi-jahl|Ikrima ibn Abi Jahl]] and [[person/safwan-ibn-umayya|Safwan ibn Umayya]]; twelve polytheists were killed and the rest fled {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.384} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.385}. The Prophet then circled the House and with his bow struck at the three hundred and sixty idols around the [[building/kaaba|Ka'ba]], reciting from the Qur'an {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.385}. At the door of the Ka'ba he addressed Quraysh and pardoned them, saying: "Go, for you are free" {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.385} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.386}. Al-Mubarakpuri counts it the conquest that ended idolatry as a power in Arabia {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.391}.

## Mecca and the Muslims before the conquest
The conquest of Mecca, called the *fath*, was the entry of Muhammad and a large Muslim army into his native city in Ramadan of 8 AH. The Muslim sources collected in this record present it as a day of mercy: the army entered almost without fighting, the idols around the Kaaba were removed, and the people of Quraysh were told they were free. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, a second edition academic reference, describes the same events as the surrender of the town almost without a blow, and notes that it followed the breach of the truce of al-Hudaybiyya by allies of the Meccans. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 156} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 424}

The encyclopaedia dates the departure to Ramadan 8, which it equates with December 629. The date field of this record gives January 630 by tradition, and the sira passages below name the tenth of Ramadan for the departure. Reports on dates and numbers differ in the sources, so each figure below is attributed to the work that gives it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 424}

The encyclopaedia's article on Mecca recalls that Muhammad was born in the city about 570 into the clan of Hashim, and that the Qur'anic revelation began to come to him around 610. After Quraysh opposition grew, he moved to Medina in 622. Most of the years between that migration and his death, the article says, were dominated by the struggle between his supporters and the leading merchants of Mecca. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 156}

It summarises the main stages of that struggle: the capture of a small caravan at [[place/nakhla-al-yamaniya|Nakhla]] early in 624, the [[event/battle-of-badr|battle of Badr]] in which the Meccans lost many of their leaders, [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-harb|Abu Sufyan]]'s guidance of Meccan affairs afterwards, the fighting at [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]] in 625, and the failed attempt of 627 that ended when Muhammad adopted the trench and the alliance broke up. The article adds that Abu Sufyan then seems to have worked for peace and reconciliation with the Muslims. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 156} See also [[event/battle-of-the-trench|the battle of the Trench]].

## From al-Hudaybiyya to the march
In 628 the Meccans prevented Muhammad and about 1,600 Muslims from making the pilgrimage, yet they concluded the [[event/treaty-of-hudaybiyya|treaty of al-Hudaybiyya]] with him as with an equal. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 156} The article on Muhammad in the same work gives the sequence in more detail. In Dhu al-Qa'da of 6 AH, March 628, he asked his followers to bring sacrificial animals and set out on an *umra*, saying, in the encyclopaedia's wording, that a vision had promised him this goal. An armed Meccan body stopped him, he camped at [[place/hudaybiyya|al-Hudaybiyya]], and negotiations began. When a rumour spread that [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]], his envoy, had been killed, he gathered his followers under a tree and took their pledge. Uthman then returned with Meccans who offered a compromise: the Muslims would go back that year and could perform the *umra* the next. A ten-year truce was agreed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 423}

According to the same article, some followers were angry at the terms, but Muhammad ordered the sacrificial animals slaughtered and had his hair cut, and the others followed. The encyclopaedia judges that the treaty was a shrewd piece of diplomacy, because the Meccans treated him as an equal and many of them admired his adoption of their old rites. This is the article's own assessment. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 423}

At the start of 7 AH the Muslims took the oasis of [[place/khaybar|Khaybar]], a conquest the article calls the first by Muhammad, which made the community wealthy for the first time and set a pattern of leaving non-Muslim inhabitants on their land in return for a yearly tribute, later called [[concept/jizya|jizya]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 423} Late in 7 AH he performed the agreed pilgrimage, an *umra* within the sanctuary at Mecca. The article stresses its meaning for a man driven from his city almost seven years earlier. It mentions a reconciliation with the clan of Hashim sealed by marriage to [[person/maymuna-bint-al-harith|Maymuna bint al-Harith]], and the conversion of a few notable Meccans, among them [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] and [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn al-As]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 424}

The article also says that [[person/abbas-ibn-abd-al-muttalib|al-Abbas]] and Abu Sufyan were by then negotiating in secret, hoping to prepare the best possible terms for what they saw as an inevitable surrender of Mecca. It records the Muslim defeat at [[event/battle-of-mutah|Mu'ta]], where [[person/zayd-ibn-haritha|Zayd ibn Haritha]] was killed, and the adoption of Islam by some Bedouin tribes in the following period. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 424}

## The breach and the march
The encyclopaedia says that, against Abu Sufyan's advice, the belligerent party in Mecca chose to support its client clan [[group/banu-bakr|Bakr]] against [[group/khuzaa|Khuza'a]], who were allied to Muhammad. By the custom of the time both sides regarded this as a breach of the treaty of al-Hudaybiyya, which released Muhammad from its terms. In Ramadan 8, it says, he set out at the head of an army of Emigrants, Helpers and Bedouins. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 424} The Meccan article gives the size of the force as some 10,000 men. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 156} For the clash at al-Watir that the Muslim sources name as the breach, see [[event/khuzaa-bakr-clash-al-watir|the clash of Khuza'a and Bakr at al-Watir]].

News of the march caused anxiety in Mecca, and the encyclopaedia says the number of those who wanted to fight shrank daily, so the more prudent gained control. Abu Sufyan went out with several others, including the Khuza'i [[person/budayl-ibn-warqa|Budayl ibn Warqa]], a friend of the Prophet, and met Muhammad not far from the town. According to the report cited, he paid homage and obtained an amnesty for all of Quraysh who gave up armed resistance. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 424}

## The entry into the city, in the sira sources
In the Muslim narrative that the museum records from al-Mubarakpuri's biography of the Prophet and the hadith collections, the ten thousand left Medina on the tenth of Ramadan after Quraysh broke the peace by aiding Banu Bakr against Khuza'a at al-Watir. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}

When the army halted at Marr al-Zahran, [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-harb|Abu Sufyan]] came out scouting for news; al-Abbas met him and brought him to the Messenger of God, and Abu Sufyan became Muslim. The Prophet made his house a place of safety for the people of Mecca, in honour of him and to win his people's hearts. {cite:sahih-al-bukhari}

At Dhi Tuwa the Prophet divided his army into four banners: [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] from the lower part of Mecca, [[person/zubayr-ibn-al-awwam|al-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam]] from Kada, [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah]] in the belly of the valley, and [[person/qays-ibn-sad|Qays ibn Sa'd ibn Ubada]] with the banner of the Ansar. The Muslims entered Mecca in peace except for a small skirmish at al-Khandama, where Khalid met a band led by [[person/ikrima-ibn-abi-jahl|Ikrima ibn Abi Jahl]] and [[person/safwan-ibn-umayya|Safwan ibn Umayya]] and defeated them; twelve polytheists were killed. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}

The Messenger of God entered Mecca with his head bowed over his mount until his brow nearly touched the middle of the saddle, in humility to God; then he circled the Ancient House while three hundred and sixty idols stood around the Ka'ba, stabbing at them with a bow in his hand saying: "truth has come and falsehood has passed away — falsehood is ever bound to pass." Then he addressed Quraysh at the door of the Ka'ba and proclaimed his historic general pardon: "O company of Quraysh, what do you think I will do with you?" They said: good — a noble brother and a noble brother's son. He said: "no reproach is on you this day; go, you are free." {cite:sahih-al-bukhari}

## The academic account of the surrender and the amnesty
The encyclopaedia's Meccan article says the town was surrendered almost without a blow and that, except for a handful guilty of specific offences against Muhammad or some Muslim, all the Meccans were assured that their lives and property would be safe if they behaved honourably. It adds that Muhammad had for some time aimed at reconciling the Meccans rather than crushing them by force. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 156} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 157}

The Muhammad article reports that he entered his native city practically without a struggle, that almost all the inhabitants adopted Islam, and that he acted generously and tried to win hearts with rich gifts, a new use of the alms called *ta'lif al-qulub*, on which see [[concept/al-muallafa-qulubuhum|those whose hearts are to be reconciled]]. It says he demanded only the destruction of the idols in and around Mecca. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 424} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 425}

The article further reads two Qur'anic passages as reflecting the exaltation of the victory, [[passage/quran-110|sura 110]] and the opening verses of sura 48, [[passage/quran-48-1|verse 1]] and [[passage/quran-48-2|verse 2]]. It points out that both speak of forgiveness after a clear victory, *fath*, and suggests that the peaceful surrender of the city was taken as a sign that God had forgiven Muhammad all his sins. This is the author's reading, and the passages are linked here rather than quoted. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 425}

## After the conquest
According to the encyclopaedia, the conquest changed the standing of the city. About a week or two after the *fath*, news came of a large gathering of nomads east of Mecca, and some 2,000 Meccans took the field with Muhammad and helped him win the victory at [[event/battle-of-hunayn|Hunayn]]. Some of the pagan Meccans became Muslims almost at once, others after a longer time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 157} The Muhammad article adds that the [[group/hawazin|Hawazin]] began preparing to fight soon after the surrender, that his inexperienced troops were at first threatened with disaster at Hunayn but recovered, and that they could not take [[place/taif|al-Ta'if]], whose people surrendered and adopted Islam almost a year later, after the expedition to [[place/tabuk|Tabuk]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 425}

The Meccan article says a young Muslim of a Meccan family was left as governor and that it was made clear Medina would remain the capital. The Kaaba, long the *qibla* toward which Muslims turned in prayer, was purged of idols at the *fath* and became a centre of Islamic worship, while the Black Stone was kept as an object of reverence and the annual pilgrimage was retained as an Islamic ceremony. The article links this to Mecca's lasting importance in Islam, and suggests that its commercial activity dwindled, perhaps because many leading men moved to Medina and were later absorbed in governing an empire. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 157} See also [[concept/kaaba|the Kaaba]] and [[concept/qibla|the qibla]].

## Different accounts
The sources do not all agree. The encyclopaedia dates the departure to Ramadan 8, equated with December 629, whereas the Arabic sira sources used in this record give the tenth of Ramadan for the departure and the twentieth for the entry, and the record's date field is January 630. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 424} The Muhammad article also says the letters to foreign rulers that tradition places around this time cannot be accepted in their present form, and that it is unlikely such letters were sent before the conquest of Mecca. That judgement is the encyclopaedia's, and it does not describe the conquest itself. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 424}

## Ali at the conquest, after al-Sallabi
The book counts several episodes involving [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] at the conquest. The first is the mission of the woman carrying a letter from [[person/hatib-ibn-abi-baltaa|Hatib ibn Abi Balta'a]] to the polytheists {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.108}, and the Prophet's refusal to let Umar kill Hatib because he had fought at Badr {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.108}. The second is that [[person/umm-hani-bint-abi-talib|Umm Hani]] gave protection to two men whom Ali wished to kill; she shut the door on them and took the matter to the Prophet, who confirmed her protection {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.108}. See [[event/umm-hani-protection-of-two-men|Umm Hani's protection]].

The third is that the Prophet declared the blood of [[person/al-huwayrith-ibn-nuqayd|al-Huwayrith ibn Nuqayd]] forfeit, among those who had harmed him, and Ali killed him when he found him {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.109}. The fourth is his mission to Banu Jadhima; see [[event/ali-sent-to-banu-jadhima|Ali sent to Banu Jadhima]].

## For Sprouts
The Prophet entered Mecca as a conqueror, humble before God. He gave the people of Mecca safety and said to them: Go, for you are free.

## For Explorers
In Ramadan of the eighth year after the Hijra the Prophet entered Mecca with ten thousand Muslims, and he came in humble before God. He cleansed the Kaaba of idols, and told the people of Mecca: Go, for you are free. It was a day of mercy and forgiveness.

## For Scholars
In Ramadan of the eighth year after the Hijra, after Banu Bakr and Quraysh broke the truce of al-Hudaybiyya, the Prophet marched with ten thousand fighters and entered Mecca with his head bowed in humility before God. He divided the army into four columns, cleansed the Kaaba of idols, and proclaimed a general pardon for the people of Mecca with the words: Go, for you are free. In the Sahih the Prophet is reported to have declared the sanctity of the city on that day. The reports give different numbers of idols and different lengths of stay, so each number needs a source. This entry presents the conquest as a day of pardon, as the sources transmit it.

## From Omar Abdelkafy's programme Bustan al-Amal
The Sheikh relates that the Prophet entered Mecca as a conqueror in humility, his beard touching the pommel of his saddle, reciting 'truth has come and falsehood has perished', then asked Quraysh: what do you think I will do with you? They said: good, a noble brother and son of a noble brother; and he said what Yusuf said to his brothers: no blame on you today, go, you are free; he says two thousand embraced Islam that day because of his mercy (needs_source: the number as the Sheikh gives it). {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy bustan-el-amal/ep11 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy bustan-el-amal/ep11 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy bustan-el-amal/ep11 00:08:00}

## Al-Sallabi's analysis: the results of the conquest
In [[work/al-sira-al-nabawiyya-sallabi|his Sira]], [[person/ali-muhammad-al-sallabi|Ali Muhammad al-Sallabi]] lists the results of the conquest of Mecca as he sees them {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.778-779}: Mecca came under Muslim control and the way opened to Hunayn and al-Ta'if; a great power arose in Arabia that no tribal coalition could withstand; and there were social, political and religious effects — [[person/muadh-ibn-jabal|Mu'adh ibn Jabal]] was left in Mecca to lead the prayer and teach the people, Attab ibn Asid was appointed its governor, and the Arabs were convinced of Islam and entered it in crowds {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.779}. Among the legal rulings drawn from the conquest he gives, relying on al-Umari and Rizq Allah, the shortening of prayer during the nineteen days the Prophet stayed in Mecca, the prohibition of temporary marriage (*mut'a*) after the conquest, the rule "the child belongs to the marriage bed" in the case of Zam'a's slave-woman's son, and the bar on bequeathing more than a third in the case of Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.778}. He notes that the Prophet camped at al-Hajun, where Quraysh had sworn to boycott Banu Hashim, and told Usama: "Has Aqil left us any houses or dwellings?" {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.778}.
