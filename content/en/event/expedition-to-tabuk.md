## Summary
The expedition of Tabuk, in which [[person/muhammad|the Prophet]] marched to [[place/tabuk|Tabuk]] in Rajab of year nine in a time of hardship and heat; no fighting took place, in Ibn Ishaq's account — the one expedition whose destination the Prophet declared openly, contrary to his custom. {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/159}

## Ali left in charge of Medina
The Prophet appointed [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] over Medina during the expedition to Tabuk. The hypocrites seized the chance to speak against him in a hurtful way, saying: "He only left him behind because he was a burden to him." The book holds that this saying is a sign of their hypocrisy {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.110}. When Ali said he would be left among the children and the women, the Prophet told him: "Are you not content to stand to me in the position of Aaron to Moses, except that there is no prophet after me?" {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.110}

The book also cites a hadith from Ali in Sahih Muslim, in which the words are: "None loves me but a believer, and none hates me but a hypocrite." {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.110}

## Notes for researchers
- The book refers to al-Bukhari, number 2404, for the hadith of status, and to Muslim, number 78, for the hadith of love, as it also mentions them in its footnotes; these references have not yet been checked.

## From al-Sallabi's book Asna al-Matalib, part two: Ali's deputising over Medina at Tabuk
The author states that the Prophet appointed Ali over Medina during the expedition to Tabuk, and the hypocrites seized the chance and said: "He only left him because he was a burden to him." Ali caught up with the army and said: "Messenger of God, are you leaving me behind among the children and the women?" The Prophet said: "Are you not content to stand to me in the position of Aaron to Moses, except that there is no prophet after me?" {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 282} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 283}

The author states that deputising does not indicate leadership of the community, and that Aaron died before Moses, so the analogy does not hold after a death. Had the Prophet intended an explicit designation, he would have said "like Joshua to Moses", because Joshua was appointed after Moses died. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 283}

The author gives examples of the Prophet appointing others than Ali during his campaigns: Ibn Umm Maktum at Badr, Siba ibn Urfuta, Bashir ibn Abd al-Mundhir at al-Sawiq, Abu Dhar at Banu al-Mustaliq, Numaylah ibn Abdullah at Hudaybiyya, Uwayf ibn al-Adhta at the Umrat al-Qada, Kulthum ibn Husayn at the Conquest, and Abu Dujana at the Farewell Pilgrimage. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 284}

The author cites al-Nawawi, Ibn Hazm, Ibn Hajar and Ibn Taymiyya as saying that the hadith contains no proof for putting Ali forward for the imamate, and that Ali's deputising at Tabuk was no more excellent than anyone else's. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 285} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 286}

The author holds that the wisdom in the Prophet not naming anyone after him is that the religion is for all humanity, that naming someone would have opened the door to hereditary succession, and that the dispute at Saqifa shows there was no testament. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 286}

What the author presents here concerns his reading of the hadith, and the Shia reading of the hadith above.

## Tabuk in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam has no separate article on the expedition; its entry is on the town of [[place/tabuk|Tabuk]], and what follows is the short account it gives. This is an academic reference work's summary, set beside the Muslim narrations above, not in place of them.

The article places the town in north-western Arabia, some 233 km south-south-east of Ma'an and cut off from the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aqaba by the Hisma mountains. It suggests the town is the Thapaua of Ptolemy and says it lay within the Roman province of Arabia, in the tribal area of the [[group/banu-kalb|Banu Kalb]], later with a Byzantine military post around which lived Arabs of the [[group/banu-lakhm|Lakhm]] and [[group/judham|Judham]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 60}

For the expedition itself the article says that in the summer of 9 AH, 630 CE, the Prophet ordered preparations for a raid from [[place/medina|Medina]] on Tabuk, where he had heard that the Byzantines and Arab tribes were gathering. It calls this his second attempt in that direction after the unsuccessful expedition of [[place/mutah|Mu'ta]] the year before. Many Muslims were unwilling to march in the summer heat and the harvest season, so the expedition was called the raid of hardship, and the article says the laggards were denounced in the ninth chapter of the Qur'an. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 60}

According to the article, the Prophet made no contact with hostile forces at Tabuk, but received the submission of some local chiefs of the region and of the coast of the Gulf of Aqaba and the Red Sea, that is, [[place/madyan|Midian]]. A force under [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] was sent against [[person/ukaydir-ibn-abd-al-malik|Ukaydir ibn Abd al-Malik]] al-Kindi, the Christian ruler of [[place/dumat-al-jandal|Dumat al-Jandal]], who also submitted and agreed to pay the [[concept/jizya|jizya]]. The Prophet then appointed Amr ibn al-As, or in another report al-Hakam ibn al-As, to govern north-western Arabia, with [[person/muadh-ibn-jabal|Muadh ibn Jabal]] as collector of the [[concept/sadaqa|sadaqa]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 60}

The article adds the later fate of the place: in 98 AH, 716 CE, the caliph [[person/umar-ibn-abd-al-aziz|Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz]] built the Mosque of Repentance at Tabuk on the spot where the Prophet had prayed, and pilgrims passing to Medina revered it for centuries; the present mosque was built by King Faysal in 1393 AH, 1973 CE. The town's importance as a station on the Syrian pilgrim route came from the water of its well, which tradition says the Prophet blessed. In the early twentieth century the Hijaz Railway connected it and made it a workshop station, and it later became the centre of a province of Saudi Arabia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 60} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 61}

## For Sprouts
In year 9 the Prophet set out with a large army to Tabuk in the far north of Arabia in a season of great heat and little food, and some turned back with excuses that saddened him. There was no fighting there, and the army returned to Medina. Three who stayed behind regretted it deeply until God accepted their repentance.

## For Explorers
The expedition of Tabuk took place in Rajab of year 9 AH, in a time of severe heat and hardship in money and supplies, and the Prophet openly declared its destination toward the Romans at the edge of Arabia, against his custom of concealment. The Muslims gathered a large force called the Army of Hardship; some stayed back on excuses, like al-Jadd ibn Qays who asked leave, and the hypocrites said 'do not march out in the heat'. By Ibn Ishaq's account there was no fighting at Tabuk; the Prophet stayed a while then returned to Medina. On the way back he rebuked those who stayed behind, and three sincere men who had no excuse were left in grief until their repentance was accepted months later — the best known is Ka'b ibn Malik.

## For Scholars
The expedition of Tabuk was the Prophet's last great campaign, in Rajab of year 9 AH (630 CE), toward Tabuk at Arabia's far north near the Roman frontier. It is remembered as the Army of Hardship: severe heat, scarcity of provisions and mounts, and a ripe harvest season that tempted staying home. The sira books relate that the Prophet announced its destination openly, contrary to his usual discretion, so the hypocrites argued excuses and men asked leave. Sources preserve the equipping drives, the wealthy Companions' donations, and the stories of laggards and fighters. By Ibn Ishaq's account there was no fighting at Tabuk; the Prophet made terms with the people of Ayla, Adhruh and Jarba for tribute, stayed twenty nights, then returned. On the way back he rebuked those who stayed behind, and three truthful men — Ka'b ibn Malik, Hilal ibn Umayya and Murara ibn al-Rabi' — were suspended until their repentance was revealed after about fifty nights; Ka'b's story is among the most famous in the sira. Historians read the expedition as the last great show of force on the Roman frontier before the Prophet's death.

## Al-Sallabi's analysis: the main results of the campaign
In [[work/al-sira-al-nabawiyya-sallabi|his Sira]], [[person/ali-muhammad-al-sallabi|Ali Muhammad al-Sallabi]] holds that the main results of Tabuk were {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.849}: breaking the awe of the Byzantines in the minds of the Arabs, Muslim and non-Muslim alike, after the defeat at Mu'ta had confirmed their belief that the Byzantines were invincible; showing the Islamic state as the one power in the region able to challenge the great powers, with the submission of the principalities of Dumat al-Jandal and Ayla and the Prophet's written agreement setting out their rights and duties; and the Arab tribes of Syria beginning to reconsider their loyalty to Byzantium {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.849}. Al-Sallabi regards Tabuk as the practical starting point of the conquest of Syria, linking it with the expedition of Usama ibn Zayd that the Prophet prepared before his death {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.849-850}. He holds that this campaign, with the conquest of Mecca and Khaybar, pushed the tribes to hasten to Islam, so that delegations multiplied, and he counts the unification of Arabia under the Prophet among its results {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.850}. He describes the hard march as intensive practical training that served the Companions in the later conquests of Syria and Persia — his own view {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.849}.
