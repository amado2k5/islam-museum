## Overview
In the rest of the poem the poet says that he is not like one who rests toward the bed, that his lance is sure to be watered from the throat with blood boiling over the bones, that whoever asks him of the night of the clamour will see how he gave himself to the terrors, that he is Ahmadi, purified, Fatimi and Qasimi, far from foulness, and that he has met a hard matter that drives sleep from the mind of the sober and is no reckless fool {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.266}. He then says that the courier came singly with glad news, that he wished he had witnessed the engagement, that he would meet the son of Muhammad with brown lances at his throat, ride the horse with his spear and strike with the Indian sword at the encounter, and that shedding the blood of the oath-breakers is for him a duty as binding as an inheritance share {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.266}.

## Detail
The first note on p. 266 says the poem was mentioned earlier when Abu al-Qasim was at Khiwan, and another gives a reading "in the previous version"; the courier verses are those of p. 181, given in [[event/al-hadi-writes-to-abu-al-qasim-of-the-victory-and-his-verse-reply-286|al-Hadi's letter to Abu al-Qasim of the victory and his verse reply]] {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.181}, which settles the pointer left open in the last batch; the editor records the readings of manuscript S in the notes. The piece follows [[event/abu-al-qasim-ibn-al-hadi-verses-on-the-lance-and-the-lions-of-war-290|the verses on the lance and the lions of war]]. The book ascribes the verse to Abu al-Qasim and the museum has not checked the attribution; the boasting and the praise are the poet's, with no verdict in the museum's voice. The museum does not quote the lines nor the words of abuse in them and gives only their sense as the book has it.
