## Overview
The battle of Siffin took place in 37 AH, 657 CE, between [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]] and [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan]]. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.5 p.17} The Encyclopaedia of Islam describes it as a series of duels and skirmishes between the Iraqis under the caliph Ali and the Syrians under Mu'awiya, the governor of Syria, rather than as a single set battle. The article calls it a major factor in shaping the regional and political identity of both the Iraqi Shi'is and the Syrian [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyads]], and quotes a Syrian saying of the kind "we are the people of Syria, we are the men of Siffin". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 567}

The article adds that the political and theological debates about the battle, and about the conflict between Ali and Mu'awiya in general, stand behind many contradictory claims in Islamic historiography. This is especially so for the biographies of some of the Prophet's Companions, or alleged Companions, which go back to a dispute over how many Companions were on each side. It also says that Shi'i apologetics account for some of the reports about Muhammad's leniency at [[event/treaty-of-hudaybiyya|al-Hudaybiya]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 567}

## The site and the long wait
Siffin was a ruined Byzantine village not far from [[place/raqqa|al-Raqqa]], a few hundred yards from the right bank of the Euphrates. The article says it is now identified with the village of Abu Hurayra near al-Raqqa, and cites al-Dhahabi for the identification. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 567} The place has its own record at [[place/siffin|Siffin]].

The two armies stayed on the battlefield for a long time before fighting broke out, and are said to have faced each other for 77 days. The article reads this as a sign of the troops' reluctance to shed Muslim blood, since units on both sides belonged to the same tribes. It adds that cousins, or a father and his son, sometimes faced each other, and that two sons of the famous [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] fought on opposite sides. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 567} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 568}

## The two camps
The article gives a detailed picture of how the two camps formed. Some of the Kindis who disliked Ali left [[place/kufa|Kufa]] when he arrived and went to [[place/al-ruha|al-Ruha]] in the Jazira, reportedly because they could not bear to stay where [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]] was being cursed; at Siffin they fought with Mu'awiya. The people of al-Raqqa were then Uthmaniyya, and a tribal leader of the Asad named Simak ibn Makhrama left Ali with a hundred of his tribesmen and then persuaded six hundred more to join him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 568}

Support for Ali in Kufa itself was not unanimous. When he set out for Siffin, people who had little respect for him spoke openly, and Abu Mas'ud al-Ansari, whom Ali had left in charge of the city, voiced indifference to the outcome and was dismissed once Ali came back. Some of Ali's troops turned back on the way to the battlefield. The article ascribes this to fierce and at times cynical propaganda, in which it judges Mu'awiya to have been on the whole more successful than Ali, and it names [[person/shurahbil-ibn-al-simt|Shurahbil ibn al-Simt]] al-Kindi as a man Mu'awiya won over and turned into a propagandist. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 568}

The article also compares the two leaders over material promises to tribal chiefs. It says Mu'awiya seems to have been less scrupulous, possibly because his position was more precarious, whereas Ali, perhaps out of self-confidence and better hopes for the conflict, dealt strictly with governors who embezzled state money, which led to their defection. Its example is [[person/jarir-ibn-abd-allah-al-bajali|Jarir ibn Abd Allah al-Bajali]], Uthman's governor in Hamadhan, whom Ali dismissed after [[event/battle-of-the-camel|the battle of the Camel]]. Jarir moved to [[place/qarqisiya|Qarqisiya]] with men of his tribal group, the Qasr of the [[group/bajila|Bajila]], and later joined Mu'awiya, so that few of the Qasr fought for Ali at Siffin. Ali reacted by destroying Jarir's house in Kufa. The article concludes that Mu'awiya's well-considered opportunism, a phrase it borrows from the scholar E. L. Petersen, proved more fruitful than Ali's strictness. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 568}

A far more influential leader was [[person/al-ashath-ibn-qays|al-Ash'ath ibn Qays]] of [[group/kinda|Kinda]], who unlike Jarir fought at Siffin on Ali's side. Uthman had secured his loyalty by appointing him governor of Adharbayjan; he was still governor for a time under Ali but was dismissed after the Camel, and Ali also removed him from the headship of Kinda and Rabi'a. At the most crucial stage of the fighting, the article says, al-Ash'ath supported the [[event/arbitration-of-siffin|arbitration]] that was to cost Ali both his title of commander of the believers and then his life. The article also notes that most of Mu'awiya's troops at Siffin were from Yemen, while most of Ali's were from the line of Nizar ibn Ma'add, that is Rabi'a and Mudar. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 568} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 569}

Ali also lost the support of [[person/ubaydallah-ibn-umar|Ubayd Allah]], son of the caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab. According to the article, he fled to Mu'awiya for fear that Ali might execute him for killing innocent Persians in avenging his father's assassination. Ubayd Allah was killed at Siffin, where he commanded Mu'awiya's cavalry. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 569}

## Its course
al-Tabari reports that Ali addressed his companions before the fighting and urged them to hold the line firm and guard the banners. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.5 p.17}

In the course of the fighting, [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn al-As]] suggested to Mu'awiya that the Qur'ans be raised. The people of Syria raised them on their spears and called for the Book of God, and many people of Iraq answered that call. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.5 p.48}

The index of the volume mentions the "night of al-Harir" and the report of Hashim ibn Utba al-Mirqal. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.5 p.627}

## The numbers according to al-Mas'udi
al-Mas'udi relates that the dead numbered seventy thousand, of them forty-five thousand from Syria and twenty-five thousand from Iraq. He says that the stay there lasted a hundred and ten days, and that the engagements were seventy. Twenty-five of the Companions were killed with Ali, among them [[person/ammar-ibn-yasir|Ammar ibn Yasir]], who was seventy-three years old. In his account eighty-seven veterans of Badr fought with Ali, seventeen of the Muhajirun and seventy of the Ansar, together with nine hundred of those who gave the Pledge of Ridwan, and the Companions in all numbered two thousand eight hundred. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.645-646}

The figures come from al-Mas'udi's account, and checking them needs an additional source.

According to al-Mas'udi, Ali left Kufa on the fifth day of Shawwal 36 AH, passed by al-Raqqa and crossed on a bridge. He says it is agreed that Ali had ninety thousand men and Mu'awiya eighty-five thousand. Mu'awiya arrived first and seized the water-point through Abu al-A'war al-Sulami with forty thousand men, so that Ali's men were cut off from water, and Mu'awiya is reported to have said: let them die of thirst as Uthman died. A note was then dropped into al-Ash'ath's tent, and he went out with four thousand horsemen and broke the siege of the water. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 667-668}

In the same account Ammar ibn Yasir, aged ninety-three, fell there, together with Hashim ibn Utba al-Mirqal, Dhu al-Kala', and Safwan and Sa'd, the two sons of Hudhayfa. When the killing grew heavy, ten thousand men or more, mostly from Rabi'a and others, volunteered for Ali, and he charged with them as one man, riding his white mule and saying "From which of my days of death shall I flee?", until he reached Mu'awiya's pavilion. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 673-677} The two ages given for Ammar, seventy-three and ninety-three, differ within al-Mas'udi's account as it stands in the record.

## What al-Sallabi adds: from Muharram to the truce
After the water had passed into the hands of the people of Syria, the two sides fought over it on the first day of Dhu al-Hijja 37 AH, and then entered Muharram in an exchange of messages. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 102} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 103} Ali sent Bashir ibn Amr al-Ansari, Sa'id ibn Qays al-Hamdani and Shabath ibn Rib'i to Mu'awiya, and Mu'awiya answered that Ali should first hand over the killers of Uthman and only then receive his pledge. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 103} al-Sallabi holds that many of the reports about these messages come by weak chains, and he cites Ibn Kathir, who criticised the long details found in Abu Mikhnaf and Nasr ibn Muzahim. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 104}

## The first days of fighting
Ali announced that Wednesday would be the day of full engagement, and Amr ibn al-As brought weapons out of the stores for those who needed them. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 104} On the first day the two armies met in centre, right wing and left wing, with Ammar ibn Yasir over the infantry, Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya carrying the banner and Ibn Abbas on the left wing. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 105} Fighting went on until sunset, and each side prayed in its own camp. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 105}

On the second day Ali changed commanders. He put Abd Allah ibn Budayl on the right wing in place of al-Ash'ath, and Ibn Budayl broke Mu'awiya's left wing. Ibn Budayl was then killed and al-Ashtar took his place, and Dhu al-Kala', Hawshab and Ubayd Allah ibn Umar ibn al-Khattab were killed on the Syrian side. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 106}

## The death of Ammar
al-Sallabi relates that Ammar ibn Yasir was killed at Siffin, that he urged people on by saying that whoever wished to be surrounded by the houris should step forward, and that his last drink was milk. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 106} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 107} The author sets out the hadith of the transgressing party, and says that Amr ibn al-As and Mu'awiya differed over how to understand it, Mu'awiya saying that the one who killed Ammar was the one who brought him out. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 111} The author answers that this reading goes against the plain wording. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 114} He adds that the man to whom the reports ascribe the killing is Abu al-Ghadiya al-Juhani, and that Ibn Kathir called him an ordinary man whom some say was a Companion. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 115}

## The night of al-Harir
al-Sallabi describes the Thursday night leading into Friday as the night of al-Harir, and quotes an eyewitness description in which the two armies bit at one another and fought with iron clubs and stones until morning. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 107} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 108}

## al-Ash'ath's call for arbitration
On the night of al-Harir al-Ash'ath ibn Qays al-Kindi gave a speech calling for the fighting to stop, saying that persisting in war would mean the destruction of the Arabs. The speech reached Mu'awiya, who said that he had spoken rightly, swearing by the Lord of the Ka'ba. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 108} al-Sallabi regards the call for arbitration as sincere, and blames the Saba'iyya for spoiling it. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 108} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 109}

## The captives of Siffin
Ali would hold a man who refused to give him allegiance and release him if he gave it. If the man still refused, Ali took his weapon and mount, made him swear not to return to the fighting, and let him go. Fifteen wounded captives were brought to him, and he washed, shrouded and prayed over those who died. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 117}

## The count of the dead
Ibn Abi Khaythama states that the dead were seventy thousand, twenty-five thousand from Iraq and forty-five thousand from Syria. al-Sallabi holds that such figures are not acceptable given the nature of the fighting, since real fighting lasted about thirty hours, and he notes that the martyrs at al-Qadisiyya numbered eight thousand five hundred. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 118}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam treats the numbers of fighters and casualties as far less secure than the formation of the two camps. One report puts both armies at about 150,000 each; another has 100,000 or more with Ali and 130,000 with Mu'awiya. The article stresses that what matters most for the study of early Islamic historiography is the conflicting statistics and contradictory claims made by the two parties' supporters. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 569}

## The effect of the war, in al-Sallabi's reading
al-Sallabi judges that Siffin was the most remarkable of the clashes among Muslims, and cites a saying of Abu Hanifa that, but for the way Ali dealt with captives and rebels, the rules for fighting the people of the qibla would not have been known. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 118}

## The Sunni position
al-Sallabi, reporting the view of the Sunnis, says that the right was with Ali and that Mu'awiya was a mujtahid who would be rewarded, and that what occurred among the Companions is to be discussed only in a way that suits their rank. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 148}

In a further passage al-Sallabi holds that Ali accepted the end of the fighting and returned to Kufa hoping the arbitration would remove the dispute, that most of his army wanted a truce, which in his view weakens the claim that raising the Qur'ans was a ruse of Amr ibn al-As, since the idea was not new and had been tried at the Camel. He also sets out the readings of Ibn Hajar, al-Nawawi, Ibn Kathir, al-Dhahabi, Ibn al-Arabi, Ibn Taymiyya, Ibn Baz and Sa'id Hawwa that the transgressing party in the hadith on Ammar's killing was Mu'awiya's army, while excusing those mujtahids as seekers of retaliation for Uthman who erred. {cite:sallabi-ali-tawzi-2004 p. 558} {cite:sallabi-ali-tawzi-2004 p. 559-562}

## Other accounts: the Companions on each side
The Encyclopaedia of Islam shows that rival accounts of who fought on which side were part of the contest over Islamic prestige, and it labels the figures as reported, not as its own. In Ali's camp there were said to be 2,800 Companions, of whom 25 were killed, and the dead were said to include 25 veterans of Badr. One scholar claimed that 70 Badr veterans fought at Siffin on Ali's side, but others rejected this and held that the only Badr veteran in Ali's camp was [[person/khuzayma-ibn-thabit|Khuzayma ibn Thabit]]. Another claim gave 130 Badr veterans, and [[person/said-ibn-jubayr|Sa'id ibn Jubayr]] is reported to have said that among Ali's troops were 900 [[group/ansar|Ansar]] and 800 [[group/muhajirun|Muhajirun]]. It is also recorded that 800 of the Companions who had pledged allegiance to the Prophet at al-Hudaybiya fought with Ali and 63 of them were killed, among them Ammar. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 570}

The article notes that, in the Shi'i account it cites from Bihar al-anwar and the Kitab of Sulaym ibn Qays, the Companions, and the Badr veterans among them, are presented as confirming that Ali was in the right and as bearing witness to the truthfulness of the Prophet's statements on which he based his claim. The two Ansar who fought with Mu'awiya, it adds, could not boast of having attended the meeting at al-Aqaba or the battles of Badr and Uhud. The article warns that a scholar who used the statistics was not fully aware of the polemical value attached to them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 570}

The same quarrel shaped individual biographies. The status of [[person/busr-ibn-abi-artat|Busr ibn Abi Arta'a]] on Mu'awiya's side as a Companion was disputed: the Syrians claimed that he heard the Prophet as a small boy and so could transmit hadith from him, while the counterclaim was that he was born two years before the Prophet's death and transmitted none. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 570}

## Writing about Siffin
The battle was a popular subject for compilers of historical monographs, who included Shi'is, scholars with Shi'i sympathies and Sunnis. The article lists twenty-two scholars known to have compiled monographs on Siffin in the first three and a half centuries of Islam, and cautions that the monographs overlap, probably considerably, and that some of those listed were only transmitters. It is the differences between them that define each. For instance, the name and tribe of the Syrian warrior who killed Ammar were disputed, and al-Baladhuri cites different claims by al-Waqidi, Abu Mikhnaf, Ibn al-Kalbi and al-Mada'ini. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 570}

Among those named are Jabir ibn Yazid al-Ju'fi, who died in 128/746; [[person/abu-mikhnaf|Abu Mikhnaf]], who died in 157/774, and whose great-grandfather [[person/mikhnaf-ibn-sulaym|Mikhnaf ibn Sulaym]] was Ali's governor in Isfahan and was killed at Siffin; [[person/hisham-ibn-al-kalbi|Hisham ibn Muhammad Ibn al-Kalbi]], who died in 204/819; [[person/al-waqidi|al-Waqidi]], who died in 207/823; [[person/nasr-ibn-muzahim|Nasr ibn Muzahim]], who died in 212/827 and compiled the famous Wak'at Siffin; [[person/ibn-abi-shayba|Ibn Abi Shayba]], who died in 235/849 and whose Musannaf has a chapter on Siffin; [[person/al-madaini|al-Mada'ini]]; and [[person/ibn-atham-al-kufi-ahmad-ibn-muhammad|Ibn A'tham al-Kufi]]. For al-Waqidi and al-Mada'ini the article says their books went on to Ali's war against the Kharijites. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 570} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 571}

## In Khalifa ibn Khayyat
[[person/khalifa-ibn-khayyat|Khalifa ibn Khayyat]] says the fighting at Siffin was on Wednesday the seventh of Safar, 37, the truce on the night of Saturday the tenth of Safar, and that Ammar ibn Yasir and Hashim ibn Utba were killed. {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 191}
