## Overview
A poem in the tawil metre under the heading "and also of his", begun at the foot of p. 315: al-Hadi sees that he will die, be raised and reckoned, be stood over every slip and perish unless God forgives, that he is a stranger and traveller in the world who returns, so he bids his soul turn from the house of passing and asks when his friend will see him standing in aid of the God of truth with a cutting sword in his hand; the line runs on into p. 316. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}

## Detail
At the foot of p. 315, under the heading "and also of his", tawil lines open: "I see, by God, that I shall die, and that I shall be raised and reckoned" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}. He says he will be stood over every slip, that he perishes if God does not forgive him, and that he looks to a day whose terror turns a child's hair grey and on which the feet of creation bear witness in dread {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}. He says he is in the world "a stranger, a traveller", and every stranger returns without fail, so he bids his soul turn from the house of passing, since he desires the house of staying {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}. He then asks when his friend will see him "standing in aid of the God of truth, in the hand a cutting" blade, and the line is cut at the foot of the page and runs on into the first line of p. 316 {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}. The footnotes say that "al-ghalt and al-ghalat are the same" and that manuscript S reads another word in an earlier line {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}. The lines carry the voice of one who reflects on his last end and then wishes for work in God's cause; this is put in the poet's own words, not in the museum's judgment, and no battle or person is named. The poem is completed on p. 316: he describes the horse as "arin", lively and growing in forgiving, with "Yemeni horsemen" around him having on terror the boldness of lions on the hunt, "stallions who answered God when He called them", with keen swords in their right hands, who sold Him a world they were sure would pass for "a garden of eternity"; he says reports have never ceased to say that armies will aid them, names these riders as going "toward the heretics", which is the poet's word for his opponents and not the museum's, and speaks of the nobility of their efforts and of their being guardians of God's religion {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.316}. He says he called "Hamdan and Khawlan all of them" and the Ahlaf, and they plunged into the deeps of death, and their intentions remind him of the Companions of Badr, al-Nadir, Khaybar and Uhud and of the merits they had in the truth; then he says they work with every tempered blade against the wicked and please a God whom the stars glorify, manifest God's rule among His servants and fill the corners with justice, and that hunger, nakedness and hardship go from them as the rain drives off drought, and the Book of God is revived after its death and East and West live through them {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.316}. The two footnotes of p. 316 say "arin" means active and that manuscript S reads "arden", and that S reads "by might" where the text has "by justice" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.316}. The book names no commander here and does not date the poem; what the poet describes is a picture of an army he wishes for and calls to, not a report of a battle, and after it in the book come [[event/al-hadi-says-the-carefree-sleep-and-sends-a-traveller-from-raydah-to-banu-hasan-in-verse-292|the lines on sleep and the message to Banu Hasan]]. [[group/banu-hamdan|Hamdan]] and [[group/khawlan-tribe|Khawlan]] are named here in the poet's voice alone. It follows [[event/al-hadi-reproaches-a-friend-who-withheld-twenty-men-in-verse-292|the lines of reproach over twenty men withheld]]. The poem is given in a Zaydi book in the voice of the imam's followers; its claims, descriptions and judgments on opponents are the poet's or the narrator's, and the museum adopts none of them, repeats no word of abuse and gives no verdict.
