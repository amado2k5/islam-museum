## Overview
A poem in the tawil metre under the heading "and also of his", begun at the foot of p. 315: al-Hadi sees that he will die, be raised and reckoned, be stood over every slip and perish unless God forgives, that he is a stranger and traveller in the world who returns, so he bids his soul turn from the house of passing and asks when his friend will see him standing in aid of the God of truth with a cutting sword in his hand; the line runs on into p. 316. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}

## Detail
At the foot of p. 315, under the heading "and also of his", tawil lines open: "I see, by God, that I shall die, and that I shall be raised and reckoned" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}. He says he will be stood over every slip, that he perishes if God does not forgive him, and that he looks to a day whose terror turns a child's hair grey and on which the feet of creation bear witness in dread {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}. He says he is in the world "a stranger, a traveller", and every stranger returns without fail, so he bids his soul turn from the house of passing, since he desires the house of staying {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}. He then asks when his friend will see him "standing in aid of the God of truth, in the hand a cutting" blade, and the line is cut at the foot of the page and runs on into the first line of p. 316 {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}. The footnotes say that "al-ghalt and al-ghalat are the same" and that manuscript S reads another word in an earlier line {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.315}. The lines carry the voice of one who reflects on his last end and then wishes for work in God's cause; this is put in the poet's own words, not in the museum's judgment, and no battle or person is named. Its completion falls to the next batch from the top of p. 316. It follows [[event/al-hadi-reproaches-a-friend-who-withheld-twenty-men-in-verse-292|the lines of reproach over twenty men withheld]]. The poem is given in a Zaydi book in the voice of the imam's followers; its claims, descriptions and judgments on opponents are the poet's or the narrator's, and the museum adopts none of them, repeats no word of abuse and gives no verdict.
