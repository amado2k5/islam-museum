## Summary
According to the sound view, the Prophet [[person/muhammad|the Prophet]] was taken by night in his body from the Sacred Mosque to [[place/jerusalem|Bayt al-Maqdis]], riding al-Buraq with Gabriel. Then he was raised from Bayt al-Maqdis through the heavens, where he met the prophets in each heaven, and prayer was enjoined on him {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.138} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.139} {cite:zad-al-maad 2/47-48}. His people were told of it in the morning, and their denial grew fierce {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.140}.

## Its date
Views differ on when the Isra' took place. Some say it was in the year he was sent as a prophet; some say five years after the mission began; some say it was the night of 27 Rajab in the tenth year of the mission; some say sixteen months before the Hijra, or a year and two months before it, or a year before it {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.137}. The book prefers that the Isra' came after the death of Khadija, because the five daily prayers were enjoined in it, and because the context of Surat al-Isra' shows that it came very late {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.137}.

## In the heavens
He met Adam in the first heaven; Yahya and Isa in the second; Yusuf in the third; Idris in the fourth; Harun in the fifth; and Musa in the sixth. Musa wept, because a youth sent after him would have more of his community entering Paradise than entered from Musa's own community. He met Ibrahim in the seventh {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.138}. Then he was raised to the Lote-Tree of the Utmost Boundary, then to the Majestic One, who revealed what He revealed and enjoined fifty prayers on him. He kept returning to Musa and asking his Lord until the prayers were reduced to five. He was called and told that he had carried out his obligation and that He had lightened it for His servants {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.138} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.139}.

## The vision
The book reports what Ibn al-Qayyim relates of the disagreement over whether the Prophet saw his Lord. It notes that seeing with the eye was not established among the Companions, that the drawing near and descent in the Isra' narration differ from what is in Surat al-Najm, and that what is in Surat al-Najm is the drawing near of Gabriel {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.139}.

## What he saw
A vessel of milk and a vessel of wine were offered to him. He chose the milk, and it was said to him: "You have been guided to the natural disposition (fitra)." He saw four rivers in Paradise; Malik, the keeper of the Fire; those who eat the property of orphans; those who eat usury; and adulterers. He also saw a caravan of Quraysh and learned of its affairs, and he told the people of Mecca what he had seen {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.139} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.140}. When they asked him about Bayt al-Maqdis, he described it for them. Then he told them about their caravan and the time of its arrival, and it happened as he had said, which only increased their aversion {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.140}.

## The reason for the journey
The book's briefest account of the reason for the journey is the statement in Surat al-Isra' that God showed him some of His signs. The book holds that this is God's way with the prophets, so that they become among the people of certainty and bear for God's sake what others cannot bear {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.141}. The author sees these verses as linking the Isra' to Bayt al-Maqdis and to the transfer of spiritual leadership from the Children of Israel to the community of Muhammad. He holds that the Isra' took place shortly before the Hijra, or between the two 'Aqabas {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.141} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.142}.

## Al-Sha'rawi's reading
Shaykh Muhammad Mutawalli al-Sha'rawi, in his book "al-Isra' wal-Mi'raj", published in the scientific series of the Academy of Islamic Research, offers a contemplative reading that begins from a verse of Surat al-Isra' {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 21}. He distinguishes the Isra', the earthly journey, from the Mi'raj, the heavenly journey. He holds that the Isra' is established by people's testimony about its place, while the Mi'raj is understood by inference from what came before it {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 19} {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 44}. He reports from the scholars that denying the Isra' is unbelief, because it contradicts an explicit text, and that denying the Mi'raj is wrongdoing (fisq), because it is established by necessary implication {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 47}.

He argues that the Isra' was in body and spirit, from the word "servant" ('abd), which applies to what unites body and spirit {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 23} {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 37}. He holds that it was while awake, because the Quraysh objected to it, and if it had been a dream there would have been no dispute over it {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 31}. He allows that the Prophet may first have seen the journey in a dream and then seen it awake, drawing support from what is reported of 'A'isha on the truthfulness of dreams {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 34} {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 35}.

He says the Isra' took place in a single night one year before the Hijra {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 19}. This view does not match the one al-Mubarakfuri preferred above, so it is recorded here as a point of disagreement. He reports that the Quraysh asked for a description of Bayt al-Maqdis, that the Prophet described it, then told them about their caravan and its time of arrival, and that it happened as he said; he counts this as a material proof of the journey {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 41} {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 42}. He reports that Abu Bakr said that if the Prophet had said it, then he had spoken the truth, and so Abu Bakr was named al-Siddiq {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 41}.

On prayer, he says that it was enjoined directly by God during the Mi'raj, and that it is the only act of worship the Prophet received from God without the mediation of Jibril {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 89}. The obligation was fifty and was lightened to five; the reward stayed the same, and the reduction was in number, not in reward {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 101} {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 102}. He holds that Musa's returning to ask for reductions was not a form of guardianship, and that Musa knew from his own people's experience what a community could not bear, so he meant his own community and not the community of Muhammad {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 99} {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 100}.

He reads what the Prophet saw of scenes as teaching by image about rulings. Choosing milk over wine indicates the natural disposition {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 73} {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 74}. Those who sow and reap together indicate that what is spent in God's way is replaced by God {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 76} {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 77}. The image of this world as an old woman adorned warns of how short its span is {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 78}. The one who eats usury swims in blood and is made to swallow stones {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 84}. The backbiter resembles one who eats the dead flesh of his brother {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 86}. Whoever is slack in prayer has his head struck with stones, and it returns to what it was {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 87} {cite:al-isra-wal-miraj-azhar p. 88}.

## The Qur'an and the three classical readings of the verse
This section and the next follow the Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, article Mi'radj. It notes that two Qur'anic passages, [[passage/quran-53-1|Al-Najm 1]] with its following verses and a passage in sura 81, describe a vision in which a divine messenger appears to the Prophet from a distance, and that nothing in them suggests he himself was carried anywhere. The experience alluded to in the first verse of Surat al-Isra' is different: it speaks of God taking His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the further place of worship {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 121}. For this verse the article says tradition gives three interpretations.

The first, which it calls the oldest and says disappears from the more recent commentaries, finds an allusion to the Ascension to heaven and takes the further place of worship to mean heaven; in the early reports the words for the night journey and for the ascent are often used as synonyms {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 121}. The second, the only one given in all the more modern commentaries, takes the further place of worship to be [[place/al-aqsa-mosque|al-Aqsa Mosque]] in Jerusalem. In this telling the angel [[person/jibril|Gabriel]] brings the Prophet a winged animal, [[concept/buraq|al-Buraq]], and they travel to Jerusalem, meeting good and wicked powers on the way and visiting Hebron and Bethlehem. In Jerusalem the Prophet meets Ibrahim, Musa and Isa, and leads the prayer, taking precedence over the other prophets present {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 121} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 122}.

The third reading rests on a later verse, [[passage/quran-17-62|Al-Isra 62]], where the word for "vision" is explained as the night journey. On this reading the journey was a vision: the Prophet saw Jerusalem from the hijr and described it to the Quraysh, who did not believe him. The article summarises the story as one in which the Prophet, unable to recall the particulars, is shown Jerusalem by God {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 122}.

## Body or spirit, asleep or awake: the Muslim discussion
The article says Muslim theologians discussed whether the night journey took place while the Prophet slept or woke, and whether his spirit or his body travelled. It reports the orthodox, Sunni scholarly opinion as body and awake, and says al-Tabari supports this decidedly in his commentary with three reasons: that otherwise the event would be no proof of his mission, that the Qur'an says God caused His servant to journey and not his spirit, and that the mount would not then have been needed {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 122}. It adds that mystics and philosophers often prefer an allegorical interpretation {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 122}. It also records that an objection was raised early to the claim that the Prophet saw God face to face, and that the question whether the ascent was a dream or a reality was raised at an early date {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 123}.

The second part of the article, on Arabic literature, lists the positions this way: a vision of the spirit during sleep; a real night journey while awake with an ascent only in spirit; and a real ascent, body and soul, by a Prophet fully conscious. It says the third reading grounds the miracle as a theological argument and gives free range to imaginative portrayal, while the first treats the event as an illumination in which the physical person is not involved {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 125}.

## Variant reports in the hadith
The article notes that the hadith is not uniform. Most reports join the ascent to the night journey, so that the Prophet rises from Jerusalem. Others begin the ascent in Mecca and do not mention Jerusalem; in one report the angels Gabriel and Michael open the Prophet's body, wash it with water of Zamzam and fill it with wisdom and belief before he is taken to the lowest heaven {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 122}. In another, [[person/muhammad-ibn-sad|Ibn Sa'd]] places the opening of the chest in childhood, not at the ascent {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 122}.

At the gate of each of the seven heavens Gabriel is asked his name and his companion's, and whether the companion has already been sent as a prophet. In each heaven they meet a messenger, usually Adam, Yahya and Isa, Yusuf, Idris, Harun, Musa and Ibrahim in order, though the article says there are variations; only Musa is reported to say that the Prophet stands higher with God than himself {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 123}. It states that the reports differ on where Paradise and Hell lie and on its rivers, that the Lote-Tree of the Boundary is usually set in the seventh heaven, and that the oldest accounts already place the Prophet before the divine throne in the seventh heaven, where the talk about the prayers occurs {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 123}. Some versions add a ladder, mi'raj, by which the ascent is made {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 123}. Popular narratives add that the Prophet held 70,000 conversations with God while his bed stayed warm and the cup he overturned was not yet empty {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 122}.

## Academic reading: origins and development
The following is the academic reading of the article's authors, B. Schrieke and J. Horovitz, drawn from their stated hypotheses, not a Muslim or Sunni position. They hold that the oldest tradition puts the ascent at Mecca in the period just after the Prophet's call, as a kind of consecration preceded by the purification of the heart, and they find parallels for such purification in other traditions, including pagan Arabia and Christian legend {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 122} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 123}. They note that [[person/ibn-ishaq|Ibn Ishaq]], in the oldest extant biography, made the Ascension fall at a later period {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 122}. They see the identification of the further place of worship with Jerusalem as possibly an Umayyad device meant to glorify Jerusalem against Mecca, then ruled by [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr]], and some connect it with the efforts of [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik]] to raise Jerusalem in believers' esteem; they say it cannot be shown to be older than his time {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 121} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 123}. They suggest that the Christian veneration of Jesus's footprint in Jerusalem and the idea of a heavenly Jerusalem may have helped the story take hold {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 123}. They also say that [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]] seems to reject the Jerusalem reading in his History {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 122}.

For the ladder they point to Jacob's ladder in Genesis, the Ethiopic Book of Jubilees, Mandaean and Manichaean imagery and Mithraic parallels. For the meeting with the prophets they propose the transfiguration of Jesus as a possible model, and for the bargaining over the prayers a passage of Genesis. They also say Muslim writers used models from Jewish and Christian apocalypses and perhaps Zoroastrian texts {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 122} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 123}. The article's wording is tentative throughout, using "may" and "perhaps". The second part of the article adds that the study of the portrayals of the journey remains to be done {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 124}.

## Sufi and philosophical readings
The article says that for the Sufis the ascension is a symbol of the soul's rise from the bonds of sensuality to the heights of mystic knowledge. [[person/ibn-arabi-muhyi-al-din|Ibn al-Arabi]] expounds it so in his Kitab al-Isra', and in his Futuhat he has a believer and a philosopher make the journey together, the philosopher reaching only the seventh heaven while no secret stays hidden from the pious Muslim {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 124}. It adds that the ascension later served as a model for the journey of the soul of the deceased to the divine judge {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 124}. The second part says the Mi'raj accounts cannot be wholly separated from the visionary texts of Ibn al-Arabi, [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]] and al-Suhrawardi {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 125}.

The article describes the [[work/risalat-al-ghufran|Risalat al-Ghufran]] of [[person/abu-l-ala-al-maarri|Abu l-Ala al-Maarri]] as a parody of the traditional accounts; that is the article's characterisation. It also reports that Christian medieval Europe knew the Muslim ascension legends, and that a Latin Book of Mahomet's Ladder, translated through Castilian from Arabic, became famous and may have inspired Dante {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 124} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 126}.

## The literature of the Mi'raj
The second part of the article treats the Mi'raj literature. It says the most widely circulated account is attributed to [[person/abdullah-ibn-abbas|Ibn Abbas]] and has often been regarded as apocryphal, yet remains a decisive text. Other versions are by scholars who took over the literature, among them [[person/al-qushayri-abu-l-qasim|al-Qushayri]], who died in 465/1073, and [[person/al-ghayti-najm-al-din|al-Ghayti]], who died in 984/1576 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 124}. It says these accounts amalgamate the purification by the angels, the night journey from Mecca to Jerusalem on al-Buraq, and the ascent proper through the seven heavens, with a glimpse of Hell, the arrival at the Throne, the dialogue with God, a visit to [[concept/jannah|Paradise]] and the return {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 124}. Similar material appears in the stories of the prophets, in general histories and in resurrection literature {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 125}.

The article records that popular preachers spread the story, and that [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ibn Hanbal]], [[person/ibn-al-jawzi|Ibn al-Jawzi]], [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]] and [[person/al-suyuti|al-Suyuti]] pursued the inventors of fabulous tales {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 125}. It observes that the dialogue between God and the Prophet in these accounts is built from Qur'anic verses and not from invented speech, and that the heavens are described as successively of smoke, copper, silver, corundum and pearl {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 126}. As a modern example it mentions that in Rajab 1387/October 1967 Egyptian State Television broadcast a film on the Isra' and Mi'raj under al-Azhar's responsibility {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 125}.

## Commemoration
The article gives Ibn Sa'd's dating of the night journey to 17 Rabi' I and of the ascension to 17 Ramadan. It adds that for centuries the pious have regarded the night before 27 Rajab as the Laylat al-Mi'raj, devoting it to reading the legend, as is done at the Prophet's birthday {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 124}.

## Notes for researchers
- The date is disputed. The book does not choose among the later views, and the event card carries the status "disputed".
- The interpretations of the purpose of the journey and of the transfer of leadership are the author's own reasoning. The museum relays them as interpretation.
- Al-Sha'rawi's book is a set of reflections he gave in question-and-answer form, not a study of the history of the event. His readings of the scenes and of the reason for the prayer are his own reasoning, and his words are relayed with that description.
- The book does not present an academic view that contradicts the reading it offers, so nothing is attributed to it that it did not say.

## For young visitors
One night the Prophet rode al-Buraq with Gabriel to Bayt al-Maqdis, then rose into the heavens, where he saw the prophets. On this journey the five daily prayers were enjoined on him, and they became part of the practice of the Muslims.

## Sha'rawi on Abu Bakr's acceptance
Sha'rawi reports that when Abu Bakr was told that his companion claimed to have been taken by night, he said that if the Prophet had said it, then it was true. He takes it as an example of knowledge of certainty, since the report came from one he trusted. {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan-pp-501-1000 p.556}

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 1)
The Sheikh mentions that the Prophet met the prophets, ascended to the seventh heaven and returned, and says that on his return it was as though time had stopped, a matter in which reason has no room {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep01 00:28:30}.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 7)
The Sheikh speaks of a prayer in which the supplicant asks his Lord for the power, strength and sight to see Him, and then says that God, in His generosity, honoured His prophet at the Miraj by showing Himself to him, after the night journey from the Sacred Mosque to al-Aqsa Mosque [[passage/quran-17-1|Al-Isra 1]] {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep07 00:15:00}. He says that time was folded in the journey, outside the laws of humankind, so that the Prophet went from Mecca to Jerusalem and then to the seventh heaven and back, while on the Buraq he brushed the branch of a tree {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep07 00:15:30}. He relates that the Prophet prayed at al-Aqsa Mosque with the prophets, ascended through the seven heavens, was charged with the prayer, and then returned {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep07 00:16:00}. He mentions that on the way back the Prophet saw a caravan and a camel carrying a black sack, and that the camel shied from the Buraq, since animals see what we do not, and its leg broke (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep07 00:16:00}. He says the branch was still swaying when he returned, and reflects on how deeply the Companions were attached to the Messenger {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep07 00:16:30}.

## In Ahmed Abdussalam's programme 'Osalli Alayh'
In the thirteenth episode of his programme [[work/ossali-alayh|'Osalli Alayh']], [[person/ahmed-abdussalam|Ahmed Abdussalam]] relates that the Prophet surprised people one morning by telling them he had been taken by night from the Sacred Mosque in Mecca to al-Aqsa in Syria, in Palestine, in a small part of the night; many were shaken, and some went to Abu Bakr, who believed him before he knew of the Ascension. {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep13 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep13 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep13 00:02:30} {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep13 00:03:00} He makes it an example of believing everything the Prophet reported. {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep13 00:02:00}
