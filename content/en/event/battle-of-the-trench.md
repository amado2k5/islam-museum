## Overview
The Battle of the Trench, also called the expedition of the Khandak or the War of the Trench, was the siege of [[place/medina|Medina]] by a Meccan-led confederacy in the fifth year of the Hijra, in which a trench dug across the open parts of the oasis stopped the attackers' cavalry and the confederacy then broke up. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1059} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 422} This page gives first the account of the Muslim sira tradition, following al-Mubarakpuri, and then, in sections that say so, the account of the Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, which reports the date, numbers and sequence somewhat differently. Where the Encyclopaedia passes a judgement, the page attributes it to the article and adopts none.

## The account in the sira tradition
In Shawwal of the fifth year of the Hijra, twenty men of the chiefs of the Jews of Banu al-Nadir and Khaybar — foremost Huyayy ibn Akhtab and Sallam ibn Abi al-Huqayq — went out to Quraysh at Mecca and to Ghatafan at Najd, inciting them to raid Medina and root out Islam; and a vast army of ten thousand fighters assembled under [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-harb|Abu Sufyan ibn Harb]]. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}

When the news reached the Messenger of God he consulted his Companions, and [[person/salman-al-farisi|Salman al-Farisi]] advised him to dig a trench barring the polytheists from Medina on its exposed northern side; three thousand Muslims went out, the Prophet leading them, digging with their own hands in bitter cold and severe hunger, and the digging witnessed prophetic marvels in the splitting of rocks and the multiplying of food. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}

When the Confederates arrived they found the trench a barrier they had not known in their wars, and laid siege to Medina for a month; at the height of the distress Huyayy ibn Akhtab tempted [[person/kaab-ibn-asad|Ka'b ibn Asad]], chief of Banu Qurayza, into breaking his pact with the Muslims, so the Muslims were threatened from behind by a war within — a fearful scene the Qur'an describes in [[passage/quran-33-10|Sura al-Ahzab, verse 10]]. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}

Then God prepared the means of victory through the secret conversion of [[person/nuaym-ibn-masud|Nu'aym ibn Mas'ud al-Ashja'i]]; the Prophet ordered him to sow doubt, and he spread suspicion between Quraysh and Ghatafan and between them and Qurayza. Then God sent on them a bitter, violent wind on a cold dark night that uprooted their tents, overturned their pots and cast terror into their hearts, and they fled in retreat; [[person/hudhayfa-ibn-al-yaman|Hudhayfa ibn al-Yaman]] slipped among them and returned to the Messenger of God with the news of their departure. {cite:sahih-muslim}

## Ali at the trench
[[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]]'s stand in the Confederates' war was heroic. When the cavalry of the polytheists forced a breach in the trench, he went out with a group of Muslims until they had the breach back in their hands; then he came out against [[event/duel-of-amr-ibn-abd-wudd|Amr ibn Abd Wudd]] when Amr called for single combat {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.98}.

The book says that Ali called this horseman to Islam and then to the fight; when he refused, dismounted and hamstrung his horse, the two engaged and grappled until Ali killed him, and his horsemen fled until they plunged into the trench in flight {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.98}. The book says that after Amr's death the polytheists offered ten thousand for his body, but the Prophet said: «Give them his body, for it is foul carrion and its blood-money is foul», and he accepted nothing from them {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p.99}.

## The Encyclopaedia of Islam: the word and the expedition
This section and the four that follow give the academic account of the Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, labelled as such. The article on the word khandak says it means a ditch, trench or moat, that it seems to have come into Arabic from Persian through Syriac, and that it is also known as a place-name. Its best-known application, the article says, is to the so-called expedition of the Khandak, in which [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] foiled a Meccan attempt to storm Medina by digging a moat at those parts of the oasis that were open to attack by cavalry. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1059}

## Date and forces in the academic account
The article dates the expedition to Dhu al-Qa'da of the year 5, April 627, which differs from the month of Shawwal and the siege of nearly a month given in the sira account above; the two are reported side by side here and not reconciled. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1059} It says the Meccans had been preparing since the battle of [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]], two years earlier, persuaded several nomadic tribes to send contingents, and had altogether about 10,000 men with 600 horses. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1059} The article on Muhammad's life gives the same order of size, an army said to have been about 10,000, raised by [[group/quraysh|Quraysh]], whom it describes as urged on by the Jews of Khaybar and, as city merchants, conscious of their slight military skill, so that they negotiated with various Bedouin tribes. It places the march only in the year 5, 626-7. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 422}

The same article notes that the sources give differing seasons, one a month after the barley harvest and another cold winter storms, the latter in agreement with the sura of the Confederates, and says they could be reconciled only by the unlikely assumption that the siege lasted a considerable time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 422}

## Digging the trench
The article says Muhammad had already harvested the grain in the northern part of the oasis, so that when the cavalry arrived they were soon short of fodder. As soon as he heard that the army was about to leave Mecca he summoned the Muslims to help dig the khandak and took part in the work himself; most, it says, worked willingly, and the digging is said to have been finished in six days. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1059} The life of Muhammad adds that the advance of so large an army caused great consternation in Medina, increased by the wavering of the Munafiqun and by the discovery, or perhaps only the suspicion, that the Jews were conspiring with the enemy. In its words, Muhammad had a trench dug in front of the unprotected parts of the settlement, a report it traces to Ibn Hisham. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 422}

On the question of Salman, the article observes that Ibn Ishaq, in the recension of Ibn Hisham, nowhere credits [[person/salman-al-farisi|Salman al-Farisi]] with the idea, while other accounts, for example Ibn Sa'd, say the trench was a Persian military device suggested by him. This sets the academic note beside the account above, which, following al-Mubarakpuri, gives Salman as the adviser. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 422}

## The siege and its end in the academic account
According to the article, the Meccan cavalry, though far superior to the Muslim infantry on level ground, were repelled when they tried to cross the khandak. With their main offensive tactic nullified, the Meccans and their allies had to besiege Medina, an operation in which the article says they were wholly unskilled. After a fortnight of little military activity and much intrigue and counter-intrigue, the confederacy broke up and the siege was abandoned. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1059} The life of Muhammad adds that the defences, however modest, were enough to deter an enemy with little experience of such tactics, that Muhammad used the time for secret negotiations with [[group/ghatafan|Ghatafan]] and stirred up distrust among his opponents, and that the besiegers lost heart as the weather turned against them and gradually withdrew. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 422} The article does not name Nu'aym ibn Mas'ud, who appears in the sira account above. It judges that the failure strengthened Muhammad's position greatly and led to dejection in Mecca. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1059}

The article on the [[group/banu-murra|Banu Murra]], a Ghatafani tribe, records that they took part in the siege organised by Quraysh and the Jews in the year 5, called al-Khandak, and were raided by Muslim forces several times afterwards. It adds that their leader al-Harith ibn Awf, fearing that the Prophet would in the end prevail, did not give the enemies of Muhammad his full support. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 683}

## After the siege: Banu Qurayza
The life of Muhammad says that hardly had the Meccans and their allies withdrawn when the Prophet declared war on the last Jewish tribe of any size in Medina, [[group/banu-qurayza|Banu Qurayza]], and besieged their forts; it calls the otherwise largely uneventful War of the Trench a bloody tragedy for this group. It says they probably hoped for terms like those given to Banu Qaynuqa and [[group/banu-nadir|Banu al-Nadir]], and were not allowed such leniency. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 422} On one account of Ibn Hisham that it cites, all the men, whose number the accounts give as between 600 and 900, were killed in accordance with a judgment given by [[person/sad-ibn-muadh|Sa'd ibn Mu'adh]] of the Aws, and the property, women and children were divided among the Muslims. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 422} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 423} The article adds that tradition tried to place the responsibility on Sa'd, but that there are various indications, in its judgement, that the decision was the Prophet's own. This is the article's reading; the Muslim sira account, which names Sa'd's judgement, is given in the sections of this page that follow al-Mubarakpuri and al-Sallabi, and the record states no verdict. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 423} The article sees the removal of these three Jewish tribes as bringing Muhammad closer to organising an umma on a strictly religious basis, while noting that some Jews from other families were allowed to stay. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 423}

## Place in the course of Muhammad's life
The article calls the failure of the Meccan siege, together with the removal of this last major Jewish tribe, one of the main turning points in the life of Muhammad and the rise of the Muslim community. It notes that from about six months after his arrival in Medina until the Meccan failure he sent a steady stream of raiding parties against Meccan caravans, and that this ceased after al-Khandak and the removal of Qurayza, about a year before the ten-year truce at [[event/treaty-of-hudaybiyya|al-Hudaybiya]], after which the Prophet turned his chief attention to the north. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 423}

## Where the accounts differ
In summary, and without choosing between them: the sira account above puts the siege in Shawwal and describes it as lasting about a month, the Encyclopaedia dates it to Dhu al-Qa'da and speaks of a fortnight of little fighting. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1059} The sira account gives Salman as the adviser of the trench, the article says Ibn Hisham's recension of Ibn Ishaq does not. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 422} The sira account ends the siege through Nu'aym ibn Mas'ud's stratagem and a night wind, the article speaks of secret negotiations, sown distrust and unfavourable weather. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 422} And on Banu Qurayza the sira account names Sa'd's judgement while the article suggests the Prophet's own responsibility. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 423}

## For Sprouts
The Muslims dug a trench around Medina to protect it. God protected the people of the city, and a strong wind sent the armies away.

## For Explorers
Large armies came to attack Medina. Salman al-Farisi suggested digging a trench to guard the open side of the city. The Muslims were patient and worked together until the armies left, by God's leave, and a strong wind was a sign of God's help.

## For Scholars
In Shawwal of the fifth year after the Hijra, a coalition of Meccans and allied tribes marched on Medina. On Salman al-Farisi's advice the Muslims dug a trench on the northern side, and they stayed patient for nearly a month. The sources tell of the Banu Qurayza breaking their pact, of Nu'aym ibn Mas'ud's stratagem that divided the armies, and of a strong wind at night. The reports differ on the size of the armies, so each number needs a source. This entry gives the events of the trench as the sira books tell them, and shows how counsel and patience protected the city.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 5)
The Sheikh relates that the night at the Trench was so bitterly cold that two of the Companions wrapped themselves in a single garment, and that the Prophet said: who will cross the trench and bring me news of the enemy, without causing any incident? No one answered him (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep05 00:52:30}. He says the Prophet repeated the request, that the night was at the end of the month and the place was dark, and that the Companion crossed and Abu Sufyan passed in front of him at a very short distance (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep05 00:53:00}. He mentions that the Companion thought that Abu Sufyan was the head of the group and that killing him might end the matter, but remembered the Prophet's words not to start anything, so he held back his sword and stopped at the limits set by God (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep05 00:53:30}.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lecture series Al Dinu Al Qayem

The speaker names the Battle of the Confederates, also called the Trench, and mentions Ghatafan in its context. He holds (speaker's view, opinion) that the present day resembles it {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep10 00:14:30}.

The speaker's view (opinion): Muslims today do not learn the language, the thinking or the view of others toward them. He says that at the Battle of the Confederates God sent a wind and hosts that they did not see, and the matter ended {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep10 00:15:00}.

He says the Qur'an describes that battle in an extraordinary way: the believers were shaken, hearts reached the throats, until God's help came {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep10 00:15:30}.

He recounts Hudhayfa ibn al-Yaman's account of a freezing night, when the Prophet asked who would go to the enemy and bring back news without starting anything. Hudhayfa rose into the dark {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep10 00:16:00}. Hudhayfa says Abu Sufyan was close to him, within a spear's thrust, and he held back because he remembered the Prophet's charge not to start anything against them {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep10 00:17:00}.

The speaker's view (opinion): one man's deed is better than a thousand men's speeches, and the Battle of the Confederates is a model of the Muslims' victory through "there is no god but God" {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep10 01:00:30}.

## In Abu Ishaq al-Heweny's «Difaf al-Kawthar»
[[person/abu-ishaq-al-heweny|Abu Ishaq al-Heweny]] narrates the Trench in explaining Anas's hadith of the rajaz in which the Prophet prayed for the Ansar and the Muhajirun and said the true life is the life of the Hereafter; he says the Prophet said it while digging the Trench and while building his mosque. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:02:30} He says the battle was in Shawwal of year 5 by the strongest view of the scholars, that Musa ibn Uqba, author of the Maghazi, held it was year 4 and al-Bukhari followed him, and that most maghazi scholars say year 5. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:03:00} He explains that Uhud was in Shawwal of year 3, that Abu Sufyan set the Muslims an appointment for the following year, that in year 4 Quraysh suffered drought and did not raid, and the matter was put off to year 5. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:04:00}

He says the polytheists, the Jews and Ghatafan gathered to fight the Prophet, with ten thousand fighters, fifteen hundred camels and three hundred horses, and that the Muslims were three thousand, or by some reports fewer; {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:04:30} the figure of ten thousand is also the one in «al-Raheeq al-Makhtum», as given above on this page. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq} He says [[person/salman-al-farisi|Salman al-Farisi]] advised digging the trench around Medina, that the ground had rock broken with picks, and that the Companions faced two problems: cold and hunger. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:05:30}

He cites from Sahih al-Bukhari the hadith of [[person/al-bara-ibn-azib|al-Bara' ibn Azib]] that the Prophet carried earth until the dust hid the skin of his belly, chanting verses of Abd Allah ibn Rawaha; {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:06:30} the hadith of [[person/anas-ibn-malik|Anas]] that the Muhajirun and Ansar dug in bitter cold and were brought barley with rancid fat (ihala sanikha), ihala being fat and sanikha meaning gone off from long keeping; {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:07:30} and the hadith of [[person/jabir-ibn-abdullah|Jabir]] that they spent three days digging without tasting food. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:08:00} In all this he sees a lesson in the responsibility of the leader: the Prophet would not eat while his Companions dug hungry. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep07 00:12:00}

## Al-Sallabi's analysis: the lessons of the Confederates
At the end of his chapter on the Confederates, [[person/ali-muhammad-al-sallabi|Ali Muhammad al-Sallabi]] gathers "lessons, morals and benefits" as a series of paragraphs: the Prophet's sensory miracles during the digging of the Trench; the gap between expectation and reality; the hadith "Salman is one of us, the people of the house"; the "middle prayer"; the lawful and the unlawful; the courage of Safiyya, the Prophet's aunt; "the unsoundness of what is reported about Hassan's cowardice"; "the first Islamic field hospital"; that a Muslim may sin but hastens to repent; the merits of Sa'd ibn Mu'adh; the killing of Huyayy ibn Akhtab and Ka'b ibn Asad; and Thabit ibn Qays's intercession for al-Zubayr ibn Bata. {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.611} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.613} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.614} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.615} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.616} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.617} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.620} {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.622} These headings and their content are his own assessment of the reports, and in some he rules on their authenticity, as with the report about Hassan. {cite:sira-nabawiyya-sallabi p.615}
