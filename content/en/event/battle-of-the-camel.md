## Overview
The battle of the Camel took place in 36 AH near Basra, between [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]] and a group that included Talha, al-Zubayr and [[person/aisha|Aisha]]. al-Tabari placed its account in 36 AH. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.4 p.583}

## al-Tabari's account
al-Tabari says that Ali went out toward Basra after Talha and al-Zubayr had asked his leave. He halted at Dhi Qar and sent al-Hasan and Ammar ibn Yasir to Kufa to rouse the people. Then the battle took place, in which al-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam was killed. Then Ali came to Aisha, and the people of Basra gave him their allegiance. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.4 p.583}

## al-Suyuti's account
al-Suyuti says that Talha and al-Zubayr went out to Mecca, and took Aisha to Basra seeking vengeance for the blood of Uthman, and that Ali met them. The battle was in Jumada al-Akhira 36 AH, and Talha and al-Zubayr were killed, and the dead reached thirteen thousand. Ali stayed in Basra for fifteen nights, then went back to Kufa. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.294}

## The numbers according to al-Mas'udi
al-Mas'udi says that the dead of the Camel were thirteen thousand, and five thousand of Ali's companions were killed. He notes that people disputed the number, some saying seven thousand and some ten thousand, according to the leaning of each side. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.645}

## What al-Sallabi adds: from Mecca to Basra
Talha, al-Zubayr and Aisha reached Mecca about four months after the killing of Uthman, in Rabi al-Akhir 36 AH. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 33} The Umayyads had taken refuge in Mecca, where Abd Allah ibn Amir, governor of Basra under Uthman, was offering money, and Yala ibn Umayya came from Yemen with money and weapons. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 34} They chose Basra because they judged it the weakest of the cities, and their declared aim was to seek vengeance for the blood of Uthman and to reconcile the people. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 34}

## In Basra before the fighting
The party camped at al-Kharayba outside Basra and sent to the notables asking for their support. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 41} Al-Zubayr sent to [[person/al-ahnaf-ibn-qays|al-Ahnaf ibn Qays]], who joined them with those who were with him, and al-Sallabi reports from al-Zuhri that most of the people of Basra followed them. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 41} Hakim ibn Jabala and his companions were killed in a fight with Aisha's companions. The account of Abu Mikhnaf says that [[person/uthman-ibn-hunayf|Uthman ibn Hunayf]] was beaten and his hair was plucked, but al-Sallabi rejects this report because he regards Abu Mikhnaf as open to suspicion. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 42} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 44}

## The final negotiations
Ali left Dhi Qar after the Kufans were slow to come. Then [[person/al-qaqa-ibn-amr|al-Qaqa ibn Amr]] went to Aisha, Talha and al-Zubayr and argued with them that the killers of Uthman in Basra were known, and that leaving them alone, or taking retribution from them all at once, would open a greater strife. They accepted delay, on condition that retribution be taken once the community was reunited. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 51} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 52}

## The night of the battle
Al-Sallabi, citing al-Tabari, says that the Saba'iyya plotted during the night to attack both sides, so the people rose up and each side thought the other had betrayed it. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 53} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 54} He notes that most of the scholars he cites, such as al-Tahawi, al-Baqillani and Ibn Hazm, held that the night was an attack by the rabble on both sides. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 54} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 55}

## The day of the battle
The fighting began with arrows, and then the battle grew fierce. Talha was first hit by an arrow whose shooter was not known; he was carried to Basra and died there. Al-Sallabi notes that Talha had come out for reconciliation, not to shed blood, and that he was hit at the start of the fighting, as al-Ahnaf said. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 58} Al-Zubayr left the field after Ali reminded him of the Prophet's saying that he would fight Ali while unjust. [[person/amr-ibn-jurmuz|Amr ibn Jurmuz]] caught up with him while he was resting at midday in Wadi al-Siba and killed him. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 57} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 86} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 87} In the second round [[person/kab-ibn-suwar|Kab ibn Suwar]], the judge of Basra, was killed while calling to the Book of God, and the Saba'iyya shot at the camel and hit the litter. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 58} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 59}

Ali told Abd Allah ibn Badil to take the litter from the camel and bring it to a safe place, and the fighting around the camel stopped. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 60} Then he gave orders that no one who turned to flee be pursued, that no wounded man be finished off, and that nothing be taken as booty except the weapons and mounts in the camp, and that anyone who found his property with a man of the army could take it back. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 60} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 63}

## Number of the dead
Al-Sallabi notes that Ibn Abi Khaythama, Khalifa ibn Khayyat and others give widely differing figures, among them twenty thousand according to Qatada, ten or fifteen thousand according to Sayf, and more than thirty thousand according to al-Yaqubi. He considers these figures exaggerated, and holds that the nearest estimate is that the dead were relatively few, because the fighting was brief and it was a defensive battle. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 60} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 61} He then inclines to think that the names preserved by Khalifa come to about one hundred, and that the upper limit is about two hundred. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 62}

## Date of the battle
Al-Sallabi, on the chain of Qatada in Khalifa ibn Khayyat, favours the view that the battle was in Jumada al-Akhira 36 AH, and that the soundest reports give a Thursday, around its middle, or a Friday. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 65}

## After the battle
The prisoners of Basra gave their allegiance to Ali tribe by tribe, and he gave Musa ibn Talha, from the income of land he owned, enough to reassure him. Al-Sallabi notes that Marwan ibn al-Hakam also pledged allegiance, after Ali had treated him well. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 64} Then Ali sent Aisha back to Medina with transport and provisions, chose forty women of Basra to accompany her, and accompanied her for several miles to see her off. The journey took place at the beginning of Rajab 36 AH. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 75}

## Assessment by the author
Al-Sallabi denies that Aisha intended to fight, and says that the claim attributed to her, that she wanted battle, is baseless, because she went out to reconcile, as the sound reports he relies on show. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 75} Al-Sallabi holds that Talha and al-Zubayr were closer to the right than Mu'awiya in four respects, among them that they had pledged allegiance to Ali willingly, and that they wanted to seek vengeance for Uthman and did not deliberately intend to fight Ali at the camel. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 20} Al-Sallabi also records the regret of Ali, Aisha, Talha and al-Zubayr after the battle: Ali said he wished he had died twenty years before it, and Aisha, whenever the camel was mentioned, wept until her headscarf was soaked. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 77}

## Points the author puts forward for critique
Al-Sallabi presents several reports about the story of Marwan ibn al-Hakam and the killing of Talha, quoting them from Ibn Kathir, and holds that the report is not established: if Marwan had killed Talha, and al-Bukhari had narrated from him, that would itself be among the things that prevent acceptance of his narration. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 62} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 63}

## The view with support
The view adopted by al-Sallabi and by Ahl al-Sunna is that Ali was closer to the truth, that both sides were exercising independent judgement, and that the Companions did not intend to fight one another. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 65} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 55}

## In Khalifa ibn Khayyat
[[person/khalifa-ibn-khayyat|Khalifa ibn Khayyat]] places the battle at al-Zawiya near the edge of Basra on Friday the tenth of Jumada al-Akhira, 36. {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 181} With Ali were eight hundred of the Ansar and four hundred who had given the Pledge of Ridwan. {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 184} The dead are given as twenty thousand by Qatada and seven thousand by Ali ibn Zayd. {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 186} He then lists the names preserved of those killed. {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 187}

## The Encyclopaedia of Islam account, and how it is labelled

The article on al-Djamal in the Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, is an academic account written by L. Veccia Vaglieri. It dates the battle to Jumada II 36 AH, about November to December 656, near Basra, and sets Ali against Aisha together with the Companions Talha and al-Zubayr. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 439} The Sunni narrations and the Sunni scholarly position are set out in the sections above, which cite al-Tabari, al-Suyuti, al-Mas'udi and Khalifa ibn Khayyat, and in the perspectives of this record from al-Sallabi. The sections below report the academic account, and where the article gives a judgement the text says it is the author's. The Encyclopaedia article does not present a Shi'i account of the battle, so none is given here, and no verdict is offered on any of the Companions involved.

## Academic account: from Mecca to Basra

According to the article, [[person/aisha|Aisha]] was completing the lesser pilgrimage in [[place/mecca|Mecca]] when she heard of the killing of the caliph [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman ibn Affan]], and on the way back toward [[place/medina|Medina]] she heard that Ali had been elected caliph amid disorder in the city. She turned back and gave an impassioned speech near the Kaba, blaming the mob for the killing and demanding punishment of those responsible, saying Uthman had been killed unjustly. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 439} The author notes that she had earlier been among Uthman's critics but did not accept his murder, and that she could not bear that Ali, toward whom she had long felt hostility, should gain from it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 439}

The article reports, citing al-Tabari, that about four months after Uthman's death [[person/talha-ibn-ubaydullah|Talha]] and [[person/zubayr-ibn-al-awwam|al-Zubayr]], after sharp exchanges with Ali, who would not give them posts in the government, asked and obtained his leave to go to Mecca for the lesser pilgrimage. The author then describes a plan against Ali involving them, some Umayyads and other Muslims alarmed by events. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 439} In the author's reading, the deeper causes were Ali's lenient attitude toward those who had killed Uthman, his weakness toward dissidents, and a policy against the Quraysh that aimed at popularity. She also notes that in [[place/kufa|Kufa]] the governor sent by Ali had been refused in favour of [[person/abu-musa-al-ashari|Abu Musa al-Ash'ari]], and that [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya]] in Syria had withheld homage. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 439}

The party chose [[place/basra|Basra]], hoping to find money and men there. Aisha agreed to go and rouse the people, because Talha and al-Zubayr had both stirred opinion against Uthman and had pledged allegiance to Ali right after his election. The article says they therefore had to claim that the pledge had been forced on them. [[person/hafsa|Hafsa bint Umar]], who at first meant to join, was dissuaded by her brother Abd Allah. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 439}

The article gives several hundred men for the party, with a query over six or seven hundred. Ali, learning of this, gathered about seven hundred men and left Medina, on the last day of Rabi II according to al-Tabari. He hoped to intercept them but learned at [[place/al-rabadha|al-Rabadha]] that they had passed, and turned toward Iraq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 439} The article also relays the story of al-Haw'ab, where barking dogs made Aisha recall a warning she attributed to the Prophet and nearly give up until she was assured that the place was a different one; the author mentions it only because the sources attach great weight to it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 439}

## Academic account: Basra before the battle

Near Basra the leaders of Aisha's party opened talks and began a campaign of persuasion. Aisha wrote to notables and sent an envoy, presenting the aim as ishlah, a word that, the article explains, meant for her party restoring the law and its penalties, avenging Uthman, and putting power in the hands of a caliph lawfully chosen by consultation, while for Ali it meant restoring his own authority, a return to the Prophet's Sunna and an end to privileges. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 439} The people of Basra divided. The governor appointed by Ali, [[person/uthman-ibn-hunayf|Uthman ibn Hunayf]], temporised while awaiting Ali, and others joined Aisha, Talha and al-Zubayr. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 439} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 440}

Disorder followed a speech at al-Mirbad, with fighting near the Dar al-Rizk in which the chief of police, Hukaym ibn Jabala, was killed. A truce was agreed: the governor would keep the palace, the great mosque and the treasury until a messenger sent to Medina returned to ask whether Talha and al-Zubayr had really been forced to pledge to Ali. The two men disputed who would lead the prayer, and Aisha settled it by having them, or in another version their sons, lead on alternate days. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 440} The messenger's inquiry favoured Talha and al-Zubayr, but a letter reached the governor saying the opposite. He refused to resign, a brawl broke out in the mosque, and Aisha's party attacked the treasury, killing or capturing its guards, and forced Ibn Hunayf out of the palace. The article says he got free and reached Ali by threatening reprisals against the families of his captors in Medina, where his brother Sahl was governor. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 440}

On who the aggressors were, the article notes that some traditions praise the moderation of Aisha's party, and that Aisha is said to have forbidden her men to use their hands except in self-defence. The author nevertheless judges that her party were the attackers, since they needed supplies and money and feared being caught between the governor and Ali's advancing force. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 440} Once Basra was held, an order called on the population to hand over those who had taken part in the siege of Uthman's house, and the article says six hundred were put to death, with only Hurqus ibn Zuhayr escaping under his tribe's protection. This and the gifts and supplies given to supporters angered part of the city, and three thousand men, among them the Banu Abd al-Qays, went to join Ali at [[place/dhi-qar|Dhi Qar]]. The article adds that [[person/al-ahnaf-ibn-qays|al-Ahnaf ibn Qays]] and his tribe stayed neutral. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 440}

## Academic account: Ali and Kufa

The article says that the talks with the governor lasted twenty-six days according to the reports, and that Ali meanwhile had gone to Dhi Qar, preferring to approach Kufa and win its people before marching on Basra. Abu Musa al-Ash'ari, though he accepted Ali's election as valid, urged the Kufans to stay out of the coming civil war. Ali's envoys, among them [[person/malik-al-ashtar|al-Ashtar]], Ibn Abbas and [[person/ammar-ibn-yasir|Ammar ibn Yasir]], persuaded part of the population to leave and join him, a number the article gives with a query as six, seven or twelve thousand. Abu Musa was removed from office. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 440} Ali then came to the outskirts of Basra and negotiated, and although everyone thought an agreement near, fighting began. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 440}

## Academic account: the fighting

Who began the fighting is, the article says, again uncertain. Some traditions say Ali had ordered his men not to attack and fought only after some of his supporters were killed, since his opponents belonged to the people of the qibla. The article cites a tradition from al-Tabari that Ali would not grant protection to those implicated in Uthman's killing, and that these, fearing for themselves, started the fight with a surprise attack unknown to him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 440} The battle lasted from morning to sunset, or seven days according to a work attributed to Ibn Qutayba. The most common date is 10 Jumada II 36, equal to 4 December 656, and the article notes that Caetani preferred 15 Jumada II, 9 December. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 440}

The author remarks that fighters often came from the same tribes, clans and even families. Aisha was present on a camel, in a litter whose cover had been reinforced, and by the end it bristled with arrows. She was not hit and received only a scratch on the arm. Fighting around the camel was the fiercest, with defenders taking the bridle one after another and reciting verse as they fell, and the dead there are given as anywhere from forty to 2,700. Ali's men won when they hamstrung the camel and it fell on its side. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 440}

By the article's account the battle was already virtually decided. Talha was struck by an arrow, which many sources attribute to [[person/marwan-ibn-al-hakam|Marwan ibn al-Hakam]], and withdrew into a house where he died soon after. Al-Zubayr, no longer certain of his cause, left the field after a conversation in which Ali reminded him of a past episode and of sayings of the Prophet. The article says he was then pursued by some Tamimis and killed in a lonely spot at Wadi al-Siba, and that al-Ahnaf was suspected of having instigated this. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 440} The author describes the battle as a series of duels and clashes between ranks in the Arab manner, and says the sources do not allow its tactical course to be reconstructed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 441}

## Academic account: numbers

The article says the strength of the forces cannot be calculated. Reported dead range from 6,000 to 30,000, and the author judges the larger figure greatly exaggerated, since Ali's own force, counting those who came from Medina and those who joined later, can hardly have exceeded 15,000. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 441}

## Academic account: after the battle

Aisha was taken but, the article says, treated with great respect. Ali firmly decided that she must return to Medina. He granted safety to all who had fought against him, and some compromised figures, Marwan ibn al-Hakam among them, went to Mu'awiya. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 441} Ali's refusal to enslave the women and children of the defeated or to seize their property, apart from what was found on the field, caused a stir among his own followers, who asked why people whose blood had been judged lawful were treated so. The article adds that the Kharijites later raised this against Ali. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 441} Ali received the homage of the people of Basra and named [[person/abdullah-ibn-abbas|Ibn Abbas]] governor, with Ziyad ibn Abihi beside him, which angered al-Ashtar because two other sons of al-Abbas already held posts in Yemen and in Mecca. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 441}

## Academic account: the author's assessment

Veccia Vaglieri judges that Aisha was the dominant figure of the movement, energetic, set on her goal except for the moment at al-Haw'ab, and obeyed. She reads Talha and al-Zubayr, who quarrelled, offered weak excuses for breaking their pledge and withdrew in the fighting, as men driven by ambition but lacking the resolve to succeed. This is the author's reading; other positions recorded for this battle, including that of the Sunni scholar al-Sallabi, describe the aims of the Companions differently. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 441} She also mentions Caetani's suggestion that Marwan was the hidden organiser behind Aisha, which she calls attractive but unconfirmed by the sources. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 441}
