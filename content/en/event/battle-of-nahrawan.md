## Overview
The battle of al-Nahrawan was [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]]'s fight against the Kharijites who rebelled against him after the arbitration. al-Tabari mentions "the report of the day of al-Nahr" within the accounts of 37 and 38 AH. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.5 p.627}

## According to al-Suyuti
al-Suyuti relates that the Kharijites came out after the arbitration, saying "No judgement but God's," and camped at Harura. Ali sent Ibn Abbas to them, who debated with them, and many of them returned. A group remained, moved to al-Nahrawan and blocked the road. Ali marched against them and killed them, and among those killed was Dhu al-Thudayya, in 38 AH. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p. 294}

## According to al-Mas'udi
al-Mas'udi relates that the Kharijites gathered four thousand men and gave their allegiance to Abd Allah ibn Wahb al-Rasibi, and that they slaughtered Abd Allah ibn Khabbab. Ali marched against them with thirty-five thousand men, and ten thousand from Basra with him. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 695-696}

## What al-Sallabi adds: leaving the army
Al-Sallabi says that the Kharijites separated from Ali's army on his return from Siffin and gathered at Harura. In one report their number is put at twelve thousand. At al-Nahrawan about four thousand of them withdrew, and only a thousand or fewer remained with them. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 168} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 177}

## Ibn Abbas's debate
Ali sent [[person/abdullah-ibn-abbas|Ibn Abbas]] to the Kharijites. He came to them at midday in Yemeni robes and debated them on three points: that Ali had made men arbiters in God's affair, that he fought without taking captives or spoils, and that he erased the title Commander of the Faithful. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 169} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 170} The outcome was the return of about two thousand of them. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 170} From the debate the author draws rules for dialogue: choose the more knowledgeable interlocutor, begin from what is agreed, refute each argument separately, and fix the point of reference. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 171}

## The three-part pledge
After they returned to Kufa, they claimed that Ali had repented of the arbitration. He addressed the people and announced three rights for them: that no one may bar them from prayer in the mosque; that they have their share of the fay for as long as they remain with the community; and that they are not to be fought until they fight. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 172}

## The killing of Ibn Khabbab
The author relates, from an eyewitness who had been a Kharijite and later left them, that they killed Abd Allah ibn Khabbab ibn al-Aratt because he had transmitted the hadith of the strife and had objected to their killing a pig belonging to a non-Muslim under treaty, and then they ripped open the belly of his wife. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 175} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 176} Al-Sallabi holds that the chain of this report is weak, but that it has supporting reports. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 175}

## The battle
Ali sent to them asking that they hand over the killers of Ibn Khabbab, and they refused. He set out with his army in Muharram 38. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 176} He placed Hujr ibn Adi over the right wing, Shabath ibn Rib'i and Ma'qil ibn Qays over the left, Abu Ayyub al-Ansari over the cavalry, Abu Qatada over the infantry, and Qays ibn Sa'd over the people of Medina. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 177} He sent Abu Ayyub to raise a banner of safe-conduct, and many groups then withdrew. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 177} Al-Sallabi dates the engagement to the ninth of Safar 38 and says that it was brief. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 178}

## Dhu al-Thudayya
The author notes that Ali ordered a search for Dhu al-Thudayya, and that they found him under the dead on the bank of the river. Ali exclaimed Allahu akbar and prostrated in gratitude. The reports differ over his name, and al-Tabari endorses the report that names him Nafi' Dhu al-Thudayya. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 179} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 179}

## Ali's policy toward the Kharijites
Ali forbade pursuing a fleeing man and finishing off the wounded. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 179} He said of the Kharijites that they were a people who rebelled against us and we fought them, and among what al-Sallabi transmits from him is that if they opposed a just imam they should be fought, but if they opposed an unjust ruler they should not be fought. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 180} From this the author draws the rules of the rebels in jurisprudence: the aim is to deter rather than kill, their property may not be seized, and no polytheist or protected non-Muslim may be enlisted against them. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 181}

## A view with support
Al-Sallabi holds that Ali rejoiced at fighting the Kharijites because the text commanded it, and that he did not rejoice in the same way at the battle of Siffin, as he quotes from Ibn Taymiyya. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 180}

## The astrologer's warning before Nahrawan
Al-Sallabi relates that when Ali wanted to set out to fight the Kharijites, an astrologer met him and said: "Commander of the Faithful, do not set out, for the moon is in Scorpio." {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 243}. Ali set out, "and blessing was given him in the journey, so he killed most of the Kharijites." {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 243}.

### Ali's position after victory
When he finished at Nahrawan he praised God and said that if he had set out at the hour the astrologer had told him, the ignorant would have said that he set out at the hour the astrologer had told him and was victorious. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 243}. Al-Sallabi holds that Ali was keen to protect the creed of his companions, and so he explained the corruption of the belief at the right time, after the fighting had ended. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 243}.

### Note
The book mentions this report in the chapter on rejecting corrupt belief, and does not examine its attribution to Ali. Part of the text is garbled in some lines, so only the legible part has been transcribed.

## In Khalifa ibn Khayyat
[[person/khalifa-ibn-khayyat|Khalifa ibn Khayyat]] reports from Abu Ubayda that Nahrawan was fought in Sha'ban 38; Abd Allah ibn Wahb al-Rasibi was killed, and Qays ibn Sa'd led Ali's right wing. {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 197}


## The Encyclopaedia of Islam on the origins of the dissidents
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, in its article on the Kharijites, traces the split to the proposal that Mu'awiya put to Ali during the [[event/battle-of-siffin|battle of Siffin]] in Safar 37/July 657, namely that the quarrel arising from the killing of Uthman be referred to two referees who would judge according to the Qur'an. Most of Ali's army accepted. A group of fighters, mainly from the tribe of Tamim, protested against setting a human tribunal above the divine word, cried that judgement belongs to God alone, left the army and withdrew to the village of Harura near [[place/kufa|Kufa]]. There they chose as their chief a little-known soldier, [[person/abdallah-ibn-wahb-al-rasibi|Abd Allah ibn Wahb al-Rasibi]]. The article says that these first dissenters were called the Haruriyya or the Muhakkima, names later extended to the whole movement. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1115}

The group grew through defections, above all when the [[event/arbitration-of-siffin|arbitration]] ended in a verdict quite contrary to what the Qur'an-readers had expected, probably in Ramadan or Shawwal 37/February or March 658. A large number of Ali's supporters then slipped out of Kufa to join Ibn Wahb, who had meanwhile moved to the Jukha country on the left bank of the Tigris. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1115} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1116} The article in the same work on Ali gives a figure of three or four thousand who left Kufa secretly once it was known that he had sent [[person/abu-musa-al-ashari|Abu Musa]] to meet [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr]], with some hundreds more leaving Basra, and names al-Nahrawan, on the canal of that name drawn from the Tigris, as their chosen rallying point. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 406}

## Abd Allah ibn Wahb
A separate entry describes Abd Allah ibn Wahb as a leader of the [[group/khawarij|Kharijites]] and a follower of the Companions from the Bajila tribe, noted for bravery and piety. He was nicknamed the man with the callosities, from the marks left on his forehead by many prostrations. He had fought under Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas in Iraq and under Ali at Siffin, and broke with Ali over the decision to arbitrate. The entry says that shortly before the final departure from Kufa in Shawwal 37/March 658 the Kharijites chose him as commander, with the title amir and not caliph as is often stated, and that he was killed in the battle at Nahrawan on 9 Safar 38/17 July 658. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 73}

## The camp on the canal
According to the article on the Kharijites, the rebel camp lay along the Nahrawan canal, and the name Khawarij, those who went out, is more likely to come from this exodus from Kufa than from a later idea that they had left the community of the faithful. The article also records that they called themselves al-Shurat, the sellers, meaning those who had sold their souls for God's cause. It describes their conduct in strong terms: it says they declared Ali's claim to the caliphate void while equally condemning Uthman, treated everyone who did not share their view as an unbeliever, and committed many murders, women included. It adds that their ranks swelled with other restless elements, among them non-Arabs drawn by the principle of equality of races in the faith. These are the article's characterisations and are reported here as such. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1116}

## The march and the battle
The article on Ali says that after the verdict was known Ali publicly protested against both arbitrators, declared that their sentence contradicted the Qur'an and the sunna, and gathered his forces to meet Mu'awiya again. On reaching al-Anbar he turned aside towards al-Nahrawan, convinced that this centre of rebellion had to be destroyed first. In the same month Mu'awiya took Egypt, in Safar 38. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 406}

Ali first tried to win the dissidents back by announcing that he would again take the field against Mu'awiya, without effect. They demanded that he admit to an act of impiety, which he refused. He promised safe-conduct to those who submitted, and some did, and then attacked on 9 Safar 38/17 July 658. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 406} The article on Nahrawan gives the place of the fighting as Jisr al-Nahrawan, also called simply al-Nahr, and states that of 2,800 men left with Abd Allah after the defections, eight fled during the battle, 400 were wounded, carried from the field and pardoned by Ali, and the rest were killed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 966} The Kharijites article says that Ibn Wahb and most of his followers were slain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1116}

These figures and dates differ from those of the other sources on this page, which give about a thousand men remaining with Ibn Wahb, place Ali's departure in Muharram, or date the battle to Sha'ban 38. The page reports each account as its source gives it and does not choose between them.

## Judgements and consequences in the article
The article on Ali judges that the action was a massacre rather than a battle, and says it seems Ali was the first to regret it. It adds that contemporaries condemned the action, since many believers of known piety fell, and that it had very grievous consequences for Ali: defections that had already begun increased, and he had to return to Kufa and give up the campaign against Mu'awiya. In its view the opposing parties were thereafter no longer a caliph and a rebel governor but two rivals for the supreme office. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 406}

The article on the Kharijites says the victory cost Ali dear. The rebellion was far from suppressed and continued in local risings in 39 and 40, and Ali himself was killed by the Kharijite [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-muljam|Abd al-Rahman ibn Muljam]], the husband of a woman whose family had lost most of its members at al-Nahrawan. It also observes that the Arab historians' accounts of the movement's origins are confused and contradictory. Its own reconstruction, followed above, links the movement closely to the arbitration; it notes that Wellhausen, followed by Lammens and Caetani, held instead that the rebellion and the arbitration were independent of one another. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1116}

## The place
Al-Nahrawan, in the Encyclopaedia of Islam, is a town and canal system in the lower Diyala region east of the Tigris in Iraq. A major canal system was created there in the sixth century by the Sasanian ruler Khusraw Anushirwan, and in early Islamic times the system reached its greatest development. The town of Jisr al-Nahrawan was divided by the canal, with markets, a mosque and khans on both sides. The system was breached in 326/937-8, and the countryside was generally abandoned by the seventh/thirteenth century. See also [[place/al-nahrawan|al-Nahrawan]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 966}
