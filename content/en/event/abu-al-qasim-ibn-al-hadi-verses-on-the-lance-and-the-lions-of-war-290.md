## Overview
On p. 264 Abu al-Qasim says that his concern is the swords and the lances, that he does not amuse himself with wives or listen to the tambourine, that he will not rest until he revives the religion after its death and sees blood flow like waves, and that he is not one to be content with a wedding feast and fine food {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.264}. On p. 265 he says that he does not seek wealth through a soft life, that he gives his life for God's pleasure and the aid of the oppressed, that he denies himself sweet sleep so long as he has not battered the tyrants and lit guidance, and then that they do not fear their enemy nor complain in misfortunes, that their father is the Messenger of God whose place they hold, that whoever keeps them from their right will get the sword, that whoever kept to covenants will get the best he hopes for, and that they are lions of war {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.265}. At the foot of p. 265 a piece begins "under the shade of the lances among the rams" {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.265}.

## Detail
The editor's note on p. 266, outside this batch, says that one of these poems was mentioned earlier when Abu al-Qasim was at Khiwan; this points to [[event/al-hadi-halts-at-khiwan-and-meets-the-sons-of-yafur-at-al-ghayl-290|al-Hadi's halt at Khiwan]], and the museum cannot tell which poem it means, so the matter stays open until p. 266 is read. The boasting of fighting and the description of lions are the poet's. Before this are [[event/abu-al-qasim-ibn-al-hadi-composes-verses-in-prison-290|the earlier pieces of the same section]]. The museum does not reproduce the lines nor the words of insult in them and gives only their sense as the book has it; the book ascribes the verse to Abu al-Qasim and the museum has not checked the attribution. The praise and the epithets in the lines are the poet's, given as the book gives them, with no verdict in the museum's voice.
