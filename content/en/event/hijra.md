## Overview
The Hijra is the migration of the Prophet [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] from [[place/mecca|Mecca]] to [[place/medina|Medina]] in 622 CE, the fourteenth year of the mission {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.163-164} {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 577}. His companions had already begun to emigrate to Medina before him {cite:ibn-ishaq-guillaume} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 3905}. The Prophet left his house on the night of 27 Safar in the fourteenth year of prophethood, corresponding to 12/13 September 622 CE {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.163-164}. He went out of Mecca with [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] and Abu Bakr's freedman [[person/amir-ibn-fuhayra|Amir ibn Fuhayra]]; their guide was [[person/abdullah-ibn-urayqit|Abdullah ibn Urayqit]] {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 577}. He hid in [[place/ghar-thawr|the cave of Thawr]] for three nights, and then travelled from the cave to Medina {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.161} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.174}.

In al-Mubarakpuri's account he reached [[place/quba|Quba]] on Monday 8 Rabi' al-Awwal, corresponding to 23 September 622 CE, and entered Medina on the Friday {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.170} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.172}. [[person/al-masudi|Al-Mas'udi]] dates his entry into Medina to Monday, when twelve nights of Rabi' al-Awwal had passed, and says that he stayed at Quba until Thursday and set out on Friday {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 577}. [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]] remained in Mecca three days after him, until he had handed over what he had been told to hand over, and then joined him {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 577}. The Prophet lived in Medina for ten years {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 577}. The [[concept/hijri-calendar|Hijri calendar]] is counted from the Hijra: in the chapter on dating in Sahih al-Bukhari, Sahl ibn Sa'd says that they did not count from the Prophet's mission or from his death, but only from his arrival in Medina {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 3934}.

## What happened
After years of hardship in Mecca, a delegation from Medina (which was then called Yathrib) pledged to protect the Prophet. His companions began to leave Mecca for the oasis in small groups. The Prophet left with [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] and reached the outskirts of Medina in autumn 622 CE. [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]] joined him a short time later. {cite:ibn-ishaq-guillaume}

The traditional date for arrival at Quba is 12 Rabi' al-Awwal of the first year AH. Historians debate the exact sequence of events, so the dates here are marked as traditional. {cite:watt-1956-medina}

## The word and its meaning
The article on the Hijra in the second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam, written by W. Montgomery Watt, points to the Arabic verb behind the word. Its first form means to cut someone off from friendly association or to avoid association with them, and its third form refers to a mutual ending of friendly relations. On this basis the author argues that hijra properly does not mean "flight", as it has traditionally been translated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 386}

## Why Muhammad left Mecca: the academic account
The sira works preserve the traditional narrative, and the article follows it in outline while labelling some of its details as doubtful. The author gives as the reason for leaving the loss of clan support after the death of [[person/abu-talib|Abu Talib]], around 619, when [[person/abu-lahab|Abu Lahab]] replaced him as head of the clan; Abu Lahab is said to have had commercial ties with some of Muhammad's bitterest opponents. After his visit to [[place/taif|al-Ta'if]], in 619 or 620, Muhammad had to seek the protection of the clan of Nawfal before he could enter Mecca again, and the author infers from this that Abu Lahab had refused to protect him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 386} The biographical article on Muhammad adds that after the deaths of [[person/khadija|Khadija]] and Abu Talib his position grew more and more hopeless, that the attempt at al-Ta'if failed and put him in physical danger, and that the sira accounts say he then obtained protection from [[person/mutim-ibn-adi|Mut'im ibn Adi]]. Its author judges that his attempt to reform his native city must on the whole be regarded as having failed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 417} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 418}

## The agreement at al-Aqaba and the departures
According to the article, Muhammad sought a new field of activity and negotiated with representatives of all the main Arab clans of Medina. He concluded an agreement with them at al-Aqaba during the pilgrimage of 622, in June and July. It is known as "the pledge of war", because the men of Medina undertook to defend him by force of arms if necessary. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 386} The Muhammad article places earlier contacts with Medinans who came as pilgrims in 621, a preliminary meeting at al-Aqaba, and then the formal agreement of 622 with a considerable number of Medinans, who pledged to take him into their community and protect him as one of their own citizens. The author notes that tradition mentions only this promise of protection, without further obligations. Museum records on these meetings are [[event/pledges-of-aqaba|the pledges of Aqaba]] and [[event/second-pledge-of-aqaba|the second pledge of Aqaba]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 418}

The article says Muhammad had already begun to encourage his Meccan followers to go to Medina, and that about seventy went in small parties. The Muhammad article adds that the negotiations could not stay hidden from the Meccans, caused great bitterness, and began, as Urwa says, a second period of trial for the believers. Followers are reported to have slipped away in large groups until only Muhammad and Abu Bakr, with the latter's servant, were left. It notes that some accounts, possibly Shi'i, say Ali also stayed in Mecca until the last moment. The author thinks Muhammad's staying behind was most likely part of a plan to keep the Hijra unobtrusive. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 386} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 418}

## What Medina wanted
The Muhammad article gives its own reading of the situation. In the author's view the Medinans wanted a political leader who could readjust relations shattered by recent tribal conflicts, which had culminated in [[event/yawm-buath|the battle of Bu'ath]], more than they wanted an inspired preacher. It adds that the many Jews living there had helped make the Arab population somewhat familiar with monotheistic ideas. The author describes the responsibility Muhammad took on as awesome, since he became answerable for the safety and welfare of followers who were breaking their ties with tribe and family. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 418}

## The departure, the cave and the road
The article retells what Ibn Ishaq reports. At a meeting of most of the clans of Quraysh, it was agreed that chosen young men, one from each clan, would attack Muhammad together with swords, so that the Hashimite clan could not take revenge on any single clan and would have to accept blood money. The young men gathered at his house, but he slipped away, leaving Ali in his bed so that they would think he was still asleep. The author says that whether or not this story is accepted, Muhammad must have left Mecca secretly in company with Abu Bakr, since a later Qur'anic passage recalls the two in the cave, [[passage/quran-9-40|Qur'an 9:40]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 386}

The two spent three days in the cave. They were then accompanied by [[person/amir-ibn-fuhayra|Amir ibn Fuhayra]], a freedman of Abu Bakr, and a nomad guide, and travelled on two camels by an unusual route to Medina. Ibn Ishaq dates their arrival at [[place/quba|Quba]], in the south of the oasis of Medina, to Monday 12 Rabi' I. The article gives 24 September 622 as the equivalent date and observes that this date was a Friday. It presumes they hid and avoided the main road because Muhammad, once out of Mecca, was no longer under the protection of Nawfal, yet would not be under the protection of his followers until he reached Medina. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 386} The Muhammad article gives the same arrival on 12 Rabi' I, year 1, 24 September 622. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 419}

## Historians on the cave story
The Muhammad article says that the traditional story, in which Muhammad and Abu Bakr stay behind until all the other Muslims have left and then hide in a cave, is embellished with later legendary details. It states that the Qur'anic verse may lend it some support, but that the verse is so ambiguous without added explanation that the cave story could equally be a later exegetical elaboration of it. The author also judges that at the time of the Hijra the Meccans probably did not see Muhammad as a threat, whether he was in Mecca or in Medina. These are the article's academic judgements; the earlier sections of this page, drawn from Muslim biographical works, relate the episode as it is traditionally told. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 386} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 419}

## The emigrants
The article notes that in the document sometimes called [[event/constitution-of-medina|the Constitution of Medina]], those who made the hijra with Muhammad appear as the emigrants, the [[group/muhajirun|muhajirun]], of Quraysh, with a collective standing comparable to that of one of the Arab clans of Medina. The status of muhajir came to be greatly prized. It could also be granted to people who had not made the journey. Members of nomadic tribes could pledge themselves to Muhammad with a pledge of migration, settle in Medina, and count as belonging to the emigrants. The tribe of Muzayna received the status without settling in Medina, and Aslam and Khuza'a were in a similar position. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 386} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 387}

Those who had gone to [[place/abyssinia|Abyssinia]] around 615 and stayed there until Muhammad brought them to Medina in 7/628 were counted as having made a hijra, namely to Abyssinia. The article suggests this may have been part of the inducement to go to Medina, and says that those who came back to Mecca before 622 and then joined Muhammad could claim two hijras. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 387} The Muhammad article adds that the emigrants who stayed with Muhammad through the hard Meccan years held a special rank among the Muslims, and that the Medinan converts, called [[group/ansar|the Ansar]], held a rank second only to theirs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 419}

The article also records a later, different use of the term. The Kharijite leader [[person/nafi-ibn-al-azraq|Nafi ibn al-Azraq]], whose followers were the [[group/azariqa|Azariqa]], held that only those who actively supported him were truly Muslims. He called them emigrants who had made the hijra to his camp, which he named the abode of hijra. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 387}

## Earlier emigration to Abyssinia
The Muhammad article places the move to Medina after an earlier emigration of some followers to Abyssinia. It reports that Ibn Sa'd describes two such emigrations, the first group mostly returning to Mecca before the Hijra to Medina and the second mostly still in Abyssinia at the time, so that they went directly to Medina. Ibn Hisham and al-Tabari speak of only one migration, and al-Tabari names the first ten to go and ends with [[person/jafar-ibn-abi-talib|Ja'far ibn Abi Talib]], followed by a steady stream of Muslims. The accounts agree that persecution in Mecca played a major part. Watt, the article says, has shown that the episode was more complex than the traditional accounts suggest, and that some emigrants may have gone to trade. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 417}

## The era of the Hijra
The article states that Muslim dates are normally given by the era of the Hijra, abbreviated A.H. That era does not begin on the day Muhammad reached Medina, but on the first day of the lunar year in which this happened, which is reckoned to coincide with 16 July 622. The author adds that this result assumes that no intercalary months occurred after the Hijra, and that this is by no means certain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 387} The Muhammad article says the year of the migration was chosen with good reason as year 1 of the [[concept/hijri-calendar|Islamic calendar]], because it marks the start of ten years in which Muhammad led the building of a new religious community, which in a remarkably short time grew into one of the great civilisations of world history. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 419}

## Significance
The community that formed in Medina became the first Islamic political entity. About fifteen years later, the caliph Umar made the start of the Hijri calendar the first of Muharram of that year, which corresponds to 16 July 622 CE in the Julian calendar.

## For young visitors
The Hijra is the journey with which a new calendar began. When someone says "year 1447 AH," they are counting the years from it.


## Stages in detail (The Sealed Nectar pp.161–174)
The account of the Hijra in this chapter divides into two stages: [[event/hijra-night-escape|the Prophet's departure from his house and his hiding in the Cave of Thawr]], and then [[event/hijra-journey-to-medina|the journey of the Hijra from the cave to Medina and his entry into it]]. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.161} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.174}

## For Sprouts
In the year 622, the Prophet Muhammad travelled from Mecca to Medina. His friend Abu Bakr travelled with him. The journey through the desert was long. He reached Medina in the autumn, and the people were happy to see him. The Hijri calendar begins with this journey, and it is called the Hijra. When someone says "the year 1447 AH", they are counting the years from it.

## For Explorers
In 622 the Prophet Muhammad and his companions had lived through hard years in Mecca. A group of people came from a town called Yathrib and promised to protect the Prophet and welcome his companions. The Muslims began to travel from Mecca to Yathrib in small groups. Then the Prophet set out with his friend Abu Bakr. They stayed in a cave on a mountain for three nights, and Asma, Abu Bakr's daughter, brought them food. After a long journey through the desert they reached Quba, at the edge of the town. The Prophet then entered the town and stayed in the house of Abu Ayyub al-Ansari, and Ali ibn Abi Talib joined him a short time later. The town got a new name, Medina, and became the first community of the Muslims. About fifteen years later the caliph Umar made the Muslim calendar begin from the year of this journey, which is why we say "the Hijri year".

## For Scholars
The Hijra is the move of the Prophet Muhammad and his companions from Mecca to Yathrib, which came to be known as Medina, in 622 CE. Years of harm in Mecca came before it, then the two pledges of Aqaba, in which a delegation from Yathrib undertook to protect the Prophet, and his companions began to leave for the town in small groups. The sira books relate that the Prophet left with Abu Bakr by night, that the two stayed three nights in the cave of Thawr, where Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr brought them news and Asma bint Abi Bakr brought them food, and that they then travelled to Medina with a guide named Abdullah ibn Urayqit. The Prophet reached Quba on the outskirts of Medina; the traditional date is 12 Rabi' al-Awwal of the first year. He then entered the town and stayed with Abu Ayyub al-Ansari, and Ali ibn Abi Talib joined him a short time later. Historians debate the exact sequence of days, so the museum marks these dates as traditional. The Hijra was a turning point: the community that formed in Medina became the first Islamic political entity. About fifteen years later the caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab made the start of the Islamic calendar the first of Muharram of the year of the Hijra, which corresponds to 16 July 622 in the Julian calendar; this is why it is called the Hijri calendar.
