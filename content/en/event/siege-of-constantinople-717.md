## Overview
A naval and land expedition sent by [[person/sulayman-ibn-abd-al-malik|Sulayman ibn Abd al-Malik]] against [[place/constantinople|Constantinople]], the capital of the [[polity/byzantine-empire|Byzantine empire]], under [[person/maslama-ibn-abd-al-malik|Maslama ibn Abd al-Malik]]. One book states that the land force was about a hundred and twenty thousand and the naval force about a thousand ships. {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 p.47} The Encyclopaedia of Islam describes it as an expedition carried out by land and sea, and says it ended without success after about a year. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 571} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 767}

## Earlier Arab attempts
According to the Byzantine chronicler Theophanes, a fleet was fitted out at Tripoli in the year of the world 6146, beginning 1 September 653, and defeated the Greek fleet at Phoenix on the Lycian coast without reaching Constantinople. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 570} In 672 a strong fleet anchored off the European shore of the Sea of Marmara under the city walls. The Arabs attacked from April to September, wintered at Cyzicus and renewed the attack the next spring, retiring after what the chronicle calls seven years of fighting; much of the fleet was destroyed by Greek fire. The article notes difficulties in dating the phases and says Arab historians vary between several years. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 571} The siege is remembered in the Arab world because the Ansari [[person/abu-ayyub-al-ansari|Abu Ayyub Khalid ibn Zayd]] fell in it and was buried before the walls. The article adds that several Arabic sources say the Byzantines respected his grave and prayed there for rain in times of drought. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 571} A truce of over forty years followed, until Sulayman came to the throne. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 571}

## Preparation
Preparation began in the days of [[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik]], and Sulayman continued it after his death in 96 AH. {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 p.47} The Encyclopaedia of Islam reports a hadith then current, that a caliph bearing the name of a prophet would conquer Constantinople, and says Sulayman took the prophecy to refer to himself and equipped a great expedition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 571}

## The commander
The Encyclopaedia of Islam presents Maslama as a son of the caliph Abd al-Malik and one of the most imposing Umayyad generals. As the son of a slave-girl he was excluded from the succession to the caliphate. Beginning in 86/705 he led the regular summer campaigns into Byzantine Asia Minor, among them the capture of [[place/amorium|Amorium]] in 88/707 and 89/708. In 91/710 he became governor of al-Jazira, Armenia and Azerbaijan, and in 95/714 he advanced to [[place/bab-al-abwab|Bab al-Abwab]] on the Caspian Sea. After Sulayman succeeded al-Walid he was given chief command of the expedition against Constantinople. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 767}

## The plan of march
The commanders disagreed about the plan. Musa ibn Nusayr advised taking the cities and fortresses first, without Constantinople. Maslama advised heading straight for it, and the caliph took his advice. {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 p.48} The Encyclopaedia of Islam says Maslama led an army equipped with siege artillery through Asia Minor, crossed the Dardanelles at Abydos and surrounded the city. The fleet anchored partly near the walls on the Marmara coast and partly in the Bosporus, while the Golden Horn was barred by a chain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 571}

## The siege and its dating
The two Encyclopaedia of Islam articles give different starting points. The article on Constantinople says the siege began on 25 August 716 and lasted a whole year. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 571} The article on Maslama says it started at the beginning of 99/mid-August 717 and ended exactly a year later without success. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 767} The museum records the date as approximate and does not choose between them.

On the causes of the outcome the two articles stress different things. The article on Constantinople says Maslama had to retire because of attacks by the Bulghars and the scarcity of provisions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 571} The article on Maslama names supply difficulties, the plague and the Byzantine use of Greek fire against the Arab fleet. It adds that the loss did not harm Maslama's military reputation, though it interrupted his activity in Asia Minor for some years, and that legend later turned the failure into a victory. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 767}

## The withdrawal
At Sulayman's death [[person/umar-ibn-abd-al-aziz|Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz]] ordered Maslama to return. The army withdrew after a truce with the Byzantines. {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 p.49} According to the article on Maslama, Umar II ordered him in 100-1/719 to lead the summer expedition again, but Maslama had to use that army in Iraq, first against the Kharijites. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 767}

## The reasons for failure in the author's account
One book attributes the failure to what it describes as treachery and incitement, to the severity of the winter of 99 AH, and to Maslama's lack of military experience. {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 pp.50-52} The Encyclopaedia of Islam gives other reasons, set out above.

## Traces and memory
Later Arab writers, the article says, still knew of a well at Abydos called after Maslama, where he had encamped, and of a mosque he built there. One of his comrades, Abd Allah ibn Tayyib, is named as the first Muslim to attack the gate of Constantinople. Some sources say Maslama made a house near the imperial palace for Arab prisoners a condition of the peace and built the first mosque in the city; others credit him with the Tower of Galata. The Ottoman traveller Evliya and his sources, the article remarks, made two sieges out of this single campaign. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 571}

No definite trace of the mosque of Maslama has yet come to light. It is first mentioned by Constantine Porphyrogenitus, and the article says it was destroyed in a popular rising in 1200 and pillaged by the Crusaders in 1203. According to Ibn al-Athir it was restored in 441/1049-50 by Constantine Monomachos at the request of the Seljuk Toghril Beg, and according to al-Maqrizi the Byzantine emperor Michael VIII built a mosque about 660/1261-2. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 571} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 572}
