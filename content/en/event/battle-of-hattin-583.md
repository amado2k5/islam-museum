## Overview
The battle of Hattin, in Arabic Hittin, was fought in 583/1187 between the forces of [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Salah al-Din]], known in Europe as Saladin, and the combined armies of the Crusader states. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose accounts this page follows, describes it as the battle that destroyed the power of the Crusaders, and speaks of an annihilating defeat for them. Its article on the village of Hattin places the fighting on the uneven tableland south-east of a rocky ridge near [[place/tiberias|Tiberias]], while the article on Salah al-Din places it at the Horns of Hattin, on the heights to the west of Tiberias. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 542} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 977}

## The place
Hittin or Hattin, called Kefar Hattiye in the Talmud, was a village to the west of and above Tiberias, on a fertile plain whose southern border is a steep limestone ridge. At the western and eastern ends of the ridge rises a higher summit, and each is called Kurun Hattin, the [[place/horns-of-hattin|Horns of Hattin]]. The article notes a tradition, known in the sixth/twelfth century though of uncertain origin, that places the tomb of the prophet Shu'ayb, Jethro, here. The small chapel there has been rebuilt several times and is still visited every year by the Druzes. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 542}

## The road to the battle
The article on Salah al-Din treats Hattin as the outcome of a long effort to bring the Muslim forces of Egypt, Syria and the Jazira under one leadership. A Kurd whose family served the Seljuks and then Zangi and his son [[person/nur-al-din-mahmud-ibn-zangi|Nur al-Din Mahmud]], Salah al-Din rose to power in Egypt, ended the Fatimid caliphate there in 567/1171 and returned Egypt to Abbasid allegiance, and took over Damascus in 570/1174. The article explains that if the Franks were ever to be expelled, large Muslim forces had to be united, and that this obliged him to fight other Muslims who would not answer the call to jihad, which exposed him to the criticism of neglecting the war against the Franks. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 976} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 977}

His own campaigns against the Franks had an uneven start. A raid into Palestine ended in defeat between Ascalon and the unidentified Mons Gisardi in Jumada 573/November 1177, a lesson the article says he took to heart. Better success followed with the defeat of King Baldwin at Marj Uyun in Muharram 575/June 1179. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 977}

Reynald de Chatillon, lord of Transjordan, figures twice in the article. In the spring of 579/1183 his naval raid into the Red Sea disturbed Salah al-Din but ultimately failed, and countering this threat to the Hijaz gave Salah al-Din further propaganda opportunities. The article also mentions two attacks aimed at [[place/al-karak|al-Karak]], the second in Jumada I-II 580/August-September 1184, and says that besides the wish to punish [[person/raynald-de-chatillon|Reynald]] for his raids, taking al-Karak would have improved communications between Egypt and Syria. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 977}

Salah al-Din's attention then turned to Mosul. After a siege and his illness he withdrew in Ramadan 581/December 1185, and a Mosul embassy reached him at Harran in Dhu al-Hijja 581/February 1186. It accepted the obligation to supply troops for the jihad, and his overlordship was finally recognised. A year of recuperation and internal reorganisation followed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 977}

## The campaign and the fighting
The article says the long-awaited campaign was launched at the beginning of 583, in the spring of 1187. Salah al-Din's allies were summoned from all quarters, and the full forces of the Crusader states, except Antioch, with which a truce had been made, were brought to battle at the Horns of Hattin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 977}

The article on Hattin itself gives a short account of the fighting. Some of the Frankish troops, tormented by heat and thirst, were cut down and others put to flight. The remainder withdrew to the eastern summit, and many were thrown over the steep southern side. In memory of this the victor built a small chapel on the summit, called Kubbat al-Nasr, the Dome of Victory. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 542}

## The date
The two articles give the day differently. The article on Hattin dates the great victory to 5 July 1187. The article on Salah al-Din gives Saturday, 24 Rabi' II 583, equivalent to 4 July 1187. The OCR text of the articles does not say how the difference arises, and this page records both. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 542} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 977}

## What followed
According to the Salah al-Din article, the Crusaders were demoralised and their garrisons reduced, so many towns and strongholds surrendered or fell quickly. [[place/akka|Acre]] surrendered on 1 Jumada I, 9 July, and by the middle of Jumada II, early September, all the coast from Gaza to Jubayl was in his hands, apart from [[place/tyre|Tyre]]. He then moved to [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]], which the article calls the symbolic goal of the jihad. After nearly two weeks of siege and some spirited resistance the city surrendered on 27 Rajab, 2 October. He solemnly installed in the Aqsa Mosque the minbar that Nur al-Din had prepared for the moment. The museum treats that event on its own page, [[event/saladin-jerusalem-583|Salah al-Din recovers Jerusalem]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 977} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 978}

The article records criticism that he did not give high priority to taking Tyre, where many refugees from the interior gathered; a siege begun in Ramadan 583/November 1187 proved fruitless. It adds that he was not to know that Conrad of Montferrat would arrive just in time to stiffen its defence. It also notes that it was strategically important to reduce the inland castles, and that the terms he offered, backed by the confidence people had in his word, hastened the surrender of many places. Some judge him only a moderately good strategist and tactician, while another reading of his strategy at this stage relates it to the military treatise of al-Harawi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 978}

Other articles in the encyclopaedia show the reach of the victory. The fortress of [[place/safad|Safad]] was kept under observation after Hattin and surrendered in Shawwal 584 after a fierce siege, its garrison receiving safe-conduct and leaving for Tyre. The article on Salkhad says that the victory brought Salkhad and Busra into his possession. The article on al-Salt says the Muslims established their control over lands in Transjordan after Hattin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 812} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1067} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1071}

## Wider assessments
The encyclopaedia's article on the fortress, Hisar, in a passage on the weakness of the Mamluk navy, remarks that after Hattin the Muslims were within sight of a total expulsion of the Crusaders, but lost their chance because of European naval supremacy. This is the author's judgement, offered in passing. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 504}

For the battle itself the Hattin article refers readers to the general histories of the Crusades by Grousset, Runciman and Setton, and to studies by Marshall W. Baldwin, Jean Richard, R. C. Smail, J. Prawer and P. Herde. This page does not draw on those studies directly. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 542}

## The battle as Ibn al-Athir tells it
Ibn al-Athir relates that [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Salah al-Din]] and the Muslims rose on Saturday, five nights before the end of Rabi' al-Akhir 583 AH, and fought the Franks, who were tormented by thirst. The Franks were marching toward Tiberias to get water, but Salah al-Din blocked them and stood with the army in their faces. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 146}

## The course of the fighting
Ibn al-Athir says that the Muslim archers hit many of the Franks' horses, and that Salah al-Din went himself among the Muslims urging them on. Then some of the volunteers set fire to the dry grass, and the wind carried its heat and smoke to the enemy, so thirst, the heat of the fire and the fighting came together upon them. Those who were left climbed to a hill in the direction of Hattin. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 146-149}

Ibn al-Athir relates, quoting [[person/al-malik-al-afdal-ali|al-Malik al-Afdal]], son of Salah al-Din, who was beside his father, that his father said to him: "We will not defeat them until their kingdom's tent falls." When it fell, the sultan dismounted and prostrated in thanks. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 146-149}

## The captives
The king was captured, with his brother and the prince Reynald [[person/raynald-de-chatillon|(Renaud de Chatillon)]], and a number of the Templars and Hospitallers, and the Muslims took their great cross. Ibn al-Athir says that Salah al-Din struck Reynald's neck with his own hand, saying that he had vowed to kill him. He ordered about two hundred of the Templars and Hospitallers put to death. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 146-149}

The source also mentions [[person/taqi-al-din-umar|Taqi al-Din Umar]] as commander of one wing of the army. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 146-149}
