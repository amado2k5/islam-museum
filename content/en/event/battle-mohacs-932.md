## Overview
The first battle of [[place/mohacs|Mohács]], a town in Baranya county in southern [[place/hungary|Hungary]], was fought on 21 Dhu 'l-Qa'da 932 AH, in August 1526. The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls it the more decisive of the two battles fought near the town. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 246}

Two accounts of the same campaign sit side by side below. The older text of this record follows an Arabic history of the Ottomans; the encyclopaedia article adds the political background, the forces, the course of the fighting and the consequences. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 246} {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.201-202}

## Report of the Arabic history
The sources report that the king of Hungary, Vladislaus II, wanted to rid himself of the undertakings of his predecessors to the Ottoman sultans, and he killed the envoy of Sultan [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Suleiman the Magnificent]] who demanded from him the annual tribute. Suleiman responded with a great campaign. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.201-202}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam, by contrast, names the Hungarian king of these years as Louis II, and says he detained the sultan's envoy rather than killing him. The two accounts differ on both points, and the museum records the difference without choosing between them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 247}

## Background
The article places the battle in a new era of Ottoman western policy that began when Suleiman I came to the throne in 926 AH, 1520. His father [[person/selim-i-uthmani|Selim I]] had mainly looked east, while the son turned towards Europe. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 246}

Hungary had once been a strong, centralised kingdom under Matthias Corvinus, who reigned from 1458 to 1490. Under the Jagiellonian kings it gradually lost its wealth and political power, mainly because of the heavy burden of continuous warfare with the Ottomans. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 246} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 247}

Seeking a way out, Louis II did not renew the peace treaty with Suleiman and detained his envoy. According to the article this led to the siege and capture of Belgrade, the key fortress of the southern marches. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 247} The museum has a separate record of that event, [[event/conquest-of-belgrade-1521|the conquest of Belgrade]]. In the years that followed, the article says, the king and his entourage could not obtain the international help they needed, so the country faced the Ottomans almost entirely alone, although a fairly considerable sum of money did arrive from abroad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 247}

## The armies
Both rulers took part, each heading his own army. The practical command on the Hungarian side lay with Pál Tomori, Archbishop of Kalocsa and chief commandant of Lower Hungary, who was also a soldier. On the Ottoman side it lay with Ibrahim, the new grand vizier. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 247}

No muster was taken on either side, so the numbers can only be estimated. The article is more confident about the Ottomans, using figures from the state budget of 931 AH, 1525. These suggest about 60,000 regular soldiers and timar-holders could be mobilised, and an equal number of irregular and non-military men could be added as a possible maximum. For the Hungarians the lowest figure still accepted is 25,000, while others hold that 40,000 or more is possible. In any case the article judges the Ottoman army superior in discipline and organisation. One important element was missing from it, namely heavy cavalry, which the Ottomans therefore feared. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 247}

## The battle
The exact place of the encounter is unknown. Only some thousand Hungarian skeletons have been found, in one group, and almost none of the Ottomans. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 247}

Tomori chose a mounted attack against the Turks as they came down a slope. It caused serious confusion for a short time, but the steadiness of the [[group/janissaries|Janissaries]] and an unexpected attack on the flank by the forces of Bali Beg turned the course of events. In an hour and a half the Hungarian army was completely beaten, losing its most prominent churchmen and political leaders. The king was among the dead, drowned in the Csele stream. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 247}

## Aftermath
The article says Suleiman was himself surprised by his success. He was able to enter the capital, [[place/buda|Buda]], without meeting resistance. The Arabic history gives the date of his entry as 11 September 1526, and adds that Hungarian resistance went on and that his armies reached the walls of [[place/vienna|Vienna]] in 1529. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.201-202}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam says Suleiman did not annex Hungary to his empire, yet from then on regarded it as his own possession. It sets this within the general Ottoman ideology and method of conquest, and adds that his soon accepting John I on the Hungarian throne as a vassal does not seem to contradict that view. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 247}

For Hungary the defeat was a turning point. The king's death opened a long period of anarchy, and the loss of Syrmia made the borders impossible to defend. The country became the battlefield of two world powers, and its territory was split into three zones, Ottoman, Habsburg and Transylvanian, for more than 150 years, which the article says held back development in many fields. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 247}

## A later battle of the same name
A second battle near the town, in 1687, ended in an Ottoman defeat. The museum treats it in [[event/battle-of-mohacs-1687|its own record]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 247}

## Note on the date
The Hijri year 932 AH is derived. The encyclopaedia gives the day as 21 Dhu 'l-Qa'da 932, in August 1526. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 246}

## Scope of this page
The Encyclopaedia of Islam treats the battle in a short section of its entry on Mohács, followed by a bibliography, and has no article of its own on it. This page is therefore shorter than most. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 246}
