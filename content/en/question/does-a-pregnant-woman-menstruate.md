## Summary
In the book of the major ablution in [[work/bidayat-al-mujtahid|Bidayat al-Mujtahid]], [[person/ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd]] treats the blood a pregnant woman sees, saying the jurists have differed "of old and of late" over whether it is [[concept/hayd-menstruation|menstruation]] or irregular bleeding. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 71}

## The views
- **A pregnant woman menstruates**: held by [[person/malik-ibn-anas|Malik]], by [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]] in the sounder of his two views, and others. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 71}
- **She does not menstruate**: held by [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]], [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad]], [[person/sufyan-al-thawri|al-Thawri]] and others, who say the blood she sees is the blood of disorder and illness. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 71}
- **The point of consensus**: they agree that blood seen once labour pains begin is lochia, with the rulings of menstruation in barring prayer and the rest. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 71}

## Maliki views on continued bleeding
Ibn Rushd notes that Malik and his followers have views he calls inconsistent on when a pregnant woman whose bleeding continues passes from the ruling of menstruation to that of irregular bleeding: that she is like any menstruating woman, waiting the maximum days of menstruation or adding three days to her usual days provided the total does not exceed fifteen; that she waits double the maximum days of menstruation; or that she multiplies the maximum days of menstruation by the number of months of her pregnancy. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 71}

## The cause of disagreement, and Ibn Rushd's use of medicine
Ibn Rushd traces the disagreement to the difficulty of settling the matter by experience and the mixing of two cases: the blood a pregnant woman sees is sometimes menstrual blood, when her strength is ample and the foetus small - which is how a pregnancy upon a pregnancy becomes possible, as Hippocrates, Galen and the other physicians report - and sometimes the blood of illness, from weakness and illness of the foetus following her own, and most often it is the latter. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 71} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 72} The editor of the Bayt al-Afkar edition gives this passage as an example of Ibn Rushd's use of medicine in law. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 15}

## The view of Ibn Hazm the Zahiri in al-Muhalla

In the Book of Menstruation and Irregular Bleeding of [[work/al-muhalla-ibn-hazm|al-Muhalla]], [[person/ibn-hazm|Ibn Hazm]] lays down that any blood a pregnant woman sees before she delivers the last child in her womb is neither menstruation nor post-natal bleeding and prevents nothing; for the proof that it is not menstruation he refers back to the Book of Purity, and he says it is not post-natal bleeding because she has not yet delivered, and that there is no consensus that it is either. {cite:muhalla-dar-ibn-hazm-2016-v3 p.234} From this he concludes that prayer, fasting and the permission of intercourse, whose obligation or permission is established, do not lapse for her without a firm text. {cite:muhalla-dar-ibn-hazm-2016-v3 p.234} The editors note that this is the view of Abu Hanifa and his companions, of Ahmad and Ishaq ibn Rahwayh, and al-Shafi'i's earlier view; that Malik distinguished between bleeding early and late in pregnancy; and that al-Shafi'i's later view, the one relied on in his school, is that it is menstruation. {cite:muhalla-dar-ibn-hazm-2016-v3 p.234}
