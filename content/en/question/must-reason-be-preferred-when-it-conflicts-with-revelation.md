## Summary
When a rational proof and a scriptural proof appear to conflict in matters of belief, which is preferred? This is the question of the "universal law" on which [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] wrote [[work/dar-taarud-al-aql-wal-naql|Dar' Ta'arud al-'Aql wa'l-Naql]]; its editor Muhammad Rashad Salim gives in his introduction the texts of the theologians and philosophers who held the law. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 10} {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 19}

## The law
As Ibn Taymiyya quotes it at the start of the book: if scriptural and rational proofs conflict, either both are accepted, which is impossible since it combines contradictories; or both are rejected; or scripture is put first, which is impossible since reason is the root of transmission and putting scripture first would impugn its root; so reason must be put first, and the text is then either interpreted or its meaning left to God. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 96} He calls this a "universal law" of [[person/fakhr-al-din-al-razi|al-Razi]] and his followers, anticipated by [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]], the judge Abu Bakr Ibn al-Arabi, [[person/imam-al-haramayn-al-juwayni|al-Juwayni]] and [[person/al-baqillani|al-Baqillani]]. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 10}

## Those who put reason first
- **Al-Razi**: in *Asas al-Taqdis* he divides the case where certain rational proofs establish something and transmitted proofs seem to say otherwise into four options, rejects three, and concludes that one must hold to what reason requires and judge the texts either inauthentic or not meant in their apparent sense; then, if interpretation is allowed, one interprets, and if not, one leaves their knowledge to God – "the universal law to which one refers in all ambiguous texts". {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 11} {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 12} In *Muhassal Afkar al-Mutaqaddimin wa'l-Muta'akhkhirin* he says that verbal proof gives certainty only when ten conditions are met, one being the absence of a rational objection. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 13}
- **Al-Ghazali**: in *Qanun al-Ta'wil* he counsels never to give the lie to rational demonstration, since it is by reason that we came to know revelation, gives examples of what must be interpreted, and then counsels refraining from fixing an interpretation when possibilities compete, holding suspension safer. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 14} {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 15}
- **Al-Juwayni**: in *al-Irshad* he divides the foundations of belief into what is known by reason only, what is known by revelation only, and what may be known by both. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 15}

## The philosophers
Ibn Taymiyya says these theologians are less extreme than the philosophers who claim that the prophets' texts do not match reality and were addressed to the common people in terms suited to their minds, such as the law [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]] set out in *al-Risala al-Adhawiyya*. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 11} The editor quotes Ibn Sina's view that pure tawhid, put to the masses as it is, would have met obstinate refusal, so the revealed laws addressed them through likeness and image; and [[person/ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd]] in *Manahij al-Adilla*, that the apparent sense is the duty of the masses and the interpreted sense the duty of scholars, and in *Fasl al-Maqal*, that where demonstration conflicts with the apparent sense of revelation, that sense admits of interpretation according to the Arabic rules of interpretation. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 17} {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 18} {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 19}

## Ibn Taymiyya's answer
After a general answer, Ibn Taymiyya says that asserting a conflict between rational and scriptural proof and insisting on preferring the rational is "known to be corrupt by necessity", then gives his detailed answer in aspects. The first: if the two conflicting proofs are certain, their conflict is not conceded as possible; if they are probable, the stronger is preferred absolutely; if one is certain, the certain one is preferred, and if the rational one is the certain one it is preferred for being certain, not for being rational. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 178} {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 179} The second: the division into four options is not exhaustive, since the rational may be preferred at one time and the scriptural at another according to certainty and strength. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 179} The third: the claim that "reason is the root of transmission", if it means that reason is the root of revelation's truth in itself, is something no rational person says, for what the Messenger reported is true in itself whether or not we know it; reason is only the root of our knowledge of its truth. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 179} {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 180} He takes the aspects to forty-four. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 19}

:::perspectives
**Ahl al-Sunna (Hanbali): Ibn Taymiyya.** Two certain proofs never conflict; the certain or stronger is preferred as such, not for being rational; reason is the root of our knowledge of revelation, not of its truth. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 178} {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 179} {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 180}

**Ahl al-Sunna (Ash'ari): al-Razi, and before him al-Ghazali and al-Juwayni.** Certain rational proof is preferred; the text is then interpreted or its meaning left to God. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 11} {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 12} {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 14}

**Philosophers: Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd, as the editor presents them.** The apparent sense of revelation addresses the masses and is interpreted where it conflicts with demonstration. {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 17} {cite:dar-taarud-salim-v1-1411 p. 18}
:::
