## Summary
While answering what [[person/al-allama-al-hilli|Ibn al-Mutahhar al-Hilli]] said about the killing of [[person/husayn-ibn-ali|al-Husayn]], [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] devoted a chapter of [[work/minhaj-al-sunnah|Minhaj al-Sunna]] to "people on Yazid: two extremes and a middle", setting out three positions on [[person/yazid-ibn-muawiya|Yazid ibn Mu'awiya]], and then returned to the accounts of the killing to sort what he considered established from what he considered fabricated. {cite:minhaj-sunna-shamela p. 1355} {cite:minhaj-sunna-shamela p. 1356}

## The two extremes
**The first extreme.** Some people believe, as Ibn Taymiyya relates, that Yazid was a Companion, one of the rightly guided caliphs, or a prophet; he judges all of this false. {cite:minhaj-sunna-shamela p. 1356}

**The second extreme.** Others believe he was a hidden unbeliever and hypocrite who meant to avenge his kinsmen among the pagans of Quraysh killed at Badr. They cite verses attributed to him, said when the heads of the slain came into view on the heights of Jayrun, and his quoting the poem of Ibn al-Ziba'ra in which the poet wishes his elders at Badr could witness what had happened. {cite:minhaj-sunna-shamela p. 1356}

## The middle, for Ibn Taymiyya
Ibn Taymiyya says that both views are false, as every rational person knows, and that the man was "one of the kings of the Muslims, a caliph of the kingly kind, neither this nor that". {cite:minhaj-sunna-shamela p. 1356} In the same place he states that al-Husayn was killed wrongfully, a martyr, and that killing him was disobedience to God and His Messenger on the part of whoever killed him, helped to kill him or was content with it. {cite:minhaj-sunna-shamela p. 1356}

## Yazid's responsibility for the killing
Ibn Taymiyya reports from several authorities that Yazid did not order al-Husayn's killing and had no aim in it; he preferred to honour him, as his father [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya]] had advised, though he wanted to keep him from rising against him. When the news reached him he was grieved and his household wept; he cursed "the son of Marjana", meaning [[person/ubayd-allah-ibn-ziyad|Ubayd Allah ibn Ziyad]], said he would have been content with the obedience of the people of Iraq without al-Husayn's death, and equipped al-Husayn's family well and sent them to Medina. {cite:minhaj-sunna-shamela p. 1361} Yet, Ibn Taymiyya states, Yazid "did not take al-Husayn's side, did not order his killer killed, and did not avenge him". {cite:minhaj-sunna-shamela p. 1361}

## Criticism of the reports
Ibn Taymiyya holds that those who related al-Husayn's death added falsehoods to it, as was done with the killing of Uthman and with the campaigns, and that even the scholars who wrote on the killing, such as al-Baghawi and Ibn Abi al-Dunya, transmit broken-chained reports. {cite:minhaj-sunna-shamela p. 1360} What is established, for him, in the Sahih is that al-Husayn's head was brought before Ubayd Allah ibn Ziyad, who struck at his front teeth with a rod while Anas ibn Malik and Abu Barza al-Aslami were present; the report that this happened before Yazid has an unknown chain, and he argues it is false because the Companions present at the striking were in Iraq, not Syria. {cite:minhaj-sunna-shamela p. 1360} He rejects the reports that the women of Banu Hashim were taken captive and paraded through the lands on camels without saddles, saying that Muslims never took a Hashimi woman captive. {cite:minhaj-sunna-shamela p. 1361}

**The position of reverence, as Ibn Taymiyya relates it.** Yazid was a Companion, a rightly guided caliph or a prophet; its holders are not named in this passage. {cite:minhaj-sunna-shamela p. 1356}

**The position of unbelief, as Ibn Taymiyya relates it.** Yazid was a hidden unbeliever seeking revenge for the dead of Badr, as shown by verses attributed to him and his quoting Ibn al-Ziba'ra. {cite:minhaj-sunna-shamela p. 1356}
