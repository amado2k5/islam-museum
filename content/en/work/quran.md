## Overview
The holy book of Islam. Muslims believe that it was sent down to the Prophet Muhammad. {cite:tanzil-quran}

It contains 114 suras and 6,236 verses, in the Hafs transmission, as in the Tanzil project text. {cite:tanzil-quran}

The museum does not write out the text of the verses on its pages. The verses are taken from the frozen Tanzil files, and the pages for individual verses refer to them.

## How the Encyclopaedia of Islam presents the book

The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, defines the Qur'an as the Muslim scripture, containing the revelations recited by [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] and preserved in a fixed, written form. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 438} The article is signed by A. T. Welch. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 466} This page reports what it says, and it keeps two kinds of account apart: the understanding held by Muslims, which comes first in each section, and the accounts of academic scholars, which are given as the views of the article and of the scholars it names. The museum states no verdict between them. The museum does not type the text of the book; it refers to suras by name or number only, as the article does.

## The name and its related terms

The article reports two scholarly explanations of the word qur'an. Most Western scholars, it says, derive it from a Syriac word for a scripture reading used in Christian liturgy, while the majority view among Muslim authorities is that it is simply the verbal noun of the Arabic verb meaning to read or recite. The author's own conclusion is that the term arose within the book itself, modelled on the Syriac word but built on an Arabic form from the verb "to recite". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 438}

Within the book the word has a range of meanings, according to the article: the act of reciting, an individual passage recited, and the revelation that God sends down upon Muhammad at intervals. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 439} The article also discusses the related terms [[concept/ayah|aya]], usually rendered sign or verse, and [[concept/surah|sura]], and says that later Muslim scholars took aya to mean verse. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 439} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 440}

## Revelation, as Muslims understand it

According to the article, the Muslim understanding is that the book consists of messages brought from God to Muhammad by [[person/jibril|Gabriel]], with God as the speaker, and so it is thought of as God's speech, kalam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 464} The article notes that the commentators identified the "spirit" named in several passages with Gabriel and placed him at the very start of Muhammad's ministry as the agent of revelation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 441} It also says that, in the commentators' reading, passages about a preserved tablet and a "mother of the Book" refer to a heavenly archetype of the Qur'an, and that the early discussions of whether the book was created did not mention them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 440} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 464}

The article's author adds his own academic reading here: that the verses usually cited for a heavenly original could equally be taken as metaphorical references to God's knowledge and decrees, and that the concept of an archetype was read into the text by later commentators. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 439} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 440} This is the author's view, and it differs from the commentators' reading just described.

## Collection and written text: the orthodox view

According to the article, the orthodox view is that the book was perfectly preserved in oral form from the beginning. It was written down during Muhammad's lifetime or shortly afterwards, when his Companions collected and arranged it for the first time. The complete consonantal text is believed to have been established in the reign of the third caliph, [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]], and the final vocalised text in the early fourth Islamic century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 442}

The most widely accepted account of a first collection, as the article summarises it from the hadith literature, places a written copy of the whole text in the reign of the first caliph, [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]]. After many reciters died at the battle of [[event/battle-of-yamama|al-Yamama]], [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar ibn al-Khattab]] urged that the text be written out so that none of it would be lost. Abu Bakr asked [[person/zayd-ibn-thabit|Zayd ibn Thabit]], one of the Prophet's secretaries, to gather it, and Zayd wrote it on sheets of equal size from fragments written on various materials and from what men kept in memory. The sheets passed to Umar and then to his daughter [[person/hafsa|Hafsa]], a widow of the Prophet. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 442}

The accounts of the work under Uthman say that the final consonantal text was established in the latter half of his reign, about twenty years after Muhammad's death. In the dominant version, a general named [[person/hudhayfa-ibn-al-yaman|Hudhayfa ibn al-Yaman]] reported a dispute among troops from Iraq and Syria over how to recite, and Uthman borrowed the sheets from Hafsa, appointed a commission that included Zayd, and had copies made. He kept one in Medina and sent others to Kufa, Basra, Damascus and, in some accounts, Mecca. The museum records [[event/uthman-standardizes-mushaf|the standardisation of the mushaf]] and the [[work/mushaf-uthman|Uthmanic codex]] on their own pages. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 443}

## The academic discussion of the collection accounts

The article states that the history of the text after the death of Muhammad in 632 is, in its author's words, still far from clear, and that three stages are hard to reconstruct and date: the collection and arrangement of the text, the establishment of the final consonantal text, and the acceptance of several readings as canonical. It adds that the classical literature records thousands of variants, none of which is found in the manuscripts known to Western scholars. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 442}

The author then gives his own assessment of the two collection stories. In his view, the story of Abu Bakr's collection is contradicted by other accounts in the canonical hadith collections, which name each of the first four caliphs as the first collector, and he judges the story unhistorical, suggesting that it served to obscure Muhammad's role in preparing a written text and to establish the priority of the Uthmanic text over the Companion codices. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 443} Of the account under Uthman he argues that it fails critical analysis in its details, for example that the book is not in the Quraysh dialect, but that most Western scholars accept its central point: that the Qur'an of today, in the number and order of its suras and the basic structure of its consonantal text, goes back to Uthman's time. The article adds that this text was certainly not fixed beyond all variation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 443}

The article names two scholars with other positions: John Burton holds that both stories are fiction and that the final text was produced by the Prophet himself, and John Wansbrough holds that the text was not compiled until the third Islamic century or later. The author judges that each makes a valid point but overstates the case, and that neither gives convincing reasons for rejecting the Muslim accounts altogether. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 443} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 444} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 445} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 446}

## The Prophet's part in the written book

On this the article reports the hadith that Muhammad dictated to scribes and told them how to arrange the revelations. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 441} The author concludes from this and from the book's own contents that Muhammad began to prepare a written scripture in his early years at Medina, directed the work, and left it unfinished because of the burden of leading a growing community. He also says that the Qur'an itself acknowledges changes to earlier revelations and gives three explanations for them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 441} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 442} These are the author's academic conclusions, drawn partly from the work of Richard Bell, and they are not the orthodox account given above.

## Companion codices and variant readings

The article says the Uthmanic text tradition was only one of several in the first four Islamic centuries, and that the sources speak of rival readings and of codices attributed to Companions. Ibn al-Nadim lists eleven works on the "disagreement of the codices". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 444}

The best known are the codices of [[person/abdullah-ibn-masud|Ibn Masud]], said to have prevailed in [[place/kufa|Kufa]], of [[person/ubayy-ibn-kab|Ubayy ibn Kab]], in Syria, and of [[person/abu-musa-al-ashari|Abu Musa al-Ashari]], in Basra. The article reports that Ibn Masud refused to destroy his copy when the Uthmanic text became official, and that many in Kufa followed his reading for a time. It also lists further Companions to whom codices are attributed, including [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] and [[person/abdullah-ibn-abbas|Ibn Abbas]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 444} The sources say fairly consistently that the codex of Ibn Masud lacked the first sura and the last two, although Ibn al-Nadim reported seeing an old copy of it that included the first. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 445}

On their value, the article says that Western scholarship has no consensus. Some scholars came to doubt the variants as they were gathered, one concluded that most were later philological emendations, and Burton and Wansbrough judged all such accounts to be later fabrications, each for a different reason. The author does not accept this and holds that a full study of the relation between the Seven or Ten readings and the other readings is still needed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 445} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 446} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 447}

## The canonical readings

The article describes the Arabic script of the first Islamic century as consonantal and without vowel signs or dots, which left room for different recitations of the same letters. It reports that the vowelled and dotted script came into use slowly, and it notes the popular view that the governor [[person/al-hajjaj-ibn-yusuf|al-Hajjaj]] introduced vowel signs and dots, and says that the Muslim accounts of this introduction vary and are unreliable. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 446}

By the early fourth Islamic century, the article says, [[person/ibn-mujahid|Ibn Mujahid]], who died in 324/936, limited the accepted readings to seven, one each for Medina, Mecca, Basra and Damascus and three for Kufa, relying on the saying that the book was revealed in seven ahruf. The meaning of that saying, the article notes, is uncertain. His view was made official in 322/934, when one scholar was forced to retract a contrary position, and a second scholar was condemned the following year. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 446} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 447} The seven were [[person/nafi-al-madani|Nafi]], [[person/ibn-kathir-al-makki|Ibn Kathir]], [[person/abu-amr-ibn-al-ala|Abu Amr]], [[person/ibn-amir-al-shami|Ibn Amir]], [[person/asim-ibn-abi-al-najud|Asim]], [[person/hamza-al-zayyat|Hamza]] and [[person/al-kisai|al-Kisai]]. Later scholars spoke of Ten and even Fourteen readings, adding among others [[person/al-hasan-al-basri|al-Hasan al-Basri]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 447} The museum has separate pages on [[concept/qiraat|the readings]] and on [[concept/tajwid|recitation]].

At the time of the article, two transmissions were in general use: that of Hafs from Asim, which became the basis of the Egyptian standard edition of 1924, and that of Warsh from Nafi, followed in parts of Africa other than Egypt. The article calls the Egyptian edition the best available but notes that it rests on oral tradition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 447}

## Structure: suras, verses and the opening formula

The article states that the book has 114 suras, divided into verses ranging in number from three to 286 or 287. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 447} The first, [[concept/al-fatiha-umm-al-kitab|al-Fatiha]], is a prayer, and the last two are the suras of taking refuge; the article says these serve as an introduction and a closing pair. After al-Fatiha the suras follow a rough order of descending length, beginning with The Cow, with some departures explained by groups of suras that share an opening or letters. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 447} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 448}

Muslim writers normally refer to suras by name rather than number. The article says the names were not fixed in the Prophet's lifetime and mostly come from a key word that identified the sura for those who knew it by heart, which, in the author's view, shows their origin in the oral tradition. The Egyptian standard edition fixed the usual names. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 448} Different Muslim editions number the verses differently, depending on whether the opening formula and the disconnected letters are counted. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 449}

All suras but one begin with [[concept/al-basmala|the basmala]], the formula that invokes God's name. The article reports that some Muslims believe it was part of the revelation at the head of each sura from the start, and the author gives a different, academic account, in which it came into use after a debate over divine names and was placed at the head of the suras only in their final written form. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 449} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 450}

In 29 suras, letters or groups of letters follow the formula, called the openers of the suras or the disconnected letters. The article reports that Muslim scholars have understood them as abbreviations, as sounds to attract attention, as symbols with numerical value, or as signs whose meaning is known only to God, the last being the conclusion of [[person/al-suyuti|al-Suyuti]]. Western scholars have proposed many theories, and the author concludes that the letters are probably part of the revelation and were recited as letters from the beginning. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 450} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 451} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 452}

## Chronology: the traditional and the academic dating

The article says the book responds to the Prophet's historical situation and that early Muslim scholars studied the question of the order of the text. It reports that, in the traditional Muslim view, the text is dated through the literature on [[concept/asbab-al-nuzul|the occasions of revelation]] and through the doctrine of [[concept/naskh|abrogation]], by which a later verse supersedes an earlier one. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 452} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 453} Suras are classed as Meccan or Medinan, and the Egyptian standard edition prints a chronological order, which goes back to Ibn Abbas, in the heading of each sura. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 454}

The article says that dating by historical events is possible mainly for the Medinan period, for example the battle of [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]] in the spring of 624, which the book mentions by name. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 453} Academic scholars have proposed several systems. The most widely adopted, associated with Gustav Weil, Theodor Noldeke and Regis Blachere, divides the Meccan suras into three periods and adds a Medinan one. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 454} The author judges that this scheme is little more than a European variation of the traditional dating and that its confidence is not justified. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 455}

He favours instead the approach of Richard Bell, who treated passages rather than whole suras as units and held that the revelations were revised under Muhammad's supervision. In this reading, most suras contain material of different dates, the suras cannot be put in order as wholes, and the main turning point in the book's development was not the migration to Medina but the events around Badr. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 455} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 456} This is the author's position, and the article itself says that Bell's work still needs a full critical study. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 456}

## Language

Most medieval Muslim scholars, the article reports, held that the book is in the Quraysh dialect, which they took to be the pure Arabic of the Bedouin. The author states that most Western Arabists now see its language as the shared poetic language of classical Arabic verse, with some adaptation to Meccan speech. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 457}

## The book in Muslim life and thought

For Muslims, the article says, the book is much more than scripture in the usual Western sense. Its primary significance for the great majority through the centuries has been in its oral form, the recitation first chanted by Muhammad to his followers over about twenty years. Parts were memorised by his followers during his lifetime, and the oral tradition has continued without a break, maintained by professional reciters, while all Muslims memorise parts for the daily prayer. In the author's account, the oral tradition became the standard by which the written text was judged, and even the Egyptian edition was prepared from it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 463} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 464}

The article traces the debate over whether the book is created. About the time of Harun al-Rashid, theologians began to discuss it. The [[group/mutazila|Mutazila]] held that it was created, and the caliph [[person/abd-allah-al-mamun|al-Mamun]] set up the [[event/mihna-of-the-quran|mihna]], an inquisition, in 218/833. Nearly all officials submitted, but [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]] and a few others refused, and the mihna was abandoned in 234/848. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 464} The article reports that Ibn Hanbal came to accept the description "uncreated", that [[person/al-ashari|al-Ashari]] and others argued for its eternity, and that this became the standard Sunni view. It adds that [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] disagreed with the formulation of eternity and argued that the early authorities who affirmed that the book was uncreated did not mean to assert its eternity. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 464} The matter is treated further in the page on [[concept/khalq-al-quran|the creation of the Qur'an]].

Alongside it arose the doctrine of inimitability, [[concept/ijaz-al-quran|ijaz]]. The article says the book had been seen from the start as a sign of Muhammad's prophethood, that early discussion centred on the challenge to produce its like, and that later authors such as [[person/al-baqillani|al-Baqillani]] stressed its style and its reports of past and future events. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 464}

The article lists the book's reach in Islamic civilisation: it became the first source of the Sharia, its grammar became the standard of later Arabic, its script was adopted for Persian, Turkish and Urdu, and its verses became the main subject of calligraphy and a principal motif of religious art and architecture. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 465} The author also gives his own opinion that these doctrines have made it hard for the modern Muslim community to accept historical and literary criticism of the book; the museum reports this as his view only. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 465}

## Translation

The article reports that the book was revealed expressly in Arabic and was not originally intended for non-Arabs. As the faith spread, jurists took a strict view: the Maliki, Shafii and Hanbali schools required the opening sura in Arabic in prayer, and the Hanafi school, citing [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]], allowed Persian for those unable to speak Arabic. Scholars held that a true translation was not possible because the wording is inimitable, but allowed a translation in the sense of a commentary printed beside the Arabic. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 467} It adds that a more conciliatory view later prevailed: the al-Azhar scholar Muhammad Mustafa al-Maraghi judged that a Muslim without Arabic should use a translation, holding that it carries the meaning of God's word although not its wording. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 467}

## For Explorers
The holy book of Islam. Muslims believe it is the word of God, sent down to the Prophet Muhammad. It has 114 suras. Muslims recite it in their prayers, and many memorise it by heart.

## For Scholars
The scripture of Islam, which Muslims believe was revealed to the Prophet Muhammad. It has 114 suras and 6,236 verses in the Hafs reading, as given in the Tanzil text. It was revealed in parts over about twenty-three years and gathered into one mushaf in the time of Uthman. The museum shows the verses only from the frozen Tanzil text, and never types the Qur'an in any other way. The sciences of the Qur'an, including commentary, recitation and its readings, have their own entries.
