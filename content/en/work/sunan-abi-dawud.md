## Summary
The Sunan is the book of [[person/abu-dawud-al-sijistani|Abu Dawud Sulayman ibn al-Ash'ath al-Sijistani]] (202–275 AH), one of the [[concept/kutub-al-sitta|six books]]. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud} {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 9} Its author arranged it by the chapters of law, confining himself to reports on rulings, and in his letter to the people of Mecca he set out his method in what he included and left out. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud} The publisher of the Dar Ibn Hazm edition calls it the soundest of the Sunan books and the third book of hadith after the two Sahihs of al-Bukhari and Muslim. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 3}

## How it was compiled
[[person/ibn-dasa|Ibn Dasa]] reports hearing Abu Dawud say that he wrote down five hundred thousand reports from the Prophet, selected from them what he put into the Sunan, and gathered there 4,800 reports, setting down "the sound, what resembles it and what comes near it". {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 10} The editors count 35 books and 5,274 reports, following the introduction of Muhammad Muhyi al-Din 'Abd al-Hamid. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 8} Sunnah.com gives 5,274 reports in 43 books in its own numbering. {cite:sunnah-com abudawud} The gap between the two figures (4,800 and 5,274) is the gap between the author's own count and the numbering of modern editions, which count repeated reports and chains.

## Structure, as seen in volume 1
- **Book of Purification**: its heading says it contains 143 chapters; it opens with the chapter on withdrawing to relieve oneself. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 15} {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 17} It holds chapters on the etiquette of the privy, [[practice/istinja|istinja']], [[practice/miswak|the tooth-stick]], water, [[practice/wudu|ablution]], [[practice/mash-ala-al-khuffayn|wiping over leather socks]], what breaks ablution, the full bath, [[concept/istihada|istihada]], [[practice/tayammum|tayammum]] and impurities. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 493-497}
- **Book of Prayer**: its heading says it contains 367 chapters and 1,165 reports. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 195} It opens with the obligation of prayer, then the times, mosques, [[practice/adhan|the call to prayer]], congregation, leading the prayer, dress in prayer, the form of the prayer and [[practice/sujud-al-sahw|the prostrations of forgetfulness]], then "the branches of the chapters on Friday", the two festival prayers, "the collected chapters on the rain prayer" and [[practice/salat-al-kusuf|the eclipse prayer]], with which volume 1 ends. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 197} {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 497-508}

## Abu Dawud's remarks
Abu Dawud follows many reports with short remarks introduced by "Abu Dawud said", noting how transmitters differ in chain and wording, who alone transmits a report, and who stops it at a Companion or drops a link. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 44-45}
- On the report of [[concept/qullatayn|the two qullas]] he judges the correct name to be "Muhammad ibn 'Abbad ibn Ja'far", and notes that Hammad ibn Zayd transmitted it from 'Asim as a Companion's saying. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 44-45}
- On the report of Ubayy ibn 'Imara about wiping over leather socks he says its chain is disputed and it is not strong. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 85}
- He relates that 'Abd al-Rahman ibn Mahdi would not transmit the report of wiping over woollen socks, because what is known from al-Mughira is wiping over leather socks. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 86}
- In the chapters on istihada he says that Qatada heard nothing from 'Urwa, and that Ibn 'Uyayna's addition "she leaves the prayer during her days of menstruation" is his error, absent from the report of the memorisers from al-Zuhri. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 140}

At times the book records the author's own inspection: in the chapter on [[place/bir-budaa|the well of Buda'a]] he says he asked its keeper about its depth and measured its width with his cloak, finding it six cubits. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 46}

## Its standing among scholars
The notice of the author in this edition gathers judgements on the book:
- [[person/ibn-al-arabi-ahmad|Abu Sa'id ibn al-A'rabi]], who taught the book, said — as [[person/abu-sulayman-al-khattabi|al-Khattabi]] relates — that a man who owned no book of learning but the Qur'an and then Abu Dawud's book would need nothing further. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 10-11}
- Al-Khattabi: "No book like it has been composed on the rulings of religion"; it became an arbiter between the groups of scholars and the ranks of jurists, and the people of Iraq, Egypt and the Maghrib rely on it. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 11}
- [[person/al-nawawi|Al-Nawawi]], in his commentary on the Sunan: whoever studies law should give it attention, since most of its reports are used as proof and it is easy to consult. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 11}

## Its transmission
The editors name [[person/ibn-dasa|Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr ibn 'Abd al-Razzaq ibn Dasa al-Tammar]] and [[person/abu-ali-al-lului|Abu 'Ali Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Lu'lu'i]] as the two through whom the book is transmitted from Abu Dawud. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 10} Among the authors of the other collections who transmitted from Abu Dawud were [[person/al-tirmidhi-muhammad-ibn-isa|al-Tirmidhi]] and [[person/al-nasai|al-Nasa'i]]. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 10}

## In the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The article of the Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, on [[person/abu-dawud-al-sijistani|Abu Dawud]] describes the Sunan as his principal work and one of the six canonical books of tradition accepted by Sunnis. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133} The article adds that he is said to have submitted it to [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]], who approved it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133} It also notes that the author was born in 202 AH, travelled widely in search of knowledge and settled at [[place/basra|Basra]]. According to the article, this led some to hold wrongly that his name al-Sijistani comes from a village near Basra, when it comes from the province of [[place/sijistan|Sijistan]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133} It gives his death as Shawwal 275 AH. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}

### Size and grades of the reports
The article repeats the account of [[person/ibn-dasa|Ibn Dasa]], that Abu Dawud said he drew 4,800 reports from a mass of 500,000, and that the book holds reports that are sound, reports that seem to be so, and reports that come near to it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133} It quotes a further saying of the author: where a report carries great weakness he has made it clear, and where he has said nothing the report is acceptable, some being sounder than others. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133} This refers to the notes he often adds to a report to give his view of its worth. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}

### Place in the criticism of hadith
The article observes that [[work/sahih-muslim|Muslim]] prefaced his Sahih with a discussion of general questions of criticism, but that Abu Dawud was the first to give such detailed notes on individual reports. In its view this prepared the way for the more systematic criticism found in the collection of his pupil [[person/al-tirmidhi-muhammad-ibn-isa|al-Tirmidhi]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133} It adds that Abu Dawud cites transmitters absent from the two Sahihs, his principle being that a transmitter counts as trustworthy unless there is formal proof against him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133} The work deals mainly with matters that the law commands, permits or forbids. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}

### Praise and criticism
The article records two judgements of praise. Abu Sa'id ibn al-A'rabi said that a man who knew only the Qur'an and this book would have sufficient knowledge, and Muhammad ibn Makhlad said that the traditionists accepted it without question, as they accepted the Qur'an. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133} The article finds it surprising that, although many in the fourth century AH praised the book, the Fihrist does not mention it and names Abu Dawud only as the father of his son. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133} It also reports later criticism: [[person/abd-al-azim-al-mundhiri|al-Mundhiri]], who made a summary of the Sunan, criticized some reports left without notes, and [[person/ibn-qayyim-al-jawziyya|Ibn al-Jawziyya]] added further criticisms. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133} The article concludes that, whatever faults have been found, the work keeps an honoured place. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}

### Transmission and related work
The article states that the Sunan was transmitted by several lines, some versions said to hold material absent from others, and that the version of [[person/abu-ali-al-lului|al-Lu'lu'i]] has found most favour. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133} It notes that many editions have been printed in the East. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133} It also mentions a small collection of mursal reports by Abu Dawud, the [[work/kitab-al-marasil|Kitab al-Marasil]], published in Cairo in 1310 AH/1892 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}

## Commentaries and editions
- Its oldest known commentary is [[work/maalim-al-sunan|Ma'alim al-Sunan]] by al-Khattabi (319–388 AH), printed in the footnotes of the Dar Ibn Hazm edition. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 1} {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 8}
- The book has been printed many times in India and Egypt; among the editions are the second edition of Muhammad Muhyi al-Din 'Abd al-Hamid (1950), with numbered chapters and reports, and the Nawalkishore press edition of Lucknow (1312 AH/1895 CE). {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 8}
- Others are the edition of al-Matba' al-Majidi in Kanpur (1375 AH) and of al-Maktaba al-Rahimiyya in Deoband. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud}
- The Dar Ibn Hazm edition (Beirut 1418 AH/1997 CE) is based on the edition of 'Izzat 'Ubayd al-Da''as and 'Adil al-Sayyid; the publisher set the Qur'anic verses in the 'Uthmani script, corrected misprints and numbered the chapters as in the Concordance of Hadith (al-Mu'jam al-Mufahras). {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v1 p. 2-3}

## The Book of the Sunna (volume 5)
In volume 5 of the Dar Ibn Hazm edition stands the Book of the Sunna, whose heading gives thirty-two chapters and 177 reports. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v5 p. 5} It opens with the explanation of the Sunna and the report of [[concept/al-iftiraq-al-ummah|the community's division into sects]], then the prohibition of disputation and of following the ambiguous verses, keeping apart from the people of whims, holding to the Sunna, precedence, the caliphs, the merit of the Companions and the prohibition of reviling them; then the refutation of irja' and [[question/does-faith-increase-and-decrease|the increase and decrease of faith]], predestination, [[group/jahmiyya|the Jahmiyya]], the vision of God, the Qur'an, intercession, the resurrection, the creation of Paradise and the Fire, [[concept/al-hawd-al-nabawi|the Basin]], [[concept/adhab-al-qabr|the questioning in the grave]], the Balance and the Dajjal, [[group/khawarij|the Kharijites]] and fighting robbers. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v5 p. 7-83} The Book of Manners follows. {cite:sunan-abi-dawud-ibn-hazm-1997-v5 p. 87}
