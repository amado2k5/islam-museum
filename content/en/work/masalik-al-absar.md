## Summary
*Masalik al-absar fi mamalik al-amsar* ("Paths of Vision through the Realms of the Cities") is a geographical encyclopedia by [[person/ibn-fadl-allah-al-umari|Ibn Fadl Allah al-Umari]]. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.11-14} Its author divides it into two parts, on the earth and on the earth's inhabitants, {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.46} the first part being of two kinds, routes (*masalik*) and realms (*mamalik*). {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.47} The first part covers the earth: its measure, climes, seas, qibla, roads and the realms of its cities; the second its inhabitants: nations, religions, devotees and dynasties before and under Islam. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.11-14}

## The author's plan
Al-Umari says he took pains to correct what he wrote as far as he could, without exhausting the subject or padding it; that he dealt only with the great realms of the inhabited world, keeping to the lands under Muslim rule and mentioning non-Muslim realms only in passing or as neighbours, promising to add them in an appendix if he lived long enough. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.44} {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.45} He says he reported no marvel without investigating it and no oddity without naming its source, so that responsibility lay with that source; he starts in the east and ends at the far west on the Encircling Ocean, except for the climes, whose longitudes are counted from the Eternal Isles. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.45} He began it under Sultan al-Malik al-Nasir Abu al-Ma'ali Muhammad ibn Qalawun, whose titles he lists at length, and says: "I named it Masalik al-absar fi mamalik al-amsar". {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.45} {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.46}

## Contents of volume one
Volume one opens with a chapter on the measure and state of the earth in six sections: its sphericity and the proof of it; the dimensions given by the Indians, Hermes, Eratosthenes and Ptolemy; al-Ma'mun's practical survey of the earth's dimensions, whose result the author prefers; Ibn al-Shatir of Damascus's correction of Ptolemy; the inhabited part; then the words for earth, dust, dust-clouds and sand; then mountains and rivers. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.13} {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.14} {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.15} It then describes the holy places: the Ka'ba and its covering, the Masjid al-Haram and the boundary markers of the sanctuary, the Prophet's Mosque and the mosques of Medina, al-Masjid al-Aqsa and the Rock, the sanctuary of Hebron, and the Umayyad Mosque of Damascus. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.18} {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.20} {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.21} {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.23}

## The author as witness
Al-Umari mixes quotation and observation: on the [[object/kiswa|kiswa of the Ka'ba]] he quotes al-Azraqi, Ibn Ishaq and others, then says that on his pilgrimage of 738 AH he went up on the roof of the Ka'ba with the commanders of the Egyptian caravan and with his own hands took off the old covering and put on the new. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.137} {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.138} For the [[building/dome-of-the-rock|Dome of the Rock]] and [[building/al-aqsa-mosque|al-Masjid al-Aqsa]] he describes the mosque "as its building stood in 743 AH", quoting measurements in cubits from *Silsilat al-asjad fi sifat al-sakhra wal-masjid* by al-Sahib Taj al-Din Ahmad ibn Amin al-Mulk. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.177} {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.178}

## The edition read
Volume one of [[person/ahmad-zaki-pasha|Ahmad Zaki Pasha]]'s edition, in the series *Ihya' al-adab al-arabiyya*, printed by the Dar al-Kutub al-Misriyya press in 1924, was read. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.3} The editor says the book was used by the great scholars of the East and later by orientalists such as Quatremère and Amari; that he searched for it for a quarter of a century in the libraries of Europe and Constantinople and found the first volume, given up as lost, in the Topkapi palace under the title "Mir'at al-ka'inat", with corrections and additions in the author's hand. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.9} He says he travelled to Palestine to check the author's architectural account of al-Aqsa on the spot, and that the book was the main source of al-Qalqashandi's *Subh al-a'sha*. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.10}
