## Summary
*Sharh Usul I'tiqad Ahl al-Sunna wa-al-Jama'a min al-Kitab wa-al-Sunna wa-Ijma' al-Sahaba wa-al-Tabi'in wa-man ba'dahum* ("Exposition of the Principles of the Creed of Ahl al-Sunna wa-al-Jama'a from the Book, the Sunna and the Consensus of the Companions, the Successors and Those After Them") is a narrated book on the creed of the hadith scholars. Its author is the Shafi'i hadith master Abu al-Qasim Hibat Allah ibn al-Hasan [[person/al-lalakai|al-Lalaka'i]], and according to the chain at its head he dictated it in Baghdad in Rabi' I 416 AH. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 31}

## Why it was written and what it aims at
Al-Lalaka'i says that people of learning asked him again and again to expound "the creed of the schools of the hadith scholars", and he agreed because he saw benefit in it. This was especially so, he says, in an age whose scholars had forgotten the marks of the Sunni way and busied themselves with newly invented sciences. He wanted to renew this way so that its meanings and proofs would be known. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 44} He opens the book by saying that the first duty of a person is to know the creed of the religion. The greatest proofs, in his view, are God's Book, then the words of the Messenger and his Companions, then what the early generations agreed on. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 32}

## Method
Al-Lalaka'i says that before starting he went through most of the books of earlier imams. He then set out the disputed questions one by one, stating under each when the dispute arose and who started it, so that it would be known that the doctrine had no root among the first generation. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 45} He then argues from the Qur'an and hadith. Failing those he uses the sayings of the Companions, and failing those the sayings of the Successors, and he relies on what they agreed on. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 45} At the start of the book he names those from whom this way is to be taken, so that they may be known and prayed for. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 45} His chapters are headed "the account of what is narrated on...", and he narrates hadiths and reports in them with his own chains. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1600}

## His view of his opponents
In his introduction he praises the hadith scholars as those through whom the principles of the Sunna were preserved. Every school, he says, is named after whoever originated it, except the hadith scholars, whose master is the Messenger. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 42} He attacks the [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazila]], naming al-Nazzam, al-Allaf, al-Jubba'i and his son. He rejects their charge that Sunnis are blind imitators and literalists, and holds that the doctrines of innovators prevailed only with the backing of a coercive ruler. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 34} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 35} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 36} These are the author's judgements, which the museum presents as his.

## Contents
The editor lists the book's subjects:
- the people marked out as imams of the Sunna after the Messenger;
- holding to the Book and the Sunna;
- the names and attributes of God;
- the Qur'an;
- predestination;
- prophethood;
- faith;
- what comes after death and the signs of the Hour;
- the virtues of the Companions;
- the miracles of the saints. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 16}

Chapters in its index include the following:
- that the name and the named are one;
- the unbelief of whoever suspends judgement on the Qur'an;
- the believers' vision of their Lord, in the narrations of several Companions;
- the prohibition on reflecting on God's essence;
- predestination as the first polytheism to appear in Islam;
- the Qadariyya's claim that God did not create human acts;
- the Prophet's virtues and miracles;
- obedience to imams and rulers;
- the order of the caliphates of Abu Bakr and then Uthman. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1601} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1603} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1606} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1608} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1611} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1612} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1613} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1618} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1619}

## The Qur'an as God's speech
One of its best-known passages lists the names of those who said the Qur'an is God's speech and uncreated. He then says that these are 550 or more Successors, their followers and imams, not counting the Companions, among them about a hundred imams, and that he dropped the chains for brevity. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 256} He states that the community agrees that the first to say the Qur'an is created was [[person/jad-ibn-dirham|al-Ja'd ibn Dirham]], "in the years of twenty-odd", as he puts it (without naming the century), and then [[person/jahm-ibn-safwan|al-Jahm ibn Safwan]]. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 256} See [[concept/khalq-al-quran|the createdness of the Qur'an]].

## The miracles of the saints
The book closes with *Karamat Awliya' Allah*. It has chapters on the miracles of al-Husayn ibn Ali, Abd Allah ibn Umar and Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr, of the Successors of Mecca and Syria, and of the people of Baghdad such as Ma'ruf al-Karkhi and Bishr al-Hafi. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1621} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1624} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1625} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1626}

## The edition in the museum
This is a complete text with hadith references and notes by Abu Ya'qub Nash'at ibn Kamal al-Misri, prepared under the Dar al-Basira office in Alexandria together with Dar al-Athar in Sana'a. It opens with the editor's introduction, dated Cairo, 14 Jumada II 1422 AH (2 September 2001), and a life of the author. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 1} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 2} {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 16} The editor notes a manuscript abridgement by an unknown author, *Kashif al-Ghumma*, which drops the chains and keeps the texts. {cite:sharh-usul-itiqad-basira p. 21}

## Not covered here
The book's chapters themselves have not yet been read, apart from the passage on the Qur'an. The Ash'ari and Maturidi positions on God's speech, and historians' readings of the inquisition over the created Qur'an, need their own sources.
