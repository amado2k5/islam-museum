## Overview
Talbis Iblis ("The Devil's Delusion") is a book by the hadith master Abu al-Faraj 'Abd al-Rahman [[person/ibn-al-jawzi|Ibn al-Jawzi]] of Baghdad, who died in 597 AH according to the title page of the Muniriyya printing. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 5} In his preface he says that after the generation of the Companions passions again began to produce innovations, most people split their religion into factions, and the devil rose "to dress up and embellish", which only works for him "in the night of ignorance"; so he resolved to warn of the devil's plots and point out his snares, since knowing evil is a warning against falling into it, and he cites the hadith of Hudhayfa, who asked about evil for fear it would overtake him. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 8} He then says he composed the book to warn of the devil's trials and uncover what is hidden, and divided it into thirteen chapters "which together uncover his deception". {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 8}

## The chapters
The author lists the chapter headings at the start of the book; in the Muniriyya printing they fall on these pages: {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 9}

- Chapter 1: on the command to hold to the Sunna and the community. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 9}
- Chapter 2: on the censure of innovations and innovators. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 15}
- Chapter 3: a warning against the devil's trials and plots. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 27}
- Chapter 4: on the meaning of talbis (deception) and ghurur (delusion). {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 41}
- Chapter 5: his deception in creeds and religions; according to the edition's table it treats the sophists, the dahriyya (eternalists), the naturalists, the dualists and the philosophers, worshippers of idols, fire and stars, the Jahiliyya, deniers of prophecy, Jews, Christians, Sabians and Magians, astrologers, deniers of resurrection and believers in transmigration, and then the sects of this community, among them the Batiniyya and their branches. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 43} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 409} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 410}
- Chapter 6: his deception of scholars in the branches of learning: Qur'an reciters, hadith scholars, jurists, preachers and storytellers, philologists and men of letters, and poets. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 116} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 411}
- Chapter 7: his deception of governors and sultans. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 135}
- Chapter 8: his deception of worshippers in acts of worship: purification, the call to prayer, prayer, Qur'an recitation, fasting, pilgrimage, campaigning, and commanding right. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 138} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 411} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 412}
- Chapter 9: his deception of ascetics. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 154}
- Chapter 10: his deception of the Sufis, the longest chapter, running from page 164 to just before chapter 11 on page 381. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 164} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 381}
- Chapter 11: his deception of the devout through things resembling marvels (karamat). {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 381}
- Chapter 12: his deception of the common people. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 391}
- Chapter 13: his deception of all people through long hope; he closes by saying that whoever pays attention to himself knows he stands "in a line of battle" and that his enemy never slackens. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 406} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 407}

## The chapter on the Sufis: how he presents it
Ibn al-Jawzi opens the chapter by saying that the Sufis are "among the ascetics", but set themselves apart with particular traits and states, so he had to treat them separately; Sufism, he says, was a way that began in complete renunciation, after which its adherents allowed themselves audition (sama') and dancing. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 164} He then discusses the origin of the name: in the Prophet's time people were called after faith and Islam, then the names "ascetic" and "worshipper" arose, and then a group took their name from a man called "Sufa", al-Ghawth ibn Murr, who had devoted himself to serving the Sacred House; he supports this with reports from 'Abd al-Ghani ibn Sa'id, al-Zubayr ibn Bakkar and Hisham al-Kalbi. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 165} He then presents the view of those who traced them to the [[concept/ahl-al-suffa|People of the Bench (ahl al-suffa)]]. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 166}

Ibn al-Jawzi does not condemn the first Sufis wholesale. He quotes [[person/al-junayd-ibn-muhammad|al-Junayd]]'s definition of Sufism as leaving every base trait and entering every noble one, and Ruwaym's saying that this group demanded of themselves the reality of scrupulousness and constant truthfulness, and then says: "This is how the first of them were"; the devil's deception of them, in his account, grew century after century until it took full hold of the later ones. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 167} He traces its root to the devil turning them away from knowledge and persuading them that action was what mattered, and he describes some of the early figures as people whose "aims were good, though they were off the main road". {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 167}

## His critique of the Sufi books
Ibn al-Jawzi goes through the books composed for the Sufis one by one. This is among the most useful parts of the book for the history of Sufi writing; the judgements are his own: {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 168} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 169}

- [[person/al-sulami|Abu 'Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami]] compiled a "Book of Sunan" for them and gathered the [[work/haqaiq-al-tafsir|Haqa'iq al-Tafsir]], in which he reported their interpretation of the Qur'an by what occurred to them, without grounding it in any principle of learning; Ibn al-Jawzi quotes, through al-Khatib al-Baghdadi, Muhammad ibn Yusuf al-Qattan's statement that al-Sulami was unreliable and fabricated hadith for the Sufis. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 168} The charge and the replies to it are set out in [[question/reliability-of-al-sulami|the card on al-Sulami's standing in hadith]].
- [[person/abu-nasr-al-sarraj|Abu Nasr al-Sarraj]] wrote [[work/kitab-al-luma-fi-al-tasawwuf|Luma' al-Sufiyya]], which, Ibn al-Jawzi says, contains "ugly belief and despicable speech" of which he will quote samples; in fact he quotes it at length in the chapter. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 168} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 173} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 174}
- [[person/abu-talib-al-makki|Abu Talib al-Makki]] wrote [[work/qut-al-qulub|Qut al-Qulub]]; Ibn al-Jawzi faults it for false hadith and fabricated prayers for particular days and nights, and for reporting from some Sufis that God manifests himself in this world to his friends. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 168}
- [[person/abu-nuaym-al-asbahani-ahmad|Abu Nu'aym al-Isfahani]] wrote [[work/hilyat-al-awliya|Hilyat al-Awliya']]; Ibn al-Jawzi objects that he counted among the Sufis Abu Bakr, 'Umar, 'Uthman, 'Ali and the leading Companions, as well as Shurayh, al-Hasan al-Basri, Sufyan al-Thawri and Ahmad ibn Hanbal, just as al-Sulami in [[work/tabaqat-al-sufiyya-sulami|Tabaqat al-Sufiyya]] counted al-Fudayl, Ibrahim ibn Adham and Ma'ruf al-Karkhi. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 168} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 169}
- [[person/al-qushayri-abu-l-qasim|'Abd al-Karim ibn Hawazin al-Qushayri]] wrote [[work/al-risala-al-qushayriyya|the Risala]]; Ibn al-Jawzi criticises its discussion of annihilation and subsistence, contraction and expansion, the moment and the state, ecstasy, union and separation, sobriety and intoxication and similar terms. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 169}
- Muhammad ibn Tahir al-Maqdisi wrote Safwat al-Tasawwuf, which, Ibn al-Jawzi says, contains things "a sensible man would be ashamed to mention". {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 169}

From this Ibn al-Jawzi concludes that Sufism is "a known school that goes beyond asceticism"; his evidence is that no one ever censured asceticism, while Sufism has been censured. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 169} He explains the number of these books by their authors' little knowledge of the sunan and the reports, and by the fact that their way "outwardly is cleanliness and devotion, and inwardly ease and audition"; he notes that the early Sufis shunned sultans and emirs "and then became their friends". {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 169} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 170}

## The early authorities and talk of passing thoughts
As evidence of the early authorities' reserve towards "inner knowledge", Ibn al-Jawzi reports that [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]], asked about whisperings and passing thoughts, said that neither the Companions nor the Successors spoke of them; that he advised a companion not to sit with [[person/al-harith-al-muhasibi|al-Harith al-Muhasibi]] and his circle; and that [[person/abu-zura-al-razi|Abu Zur'a al-Razi]] warned against al-Muhasibi's books and said: "Hold to the reports." {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 170} He quotes al-Sulami that [[person/dhu-al-nun-al-misri|Dhu al-Nun al-Misri]] was the first in his town to speak of the ordering of states and the stations of the friends of God, and that 'Abd Allah ibn 'Abd al-Hakam objected and the scholars of Egypt shunned him. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 170}

## Indwelling and ecstatic sayings
In a section on "what is reported of the group's bad belief", Ibn al-Jawzi gathers reports, most of them taken from al-Sarraj: that [[person/muhammad-ibn-ibrahim-abu-hamza-al-sufi|Abu Hamza]] was accused of believing in indwelling (hulul) because he would say "Labbayk, labbayk" on hearing a crow's cry or the blowing of the wind; that scholars objected to phrases of Abu Sa'id al-Kharraz in his Kitab al-Sirr; that Ibn 'Ata' and al-Junayd were accused of unbelief and heresy; and that Ghulam Khalil testified that Abu al-Husayn al-Nuri had said "I love God passionately ('ishq) and He loves me passionately". {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 173} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 174} Ibn al-Jawzi rejects that phrase on three grounds: language; the fact that God's attributes are transmitted, so one says He loves (yuhibb) and not that He loves passionately (ya'shaq); and the claim that God loves the speaker is a claim without proof. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 174}

He then gives reports about [[person/al-hallaj|al-Husayn ibn Mansur al-Hallaj]] and concludes: "The scholars of the age agreed that al-Hallaj's blood was lawful"; the first to say so was Abu 'Umar the judge, the scholars agreed with him, and only Abu al-'Abbas ibn Surayj held back, saying: "I do not know what he is saying." {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 175} He says he compiled a book on al-Hallaj's reports setting out "his tricks and deceptions". {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 176} The ecstatic sayings of [[person/bayazid-al-bistami|Abu Yazid al-Bistami]] are presented in the section on ecstatic utterances and claims; his position on them is set out in [[question/shath-abu-yazid|the card on Abu Yazid's "Glory be to me"]]. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 347} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 348}

## Other subjects in the Sufi chapter
The chapter is made of sections headed "the devil's deception of the Sufis in…", among them: purification, prayer, audition, dancing and ecstasy, ecstasy on its own, the company of youths, the claim of reliance on God, abandoning medical treatment, journeys and wandering, entering the desert without provisions, returning from travel, neglecting learning, objecting to those who pursue learning, their talk about knowledge, then ecstatic sayings and claims, and interpretation that departs from the plain sense of the Qur'an; each he presents with its chains of transmission and then comments on with the words "the author says". {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 177} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 178} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 226} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 254} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 268} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 282} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 291} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 301} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 303} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 321} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 324} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 332} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 334} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 347} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 350}

## Method
Ibn al-Jawzi gives most of his material with chains of transmission from his teachers, such as Muhammad ibn Nasir and Abu Mansur al-Qazzaz, and then adds "the author says" where he states his own view; he uses the Sufis' own books, such as al-Sarraj's Luma' and al-Sulami's works, as sources for his critique. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 168} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 173} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 347} He writes as a Baghdadi Hanbali preacher and hadith master defending what he calls the Sunna and the community, as the title of his first chapter shows. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 9}

## Editions present
- The second Muniriyya printing, corrected and annotated by Muhammad Munir 'Abduh Agha al-Dimashqi al-Azhari in 1347 AH and printed by Idarat al-Tiba'a al-Muniriyya in Egypt, with 1928 on the title page. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 5} In his closing note the corrector says that when he first printed it he had two manuscripts of different dates, and for the second printing he found a third, so that it came out "far better than the one before". {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 407} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 408} His footnotes are few, and some connect the author's words to customs of the corrector's own time, such as his objection to vowing sheep and cattle to saints. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 8}
- A critical edition of 1,571 pages, which collates the base copy with two manuscripts marked «أ» and «ت», documents the hadith, explains rare words and closes with ten indexes; its title page and introduction are not in the scan, so its editor and publisher are unknown. {cite:talbis-iblis-critical-noor p. 1} {cite:talbis-iblis-critical-noor p. 2} {cite:talbis-iblis-critical-noor p. 1571}

## Its place
The book is a critique of religious practice written by a Hanbali hadith master from within the Sunni tradition; because Ibn al-Jawzi quotes the Sufis' own books with chains of transmission and names them, his quotations remain a witness to what of them was in circulation in his time. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 168} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 169} The judgements on people and books on this page are Ibn al-Jawzi's, as his book reports them, not the museum's.
