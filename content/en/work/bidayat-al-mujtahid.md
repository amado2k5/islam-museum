## Summary

Bidayat al-Mujtahid wa-Nihayat al-Muqtasid is a work of law by the judge Abu al-Walid [[person/ibn-rushd|Muhammad ibn Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn Rushd of Cordoba]], known as Ibn Rushd al-Hafid ("the grandson", 520-595 AH). {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 1} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 6} Al-Zirikli lists it among his roughly fifty works. {cite:al-aalam p. 849} It covers every chapter of law, from purity in the acts of worship to judgeships, and does not confine itself to the [[group/maliki-madhhab|Maliki school]] to which he was known to belong, but states each question on all the recognised views from the time of the Companions onwards. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 6}

## The author's purpose

Ibn Rushd says at the start that his aim is to set down for himself, "by way of a reminder", the legal questions agreed and disputed with their evidence, pointing out the crux of each disagreement, so far as they serve as principles and rules for the questions on which the law is silent and which may come before the mujtahid. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 17} He defines his material as, for the most part, the questions the law states expressly or that are closely tied to what it states, on which there was agreement or a well-known disagreement among the jurists from the Companions until imitation (taqlid) spread. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 17}

## The legal-theory introduction

Ibn Rushd prefaces the book with a short introduction on three things: the kinds of channel through which rulings are received, the kinds of ruling, and the kinds of cause of disagreement. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 17}

- **Channels**: in genus there are three - word, act and tacit approval. For what the Lawgiver left unaddressed, the majority hold that the way to it is [[concept/qiyas|analogy]]; the Zahiris hold that analogy in the law is void and that what the Lawgiver left unaddressed has no ruling. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 17}
- **Kinds of wording**: a general term taken as general, a specific term taken as specific, a general term meaning the specific, and a specific term meaning the general - which includes pointing from the greater to the lesser and the reverse; the fourth, disputed, is "the implication of the address" (dalil al-khitab), illustrated by the hadith on zakat on grazing sheep. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 17} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 18}
- **Consensus**: he holds that it rests on one of these channels; when it occurs on one of them that is not certain, it moves the ruling from probability to certainty, but it is not an independent source in itself. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 26}
- **The five rulings**: obligatory, recommended, forbidden, disliked and permitted. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 26}
- **The six causes of disagreement**: a wording hesitating between the general and the specific or having an implied contrary meaning; ambiguity in a single word such as qur' (which means both purity periods and menses) or in a compound phrase; differences of grammatical case; hesitation between the literal and a figurative sense; a term stated absolutely in one place and qualified in another; and conflict among words, acts, tacit approvals and analogies. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 26} {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 27}

## Structure

The book is divided into "books" (kutub), each divided into chapters that set out "the comprehensive account of the principles" of the act of worship or contract, and then into questions; the book of ablution, for example, has five chapters: the evidence that it is obligatory, its acts, what it is done with, what invalidates it, and what it is done for. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 30} The Bayt al-Afkar edition numbers the books, reaching sixty-eight: the first is Purity from Ritual Impurity (p. 21); among them zakat (p. 282), hajj (p. 364), jihad (p. 425), hunting (p. 502), the aqiqa (p. 512), marriage (p. 528), divorce (p. 588), sales (p. 651), inheritance (p. 870), crimes (p. 926) and brigandage (p. 987); the last is Judgeships (p. 993). {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 7} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 21} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 993}

## His method in a question

Ibn Rushd gives the views on a question, usually starting with Malik, then Abu Hanifa, al-Shafi'i and Ahmad, often calling the first three "the majority"; he adds the views of independent jurists within the schools and those of the [[group/zahiri-madhhab|Zahiris]], [[person/al-awzai-abd-al-rahman|al-Awza'i]], [[person/sufyan-al-thawri|al-Thawri]], [[person/abu-thawr|Abu Thawr]] and others; then the basis of each view, then "the cause of their disagreement", then discussion and preference. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 6} An example is intention in ablution: he names those who made it a condition and those who did not, then traces the disagreement to ablution hesitating between pure worship and worship whose rationale is understood. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 22} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 23} The editor of the Hallaq edition notes Ibn Rushd's terms: when he calls a hadith "established" (thabit) he means one in al-Bukhari or Muslim; by "the majority" he counts Malik, al-Shafi'i and Abu Hanifa; and he calls hadiths "athar". {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 20} He attributes his own preferences to himself with the words "the Judge said". {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 15}

## His sources

At the end of the book on purity from filth, Ibn Rushd says that what he relied on most in ascribing the views to their holders was al-Istidhkar by [[person/ibn-abd-al-barr|Ibn Abd al-Barr]], and that he allows anyone who finds a slip of his to correct it. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 111} The editor of the Bayt al-Afkar edition also names among his sources al-Khattabi's [[work/maalim-al-sunan|Ma'alim al-Sunan]] and al-Baji's [[work/al-muntaqa-sharh-al-muwatta|al-Muntaqa]]. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 15} He often reports from Ibn al-Mundhir and quotes the [[work/al-mudawwana-al-kubra|Mudawwana]]. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 72} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 246}

## Medicine and astronomy

The editor of the Bayt al-Afkar edition notes that Ibn Rushd draws on other sciences in law. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 15} On the blood a pregnant woman sees, he says it is sometimes menstrual blood, when the woman's strength is ample and the foetus small, as Hippocrates, Galen and the other physicians report, and sometimes the blood of illness. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 71} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 72} On sighting the new moon by day, he says that reasoning and experience show that the moon is not seen while the sun has not set, with no difference between before and after noon. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 327}

## The ending

Ibn Rushd closes the book of judgeships by noting that legal rulings are of two kinds: those judges enforce, which is most of the book, and those judges do not enforce, mostly recommended acts such as returning a greeting and blessing one who sneezes, which jurists put in the "collections" (jawami') at the end of their books. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 1008} He then states that the purpose of the practical sunan is the virtues of the soul, and distributes them among honouring those who must be honoured, temperance in food, drink and marriage, justice in property and persons, generosity, and the norms of social life, leadership and commanding right and forbidding wrong. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 1008} He ends by saying that with the book of judgeships "the whole register" is complete. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 1008}

## Its standing as the editors describe it

The editor of the Bayt al-Afkar edition calls the book "the fruit" of Ibn Rushd's writing and lists among its merits comprehensiveness, concision, good arrangement, avoidance of far-fetched ramifications, evidence for every question, and rejection of views clashing with a text or consensus, which he labels as anomalous; he takes this from the introduction of Salih ibn Ali al-Shamrani's al-Aqwal al-Shadhdha fi Bidayat al-Mujtahid. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 14} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 15} The same editor faults it for often neglecting the school of [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad]]. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 14} The editor of the Hallaq edition quotes al-Maqqari: Ibn Rushd brought Malik's school closer than anyone before him. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 14} He says Ibn Rushd sets out a question on Malik's view, then on the views of the recognised jurists, and then prefers what he thinks right. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 11}
