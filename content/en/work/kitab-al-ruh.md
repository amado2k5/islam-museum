# Kitab al-Ruh

## Summary
Kitab al-Ruh ("The Book of the Soul") is a treatise by Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya (691–751 AH) on the nature of the soul and its states after death. It is set out in twenty-one questions, from whether the dead know the visits of the living to whether the self is one or three. The edition's opening text describes it as containing "twenty-one questions on the soul and what is connected with it"; the editor notes that this preface was taken from the author's Tuhfat al-Mawdud and added to the book by some of its copyists, since they found it without one {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 4}. The copy read here is the third edition of Dar Atta'at al-Ilm (1440 AH / 2019 CE), which reissues the first edition of Dar Ibn Hazm {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 2}. The edition is edited by Muhammad Ajmal Ayyub al-Islahi and Kamal ibn Muhammad Qalmi. The opinions below are the author's unless marked as the editor's.

## How the book is built
The treatise opens with a short preface, and each question is numbered and titled. A question usually sets out the problem, then the reports, proofs and opposing views, then the author's answer. Some questions have separate sections inside them. The first question includes sections on Abd al-Haqq al-Ishbili's account of the dead being asked about the living, on the instruction of the dead in the grave, on the stories of al-Sa'b ibn Jaththama and Thabit ibn Qays ibn al-Shammas, whose wishes after death were carried out as shown in dreams, and on the ruling on circumstantial evidence (al-lawth) in property and blood {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 870}. Within the nineteenth and twentieth questions the author answers a series of objections, numbered from the fourteenth to the twenty-second, each in its own section, to the claim that the soul is a body {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 880}.

## Questions one to six: the dead, the living and the soul
**Question one** (p. 5): do the dead know the visits and greetings of the living? The author opens with a report from Ibn Abd al-Barr about a dead man who knows the one who passes by his grave and greets him. The editor's notes show that the report is not established, neither as a saying of the Prophet nor as a saying of a Companion, and that it is munkar, as Ibn Rajab said {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 6} {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 7}. The author then cites the account in the two Sahihs of the Prophet addressing the dead of Badr, and a chapter of Ibn Abi al-Dunya's Kitab al-Qubur on the dead knowing the visits of the living. He states that the early scholars agreed on this point and that reports to that effect are many {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 7} {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 8}. The editor notes that the printed Kitab al-Qubur is incomplete and lacks some of the reports the author quotes {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 8}.

**Question two** (p. 44): do the spirits of the dead meet, visit one another and talk? The answer divides spirits into two kinds: tormented and blessed. The tormented are occupied with their torment and cannot visit. The blessed spirits that are released and not held back meet, visit and discuss what happened in the world and what is happening in it. The author supports this with a verse about obeying God and the Messenger being with those whom God has blessed, and with a report from the Companions about not wanting to part from the Prophet {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 44} {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 45}.

**Question three** (p. 56): do the spirits of the living and of the dead meet? The author says the evidence is more than can be counted. He cites a verse on God taking souls at death and in sleep, and a report from Ibn Abbas, transmitted through Abu Abd Allah ibn Mandah, that the spirits of the living and the dead meet in sleep, talk, and that God keeps back the spirits of the dead {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 56}. The index shows that the question goes on to the nature and kinds of dreams, the disagreement about them, and the benefit people derive from dreams {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 870}.

**Question four** (p. 97): does the spirit die, or does death belong to the body alone? The author sets out two positions. One holds that the spirit tastes death, since every soul tastes death. The other holds that spirits were created to last, and that only the body dies, citing reports on the bliss and torment of spirits after separation until God returns them to their bodies {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 97}. The editor notes that Ibn Abi al-Izz summarised this question in his commentary on al-Tahawi's creed {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 97}.

**Question five** (p. 107): once spirits are freed from their bodies, by what means do they tell one another apart and recognise one another, and do they take the shape of their bodies? The author says this question is rarely discussed. Those who hold that the spirit is free of matter, or that it is an accident of the body, have no answer to it on their own principles {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 107}. The editor refers the reader to question nineteen for the nature of the spirit {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 107}.

**Question six** (p. 115): is the spirit returned to the dead person in his grave at the time of questioning? The author holds that the long report of al-Bara ibn Azib at a burial already settles the matter, since it states that the spirit is returned {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 115}.

## Questions seven to fourteen: the grave and its states
**Question seven** (p. 181): the answer to materialists and heretics who deny the punishment of the grave, its wideness and narrowness, whether it is a pit of the Fire or a garden of Paradise, and whether the dead sit up in it. The question begins with their argument: when they open a grave they find no angels and no serpents {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 181}.

**Question eight** (p. 218): why the punishment of the grave is not mentioned in the Qur'an, given how much one needs to know it and believe in it. The author answers in two parts, a general answer and a detailed one. The general answer is that God sent two revelations, the Book and the Wisdom, and commanded belief in both {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 218}.

**Question nine** (p. 223): what causes the occupants of graves to be punished? The general answer is ignorance of God, neglect of His command, and committing what He forbids. God does not punish a spirit that knew Him, loved Him, obeyed His command and avoided what He forbade {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 223}.

**Question ten** (p. 231): what causes save from the punishment of the grave? The general answer is to avoid the causes that call for punishment. Among the most useful is to sit, when one wishes to sleep, for a while and take account of one's gains and losses that day, then renew a sincere repentance to God {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 231}.

**Question eleven** (p. 252): is the questioning in the grave general to Muslims, hypocrites and disbelievers, or is it limited to believers and hypocrites? The author holds that the trial in the grave is only for a believer or a hypocrite who was counted among the people of the qibla by outward profession of Islam. The open disbeliever is not asked about his Lord, his religion or his prophet {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 252}.

**Question twelve** (p. 261): is the questioning by Munkar and Nakir specific to this community or shared with others? The author cites Abu Abd Allah al-Tirmidhi's view that the questioning is specific to this community, since earlier nations were visited by messengers, and when those nations refused, the messengers held back and withdrew, and the nations were hastened to punishment. He then turns to the debate {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 261}.

**Question thirteen** (p. 265): are children tested in their graves? There are two views, which the author reports as two opinions among the followers of Ahmad. Those who hold that children are questioned argue from the prescribed prayer over them and the supplication for them to be spared the punishment and trial of the grave, citing the report of Abu Hurayra over a child's funeral in the Muwatta' of Malik {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 265}.

**Question fourteen** (p. 269): is the punishment of the grave lasting or does it end? The answer is that it is of two kinds. One is lasting, apart from what some reports say about relief between the two blasts of the Trumpet. The author argues that it lasts from a verse about the people of the Fire being shown the Fire morning and evening, and from the report of Samura that the book discusses {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 269}.

## Questions fifteen to twenty-one: where spirits dwell, and the self
**Question fifteen** (p. 274): where do spirits stay between death and the Resurrection? Are they in heaven or on earth, in Paradise or in the Fire, and are they placed in other bodies? The author says the question is known only through transmitted report. He sets out the views, among them that the spirits of the believers are with God in Paradise {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 274}.

**Question sixteen** (p. 352): do the dead benefit from the efforts of the living? The author states two matters agreed by Sunni jurists, hadith scholars and exegetes: what the dead person caused during his life, and the prayers, forgiveness, charity and pilgrimage that Muslims perform for him. There is a dispute about whether the reward of the spending or of the deed reaches him, and the author records that the majority holds the reward of the deed reaches him {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 352}.

**Question seventeen** (p. 420): is the spirit eternal or created? The author states the difficulty of the attribution of the spirit to God: if the spirit is created and of God's command, how can God's command be created? He also asks what the attribution of the spirit to God means, and says that a scholar slipped in this question {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 420}.

**Question eighteen** (p. 453): was the creation of spirits before the bodies, or after them? There are two known views, reported by Ibn Taymiyya and others. Muhammad ibn Nasr al-Marwazi and Ibn Hazm held that spirits were created first, and Ibn Hazm reported this as a consensus {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 453}.

**Question nineteen** (p. 511): what is the self? Is it a part of the body, an accident of it, a body that dwells in it, or a simple substance? Is it the spirit or something else, and are the commanding, the reproaching and the tranquil selves one self or three? The author states that the sects disagreed widely on these points and made many mistakes {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 511}.

**Question twenty** (p. 613): are the soul (nafs) and the spirit (ruh) one thing or two? The majority holds that the two names refer to one thing, and others hold that they differ. The author sets out the senses in which "soul" is used, and cites al-Jawhari that the soul is the spirit {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 613}.

**Question twenty-one** (p. 622): is the self one or three? Many people say that the son of Adam has three selves, the tranquil, the reproaching and the commanding, and that some are dominated by one and some by another. They argue from verses that name these selves {cite:kitab-al-ruh-atta-2019 p. 622}.

## Limits of this reading
- Page numbers are the printed foot numbers, which match the scan numbers from page 3 to the end of the text.
- Bracketed numerals in the text, such as [110] on p. 44, belong to another pagination and are not cited.
- The OCR is noisy, so no quotation is reproduced. Only the opening of each question from seven to twenty-one was read, and the answers to those questions are not yet cited. The answers to questions one to six were read only in parts. This is a first pass and will be completed in revision.
- The editor's introduction (muqaddimat al-tahqiq), which covers the attribution of the book, its title, its date and the manuscripts, is listed in the index at pp. 869 and 870 and was not read for this draft.
