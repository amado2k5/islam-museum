## Summary
*The Canon of Medicine* (al-Qanun fi al-Tibb) is a medical encyclopedia written by [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]]. In his introduction he divided it into five books. The first begins with the general principles of the science of medicine, in four sections, and the second begins with simple drugs {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.10}.

## Sections of the book
The first book has four sections: the definition of medicine and its subject matter; then general diseases, causes and symptoms; then the cause of health and sickness and the necessity of death; then the classification of the methods of treatment. These sections begin on pages 13, 103, 200 and 267 of the first volume {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.11} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.13} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.103} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.200} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.267}.

The second book, on simple drugs, begins on page 313 of the first volume and ends on page 728 {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.313} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.728}.

The third book, on diseases affecting the parts of the body one organ at a time, begins on page 3 of the second volume. The fourth book, on diseases not specific to one organ and on cosmetics, begins on page 3 of the third volume and ends on page 393. The fifth book, on the compounding of drugs, which is the Aqrabadhin, begins on page 395 of the third volume {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.3} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v3 p.3} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v3 p.393} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v3 p.395}.

## The Canon in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam article on [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]] treats the Canon as a work of medicine kept apart from his philosophy. His main treatise on the natural sciences sits inside the great [[work/kitab-al-shifa|Book of Healing]], as does his metaphysics, while the Canon stands as a separate book {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 974}. The article describes it as a clear and ordered summa of the medical knowledge of Ibn Sina's day, enriched by his own observations, and says it was more consciously unified than his philosophical writings {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 974}.

The article's account of the five books runs as follows. The first holds general matters on the human body, sickness, health and general treatment. The second is the materia medica and the pharmacology of herbs. The third is special pathology, studied organ by organ, or more exactly system by system. The fourth opens with the well-known treatise on fevers, followed by treatises on signs, symptoms, diagnosis and prognosis, minor surgery, tumours, wounds, fractures and bites, and one on poisons. The fifth is the pharmacopoeia {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 974}.

The article singles out a page on experiment in medicine in the second book, cited from the Rome edition of 1593. It reads that passage as setting out three methods, agreement, difference and concomitant variation, which are usually thought characteristic of modern science {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 974}. This is the article's own reading; the museum's page on [[invention/ibn-sina-seven-drug-assessment-conditions|the drug-assessment conditions]] below treats the passage on the evidence of the Arabic text.

## Authority and teaching
The article gives several reasons for the Canon's long authority: it was a monumental unity, it kept its standing until experimental science began, and it was easier to use than [[person/buqrat|Hippocrates]] and [[person/jalinus|Galen]]. On this basis it served, the article says, for seven centuries of medical teaching and practice {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 974}. It adds that [[person/al-razi-abu-bakr|al-Razi]] was also known and was regarded as the better clinician, but that the Canon supplied an unmatched teaching corpus, since the Kulliyyat of [[person/ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd]] covered only the first part of it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 976}.

## Translation into Latin and Hebrew
According to the article, [[person/gerard-of-cremona|Gerard of Cremona]] translated the whole Canon between 1150 and 1187, and eighty-seven translations in all were made, some of them partial. Most were into Latin, with several Hebrew versions made in Spain, Italy and the south of France {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 976}. The article judges the medical translations weaker than the philosophical ones: some Arabic words taken from Greek were not understood, and some Arabic technical terms were simply carried into Latin and stayed obscure {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 976}. It dates the first printed edition of Gerard's translation to 1497, and the printing of the Arabic text at Rome to 1593, which it takes as a sign of lasting esteem {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 976} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 977}.

## Place in European universities
The article reports that the Canon formed the basis of teaching at all the universities. It appears in the oldest known syllabus of the medical school at Montpellier, a bull of Clement V dated 1309, and in every later syllabus until 1557. Ten years after that Galen was preferred, yet Ibn Sina continued to be taught until the seventeenth century {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 976}. The article also notes that Chaucer's Prologue to the Canterbury Tales names Ibn Sina among the authorities a doctor should know, and that nearly all physicians owned parts of the Canon, above all the sections on fevers and on diseases of the eye, or shorter works such as those on the pulse and on diseases of the heart {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 976}.

In the West, the article says, some physicians learned Arabic in order to read him. Its first known traces are in the Danish royal physician Henrik Harpestraeng, who died in 1244, and Arnold of Villeneuve translated the treatise on diseases of the heart {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 976} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 977}.

## Reception among Arabic authors and shorter derivatives
The article states that all Arab authors from the seventh/thirteenth to the tenth/sixteenth century depended on the Canon, whether they questioned it, as it says the father of Ibn Zuhr did, or added to and corrected it, as [[person/ibn-al-nafis|Ibn al-Nafis]] did. It records that Ibn al-Nafis set down his account of pulmonary circulation in a commentary on the Canon, and that he wrote a summary which physicians could obtain more easily than the full text {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 976}.

Several shorter treatises drew individual topics out of the Canon, and their small size gave them wide circulation. Among them the article names a mnemonic poem on medicine, translated into Latin several times between the thirteenth and seventeenth centuries as the Cantica Avicennae {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 974}.

## Note on the edition
The three volumes are from the Dar al-Kutub al-Ilmiyya edition in Beirut, first edition 1420 AH / 1999 CE, with notes by Muhammad Amin al-Dannawi. The edition has not yet been checked against a library catalogue, so the date rests on the title page alone. The point where the third book ends is not identified in the second volume, and the text of the third volume breaks off before the end of the fifth book. The page numbers here are scan numbers, which match the printed numbers at the points we checked.

## The experimental chapter in Book 2
In the first general division of Book 2, the second maqalah presents the [[invention/ibn-sina-seven-drug-assessment-conditions|drug-assessment conditions]]; both page images 318–319 were inspected, and their printed numbers match the scan numbers at these particular locations {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319}. The author names experience and analogy as two routes to knowledge, discussing the former's conditions before turning to analogy in the third maqalah; that arrangement prevents treating the passage as a rejection of all non-experimental knowledge {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319}.

The conditions concern acquired qualities of the substance, a simple condition, contrary cases, relative power, timing, consistency or usual occurrence, and differences between human and other bodies; intrinsic and incidental action are distinguished in humoral language throughout {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319}. The passage supplies no observation count, outcomes table, randomization or masking procedure: it argues about interpretation rather than reporting implementation of a modern protocol {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319}.

## Two witnesses and translation limits
The 2009 study presents Aida Tibi's translation from a Rome 1593 witness in the Sibbald Library, whereas our inspected images are from 1999; the witnesses' dates and pagination remain distinct {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 pp.78–79} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319}. In rule four, “dosage” is bracketed in translation, while the Arabic discusses power, the condition and the weaker case; that interpretive addition remains visible, neither attributed to the author's wording nor converted into treatment advice {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.80} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.318}. The fifth condition's Arabic continuation also retains the complication of an acquired quality acting before a natural power, so timing alone does not become a guarantee of intrinsic action {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.319}.

This is a reading of one chapter, not a claim to have read the complete work or original manuscripts; the older structure and edition evidence above remain without unsupported revision {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319}. The Canon's known circulation does not alone establish that every reader implemented the conditions or every later protocol descended directly from them {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 pp.78–80}.
