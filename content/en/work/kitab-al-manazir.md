## Summary
"Kitab al-Manazir" (The Book of Optics) by [[person/ibn-al-haytham|Ibn al-Haytham]], his most important book on [[concept/optics|optics]]. In it he studies the properties of light in its three states: its radiation in straight lines, its reflection and its refraction, through experiment together with mathematical methods {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.9}.

## Divisions of the book
The editor of the edition holds that the book divides into two parts. The first deals with the radiation of light and the seeing of visible objects in straight lines, which is the subject of the first three treatises. The second deals with reflection and refraction and what follows from them in the perception of visible objects in mirrors and transparent bodies, which is the subject of the last four treatises {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.10}.

## On the edition
The Arabic text available to us is the first volume of the edition by ʿAbd al-Hamid Sabra, which includes the first three treatises. It was published in Kuwait in 1983 in the heritage series. The details of the edition have not yet been verified from an independent source {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.5}.

## Article 1: Vision in General

The first volume of Sabra's edition carries the full text of Article 1, "On Vision in General," in eight chapters {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.60}. In the book's opening the author surveys earlier opinion: the natural philosophers held that vision occurs by a form passing from the seen object to the eye, while the people of demonstration — Euclid and Ptolemy foremost — held that vision occurs by a ray leaving the centre of the eye for the object, a cone whose apex is the eye's centre and whose base is the object's surface, though they disagreed on the ray's nature and origin {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.61} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.62}. The author then states his method: induction over observed particulars, then ascending by degrees in inquiry and demonstration, "seeking the truth, not inclining with opinions" {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.63}.

In Chapter 3, "On the Properties of Lights and the Manner of Their Radiation," he sets out some 144 inductive propositions proving that light radiates only along straight lines: light admitted through a hole into a darkened room is stopped where an opaque body interrupts the straight path; starlight seen in the room moves with its star; shadows extend in straight lines {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.73} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.74} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.75}. He shows that light's strength grows with the visible portion of its source, as at sunrise and during an eclipse {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.76}, and that colours accompany light {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.121}. Chapter 4 then treats what happens between sight and light — brilliance, glare and the eye's fatigue, so that a viewer of strong light retains an image of it after looking away {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.122} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.123}. Chapter 5 describes the eye's structure — layered tunics, membranes and bodies arising from the brain's anterior — and Chapter 6 the manner of vision, the light in the seen object falling upon the eye's surface and affecting it {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.128} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.138}. Chapter 7 explains the use of those layers as the instruments by which vision is completed, the first of them the cornea {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.184}; and Chapter 8 closes with the conditions without whose combination vision cannot occur: distance, facing, light, a size fit for the power of sensation, a transparent continuous medium, and the object's opacity {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.190}.

## Article 2: The Details of the Meanings Sight Perceives, Their Causes, and the Manner of Perceiving Them

Article 2 follows Article 1 in four chapters {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.198} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.200}. Its opening establishes that sight perceives many meanings from visible objects besides light and colour, that perception does not occur in the same way at all times and for all objects, and that it sets out to distinguish the states of the ray-lines, to separate all the meanings sight perceives, to show how each one is perceived, and how sight distinguishes visible objects {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.200} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.201}.

In Chapter 2, "On Distinguishing the Ray-Lines," he reaffirms that the ray-lines by which sight perceives objects are the straight lines extended from the centre of sight to the eye's surface {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.201}, then shows that the vitreous humour's surface — the common boundary between the vitreous and the last sensitive body — is either plane or spherical with a centre other than the centre of sight {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.205}, and that the images ordered on the ray-lines at the vitreous humour are refracted at that surface along lines crossing them {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.205} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.210}. The point falling on the central ray is clearest and most certain, and what is nearer to it is more certain than what is farther {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.215} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.217}.

Chapter 3, "On Visible Objects Other Than Light and Colour," separates the meanings sight perceives beyond light and colour: many are perceived by mere sensation; some by acquaintance, as the recognition of familiar written words; and some by a distinguishing measure beyond acquaintance, as perceiving that the whole is greater than the part {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.222} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.226} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.230}. A meaning repeated until its understanding settles in the soul comes thereafter to be perceived by acquaintance {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.230}. Chapter 4 closes the article with the manner of perceiving each of the partial meanings grasped by the sense of sight, the images of visible objects being compounded of those meanings {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.234} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.309}.

## Article 3: Errors of Sight in What It Perceives Straight On, and Their Causes

The third treatise opens with its title and states that it has seven chapters {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.340} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.341}. In its opening chapter Ibn al-Haytham argues that sight does not perceive every visible object as it is, and not every meaning that the viewer believes he has grasped is grasped correctly. Sight often errs in what it perceives, and sometimes the viewer senses the error and sometimes not {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.342}.

He gives examples. A visible object seen from a varying distance appears smaller than its true size, and one seen very close appears larger. A square or many-sided figure seen from a distance looks round if its diameters are equal and long if they differ, and a sphere seen from a varying distance looks flat {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.343}. A moving star appears still, and a person moving very slowly appears still if the gaze is brief and the viewer has no prior knowledge of the motion {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.343}.

The second chapter, on what must be set out before explaining errors of sight, begins {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.344}. It holds that sight perceives nothing except through the ray-lines, and that a single object seen with both eyes is perceived as one when its position relative to the two eyes is alike, and as two when it differs {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.344}. The two eyes are equal in their states and actions: when one moves to see, the other moves with the same movement, and they separate only through an obstacle or a screen {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.345}.

He imagines a cone between each visible object and the centre of sight, with its apex at the centre and its base on the object's surface. The point where the two ray-lines meet has the same position for both eyes, since it faces the middle of the two eyes {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.345}. Points close to the meeting point lie at equal distances from the ray-lines as far as sensation can judge, and the difference in the oblique lines matters to sensation only in proportion to how far the object lies from the meeting point {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.346}. When the two eyes move over the object and the ray-lines travel along its diameters, the image of each part is formed at two positions of like arrangement, and the images of all its parts become alike for both eyes {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.347} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.348}.

He then takes up separate objects perceived at the same time. The object on which the ray-lines rest has its image formed at two corresponding positions, while objects farther from it are alike in direction but not in distance, so their images remain confused until the eyes move and the ray-lines settle on each of them {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.348} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.349}. He then shows that the common ray-line passes through the centres of the layers of sight, and that the two corresponding images end at one point in the cavity of the common nerve {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.349} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.350} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.351}. ## Article 3, chapter 2 (continued): how one object seen by two eyes becomes one image

Here Ibn al-Haytham shows that the two images formed in the two eyes of one object on which the ray-lines meet end at one point in the cavity of the common nerve, where they coincide and become a single image {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.351} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.352}. The images of the points around the meeting point fall in corresponding positions and coincide as well, so sensation perceives the object as one although it has two images in the two eyes {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.352}.

He shows that the point on the common ray-line, where the two lines from the centres of the bone holes meet, remains one fixed point, and he names it the centre {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.353}. An object on the common ray-line has a sharper image than one beside it. A small object near the common ray-line gives one clear image, while a large, wide object has its part around the meeting point clear and its edges and borders confused {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.353} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.354}.

An object outside the common ray-line and at a distance gives two images that overlap or stand apart, depending on direction and distance, and they remain confused until the eyes are brought toward it and the ray-lines settle on it {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.355} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.356}. An object lying on one ray-line and outside the other gives two images, one on the centre and the other inclined from it, which do not coincide {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.356}.

He then turns to a test to verify all this. The board is of light polished wood, as long as a forearm bone and four fingers wide, with two crossing lines and a round hole at the middle of its width that takes the bridge of the nose. Three small white wax figures, each a different colour, stand on it, one at the centre and two at the ends of the crosswise line {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.357}. When the observer stares at the middle figure, both ray-lines meet on it; when he stares at an end figure, they meet there; in each case he notes by sight the repetition of figures and lines on the board, to confirm what has been said {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.358}. ## Article 3, the board test (continued): repeated figures and lines

The description of the test continues: the observer moves two of the wax figures to different places on the board, then stares at the middle figure. Figures not on the single middle line appear doubled, and the effect changes with each figure's place on the two lines {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.358} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.359}. The figures and lines are seen as described so long as the gaze stays on the middle figure or on a point of the crosswise line; looking at a figure off that line makes the middle figure appear double too {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.359} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.360}.

He then gives a geometric proof with lettered figures. A figure near the meeting point receives the rays from both eyes along lines nearly equal in length, so it is seen as one. A figure far from the common line has two angles that differ noticeably, so it is seen as two {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.360} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.361}.

He sums up at the end of the chapter. The object on which the ray-lines meet is always seen as one. An object whose rays are alike in direction but differ in distance is seen as two, and so is an object whose rays differ in direction even when their lengths are equal. Familiar objects are seen as one because they face both eyes and their rays are alike in direction and near in distance; seeing two is rare {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.362} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.363}.

He ends with what the observer sees when he lifts the middle figure and looks at the crossing point in the middle of the board. He sees four lines, two near together and two apart. With one eye covered he sees two lines, and the gap between them looks wider than it is. He explains the close pair by the nearby eye, whose rays lie near the ray-line, and the apart pair by the oblique rays from the other eye, and he explains the apparent widening by the fact that each eye perceives the gap between the lines larger than its true size {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.363}. ## Article 3, the board test (continued again): the widening gap and the position of the object

Ibn al-Haytham shows that when the observer lifts the middle figure and looks at the crossing point, he sees four lines, two near and two apart, and each apart line appears farther from the middle than it truly is {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.363}. With one eye covered he sees two lines, with a wider gap than the true one. He explains this by the gap between the lines lying very close to the eye, and whatever lies very close is seen larger than it is; the full cause is to be shown in its place, in the discussion of errors of sight {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.364}.

From the lines and the figures fixed on them he concludes that an object lying on the common ray-line and seen by a ray-line is perceived in its true place, whether seen with one eye or both. An object off the common line is perceived nearer to that line than its true place, and the rest of its points are perceived the same way because they join the part at the end of the ray-line {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.364}. The same holds for an object off the common line at which the two ray-lines meet, though that arrangement is rare, since the common ray-line usually passes through the object. It does not show in familiar objects, because a shift of one object relative to its neighbours is not noticed {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.364} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.365}.

He then proposes a test with paper. Three small equal slips are cut, each bearing a clear word; one is fixed at the middle figure, and another at a second point on the board. Staring at the middle slip, the word on it is seen more clearly than its neighbour {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.365}. As the side slip is moved from the middle, its word becomes less clear with each step, until it cannot be understood {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.366} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.367}.

From these tests he concludes that the clearest objects facing the eye and seen with both eyes are those at the meeting point of the ray-lines; objects nearer that point are clearer than those farther; a wide object is seen clearly only when the ray-line moves over all its parts; and a steady eye facing a wide object sees clearly only what lies on the ray-line and near it, seeing the rest less clearly {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.367}. He then begins a further test with paper bearing fine lines of text, in which the observer reads the centre of the sheet more clearly than its surroundings {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.367}. ## Article 3, the paper test: facing and tilt

Ibn al-Haytham returns to the paper bearing fine lines of text. Set at the centre and stared at with both eyes, it reads clearly, but the writing at its centre is clearer than at its edges so long as the gaze stays on the centre and does not move over all its parts {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.367}. When the sheet is tilted across the crosswise line until the writing falls on the meeting point, its clarity falls. The greater the tilt, the greater the confusion, until the sheet nears the line along the middle of the board and the text cannot be read {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.367} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.368}.

He repeats the test with one eye. Covering one eye, returning the sheet to its first position and tilting it, the clarity of the text drops at each step, until the sheet nears the diagonal next to the viewing eye and the observer can no longer read it {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.368} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.369}. From this he concludes that an object set straight in front is clearer than an oblique one, that what lies near the facing position is clearer than what lies farther from it, and that an object at an uneven tilt is confused whether seen with two eyes or one {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.369}.

He then returns the central figure to the board and sets the sheet on one half of the crosswise line in the facing position. The part of the writing next to the central figure is clear, and the part farther from it is confused and hard to see {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.369} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.370}. He repeats the test along the width of the board and at its end, with the same result, and finds that the distance at which the outer word becomes confused stands in one ratio to its distance from the eye in both places {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.370} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.371}.

He also notes that when the sheet is held farther from the eye than the length of the board and tilted slightly and then more, its clarity falls at each step until the writing is confused. When one slip is moved away from the middle slip, the word on it becomes more confused, until it is no longer visible {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.371} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.372}. The summary opens at the foot of page 371 and continues on page 372: at every distance the clearest objects are those on the ray-line; what lies near the ray-line is clearer than what lies farther; an object far from the ray-line at an uneven distance is confused, whether seen with one eye or two; and an object facing the eye is clearer than an inclined one at every distance {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.371} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.372}.

## Article 3, chapter 2 (end): steep tilt and facing

Ibn al-Haytham shows that an object at a steep tilt has its image gathered on the surface of the eye, because the angle it subtends at the centre of sight is small; its small parts become insensible, and sight does not perceive its subtle meanings {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.372} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.373}. An object facing the eye has its image arranged in the eye as its parts are arranged on its surface, so its small parts are clear {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.373}.

He holds that subtle meanings and the arrangement of an object's parts are perceived with certainty only when the image is impressed on the surface of the sensing organ and each part of it falls on a sensible part of that surface; a steeply tilted object is not impressed in this way {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.373}. He shows that perceiving the true size of a steeply tilted object comes not from its image alone but from a measure outside the image: the sense perceives the difference in distance of its two ends {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.374}.

He concludes that a steeply tilted object is confused because its image is gathered and its small parts are hidden, while an object at a moderate distance is clear because its image is impressed as it is and sensed in its small parts {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.374}.

## Article 3, chapter 3: the causes of error in sight

Chapter 3 opens with its title, "on the causes for which error comes to sight". Sight perceives nothing straight on unless several conditions are met for the object: distance, facing, light, a sufficient size, density or some density, transparency of the air between object and eye with no dense body in between, time, and the health of the eye, free of ailments and obstacles {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.374} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.375}. Elsewhere he gathers these into eight: distance, the particular position, light, sufficient size, density, transparency of the air, time, and the health of the eye {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.375}.

Each condition has a measure of moderation. While each stays within its measure, sight perceives the object as it is; if one or more exceed it by excess, sight does not perceive the object as it is. An object very far or very near is not perceived correctly, and between these limits there are distances at which sight perceives correctly without confusion, which differ by object {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.375} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.376}. ## Article 3, chapter 3 (continued): the conditions for correct perception

The remaining conditions are set out here. A small object may have its image hidden, or confused, at a distance where the image of a large one is not, and a strongly lit object may be perceived at a distance where a faintly lit one is hidden {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.375} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.376}. An object at an uneven tilt from the middle of sight, or one that the ray-line neither meets nor approaches, is not perceived correctly. An object facing the middle of sight, with the ray-line on or near one of its points, is perceived correctly if it is not wide in extent, even when the ray-line has not moved over all its diameters {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.376} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.377}.

The slight tilt or slight distance from the ray-line at which an object's true form is still perceived depends on its subtle meanings. An object without subtle meanings is perceived truly even a little off the ray-line; one with subtle meanings may be confused at that same distance {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.377} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.378}.

Light that is too faint, too strong, or thrown on a polished surface prevents correct perception, while between the extremes lies a range of lights in which it occurs; the suitable light depends on the object's meanings and size {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.377} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.378}. An object of extreme smallness with subtle meanings and distinct parts, such as very small animals whose distinct organs exceed the power of sight, is not seen correctly; a larger object whose organs are proportionate to it is seen fully {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.378}.

On density and air: an object of very slight density is not perceived correctly, while a clearly dense one is. A thick, murky air such as fog, mist or smoke prevents the perception of fine objects, while clear and uniformly transparent air does not, and a slight thickness does not hinder the perception of objects that are not extremely small or finely detailed {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.379} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.380}. On motion: an object moving very swiftly, like a glimpse through a hole, is not perceived in its motion or true form; a whirling object moving very fast appears still, and a very slow motion appears still over a measurable time {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.380} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.381}. On the eye: a damaging ailment prevents correct perception, while a slight affliction does not hinder the perception of objects that are neither extremely small nor finely detailed {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.381}.

He concludes the chapter by stating that each condition has a measure of moderation, and that departing from it by excess, in the upward or downward direction, prevents correct perception. This holds for distance, position, light, size, density, air, time and the health of the eye alike, and any position that does not allow correct perception is outside moderation {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.381}.

## Article 3, chapter 3 (continued): the measure of moderation

Ibn al-Haytham calls the range within which sight perceives an object as it is the measure of moderation, and defines it as the range in which there is no perceptible difference, affecting its true form, between the image sight takes of the object and its real image {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.381} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.382}. The extent of this range for each condition differs by object, and depends on the object's colour, its subtle meanings, and the other conditions, so that distance, position, light, size, density, air, time and the eye's health each have their own measure.

He details this for distance. A white, bright object shows its true form from a greater distance than a dull, earthy-coloured one that is alike in all other respects. An object without subtle meanings is perceived from farther off than one with fine meanings; an object in front of the eye, or on the meeting point of the ray-lines, is perceived from farther than an inclined one; a brightly lit object is perceived from farther than a dim one; a large object from farther than a small one; a thick or lightly dense object from farther than a very transparent one; and clear, uniformly transparent air permits a farther distance than thick or obstructing air {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.382}.

Time works in the same way. A near object is perceived in its true form in less time than a distant one, especially when it has subtle meanings. When the time for examining an object is limited, the range of distance within which it can be perceived correctly narrows. A strong and healthy eye perceives an object's true form from a greater distance than a weak or obstructed one {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.383} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.384}.

He then states that the measure of moderation for position, light, size, density, transparency of the air, time and the eye's health each depends on the same factors, namely the colour of the object, its subtle meanings, its distance and the remaining conditions, so that each has its own range. He lists these in detail on the following pages {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.384} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.384}.

## Article 3, chapter 3 (end): the cause of error

Ibn al-Haytham shows that the measure of moderation for each condition is fixed by the other conditions present in the object. When every condition by which perception is completed lies within its measure in relation to the object, sight perceives it as it is; when one or more lies outside its measure, sight perceives it otherwise {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.385}. He concludes that the cause of all errors of sight is that one or more of these conditions lies outside its measure, and that error is the perception of an object otherwise than it is {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.386} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.387}.

## Article 3, chapter 4: distinguishing the errors of sight

Chapter 4 opens with its title, "on distinguishing the errors of sight". Sight perceives objects in three ways: by mere sensation, by knowledge, and by inference and discrimination at the moment of seeing. An error in the first is an error in sensation itself; an error in the second is an error of knowledge; an error in the third is an error of inference or discrimination, or of the premises from which the inference was drawn {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.386}. The second treatise showed that what sight perceives by mere sensation is light as light and colour as colour {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.386}.

Knowledge, he says, builds through repeated perception. Familiar lights, such as those of the sun, the moon and fire, and familiar colours were first perceived by sensation, then recognized by sight through repetition. Familiar compound forms, such as the shapes of animals, fruits, plants and tools, and general meanings such as roundness, straightness, triangularity and squareness, smoothness and roughness, and the general form of a human or of a tree, were first perceived by inference and discrimination, and after their forms settle in the mind they are recognized by knowledge and by signs alone {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.386} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.387}. What is perceived by inference and discrimination at the moment of sensation is a compound form not yet repeated, and the particular meanings of particular individuals {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.387}.

He holds that the errors of sight are of three kinds: error in mere sensation, error in knowledge, and error in discrimination and inference {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.388}. An example of error in mere sensation is a body with strong colours, such as dark blue, wine-red and purple, in an extremely dark place where only faint light falls; the viewer sees it as one dark colour and does not grasp its colours. The cause is light falling below its measure by excess of deficiency; once the object is brought into moderate light, its colours appear distinct {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.388}. ## Article 3, chapter 6: the manner of errors in knowledge

Chapter 6 opens with its title, "on the manner of the errors of sight that occur in knowledge according to each of the causes", and notes that the quiddity of every object is perceived through knowledge: the object's form is compared with the forms of its kind that sight already knows, and that is knowledge by kind, as the second treatise showed {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.398}.

## Article 3, chapter 6 (continued): comparing a form with its like

Ibn al-Haytham shows that knowing a person rests on comparing his form with the form already perceived and remembered. An error in the quiddity of an object is an error of knowledge and concerns its kind; taking one person for a known one and believing him to be that very person is an error of knowledge; mistaking the form of one person for that of another particular person is an error of individuality. Knowledge is possible only of the individual, of the kind, or of both together {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.398} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.399}.

Sight, while it looks at an object, compares its form with like forms it knows well, and compares the meanings in the object with like meanings, so that it recognises the individual or kind and its meanings. When sight doubts the quiddity of an object, or one of its meanings, and does not recognise it at the moment, it compares it with the nearest thing it knows and has familiarised itself with. This disposition belongs to all the senses. Error arises when perception is not fully certain; it occurs only when one or more of the conditions lies outside its measure {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.399}.

An example of distance outside its measure: a person seen from an uneven distance is taken for a known person and believed to be him, when he is not. If the same person is near, or at a moderate distance, his form is not confused with another's {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.399} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.400}. This kind of error is frequent: sight may see a person from afar and take him for someone it knows, or see a horse, a mule, an ass, a bull, a tree, a fruit, a plant, a stone, a cloth, a vessel or a tool from so great a distance that it compares it with a similar object it has known and remembers {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.400}.

A stranger example: seeing at night, from an uneven distance, a fire on a hill-top of which he had no knowledge, the viewer may take it for a star in the sky. ## Article 3, chapter 6 (continued): knowledge errors from position and light

Ibn al-Haytham shows that sight compares an object lying obliquely and unevenly outside the ray-line with whatever resembles it in some of its meanings. When the object is inclined unevenly to the ray-lines, and carries fine markings or subtle meanings, sight fails to perceive them and sees the object as bare, and it compares the bare form with bare objects alike in colour, shape, appearance or size, mistaking its kind. ## Article 3, chapter 7: errors in inference

Chapter 7 opens with its title, "on the manner of the errors of sight that occur in inference according to each of the causes". It notes that most of the meanings sight perceives are perceived by inference, and that the forms of all visible objects are compounded of particular meanings; most of sight's errors concern these particular meanings and the forms of objects, and these are errors of inference {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.411} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.412}.

Error in inference is of two kinds: error in the premises, and error in the order of the inference. Error in the premises has three forms: discrimination takes a false premise for a true one; it takes a particular premise for a universal one; or it fails in acquiring the premises. The last occurs when an object has apparent meanings and hidden ones that would appear on close examination, and sight relies on what appears without surveying all the meanings, either through inattention and weak discrimination or because it cannot examine the object at the moment. If sight examines the object closely and finds the hidden meanings, the conclusion differs from that of the apparent meanings alone; if it cannot examine and senses this, it does not commit itself to the conclusion of the apparent premises {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.412}.

## Article 3, chapter 7 (continued): error in inference from uneven distance

Ibn al-Haytham shows that when sight does not survey all the meanings of an object and relies on the apparent ones, drawing their conclusions without doubt, it errs in inference, because it concludes from some of the premises and not from all of them. If it doubts and does not commit itself to the conclusion, it is not in error in this respect {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.412} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.413}.

He then begins the error in inference that arises from the distance lying outside its measure, starting with the error about the magnitude of a distance. Upright objects on the ground, such as palm trees, trees and columns, seen from a widely uneven distance, where they lie at different distances, do not hide one another, and look alike in colour and in the light falling on them, are not seen as differing in distance; the far one is not distinguished from the near one, and sight may take some or all of them to be equidistant. An error about distance, about its difference, or about its equality is an error of inference, since these are grasped by inference. Its cause is that the distances of such objects lie outside their measure {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.413} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.414}.

He shows that sight does not perceive the magnitudes of uneven distances with certainty, and so does not perceive that one exceeds another. The same error occurs whenever one looks at the stars and a wandering star lies among them: sight does not perceive the difference between its distance and those of the fixed stars, and sees them all as if on one surface {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.414} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.415}.

He also says that sight errs about the positions of objects from distance lying outside its measure. An object inclined to the ray-lines, not facing the eye, is seen as if it faced it, and its tilt is not sensed. Thus a square with equal sides, or a flat circle, seen from an uneven distance while tilted, appears oblong; had sight sensed the tilt, it would have perceived the equal sides of the square and the roundness of the circle. This is an error of inference, since position is grasped by inference {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.415}.

## Article 3, chapter 7 (continued): errors of position, shape and solidity

Ibn al-Haytham completes the reason why an inclined object is seen as oblong. Inclination is perceived through sight's sense of the difference in distance between the two ends of the inclined object. When sight does not sense that difference, it does not sense the inclination; because the difference between the two ends is slight relative to the whole distance, sight takes the two ends to be equidistant, and so sees the object as facing it {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.415} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.416}.

The angle subtended at the eye by the inclined breadth of a square is smaller than the angle subtended by its facing length, and the inclined diameter of a circle subtends a smaller angle than its facing diameter. Sight therefore perceives the breadth as shorter than the length, and so sees an equal-sided square and a circle as oblong. Error in the position of such objects thus goes with error in their shape and in the measure of their breadth. At a moderate distance sight perceives the difference between the distances of their ends, and so perceives their inclination and shape as they are {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.415} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.416}.

He notes that sight may see a square or a circle as oblong while sensing their tilt, when the tilt is steep; but then it judges the tilt to be less than it is, and so judges the inclined breadth to be less than its true size. The error in these shapes while the tilt is sensed is slight, unlike the error from a very uneven distance {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.416} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.416}.

## Article 3, chapter 7 (continued): shape, solidity and curvature from an uneven distance

Ibn al-Haytham holds that the form of a surface is of two kinds: the shape of its boundary and the shape of its whole. Error in solidity falls under error in shape together with error in position because of uneven distance {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.416} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.417}. A many-sided body of equal diameters, seen from an uneven distance while facing the eye, is perceived as round. Error about shape and form from an uneven distance is an error of inference, because shape from an uneven distance is grasped only through reflection and inference at the moment of sensation, and not by immediate perception or by signs {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.417}.

The cause is that an object at a great distance is hidden according to its size. Each corner of a polygon is smaller than the whole, and each corner may be hidden at a distance where the whole still shows. If the object lies at an uneven distance, its diameters are equal or nearly so, and it faces the eye, sight perceives the whole and none of its corners, and so takes it for round {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.417} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.418}.

This error of shape from uneven distance is an error of inference, and its cause is that the distance lies outside its measure. ## Article 3, chapter 7 (continued): the flattening of convex bodies and the error of magnitude from uneven distance

Ibn al-Haytham completes the account of curvature: the error about the curvature of a body at an uneven distance does not arise when the other conditions are within their measure, and at a moderate distance sight perceives the convexity or concavity of the body {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.418} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.419}.

He shows that the shape of a surface is also misperceived from an uneven distance. A spherical body appears from such a distance as a flat round thing, and so do convex bodies and bodies with convex parts; concave ones are seen in the same way. This is an error of inference, since convexity, concavity and flatness are perceived by inference, and its cause is that the distance lies outside its measure {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.419}.

He explains why a sphere is seen flat from an uneven distance: convexity is perceived only when sight senses that the middle parts are near and the outer parts are far. The difference between the distances of the edges and of the middle is slight relative to the whole distance, and so is not perceived. That is why the disc of the sun and the moon look like two flat surfaces though they are spherical. At a moderate distance sight senses the differences in distance of the parts and the bending of the surfaces, and perceives convexity, sphericity and solidity; the same holds for concave bodies at an uneven distance, whose concavity is not perceived {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.419} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.420}.

## Article 3, chapter 7 (continued): the sensed point and the underestimation of size from uneven distance

Ibn al-Haytham completes the account of magnitude. Sense perceives the part of the sensing organ's surface only by the distance between the two ends of its breadth. The end by which sense bounds that breadth is not a magnitude sense counts, but it has a measurable ratio to the breadth of the part on which the image falls, and it adds only a slight amount to the breadth over the sensed point. So when sight perceives the breadth of the part bearing the image of an object at an uneven distance, it perceives it smaller than it truly is, by the amount of the two end points {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.421} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.422}.

He shows that the point, which is the smallest part sense perceives on the surface of sight, is a fixed magnitude that does not change whether the part bearing the image grows or shrinks, because anything smaller is not perceived. The part of the object whose image falls on that point is larger when the distance is uneven than when it is moderate. A slight difference between two motions is perceived only through a thorough examination, which occurs only in an ample time, and never in the least moment of time, which is the glimpse of sight {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.510}.

When sight perceives two different motions as equal, it is in error about their equality. The error about motions and about the equality of motions is an error of inference, because motions and their equality are perceived only by inference. The cause of this error is that the time in which sight perceives the two such motions lies outside equilibrium; for when sight perceives two things moving with different motions in an ample time, and examines their two motions in that time with a thorough examination, it perceives the difference between their motions, and no error arises for it about the manner of their motion, provided the remaining attributes of those two objects of sight remain in equilibrium {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.511}.

The error of stillness also arises from the time lying outside equilibrium. When sight looks at an object that moves very slowly, extremely slowly, and sight does not remain on that object except for a brief time, sight perceives the object as still, whether its motion is along a straight line or in a circle, and even at a moderate distance. An object that moves extremely slowly covers, in a perceptible time, a distance that is not perceptible; so if sight looks at it and remains facing it only for that span of time, or part of it, during which the object covers an imperceptible distance, sight does not sense the distance the object covers in that span, and whoever does not sense the distance covered perceives the object as still {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.511}.

When sight looks at a moving object and does not remain facing it except for a brief time, and the object moves slowly, and the distance it covers in that span of time is imperceptible, sight perceives the object as still, because when sight perceives an object at one place and in one position for a perceptible time, it perceives it as still for that span and does not sense its motion during that span; and this is why it perceives the stars as still when the gaze does not last long on them {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.512}

## Article 3, chapter 7 (continued): the spinning top, and errors of roughness and smoothness from the time of seeing

Sight may also perceive a moving object as still when its motion is extremely fast, as with a spinning top moving very fast. Sight perceives each part of the top over the whole circle that part traverses in an extremely small time, too small for sense to distinguish its parts; and it has been shown earlier that sight perceives motion only in a perceptible time {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.512}.

When sight perceives each part of the top over the whole circumference in the least portion of time, which for sense is like an instant, and the top's motion is then continuous, sight perceives the colour of each point of the top over the whole circumference during the time in which the top turns quickly. If the top is of one colour, its parts are not distinguished for sight while it moves. If it has different colours, its colour appears as one colour blended from them. If it has scattered points, or points of a colour different from the colour of the whole top, the colour of each point appears over the whole circle that point traces, and that colour stays circular in one way while the top moves quickly, so that sight perceives the top as if its surface had circles of colours differing from the rest of its colour {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.512} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.513}.

Thus, however the top's colours change, when it turns quickly sight perceives the image of its colour or colours in one form that does not change for a perceptible time, which is the span during which the top turns quickly. Sight perceives circular motion from the change of the parts of the moving thing relative to sight itself or to another body, and perceives stillness from holding the object in one state and one position for a perceptible time. When sight perceives the top in one state for a perceptible time, and perceives its parts in one arrangement without sensing any change in them, it perceives the top as still and does not sense its motion {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.513}.

When sight perceives a moving top as still, it is in error about its stillness, and its error is an error of inference. The cause of this error is that the time in which sight perceives each part of the top over the whole circle that part traces lies outside equilibrium, by excessive shortness: when that time is extremely short, sense does not distinguish its parts; when sense does not distinguish its parts it does not distinguish the parts of the motion; when it does not distinguish the parts of the motion it does not distinguish the change of the top's parts; and when it does not distinguish that change it does not perceive the top's motion. The cause of this error is therefore the shortness of the time in which sight perceives the moving object across the whole distance it traverses {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.513}.

The error of roughness and smoothness also arises from the shortness of time. When sight glimpses an object for a brief moment and turns away at once, and the object has a slight roughness, sight does not perceive the slight roughness of its surface, because slight roughness is perceived only by a thorough examination, and a thorough examination occurs only in a time of some extent. When sight glimpses the object briefly in a short span, it does not perceive the roughness in it; and when it does not perceive the roughness of the surface, it perceives the object as smooth, because it perceives it as it perceives smooth surfaces and does not distinguish it from them {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.513}.

Likewise, if the object is smooth and sight glimpses it briefly, it cannot verify its smoothness. If the object is among those that may have roughness, most of which have rough surfaces, sight may take it to have a rough surface when it has not verified its smoothness. When sight takes a rough thing to be smooth, or a smooth thing to be rough, it is in error about roughness and smoothness. This error is an error of inference, because roughness and smoothness are perceived by inference. The cause of the error is that the time in which sight perceives the object lies outside equilibrium: when sight perceives the object and examines it correctly in a time of some extent, it perceives its roughness if it is rough and its smoothness if it is smooth, provided the remaining attributes of the object are in equilibrium {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.514}.

## Article 3, chapter 7 (continued): transparency, density, shadow, beauty and ugliness from the time of seeing

Sight may also err about transparency and density from the time of seeing lying outside equilibrium. When sight glimpses a transparent body for a moment and turns away, and light appears behind the body while sight is looking, and sight senses the body's transparency, but the light behind it reaches the body along lines slanting to its surface and not along perpendicular lines, sight perceives the transparency of the body other than its true transparency. The true transparency of a transparent body is perceived only when the light appearing behind it lies along lines perpendicular to it {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.514} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.515}.

When sight glimpses a transparent body in the position described, and perceives its transparency as its position requires, and then turns away at once, it does not sense the true position of the body relative to the light appearing behind it when it turns away quickly, and in the brief span of a glimpse it does not distinguish the light facing the object from the slanting light. It may take the transparency it perceived to be the full extent of the body's transparency. If sight takes a body's transparency, which is less than its true transparency, to be its full transparency, it is in error about that body's transparency {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.515}.

If sight glimpses a transparent body lying on the ground with no light appearing behind it, and the body has a strong colour, sight perceives the body as dense and does not sense its transparency; in the span of a glimpse it cannot distinguish the colour of the body from the colour of what lies behind it. If the viewer has no prior knowledge of the body's transparency, he does not doubt its density. If sight takes a transparent body to be dense, it is in error about its density. The errors about transparency and density are errors of inference, because transparency and density are perceived by inference. Their cause is that the time lies outside equilibrium: when sight perceives a transparent object and is able to examine it and to see through it, it is not subject to error about its transparency or, if it is dense, its density, provided the remaining attributes of the object are in equilibrium {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.515}.

The error about shadow also arises from the shortness of time, and in darkness. When sight glimpses a wall inside a house in the darkness of night, part of the wall is bright and part of it is dark, there is a weak firelight on the wall, the viewer has no prior knowledge of the wall, and he glimpses it briefly and turns away at once, then, if he does not examine it correctly, he may take the dark place of the wall to be a shadow {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.515} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.516}.

Likewise, if part of a wall is black and part of it white, and sight looks at it in daylight, with no prior knowledge of the wall's blackness, and the light on it is not very bright, the viewer may take the blackness to be a door or an opening, and take its blackness to be the darkness of a place, if he does not examine the place correctly and only glimpses it {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.516}.

When sight perceives a place lit by firelight that has no shadow as shaded or dark, it is in error about its shading and its darkness. This error is an error of inference, because shadow and darkness are perceived by inference. Its cause is that the time in which sight glimpses such an object lies outside equilibrium; for when sight examines such an object thoroughly, it perceives the light on it and senses the variation of its colours, and no error arises for it about any of that, provided the remaining attributes of the object are in equilibrium {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.516}.

The error about beauty and ugliness also arises from the time lying outside equilibrium. When sight glimpses an object such as a human figure, whose complexion is fine and whose overall face and visible features are good in shape or in part, but which also has subtle qualities that disfigure it, such as disproportion among its limbs or marks on its face that make its form ugly, and sight glimpses that form briefly and turns away at once, sight perceives its beauty while it looks, and does not perceive the subtle qualities that disfigure it, because such qualities are not perceived in the glimpse of sight. Thus when sight glimpses such an object it perceives it as beautiful while it is in fact ugly. The same holds for all objects, whether inanimate things, animals or plants, when they have apparent good qualities and subtle bad ones: if sight glimpses them and cannot examine them correctly, it perceives them as beautiful while they are in fact ugly {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.516} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.517}.

Likewise, if an object has apparent bad qualities and subtle good ones, and sight glimpses it briefly, it perceives it as ugly and does not perceive its beauty. If sight perceives an ugly object as beautiful, it is in error about its beauty; if it perceives a beautiful object as ugly, it is in error about its ugliness {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.517}.

This same error arises for sight when the viewer looks through a door or a gap, and the object is moving or passing by that door or gap, and sight perceives it in the span of time during which the object crosses the width of that door or gap and sight cannot examine it. Then sight perceives an ugly object of the kind described as beautiful, and perceives a beautiful one as ugly. Likewise when the viewer is moving and the object is still, or moving in the direction opposite to the viewer's movement, and sight glimpses the object while it is moving through a door or a gap {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.517}.

Likewise, if sight glimpses two objects that resemble one another in apparent qualities but differ in subtle ones, and does not examine them correctly but only glimpses them briefly and turns away, it perceives the two objects as alike and does not sense their difference. If they differ in apparent qualities and resemble one another in hidden ones, sight perceives them as different and does not sense their likeness. If sight perceives two objects that differ in some respect as altogether alike, it is in error about their likeness; if it perceives two objects that are alike in some respect as altogether different, it is in error about their difference {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.517}.

The errors about beauty, ugliness, likeness and difference are errors of inference, because these qualities are perceived by inference. Their cause is that the time in which sight perceives each of these objects lies outside equilibrium; for when sight perceives these objects and is able to examine them and look over the qualities in them, it perceives each one as it is, perceiving the beautiful as beautiful, the ugly as ugly, the alike as alike and the different as different, and no error arises for it about any of the qualities in them, provided the remaining attributes of those objects are in equilibrium {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.517}.

## Article 3, chapter 7 (continued): errors of distance and position from the sight's own condition

These attributes and their like give rise to the error of sight in inference, because the time in which sight perceives the object lies outside equilibrium {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.518}.

As for how the error of inference arises when the health of sight lies outside equilibrium: take the sight of a person whose vision is weakened at night, or who is ill, or whose sight is weak by nature. If he looks at two objects at equal distances, the two objects being separate and at a considerable distance from each other, one of them bright white and pure in whiteness, the other dark in colour, under equal light, sight may take the white object to be nearer than the dark one, and the dark one to be farther, even though both distances are moderate and the dark one is the farther of the two. This is especially so when both objects are raised above the ground and their distances lie level with the ground's surface. This happens because weak sight does not perceive what it perceives correctly, and the discriminating faculty senses this quality in weak or diseased sight {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.518}.

The equality of two distances is perceived by sight through perceiving the magnitude of each distance and comparing one with the other, so sight perceives the equality of two distances through a comparison that involves distance. A white object is clearer than a dark-coloured one, and an object strange to sight is clearer than a distant one. The clarity of the white and the obscurity of the dark are perceived by sense alone, and what sense alone perceives is more evident to sense than what is perceived by inference {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.518}.

When sight perceives such two objects and senses their distances and perceives the difference in their clarity, it gives precedence to the difference in clarity that it perceives by sense alone over what it perceives by inference, because it is more evident and sense of it is more reliable. If the discriminating faculty perceives that what weak sight perceives is not a correct perception, it treats what is more evident to that sight and nearer to sense as more reliable than what is more distant to sense, and than what is perceived only after reflection and measurement. So weak sight perceives the bright white object as nearer than the dark one when their distances are equal and their distance has not been verified, because it perceives the white object as clearer and because it gives precedence to what is more evident to sense. If sight perceives two objects that are equally distant as unequally distant, it is in error about the distance of each of them or about the distance of one of them {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.519}.

The same error also arises for healthy sight when the two such objects are at unequal distances and sight is not certain of their distances; for when sight is not certain of the distances of these two objects, it takes the white one to be nearer because it is clearer {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.519}.

The same error also arises for healthy sight at a moderate distance, when it does not notice the distance and is not certain of its magnitude, and glimpses such two objects briefly and turns away at once, when it perceives them at night or in a dusty place. The error about the distances of these two objects is then an error of inference, because distance and differences of distance are perceived only by inference. The cause of this error for healthy sight is the difference in distance, or the weakness of light, or the shortness of time; and the cause of the error for weak sight, when the distances of the two objects are not different, is the weakness of sight, provided the remaining attributes of those two objects by which objects are perceived as they are remain in equilibrium {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.519}.

The error about position also arises from the departure of sight from equilibrium. When weak or ailing sight perceives an object whose surface is tilted to the lines of the ray, and the object is not on the ground but raised above it, and its tilt is slight, sight does not sense the tilt, even if its distance lies level with the ground's surface; it perceives the object as if it faced it. This is because sight perceives the tilt only through perceiving the difference between the distances of the object's two ends. When the object is raised above the ground and its tilt is slight, sight perceives the difference between the distances of its two ends only through a distant comparison and a thorough and subtle examination, and the difference between the distances of its two ends, when its tilt is slight, is not evident to sense. Weak sight does not perceive subtle attributes, and perceives only what is evident; so it perceives such objects as if they faced it {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.519} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.520}.

When sight perceives a tilted object as facing it, it is in error about its position. A consequence of this error is that the magnitude sight perceives for an object of this kind differs from what the object is; for when sight senses the object's tilt, it perceives its magnitude as greater than that of a facing object subtending an angle equal to the angle the tilted object subtends. If sight does not sense the object's tilt and takes it to face the viewer, it perceives its size as that of a facing object subtending an angle of that size, so it perceives its magnitude as smaller than its true magnitude {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.520}.

## Article 3, chapter 7 (continued): errors of shape, separation, continuity and number from weak sight and squint

Sight may also err about the shape of an object from the departure of sight from equilibrium. When an object has small angles and small projections, weak sight does not perceive those small angles and projections; and when it does not perceive the angles and projections in the object, it perceives its shape other than it is, and errs about its shape. Likewise, when an object has a slight convexity or a slight concavity, weak sight does not perceive that slight convexity or concavity; and when it does not perceive the convexity or concavity, it errs about the shape of that surface {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.520} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.521}.

Errors about separation and continuity also arise from the weakness and disturbance of sight, because weak and afflicted sight does not perceive the form of the object correctly. If the surface of an object has dark black lines, sight may take those lines to be cracks; and if those lines cross the whole breadth of a body, sight may take that body to be two or several bodies, according to the number of those lines, and take the blackness between them, which is the black lines, to be a separation between those bodies. Likewise, if closely packed bodies have a hidden separation between them, weak and afflicted sight does not perceive that hidden separation; and when it does not perceive it, it perceives those bodies as one continuous body, and so errs both about continuity and about number {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.521}.

Sight may also err about number from the departure of sight from equilibrium in another way, when there is a squint in the sight. When the viewer has a squint in one eye, or in both, and looks at objects, he usually perceives one object as two, especially when the squint is in one eye. A squint is a change in the position of the sight; when the position of one of the two eyes has changed from its natural position, the viewer who looks at an object with both eyes does not, for the most part, have rays of similar position meeting on that object, and rays of differing position meet on it instead. It has been shown in the first chapter of this article that when rays of differing position meet on an object, the object is perceived as two while it is one; for its image is formed in two places of differing position in the two eyes, and terminates at two places separated within the final place of sense, so that the image of the single object is formed as two images in the final place of sense, and for this reason the one object is perceived as two {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.521}.

If one eye has a squint and the other is sound, the viewer perceives each object as two, for the most part. If both eyes have a squint and their positions are not similar, their condition is also like the squint in one eye. If both eyes have a squint and their positions are similar, then when the two eyes move their positions differ, and at that time they perceive one object as two, at most; for when the two sights are not in their natural position, their positions are never similar when they move, and sometimes their positions during movement are similar, and sometimes they differ, because when the two sights lie outside their natural position their alignment is not balanced, and so they do not always keep similar positions in movement {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.522}.

When the squinting viewer perceives many objects at one time, he either perceives each of them as two, or perceives some of them, or one of them, as two; and this is the condition of the squinting viewer for the most part. When sight perceives the objects in this state, perceiving each of them as two, or some or one of them as two, it is in error about their number. Thus when sight has a squint and sees with two eyes, it is always subject to error about the number of objects. The errors about position, shape, magnitude, separation, continuity and number are errors of inference, because these attributes are perceived by inference. The cause of these errors, when they occur in the manner described, is the weakness of sight; for sight that is sound is not subject to error about any of these attributes, provided the remaining attributes of such objects are in equilibrium {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.522}.

## Article 3, chapter 7 (continued): errors of motion from dizziness, of stillness from weak sight, and the millstone

Sight may also err about motion from the departure of sight from equilibrium. When a person turns rapidly many times and then stops, he sees everything he perceives at that moment as turning, although it is still. This happens because the visual spirit within the eye, at that moment, moves in its place, turns when the person turns, and undulates. When the person who has turned rapidly stops, the motion remains in the visual spirit for an hour after his stillness, as motion remains in a body that a person has set turning in a circle and then released: when a person sets a body turning in a circle and releases it, the motion remains in that body for a time, so that the body moves without a mover, by what the mover has put into it, like a spinning top and similar things. In the same way the visual spirit, after the person has stopped turning, keeps the motion for a time; and while the motion remains in it, the viewer sees the objects as moving and turning, and when the motion in the visual spirit has stopped, the turning that sight perceives in those objects is removed. The condition of sight is the same when the person suffers the illness called dizziness {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.522} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.523}.

When a person turns very rapidly, or suffers the illness called dizziness, a circular motion arises in the visual spirit. If a circular motion arises in the visual spirit after it had been still, sight has departed from its equilibrium. If a circular motion arises in the visual spirit and it is not still in its natural state, it perceives objects as moving rapidly in a circle, because the images of the objects formed in it in that state move from part to part of the visual spirit by the motion of the spirit, and their movement through the body of the visual spirit is circular because its motion is circular. When the image moves through the parts of the visual spirit in a circle, it is like the motion of the image of an object that has a circular motion while the visual spirit is still, since the image of a circularly moving object moves in a circle through the parts of the visual spirit. Sight's perception of objects as moving in a circle while they are still, during rapid turning and during the illness called dizziness, is therefore due to the motion of the visual spirit and the motion of the images of objects within its parts, caused by its motion {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.523}.

When sight perceives still objects as moving, immediately after turning and in the condition of the illness called dizziness, it is in error about the motion of those objects. This error is an error of inference, because motion is perceived by inference. The cause of this error is the departure of sight from equilibrium, for sound sight does not perceive any object as moving in this manner, provided the remaining attributes of those objects are in equilibrium {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.524}.

Sight may also err about stillness from weakness and disturbance of sight. When an object moves in a circle, and that object has a uniform colour, a circular form, and uniform parts on its surface, weak sight does not perceive its motion, even if the circular motion is slow. This is because sight perceives circular motion only through the change of the parts of the moving thing in relation to sight, or in relation to another body, or by comparing a part of the moving thing with other bodies. When the moving thing has a circular shape, a uniform colour and a uniform appearance, its parts and the change of its parts are not perceived by sound sight except through a very thorough examination, when the motion is slow {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.524}.

As for an object of this kind moving very rapidly and swiftly, sight does not perceive it even if sight is sound. If the object has a uniform appearance, its parts do not appear to sound sight, nor does the change of its parts, except through a very thorough and complete examination and through perceiving the subtle parts in that object. Sight does not perceive circular motion if no part of the object is distinguished for it. When sight is weak and the parts of the object are uniform, the parts of the object are not distinguished for it; and when they are not distinguished, and the object's parts are uniform, sight does not perceive the change of its parts, or the alignment of one of its parts with other objects; and when sight does not perceive the parts of the moving object nor the change of its parts, it does not perceive its motion when its motion is circular and the moving thing remains in one place {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.524}.

This error always arises for weak sight when it looks at a millstone while it turns, since all these attributes are present in the millstone. Sound sight perceives the motion of the millstone from its perception of the change of its parts, and its perception of the change of its parts comes from its perception of its small parts. Weak sight does not perceive the motion of the millstone when it has uniform parts, because it does not perceive its small parts and does not perceive their change. So when weak and afflicted sight looks at a millstone while it moves and turns, it perceives it as still, and is in error about its stillness; this error is an error of inference, because stillness is perceived by inference. The cause of this error is the weakness of sight, for sound sight perceives the motion of the millstone as it is, provided the remaining attributes of that millstone are in equilibrium {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.525}.

## Article 3, chapter 7 (continued): errors of roughness, transparency, shadow, beauty and likeness from weak sight

Sight may also err about roughness and smoothness, and about transparency and density, from the departure of sight from equilibrium. Weak and afflicted sight does not perceive the subtle attributes of an object: if the object has slight roughness, weak sight does not perceive it, and when sight does not perceive the roughness of the object's surface, it takes the object to be smooth; and when sight perceives a rough object as smooth, it errs about its smoothness {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.525}.

Likewise, when weak sight looks at a smooth object that resembles one of the rough objects that the viewer knows and whose roughness he knows, it may take that smooth object to be rough; and when it takes a smooth object to be rough, it errs about its roughness {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.525}.

Likewise, when weak and afflicted sight looks at a transparent object that is strongly transparent and yet has some density, weak sight does not perceive its transparency as it is; for because of its weakness it perceives the slight density in the object as denser than it is; and when it perceives the density of the object as denser than it is, it perceives its transparency as less than it is; and when it perceives the transparency of the object as less than it is, it errs about its transparency {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.525} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.526}.

If the transparency in the object is slight, and the object has a strong colour, weak and afflicted sight does not perceive the transparency in that object, and only sound and strong sight perceives slight transparency. When weak sight does not perceive the transparency in a slightly transparent object, it perceives it as dense, because it likens it to other dense objects of that colour; and when it perceives a transparent object as dense, it errs about its density {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.526}.

The errors about roughness, smoothness, transparency and density in the ways described are errors of inference, because these attributes are perceived by inference. The cause of these errors in these ways is the weakness of sight and its departure from the equilibrium of its health, for sound sight perceives all these attributes in objects as they are, provided the remaining attributes of those objects are in equilibrium {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.526}.

Sight may also err about shadow from the departure of sight from equilibrium. When an object is partly of a bright colour and partly of a dark colour that has been dimmed, and light falls on that object, the light on its surface takes different forms because of the difference in the colours of the object. When weak sight perceives an object of this kind, it may take the dimmed dark places to be shadows caused by the refraction of the light in them; and when sight takes a bright object that has no shadow to be shaded, it errs about what it takes to be shadow {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.526}.

Likewise, when weak sight perceives walls, some of them black and some white, and all of them lit by an even light, it may take the black places to be dark places; and when it takes a black wall to be a dark place, it errs about the darkness it takes it to have {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.526}.

The errors about shadow and darkness are errors of inference, because shadow and darkness are perceived by inference. The cause of these two errors in the manner described is the weakness of sight, for when sound sight perceives objects of this kind, it perceives them as they are and does not err about them, provided the remaining attributes of those objects are in equilibrium {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.527}.

Sight may also err about beauty and ugliness, and about likeness and difference, from the departure of sight from equilibrium. When an object has apparent qualities that are good and subtle qualities that are bad, weak and afflicted sight perceives that object as beautiful, because weak sight perceives apparent qualities and does not perceive subtle ones. Likewise, when an object has ugly apparent qualities and subtle good qualities that beautify its form, weak and afflicted sight perceives that object as ugly and does not sense its beauty, because it perceives the apparent qualities by which it is ugly and does not perceive its subtle qualities by which it is beautiful {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.527}.

Likewise, when weak and afflicted sight perceives two objects that resemble one another in apparent qualities but differ in subtle ones, it perceives the two objects as alike and does not sense their difference. Likewise, when the two objects differ in apparent qualities but resemble one another in subtle ones, weak and afflicted sight perceives the two objects as alike and does not sense their difference; and when the two objects differ in apparent qualities and resemble one another in subtle ones, weak and afflicted sight perceives the two objects as different and does not sense their likeness {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.527}.

When sight perceives an ugly object as beautiful in some respect altogether, it is in error about its beauty; and when it perceives a beautiful object as ugly in some respect altogether, it is in error about its ugliness. Likewise, when sight perceives two objects that differ in some respect as altogether alike, it is in error about their likeness; and when it perceives two objects that resemble each other in some respect as altogether different, it is in error about their difference. The errors about beauty, ugliness, likeness and difference are errors of inference, because these attributes are perceived by inference; and the errors of this kind arise from sight relying only on apparent qualities and resting in their conclusions. The cause of these errors in the ways described is the departure of sight from equilibrium, for sound sight perceives all these attributes as they are, provided the remaining attributes of the objects by which objects are perceived as they are remain in equilibrium {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.528}.

Thus, on these attributes and their like, the error of sight in inference arises from the departure of the sight's health from equilibrium. From all that we have set out in these chapters it becomes clear how the error of sight in inference arises according to each of the causes by which sight is subject to error about the attributes perceived by inference {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.528}.

## Article 3, chapter 7 (continued): the closing summary of errors of sight in inference, and compound errors from two or more causes

We have now set out the division of all the kinds of errors of sight, and enumerated all the causes from which sight falls into error; and in each division of the errors we have given an example from the things that exist, which shows how the error occurs. All the errors we have mentioned are examples of the ways in which sight errs, not every error that sight makes; each example we have given stands as a kind of error. Errors of sight are many, but all of them fall under the kinds we have set out, and are divided among the examples we have given. The causes of all errors of sight in general, and their kinds and divisions, are the causes we have enumerated, and no error goes beyond the eight causes set out in detail above {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.528}.

All the errors we have mentioned are examples of errors whose causes are single, and each has one cause among the enumerated causes from which sight falls into error. Error may also arise from the combination of two, three or more of the causes we have mentioned; when that occurs the error is compound. An example: when sight glimpses an object at a varying distance, the object moving in a straight line across an interposed distance, but not very fast, and sight glimpses such an object from a varying distance and does not remain facing it except for a brief time before turning away, then it does not perceive the object's motion in the brief time, although sight has been able to perceive the motion of the same object in the same span of time from nearby. The distance that a moving object covers in a brief time, when that distance lies at a near range from sight and the object is not moving extremely slowly, can be perceived from that range in that span of time, provided the distance is not extremely small. When sight perceives the distance the moving object covers in the brief time, it perceives the motion of that moving thing in the brief time; but when the distance covered in the brief time lies at a varying distance, sight may not perceive that distance at its true measure when the distance varies {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.529}.

When sight does not perceive the distance a moving object covers in a brief time from a varying distance, it does not perceive the motion of that thing in that span of time from the varying distance; and when it does not perceive the motion of the thing in that span of time, it takes the thing to be still; and when it takes the thing to be still, it is in error about its stillness. The cause of this error is the variation of distance and the shortness of time together; for when sight remains facing that object at the varying distance for an ample time, during which the moving object covers a distance sufficient for sight to perceive it from that varying distance, sight perceives the motion of that object from that varying distance; and when the object is at a near distance from sight, sight perceives its motion in a brief time if the moving thing covers in the brief time a distance that sight can perceive from that small distance. In such a case the cause of sight's perception of a moving object from a varying distance in a brief time as still is not the varying distance alone, nor the shortness of time alone, but the variation of distance together with the shortness of time, combined {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.530}.

Likewise, when an object moves in a circle, and the object has varied colours, and its motion is rapid but not extremely rapid, and the object is in a place that is dim but not very dim, and sight glimpses that object from a short distance, sight perceives its motion in the moment of the glimpse because of the variation of its colours, since it is near; for when a thing of varied colours moves in a circle and not extremely fast, sight perceives the change of its parts quickly, even if the light on it is not strong, provided it is at a near distance from sight. But if sight withdraws from this object until it is at a considerable distance, and sight glimpses that same object from far away for a brief moment, it does not perceive its motion in that glimpse, because of the distance between them, the dimness of the place, and the brevity of the glance; for the arc that each part of the circularly moving object traverses in a brief time is small in extent, and when the arc traversed by a part of the object is small, and the light on the object is weak, and the distance is far, sight in the brief glance does not perceive the small arc that the circularly moving thing covers in the time of that glance {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.530}.

When sight considers an object of this kind, remains facing it for a time that has room in it, and examines it thoroughly, it may perceive the object's motion from far away, because the arc that the moving object covers in the ample time is of considerable extent, so sight can perceive it from far away despite the dimness of the place, provided the place is not extremely dim. If the light in the place is strong, and sight then glimpses that object from the same far distance from which it first glimpsed it without perceiving its motion, it may be able to perceive its motion while the light on it is strong; for the arc that the moving thing covers in the time of the glance that sight did not perceive because of weak light, together with the variation of distance and the shortness of time, can be perceived by sight in strong light even if the distance from which it is perceived is far and the time is short. For small objects that sight does not perceive in dim places may be perceived in strong light from the very same distances at which sight did not perceive them in dim places, and in the very same spans of time in which it did not perceive them in dim places {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.531}.

When sight glimpses a moving object turning from a far distance in faint light, and does not perceive its motion in the moment of the glimpse, it takes it in that moment to be still; and when sight takes that object to be still, while the object is in fact moving, it is in error about its stillness. The cause of this error is the combination of the variation of distance, the shortness of time, and the weakness of the light on the object; for sight may perceive the object's motion with one or two of these causes combined. It perceives its motion in the moment of the glance despite the weakness of light on it if it glimpses it from a near distance; it may perceive its motion from far away despite weak light if it remains facing it for a brief time; and it may perceive its motion from far away in the moment of the glance if the light on it is strong {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.531}.

Thus, when that is so, the cause for which sight perceives such a moving object as still in the way we have described is the combination of the three causes we have described, not any one of them alone, nor two of them together, but the three causes combined. It has therefore been shown from what we have mentioned that the error of sight may arise from the combination of two or more of the causes from which sight falls into error; error may arise from a single cause or from more than one cause. But all the causes from which sight falls into error, whether one or more, do not go outside the eight causes that we have set out in detail {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.531}.

## Article 3, chapter 7 (continued): the forms of visible things, their particular attributes, and the classification of errors of sight

Errors of sight about particular attributes are of the kind we have set out and exemplified in all the kinds of error. The forms of all visible things are compounded of particular attributes. When sight errs about one particular attribute in a visible thing, or about more than one of the particular attributes in visible things, it errs about the form of that visible thing; and when sight errs about the form of a visible thing, it errs about one particular attribute in that form, or about more than one of the attributes in it {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.531}.

Sight errs in what it perceives of the form of a visible thing only through its error about one particular attribute in that thing, or about several particular attributes in it; for sight perceives of visible things only the particular attributes that occur in them. Likewise, when sight errs in recognising a visible thing, its error arises from likening the attributes in that thing, or some of the attributes in it, to attributes that sight has perceived before in that same thing or in other visible things of its kind {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.532}.

All errors about particular attributes are either an error of mere sense, an error of recognition, an error of inference, an error in the combination of these three, or an error in two of them combined. No error of sight about particular attributes falls outside these kinds. So the errors of sight in every form of visible thing that it errs about may be an error of mere sense, may be an error of recognition, may be an error of inference, may be an error in the three kinds together, or may be an error in two of them together. No error of sight about the form of a visible thing falls outside these divisions. All errors in the three kinds we have mentioned arise only from sight's error about the particular attributes present in the forms of visible things {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.532}.

It has therefore been shown that all errors of sight about all particular attributes arise from the causes we have enumerated, either from one of them or from more than one. All the errors of sight in every form of visible thing it perceives, and in every particular attribute in those forms taken singly, are divided into the kinds we have set out, occur to sight in the examples we have given, and have their causes gathered under the causes we have enumerated. This is where we close this article {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.532}.

## Article 2, chapter 3 (continued): how particular attributes become recognised, and the twenty-two kinds

From all that has been explained it is clear that some of the attributes perceived by sight are perceived by bare sense alone, some by recognition, and some by discrimination and inference that go beyond recognition; and that what is perceived by discrimination and inference beyond recognition, once sight has perceived it repeatedly and its understanding has settled in the mind, is afterwards perceived by recognition. The manner in which the particular attributes perceived by sight are perceived does not in most cases appear, because what is perceived by recognition is quick, and the inference by which visible attributes are perceived in most cases is quick too; and the discriminating faculty is stamped with these measures, using them not by thought and effort but by nature and habit {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.230}.

Moreover, from childhood and from the beginning of a person's life, and over the passage of time, he perceives visible things, and his perception of visible things is repeated; so there is no particular attribute perceived by sight that sight has not perceived repeatedly. All the particular attributes perceived by inference have therefore become understood by the discriminating faculty and settled in the mind, so that the discriminating faculty perceives every particular attribute repeated in visible things by recognition and habit, and needs no renewed inference for any particular attribute repeated in visible things {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.230}.

Moreover, the attributes repeated in visible things and perceived by discrimination and inference settle in the mind without the person sensing their settling, and there is no perceptible beginning to their settling; for from childhood a person perceives visible things, and from childhood he has some discrimination, especially the discrimination by which sensible attributes are perceived. He perceives sensible attributes by discrimination and inference, and acquires knowledge of sensible attributes; and sensible attributes are repeated to him over the passage of time, so that their meanings settle in his mind without his sensing their settling {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.230} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.231}.

So the particular attribute that he perceived by discrimination and inference, and that then settled in his mind through its frequent repetition in visible things, is perceived by recognition at the moment it comes to him, and yet he does not sense the manner of his perceiving it, nor the manner of his recognising it, nor how the knowledge of that attribute settled in his mind. All the particular attributes perceived by inference and discrimination and repeated in visible things have been perceived by the person over time, their meanings have settled in the mind, and each particular attribute has come to have a universal form settled in the mind; so he perceives these attributes in visible things by recognition and habit, without renewing the discrimination and inference by which he first grasped the reality of that attribute, without sensing the manner of their perception at the moment of perceiving, and without sensing the manner of their recognition at the moment of recognising. Nothing remains that needs renewed inference and discrimination beyond recognition, except the particular attributes that belong to particular individuals, such as the shape of a specific visible thing, the position of a specific visible thing, the magnitude of a specific visible thing, the comparison of the colour of one visible thing with the colour of another, the comparison of one particular form with another, and what is like these in particular individuals. On these terms is the perception of all the particular attributes that occur in visible things {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.231}.

Now that all this has been made clear, we begin to explain the manner in which each of the particular attributes perceived by sight is perceived, and the measures by which the discriminating faculty acquires the attributes perceived by sight {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.232}.

The particular attributes perceived by sight are many; they fall in general into twenty-two kinds: light, colour, distance, position, solidity, shape, magnitude, separation, continuity, number, motion, stillness, roughness, smoothness, transparency, density, shadow, darkness, beauty, ugliness, likeness and difference, as they are in the particular attributes taken singly and in the forms compounded of particular attributes. These are all the attributes perceived by sight. If there is anything in visible things other than these attributes, it falls under some of them: arrangement falls under position; writing and designs fall under shape and arrangement; straightness, bending, convexity and concavity, which belong to configuration, fall under shape; plurality and paucity fall under number; equality and excess fall under likeness and difference; laughter, cheer, openness, frowning and scowling, which are perceived by sight from the configuration of the face, fall under shape; weeping, which is perceived from the configuration of the face together with the motion of tears, falls under shape and motion; moisture and dryness fall under motion and stillness, because moisture is perceived by sight only through the flow of a moist body and the motion of some of its parts before others, and dryness is perceived by sight only through the cohesion of a dry body and the absence of flowing motion in it. Thus every particular attribute perceived by sight, when the manner of sight's perception of it is distinguished, is found to fall under some of the kinds we have mentioned and the attributes we have set out {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.232}.

All visible attributes are perceived from the images that arise in sight from the images of the colours and lights of visible things. It has been shown that the image of the light and colour on the surface of a visible thing arises on the surface of the glacial humour, and arises there in order as it is ordered on the surface of the visible thing; that the images extend from this surface and pass through the body of the glacial humour, and pass into the sensing body within the cavity of the common nerve, and in their extension they remain in the order they have on the surface of the glacial humour; and that they reach the cavity of the common nerve in their form and order, as they are on the surface of the glacial humour and on the surface of the visible thing. The final sense perceives the image of visible things from the images that arise in the cavity of the common nerve. It has been shown that sensation is completed only by the final sense's perception of the images of visible things. When all this is so, the discrimination and inference that the discriminating faculty performs on the attributes in the images of visible things, the recognition of the forms, the recognition of the signs in the forms, and all that is perceived by discrimination, inference and recognition, arise from the discriminating faculty's distinguishing of the images that arise in the cavity of the common nerve at the moment the final sense perceives them, and from the recognition of the signs in these images that are perceived in this way {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.232} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.233}.

Moreover, the sensing body that extends from the surface of the sensing organ to the cavity of the common nerve, which is the visual spirit, is sensitive throughout; so the sensitive faculty is in the whole of this body. When the image extends from the surface of the sensing organ to the cavity of the common nerve, every part of the sensing body senses the image; when the image has arrived in the cavity of the common nerve, the final sense perceives it, and at that point discrimination and inference occur. The sensitive faculty senses the image of the visible thing from the whole sensing body that extends from the surface of the sensing organ to the cavity of the common nerve, and the discriminating faculty distinguishes the attributes in the image when the final sense perceives the image. On this manner is the perception of the sensitive faculty, the final sense and the discriminating faculty of the images of visible things. It is evident from this state that the sensitive faculty senses the place in the sensing organ in which the image arises, because it senses the image from the place in which the image arises {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.233}.

## Article 2, chapter 3 (continued): the image compounded of light and colour, and the quiddity of colour and light

Moreover, it has been shown in the preceding chapter that from every point on the surface of the glacial humour the image extends along one and the same continuous line, with its bends and curvatures, until it reaches one particular point of the place in the cavity of the common nerve where the image arises. When each point of the glacial surface sends its image to one particular point of the cavity of the common nerve, the image arising at a part of the glacial surface extends from that part to one particular part of the cavity of the common nerve. Different visible things that sight perceives together at one time each extend their image to a particular place in the cavity of the common nerve, the images of all those visible things arise together in the cavity of the common nerve, and the order of their images there, one beside another, is like the order of the visible things themselves that sight perceives together, one beside another {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.233}.

When sight faces a visible thing, the images of the light and colour in that thing arise on the surface of the sight and on the surface of the glacial humour, and extend along the particular directions defined above, in their form and order, until they reach the cavity of the common nerve. The sensitive faculty perceives them when they arise in the body of the glacial humour and when they arise in the whole sensing body; then, on reaching the cavity of the common nerve, the final sense perceives them, and the discriminating faculty distinguishes all the attributes in them. The image of the coloured sight that arises in the cavity of the nerve is the image of the colour of the visible thing and of the light in it, arranged as the surface of the visible thing is arranged. The images of colour and light reach the cavity of the nerve because the sensing body extending into the cavity of the nerve takes on the colour of the colour-image and is lit by the light-image. The image terminates in the cavity of the common nerve, so that the part of the sensing body in the cavity of the common nerve at which the image of the visible thing ends becomes coloured with the colour of that thing and lit with the light in that thing. If the visible thing has one colour, that part of the sensing body is of one colour; if the parts of the visible thing differ in colour, the corresponding parts of the sensing body in the cavity of the common nerve differ in colour. The final sense perceives the colour of the coloured visible thing from the colouring it finds in that part, and perceives the light in the visible thing from the illumination it finds in that part. The discriminating faculty perceives most of the particular attributes in the visible thing by distinguishing the attributes in that image: the arrangement of the parts of the image, the shape of the image's outline, the shapes of its parts, the differences of colour, positions and arrangements in its parts, their likeness and difference, and what is like these attributes among those that occur in visible things {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.234}.

Moreover, the light that reaches sight from a coloured visible thing does not reach it separately from the colour, and the image of the colour that reaches sight from a coloured visible thing does not reach it separately from the light. The images of the light and colour in a coloured visible thing reach sight only compounded, and the final sense perceives them only compounded. Nevertheless, sense perceives that the visible thing is luminous, that the light appearing in it is other than the colour, and that these are two attributes. This perception is discrimination, and discrimination belongs to the discriminating faculty, not to the sensitive faculty. Yet once the discriminating faculty has perceived this attribute, it has settled in the mind and needs no renewed discrimination and inference when each image arrives; rather, it is settled in the mind that every image compounded of light and colour has a light in it that is other than the colour in it {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.234} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.235}.

The discriminating faculty's recognition that the accidental light in a coloured visible thing is other than the colour in it arises from the fact that one and the same visible thing may be subject to different lights, its light may increase or decrease, and yet its colour remains one colour; the colour's brightness varies with the lights upon it, but the kind of colour does not vary. Moreover, the accidental light that arises in a visible thing may reach it through an opening or a door, and when that opening is blocked or that door is shut the visible thing becomes dark and no light remains in it. From the discriminating faculty's perception of the variation of lights on visible things, and of the illumination of a visible thing at some times and its absence at other times, it perceives that the colours of visible things are other than the lights that fall upon them; and from the repetition of this attribute across visible things it settles in the mind as a general understanding that the colours of coloured visible things are other than the lights in them {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.235}.

The image that sense perceives from a coloured visible thing is an image compounded of the image of the light and the image of the colour in the visible thing; it is a coloured light, and the discriminating faculty perceives that the colour in it is other than the light in it. This perception is perception by recognition at the moment the image reaches sense, because it has settled in the mind that every image compounded of light and colour has a light in it that is other than the colour in it {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.235}.

The first of the attributes proper to a coloured image that the discriminating faculty perceives is the quiddity of the colour. The quiddity of a colour is perceived by the discriminating faculty through recognition when the colour in the visible thing is a familiar colour. The discriminating faculty's perception of the quiddity of a colour by recognition comes from comparing the image of the colour with the images of similar colours it has perceived before, and from recalling those images. When sight perceives the red colour and perceives that it is red, it perceives that it is red because it recognises it, and its recognition of it comes from likening its image to the similar images it has perceived before. Had sight not perceived red before perceiving this red, it would not have known, at the perception of this red, that it is red. When the colour is a familiar colour, sight perceives its quiddity by recognition; when the colour is a strange colour and sight has not perceived its like before, sight does not perceive its quiddity; and when it does not perceive its quiddity and does not recognise it, it likens it to the nearest colours it knows. The first perception of a colour is by bare sense; then, when the colour has been repeated to sight, sight's perception of which colour it is becomes recognition {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.235} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.236}.

The quiddity of light is also perceived by sight through recognition. Sight recognises sunlight and distinguishes it from moonlight and from firelight; likewise it recognises moonlight and firelight; so sight's perception of the quiddity of each of these lights is by recognition {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.236}.

The strength and weakness of light is perceived by sight through discrimination and inference, and by comparing the image of the light it perceives at the moment with the images of lights it has perceived before {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.236}.
