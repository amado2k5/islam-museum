## Summary
"Kitab al-Manazir" (The Book of Optics) by [[person/ibn-al-haytham|Ibn al-Haytham]], his most important book on [[concept/optics|optics]]. In it he studies the properties of light in its three states: its radiation in straight lines, its reflection and its refraction, through experiment together with mathematical methods {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.9}.

## Divisions of the book
The editor of the edition holds that the book divides into two parts. The first deals with the radiation of light and the seeing of visible objects in straight lines, which is the subject of the first three treatises. The second deals with reflection and refraction and what follows from them in the perception of visible objects in mirrors and transparent bodies, which is the subject of the last four treatises {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.10}.

## On the edition
The Arabic text available to us is the first volume of the edition by ʿAbd al-Hamid Sabra, which includes the first three treatises. It was published in Kuwait in 1983 in the heritage series. The details of the edition have not yet been verified from an independent source {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.5}.

## Article 1: Vision in General

The first volume of Sabra's edition carries the full text of Article 1, "On Vision in General," in eight chapters {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.60}. In the book's opening the author surveys earlier opinion: the natural philosophers held that vision occurs by a form passing from the seen object to the eye, while the people of demonstration — Euclid and Ptolemy foremost — held that vision occurs by a ray leaving the centre of the eye for the object, a cone whose apex is the eye's centre and whose base is the object's surface, though they disagreed on the ray's nature and origin {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.61} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.62}. The author then states his method: induction over observed particulars, then ascending by degrees in inquiry and demonstration, "seeking the truth, not inclining with opinions" {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.63}.

In Chapter 3, "On the Properties of Lights and the Manner of Their Radiation," he sets out some 144 inductive propositions proving that light radiates only along straight lines: light admitted through a hole into a darkened room is stopped where an opaque body interrupts the straight path; starlight seen in the room moves with its star; shadows extend in straight lines {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.73} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.74} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.75}. He shows that light's strength grows with the visible portion of its source, as at sunrise and during an eclipse {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.76}, and that colours accompany light {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.121}. Chapter 4 then treats what happens between sight and light — brilliance, glare and the eye's fatigue, so that a viewer of strong light retains an image of it after looking away {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.122} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.123}. Chapter 5 describes the eye's structure — layered tunics, membranes and bodies arising from the brain's anterior — and Chapter 6 the manner of vision, the light in the seen object falling upon the eye's surface and affecting it {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.128} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.138}. Chapter 7 explains the use of those layers as the instruments by which vision is completed, the first of them the cornea {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.184}; and Chapter 8 closes with the conditions without whose combination vision cannot occur: distance, facing, light, a size fit for the power of sensation, a transparent continuous medium, and the object's opacity {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.190}.

## Article 2: The Details of the Meanings Sight Perceives, Their Causes, and the Manner of Perceiving Them

Article 2 follows Article 1 in four chapters {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.198} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.200}. Its opening establishes that sight perceives many meanings from visible objects besides light and colour, that perception does not occur in the same way at all times and for all objects, and that it sets out to distinguish the states of the ray-lines, to separate all the meanings sight perceives, to show how each one is perceived, and how sight distinguishes visible objects {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.200} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.201}.

In Chapter 2, "On Distinguishing the Ray-Lines," he reaffirms that the ray-lines by which sight perceives objects are the straight lines extended from the centre of sight to the eye's surface {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.201}, then shows that the vitreous humour's surface — the common boundary between the vitreous and the last sensitive body — is either plane or spherical with a centre other than the centre of sight {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.205}, and that the images ordered on the ray-lines at the vitreous humour are refracted at that surface along lines crossing them {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.205} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.210}. The point falling on the central ray is clearest and most certain, and what is nearer to it is more certain than what is farther {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.215} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.217}.

Chapter 3, "On Visible Objects Other Than Light and Colour," separates the meanings sight perceives beyond light and colour: many are perceived by mere sensation; some by acquaintance, as the recognition of familiar written words; and some by a distinguishing measure beyond acquaintance, as perceiving that the whole is greater than the part {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.222} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.226} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.230}. A meaning repeated until its understanding settles in the soul comes thereafter to be perceived by acquaintance {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.230}. Chapter 4 closes the article with the manner of perceiving each of the partial meanings grasped by the sense of sight, the images of visible objects being compounded of those meanings {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.234} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.309}.

## Article 3: Errors of Sight in What It Perceives Straight On, and Their Causes

The third treatise opens with its title and states that it has seven chapters {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.340} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.341}. In its opening chapter Ibn al-Haytham argues that sight does not perceive every visible object as it is, and not every meaning that the viewer believes he has grasped is grasped correctly. Sight often errs in what it perceives, and sometimes the viewer senses the error and sometimes not {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.342}.

He gives examples. A visible object seen from a varying distance appears smaller than its true size, and one seen very close appears larger. A square or many-sided figure seen from a distance looks round if its diameters are equal and long if they differ, and a sphere seen from a varying distance looks flat {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.343}. A moving star appears still, and a person moving very slowly appears still if the gaze is brief and the viewer has no prior knowledge of the motion {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.343}.

The second chapter, on what must be set out before explaining errors of sight, begins {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.344}. It holds that sight perceives nothing except through the ray-lines, and that a single object seen with both eyes is perceived as one when its position relative to the two eyes is alike, and as two when it differs {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.344}. The two eyes are equal in their states and actions: when one moves to see, the other moves with the same movement, and they separate only through an obstacle or a screen {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.345}.

He imagines a cone between each visible object and the centre of sight, with its apex at the centre and its base on the object's surface. The point where the two ray-lines meet has the same position for both eyes, since it faces the middle of the two eyes {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.345}. Points close to the meeting point lie at equal distances from the ray-lines as far as sensation can judge, and the difference in the oblique lines matters to sensation only in proportion to how far the object lies from the meeting point {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.346}. When the two eyes move over the object and the ray-lines travel along its diameters, the image of each part is formed at two positions of like arrangement, and the images of all its parts become alike for both eyes {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.347} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.348}.

He then takes up separate objects perceived at the same time. The object on which the ray-lines rest has its image formed at two corresponding positions, while objects farther from it are alike in direction but not in distance, so their images remain confused until the eyes move and the ray-lines settle on each of them {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.348} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.349}. He then shows that the common ray-line passes through the centres of the layers of sight, and that the two corresponding images end at one point in the cavity of the common nerve {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.349} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.350} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.351}. ## Article 3, chapter 2 (continued): how one object seen by two eyes becomes one image

Here Ibn al-Haytham shows that the two images formed in the two eyes of one object on which the ray-lines meet end at one point in the cavity of the common nerve, where they coincide and become a single image {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.351} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.352}. The images of the points around the meeting point fall in corresponding positions and coincide as well, so sensation perceives the object as one although it has two images in the two eyes {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.352}.

He shows that the point on the common ray-line, where the two lines from the centres of the bone holes meet, remains one fixed point, and he names it the centre {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.353}. An object on the common ray-line has a sharper image than one beside it. A small object near the common ray-line gives one clear image, while a large, wide object has its part around the meeting point clear and its edges and borders confused {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.353} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.354}.

An object outside the common ray-line and at a distance gives two images that overlap or stand apart, depending on direction and distance, and they remain confused until the eyes are brought toward it and the ray-lines settle on it {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.355} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.356}. An object lying on one ray-line and outside the other gives two images, one on the centre and the other inclined from it, which do not coincide {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.356}.

He then turns to a test to verify all this. The board is of light polished wood, as long as a forearm bone and four fingers wide, with two crossing lines and a round hole at the middle of its width that takes the bridge of the nose. Three small white wax figures, each a different colour, stand on it, one at the centre and two at the ends of the crosswise line {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.357}. When the observer stares at the middle figure, both ray-lines meet on it; when he stares at an end figure, they meet there; in each case he notes by sight the repetition of figures and lines on the board, to confirm what has been said {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.358}. ## Article 3, the board test (continued): repeated figures and lines

The description of the test continues: the observer moves two of the wax figures to different places on the board, then stares at the middle figure. Figures not on the single middle line appear doubled, and the effect changes with each figure's place on the two lines {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.358} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.359}. The figures and lines are seen as described so long as the gaze stays on the middle figure or on a point of the crosswise line; looking at a figure off that line makes the middle figure appear double too {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.359} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.360}.

He then gives a geometric proof with lettered figures. A figure near the meeting point receives the rays from both eyes along lines nearly equal in length, so it is seen as one. A figure far from the common line has two angles that differ noticeably, so it is seen as two {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.360} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.361}.

He sums up at the end of the chapter. The object on which the ray-lines meet is always seen as one. An object whose rays are alike in direction but differ in distance is seen as two, and so is an object whose rays differ in direction even when their lengths are equal. Familiar objects are seen as one because they face both eyes and their rays are alike in direction and near in distance; seeing two is rare {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.362} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.363}.

He ends with what the observer sees when he lifts the middle figure and looks at the crossing point in the middle of the board. He sees four lines, two near together and two apart. With one eye covered he sees two lines, and the gap between them looks wider than it is. He explains the close pair by the nearby eye, whose rays lie near the ray-line, and the apart pair by the oblique rays from the other eye, and he explains the apparent widening by the fact that each eye perceives the gap between the lines larger than its true size {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.363}. ## Article 3, the board test (continued again): the widening gap and the position of the object

Ibn al-Haytham shows that when the observer lifts the middle figure and looks at the crossing point, he sees four lines, two near and two apart, and each apart line appears farther from the middle than it truly is {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.363}. With one eye covered he sees two lines, with a wider gap than the true one. He explains this by the gap between the lines lying very close to the eye, and whatever lies very close is seen larger than it is; the full cause is to be shown in its place, in the discussion of errors of sight {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.364}.

From the lines and the figures fixed on them he concludes that an object lying on the common ray-line and seen by a ray-line is perceived in its true place, whether seen with one eye or both. An object off the common line is perceived nearer to that line than its true place, and the rest of its points are perceived the same way because they join the part at the end of the ray-line {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.364}. The same holds for an object off the common line at which the two ray-lines meet, though that arrangement is rare, since the common ray-line usually passes through the object. It does not show in familiar objects, because a shift of one object relative to its neighbours is not noticed {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.364} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.365}.

He then proposes a test with paper. Three small equal slips are cut, each bearing a clear word; one is fixed at the middle figure, and another at a second point on the board. Staring at the middle slip, the word on it is seen more clearly than its neighbour {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.365}. As the side slip is moved from the middle, its word becomes less clear with each step, until it cannot be understood {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.366} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.367}.

From these tests he concludes that the clearest objects facing the eye and seen with both eyes are those at the meeting point of the ray-lines; objects nearer that point are clearer than those farther; a wide object is seen clearly only when the ray-line moves over all its parts; and a steady eye facing a wide object sees clearly only what lies on the ray-line and near it, seeing the rest less clearly {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.367}. He then begins a further test with paper bearing fine lines of text, in which the observer reads the centre of the sheet more clearly than its surroundings {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.367}. ## Article 3, the paper test: facing and tilt

Ibn al-Haytham returns to the paper bearing fine lines of text. Set at the centre and stared at with both eyes, it reads clearly, but the writing at its centre is clearer than at its edges so long as the gaze stays on the centre and does not move over all its parts {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.367}. When the sheet is tilted across the crosswise line until the writing falls on the meeting point, its clarity falls. The greater the tilt, the greater the confusion, until the sheet nears the line along the middle of the board and the text cannot be read {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.367} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.368}.

He repeats the test with one eye. Covering one eye, returning the sheet to its first position and tilting it, the clarity of the text drops at each step, until the sheet nears the diagonal next to the viewing eye and the observer can no longer read it {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.368} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.369}. From this he concludes that an object set straight in front is clearer than an oblique one, that what lies near the facing position is clearer than what lies farther from it, and that an object at an uneven tilt is confused whether seen with two eyes or one {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.369}.

He then returns the central figure to the board and sets the sheet on one half of the crosswise line in the facing position. The part of the writing next to the central figure is clear, and the part farther from it is confused and hard to see {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.369} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.370}. He repeats the test along the width of the board and at its end, with the same result, and finds that the distance at which the outer word becomes confused stands in one ratio to its distance from the eye in both places {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.370} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.371}.

He also notes that when the sheet is held farther from the eye than the length of the board and tilted slightly and then more, its clarity falls at each step until the writing is confused. When one slip is moved away from the middle slip, the word on it becomes more confused, until it is no longer visible {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.371} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.372}. The summary opens at the foot of page 371 and continues on page 372: at every distance the clearest objects are those on the ray-line; what lies near the ray-line is clearer than what lies farther; an object far from the ray-line at an uneven distance is confused, whether seen with one eye or two; and an object facing the eye is clearer than an inclined one at every distance {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.371} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.372}.

## Article 3, chapter 2 (end): steep tilt and facing

Ibn al-Haytham shows that an object at a steep tilt has its image gathered on the surface of the eye, because the angle it subtends at the centre of sight is small; its small parts become insensible, and sight does not perceive its subtle meanings {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.372} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.373}. An object facing the eye has its image arranged in the eye as its parts are arranged on its surface, so its small parts are clear {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.373}.

He holds that subtle meanings and the arrangement of an object's parts are perceived with certainty only when the image is impressed on the surface of the sensing organ and each part of it falls on a sensible part of that surface; a steeply tilted object is not impressed in this way {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.373}. He shows that perceiving the true size of a steeply tilted object comes not from its image alone but from a measure outside the image: the sense perceives the difference in distance of its two ends {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.374}.

He concludes that a steeply tilted object is confused because its image is gathered and its small parts are hidden, while an object at a moderate distance is clear because its image is impressed as it is and sensed in its small parts {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.374}.

## Article 3, chapter 3: the causes of error in sight

Chapter 3 opens with its title, "on the causes for which error comes to sight". Sight perceives nothing straight on unless several conditions are met for the object: distance, facing, light, a sufficient size, density or some density, transparency of the air between object and eye with no dense body in between, time, and the health of the eye, free of ailments and obstacles {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.374} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.375}. Elsewhere he gathers these into eight: distance, the particular position, light, sufficient size, density, transparency of the air, time, and the health of the eye {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.375}.

Each condition has a measure of moderation. While each stays within its measure, sight perceives the object as it is; if one or more exceed it by excess, sight does not perceive the object as it is. An object very far or very near is not perceived correctly, and between these limits there are distances at which sight perceives correctly without confusion, which differ by object {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.375} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.376}. ## Article 3, chapter 3 (continued): the conditions for correct perception

The remaining conditions are set out here. A small object may have its image hidden, or confused, at a distance where the image of a large one is not, and a strongly lit object may be perceived at a distance where a faintly lit one is hidden {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.375} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.376}. An object at an uneven tilt from the middle of sight, or one that the ray-line neither meets nor approaches, is not perceived correctly. An object facing the middle of sight, with the ray-line on or near one of its points, is perceived correctly if it is not wide in extent, even when the ray-line has not moved over all its diameters {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.376} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.377}.

The slight tilt or slight distance from the ray-line at which an object's true form is still perceived depends on its subtle meanings. An object without subtle meanings is perceived truly even a little off the ray-line; one with subtle meanings may be confused at that same distance {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.377} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.378}.

Light that is too faint, too strong, or thrown on a polished surface prevents correct perception, while between the extremes lies a range of lights in which it occurs; the suitable light depends on the object's meanings and size {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.377} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.378}. An object of extreme smallness with subtle meanings and distinct parts, such as very small animals whose distinct organs exceed the power of sight, is not seen correctly; a larger object whose organs are proportionate to it is seen fully {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.378}.

On density and air: an object of very slight density is not perceived correctly, while a clearly dense one is. A thick, murky air such as fog, mist or smoke prevents the perception of fine objects, while clear and uniformly transparent air does not, and a slight thickness does not hinder the perception of objects that are not extremely small or finely detailed {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.379} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.380}. On motion: an object moving very swiftly, like a glimpse through a hole, is not perceived in its motion or true form; a whirling object moving very fast appears still, and a very slow motion appears still over a measurable time {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.380} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.381}. On the eye: a damaging ailment prevents correct perception, while a slight affliction does not hinder the perception of objects that are neither extremely small nor finely detailed {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.381}.

He concludes the chapter by stating that each condition has a measure of moderation, and that departing from it by excess, in the upward or downward direction, prevents correct perception. This holds for distance, position, light, size, density, air, time and the health of the eye alike, and any position that does not allow correct perception is outside moderation {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.381}.

## Article 3, chapter 3 (continued): the measure of moderation

Ibn al-Haytham calls the range within which sight perceives an object as it is the measure of moderation, and defines it as the range in which there is no perceptible difference, affecting its true form, between the image sight takes of the object and its real image {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.381} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.382}. The extent of this range for each condition differs by object, and depends on the object's colour, its subtle meanings, and the other conditions, so that distance, position, light, size, density, air, time and the eye's health each have their own measure.

He details this for distance. A white, bright object shows its true form from a greater distance than a dull, earthy-coloured one that is alike in all other respects. An object without subtle meanings is perceived from farther off than one with fine meanings; an object in front of the eye, or on the meeting point of the ray-lines, is perceived from farther than an inclined one; a brightly lit object is perceived from farther than a dim one; a large object from farther than a small one; a thick or lightly dense object from farther than a very transparent one; and clear, uniformly transparent air permits a farther distance than thick or obstructing air {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.382}.

Time works in the same way. A near object is perceived in its true form in less time than a distant one, especially when it has subtle meanings. When the time for examining an object is limited, the range of distance within which it can be perceived correctly narrows. A strong and healthy eye perceives an object's true form from a greater distance than a weak or obstructed one {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.383} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.384}.

He then states that the measure of moderation for position, light, size, density, transparency of the air, time and the eye's health each depends on the same factors, namely the colour of the object, its subtle meanings, its distance and the remaining conditions, so that each has its own range. He lists these in detail on the following pages {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.384} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.384}.

## Article 3, chapter 3 (end): the cause of error

Ibn al-Haytham shows that the measure of moderation for each condition is fixed by the other conditions present in the object. When every condition by which perception is completed lies within its measure in relation to the object, sight perceives it as it is; when one or more lies outside its measure, sight perceives it otherwise {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.385}. He concludes that the cause of all errors of sight is that one or more of these conditions lies outside its measure, and that error is the perception of an object otherwise than it is {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.386} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.387}.

## Article 3, chapter 4: distinguishing the errors of sight

Chapter 4 opens with its title, "on distinguishing the errors of sight". Sight perceives objects in three ways: by mere sensation, by knowledge, and by inference and discrimination at the moment of seeing. An error in the first is an error in sensation itself; an error in the second is an error of knowledge; an error in the third is an error of inference or discrimination, or of the premises from which the inference was drawn {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.386}. The second treatise showed that what sight perceives by mere sensation is light as light and colour as colour {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.386}.

Knowledge, he says, builds through repeated perception. Familiar lights, such as those of the sun, the moon and fire, and familiar colours were first perceived by sensation, then recognized by sight through repetition. Familiar compound forms, such as the shapes of animals, fruits, plants and tools, and general meanings such as roundness, straightness, triangularity and squareness, smoothness and roughness, and the general form of a human or of a tree, were first perceived by inference and discrimination, and after their forms settle in the mind they are recognized by knowledge and by signs alone {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.386} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.387}. What is perceived by inference and discrimination at the moment of sensation is a compound form not yet repeated, and the particular meanings of particular individuals {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.387}.

He holds that the errors of sight are of three kinds: error in mere sensation, error in knowledge, and error in discrimination and inference {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.388}. An example of error in mere sensation is a body with strong colours, such as dark blue, wine-red and purple, in an extremely dark place where only faint light falls; the viewer sees it as one dark colour and does not grasp its colours. The cause is light falling below its measure by excess of deficiency; once the object is brought into moderate light, its colours appear distinct {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.388}. An example of error in knowledge is seeing from a great distance a person who resembles Zayd, whom the viewer knows well, and taking him for Zayd when he is not {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.388}; the explanation continues on the following pages.

## Article 3, chapter 4 (continued): examples of the three kinds of error

Ibn al-Haytham explains the cause of the error of knowledge in the example of the man resembling Zayd. A nearby person is not confused with someone the viewer knows well, provided the other conditions remain within their measure; the error occurs when the distance of the person lies outside its measure {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.388} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.389}.

He gives an example of error in inference and discrimination at the moment of seeing: the moon appears to move swiftly when a thin, broken, irregularly shaped cloud crosses its face and itself moves swiftly. Motion is perceived only by comparison at the moment of sensation, so sight compares the moon with the parts of the cloud that change before it and takes the change to be the moon's motion, not the cloud's. A cloud covering the sky appears at once to be still, and its motion shows only after the viewer examines its edges over a measurable time and compares them with fixed objects. The cause is the distance at which the moon lies outside its measure; the error does not occur for objects that are near {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.389} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.390}.

He concludes that the errors of sight occur in these three ways, and that all of them fall into these three kinds {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.390}.

## Article 3, chapter 5: errors by mere sensation

Chapter 5 opens with its title, "on the manner of the errors of sight that occur by mere sensation according to each of the causes", and reminds the reader that sight perceives by sensation alone only light as light and colour as colour. An error in light can only be one of strength and weakness, since sight perceives from light only its brightness {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.390} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.391}. As for colour, the second treatise showed that sight perceives from it only a colouring resembling darkness or shade, so the error in it too can only be one of strength and weakness {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.391}.

He shows that an object of many colours, all strong and closely alike, or all faint and closely alike, may be seen as a single colour, because sight perceives from them only a darkness or a shade that is the same, and takes it for one colour {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.391}. When the colours are partly strong and partly faint, sight perceives them as neighbouring darkness and shade, so that the object appears to have two colours although it has many; the same error arises when its differently coloured parts are small {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.391}.

## Article 3, chapter 5 (continued): many colours and small parts

Here he sets out the error about many colours when the differently coloured parts of an object are very small. Sight does not perceive each part alone, so their colours are not distinguished, and it perceives the whole object as one colour. If some of the parts are small and some large, sight perceives the difference of the large parts and not the colours of the small ones {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.391} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.392}. He concludes that error by mere sensation occurs only in light as light and colour as colour, and that the reality of a colour is grasped by knowledge rather than by sensation, so an error in naming a colour's reality is an error of knowledge {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.392}.

He shows how sensory error arises for each cause. Error from the distance of an object lying outside its measure: take a large object of many colours whose coloured parts are small, at an uneven distance. Sight perceives it as one indistinct colour, because at that distance each part may be hidden alone while the whole remains visible. At a moderate distance sight perceives each part alone, with its colour as it is {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.392} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.393}.

Error from the position of the object lying outside its measure occurs in an object of strong, closely alike colours tilted unevenly from the middle of sight and far from the ray-line. Sight perceives it as one colour, because an oblique and uneven image is confused and its parts are not distinguished. When the ray-line is moved until it falls on the object and passes over each of its points, sight perceives the difference of its colours {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.393} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.394}.

He begins the account of error from light lying outside its measure with the example of the object of strong, closely alike colours seen in the light of a weak fire {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.394}; the account continues in the following pages.

## Article 3, chapter 5 (end): sensory error from light, size, density, air, time and the eye

Here he sets out examples of sensory error for each cause. A body of many colours, strong and closely alike, seen in the light of a weak fire appears entirely dark, and the viewer takes it for one colour, because strong colours look dark in faint light. The cause is light lying outside its measure {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.394} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.395}.

Error from size: an object containing pores and marks of different colours, each extremely small, is perceived as the single colour that covers the whole, because the small parts fall below the threshold of perception through their smallness {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.395} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.396}.

Error from density: a very transparent object with only a slight density, such as clear crystal or thin white glass, lying with a strongly coloured body behind it, is perceived as taking the colours behind it; its whiteness and purity of colour are not sensed, and the viewer does not know the colours belong to another body. The cause is the density of the transparent object lying outside its measure {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.396}.

Error from the air: an object seen through thick smoke takes on the colour of the smoke, so that a bright-coloured object appears dark; the cause is the transparency of the air lying outside its measure {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.396} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.397}.

Error from time: a body of different colours in a dim place, glanced at briefly and then turned from, is seen as one colour; if the eye rests on it for a measurable time, its differences appear. The cause is the time lying outside its measure, since very faint light acts on the eye only after a certain time {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.397}.

Error from the eye itself: a viewer who gazes long at strong light and then turns to a white or bright body in moderate light sees it dark, because the strong light leaves darkness in the eye for a while before it clears; likewise an ailment that darkens the eye makes everything appear dark and turbid. The cause is the eye lying outside its measure {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.398}.

He concludes that examples of sensory error have been shown for each cause {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.398}.

## Article 3, chapter 6: the manner of errors in knowledge

Chapter 6 opens with its title, "on the manner of the errors of sight that occur in knowledge according to each of the causes", and notes that the quiddity of every object is perceived through knowledge: the object's form is compared with the forms of its kind that sight already knows, and that is knowledge by kind, as the second treatise showed {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.398}.

## Article 3, chapter 6 (continued): comparing a form with its like

Ibn al-Haytham shows that knowing a person rests on comparing his form with the form already perceived and remembered. An error in the quiddity of an object is an error of knowledge and concerns its kind; taking one person for a known one and believing him to be that very person is an error of knowledge; mistaking the form of one person for that of another particular person is an error of individuality. Knowledge is possible only of the individual, of the kind, or of both together {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.398} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.399}.

Sight, while it looks at an object, compares its form with like forms it knows well, and compares the meanings in the object with like meanings, so that it recognises the individual or kind and its meanings. When sight doubts the quiddity of an object, or one of its meanings, and does not recognise it at the moment, it compares it with the nearest thing it knows and has familiarised itself with. This disposition belongs to all the senses. Error arises when perception is not fully certain; it occurs only when one or more of the conditions lies outside its measure {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.399}.

An example of distance outside its measure: a person seen from an uneven distance is taken for a known person and believed to be him, when he is not. If the same person is near, or at a moderate distance, his form is not confused with another's {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.399} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.400}. This kind of error is frequent: sight may see a person from afar and take him for someone it knows, or see a horse, a mule, an ass, a bull, a tree, a fruit, a plant, a stone, a cloth, a vessel or a tool from so great a distance that it compares it with a similar object it has known and remembers {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.400}.

Error in the kind of an object from an uneven distance: sight takes a horse seen from afar for an ass, because it looks smaller than its true size; an ass for a horse, attributing its small size to distance; a mule for a horse; a bull for an ass; a goat for a dog; a tree for a plant; a tent for a tree; a fruit or plant for another of its kind. All of these are errors of knowledge caused by the uneven distance lying outside its measure {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.400} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.401}.

A stranger example: seeing at night, from an uneven distance, a fire on a hill-top of which he had no knowledge, the viewer may take it for a star in the sky. The cause is that the distance of the fire lies outside its measure; were the fire near, it would not be taken for a star {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.401}.

The account of error from the position of the object lying outside its measure begins: an oblique object outside the ray-line and far from it, seen while the eyes are fixed on another object facing the middle of sight on the ray-line, is not perceived correctly. Thus a man may be taken for a known man, or a horse for an ass, or an ass for a horse, at an uneven distance from the ray-line {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 scan-p.401}; the account continues on the following pages.
