## Summary
*Kitab al-Iman* ("The Book of Faith"), known as *al-Iman al-Kabir* ("The Great Book of Faith") to distinguish it from *al-Iman al-Awsat*, is a book by [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] on the reality of [[concept/iman|faith]] and submission. It opens volume 7 of [[work/majmu-al-fatawa|Majmu' al-Fatawa]], titled "Faith", and is followed in the same volume by *Kitab al-Iman al-Awsat*. {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 9} {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 423} {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 429}

## The stated method
Ibn Taymiyya begins by saying that faith and submission together comprise the whole religion; that people have said much about their reality, been confused over it and written volumes on it; and that the dispute has gone on among almost all the factions since the [[group/khawarij|Kharijites]] came out. He announces that he will not begin with people's disagreements, but with what can be learned from the words of God and His Messenger, showing within that why referring disputes to God and the Messenger is better and fairer in outcome. He then starts from the Prophet's distinction, in the hadith of Jibril, between what is called islam, what is called iman and what is called ihsan. {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 11}

## Its subjects
According to the volume's table of contents, the book deals with: hadiths whose authenticity people dispute; then what the words "righteous", "martyr" and "truthful", "righteousness" and "corruption", "wickedness" and "unbelief", and "wronging oneself" cover alone and together with other words, and how unqualified and qualified use affects their meaning. {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 423} {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 424} Then a section on Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari's having championed Jahm's view of faith, and on those who championed Jahm's school making faith one of the traits of islam; sections on the Qur'an's showing that unqualified faith entails deeds, on what faith means when joined to islam or to good deeds, on conjunction implying difference, and on "faith" when unqualified meaning what "righteousness" (birr) means. {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 425} {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 426} Then on the [[group/murjia|Murji'a]]'s claim that the faith in the heart is assent alone; on the ways the increase of faith is known; on the Qur'an's affirming islam without iman; on the arguments of Muhammad ibn Nasr al-Marwazi; on the hadith of Jibril's question about islam, iman and ihsan; on people's disputes over the names "believer" and "faith", some of them verbal and many substantive; and on people's views of the exception in faith. {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 426} {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 427} {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 429}

## Degrees of faith
Ibn Taymiyya holds that people differ in the faith required of them according to their capacity, once the address has reached them all. He cites the hadith "Whoever of you sees a wrong, let him change it with his hand", and explains "beyond that there is not a mustard seed of faith" as meaning that rejection in the heart is the last limit of faith, not that one who fails to reject has not a mustard seed of faith. {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 269}

## The exception in faith
One of its longest sections concerns saying "I am a believer, God willing". He gives three views – those who require it, those who forbid it, and those who allow both in two different senses – and holds the third to be the soundest. {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 269} He attributes the prohibition to the Murji'a and the [[group/jahmiyya|Jahmiyya]], who make faith a single thing a person knows in himself, and who call the one making the exception "a doubter". {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 269} {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 270} Those who require it, he says, have two grounds: that faith is what a servant brings to his Lord at death – the ground of many later Kullabis, he says, not of the early generations – and that unqualified faith includes doing all that is commanded and leaving all that is forbidden, so that to testify to it for oneself is self-praise – the ground, he says, of the generality of the early generations who made the exception. {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 270} {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 271} {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 279} See [[question/may-one-say-i-am-a-believer-god-willing|May a Muslim say: I am a believer, God willing?]]

## His stance towards the theologians
In the course of this he states that the theologians of the Kullabiyya, the Karramiyya and the Ash'ariyya defend what is outwardly known of the Sunni positions – that the Qur'an is uncreated, the vision of God, intercession, the caliphate of the four – but on grounds other than those of the early generations, taken from others, so that contradiction enters their words; and he holds that the school of the Companions is always weightier than the views of those after them. {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 273} He traces to [[person/ibn-kullab|Ibn Kullab]] a principle on the divine attributes that a number of followers of the legal schools accepted, naming among them Abu al-Ma'ali al-Juwayni, Abu al-Walid al-Baji and Abu Mansur al-Maturidi, and builds their view of the exception on that principle. {cite:majmu-fatawa-wafa-v7 p. 272} These are Ibn Taymiyya's judgements on those schools, given here as his.
