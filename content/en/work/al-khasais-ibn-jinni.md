## Summary
al-Khasa'is is the book in which [[person/ibn-jinni|Ibn Jinni]] gathered his inquiry into the principles of grammar and into the nature of language itself: its definition, its origin and the reasons behind its rules. This page sets out what was read of its preface and of the chapters on the definition of language, nahw, i'rab, bina and the origin of language. Each statement is ascribed to its holder as it stands in the pages read, and the limits of the reading are given at the end.

## The book and its author
In the preface Ibn Jinni speaks of a work he had long kept in view and hoped to begin, and describes it as among the noblest things compiled on the knowledge of the Arabs, the most given to analogy and reasoning, and the fullest in proofs of the wisdom this language holds {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.1}. This is the author's description of his own book, not the museum's judgement. On the direction of the book he says, after the chapter on speech and saying, that his aim is not nominative, accusative, genitive and jussive, because most of the books compiled have already dealt with these, but that the book is built on "digging out the mines of meaning" and fixing the state of the origins and first principles, and on showing how their rulings run through the details and margins {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.33}. For this reason the book is counted as one on the principles of grammar and the philosophy of language rather than a teaching manual.

## The definition of language
In the chapter on language and what it is, Ibn Jinni says its definition is "sounds by which each people express their aims", then traces its morphology to lagawtu, "I spoke", with the original form lughwa, and gives the plurals lughat and lughun; he says the word laghw, "idle talk", occurs in the Qur'an and in the hadith on speaking during the Friday sermon {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.34}. al-Suyuti copied this definition at the start of al-Muzhir and followed it with the sayings of others {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.11-12}, so al-Khasa'is served later scholars as a reference for defining language.

## Nahw, i'rab and bina
Next he defines nahw as following the manner of Arab speech in its inflection {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.35}; this is set out further on the page [[concept/ilm-al-nahw|Arabic grammar (nahw)]]. He defines i'rab as making meanings plain by words, with the example of "Sa'id honoured his father" against "Sa'id's father thanked Sa'id", where the nominative and accusative show the agent from the object, and he adds that when i'rab is hidden in a sentence, word order or a clue from the meaning takes its place {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.36}. Bina, for him, is the fixing of the end of a word in one form, a vowel or a rest, not through anything the governing elements produced; it was called building because it stays in its place as a building does {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.38}.

## The origin of language: inspiration or convention
The best known chapter is the one on the origin of language. Ibn Jinni says most people of reasoning hold that its origin is mutual agreement and convention, not revelation or instruction (tawqif). He then relates that his teacher [[person/abu-ali-al-farisi|Abu Ali al-Farisi]] told him one day that it is from God and argued from the verse [[passage/quran-2-31|al-Baqara 31]]; Ibn Jinni answers that the verse can be read as God enabling Adam to set up the convention, which is from God in any case, so the argument falls in the point in dispute {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.41}. He adds that Abu Ali also spoke for agreement in some of his words and that al-Akhfash's view does not bar it, and the editor explains that the two allowed both opinions {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.42}. He also gives another reading, in which God taught Adam the names of creatures in all languages (Arabic, Persian, Syriac, Hebrew and Greek), his children then scattered and each prevailed in one language; he says that if the sound report has come in this form it must be accepted {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.42}. In a footnote the editor notes that tawqif is al-Ash'ari's view and convention the view of the Mu'tazila {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.42}; al-Suyuti, setting Ibn Faris's view beside Ibn Jinni's, describes Ibn Faris as of the Sunnis and Ibn Jinni and his teacher as Mu'tazilis {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.14}. For Ibn Faris's tawqif see [[work/al-sahibi-fi-fiqh-al-lugha|al-Sahibi]].

Ibn Jinni also asks why only names were taught when language has verbs and particles, and answers that names are the strongest of the three kinds, that every useful sentence needs a noun, and that a complete sentence can do without verb and particle {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.42-43}.

## What this page did not reach
Only the preface and the run of chapters from the definition of language to its origin were read; the chapters on reasons, analogy, hearing, regularity and irregularity, on which the rest of the book is built, were not. No date of composition was found in the pages read, so none is stated. The page numbers are those shown by the Shamela copy of Muhammad Ali al-Najjar's edition and were not compared with a paper copy. This page does not judge between the views on the origin of language, which stay with their holders.
