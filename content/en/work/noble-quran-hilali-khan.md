# Interpretation of the Meanings of the Noble Qur'an (Hilali–Khan)

## Summary
This English rendering of the meanings of the [[work/quran|Qur'an]] by Muhammad Taqi al-Din al-Hilali and Muhammad Muhsin Khan, known by their two names together, is among the most widely circulated Islamic texts in English and has been published more than twenty times since 1977. {cite:gloqur-hilali-khan-part-1} It is printed today by the [[group/king-fahd-complex-for-printing-the-holy-quran|King Fahd Complex for the Printing of the Holy Qur'an]] in Medina. {cite:wikipedia-noble-quran-hilali-khan-en}

## The translators
Al-Hilali (1893–1987) travelled between Morocco, India, Germany and Saudi Arabia and was a friend of [[person/ibn-baz-abd-al-aziz|Abd al-Aziz ibn Baz]], who invited him to teach in Medina in the late 1960s. {cite:gloqur-hilali-khan-part-1} Muhammad Muhsin Khan (1927–2021) graduated from the University of the Punjab and the University of Wales, studied medicine in Saudi Arabia, and began an English translation of *Sahih al-Bukhari* in 1376 AH, finishing it in 1968; Yakubovych thinks he was the main initiator of the Qur'an project. {cite:gloqur-hilali-khan-part-1}

## Editions
The translation was finished in Medina in 1972. The first edition appeared in Chicago in 1977 from [[listing/kazi-publications|Kazi Publications]], with the Arabic text, under a title presenting it as an explanatory translation summarising [[work/tafsir-ibn-kathir|Ibn Kathir's tafsir]] and supplemented by [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]] and *Sahih al-Bukhari*; it passed almost unnoticed. {cite:gloqur-hilali-khan-part-1} A second edition followed in Ankara in 1978, opening with a letter from the Islamic University of Medina vouching for the translators, and a third in 1985 with almost the same content. {cite:gloqur-hilali-khan-part-1} In 1994 [[listing/darussalam|Darussalam]] issued a completely revised text in one volume and in nine, and in 1997 the King Fahd Complex printed it, largely on the basis of Darussalam's edition; that printing became the standard text reproduced online. {cite:gloqur-hilali-khan-part-1}

## What it contains
The translation uses contemporary English rather than the archaic King James style of earlier translations, and its makers presented it as exegesis in English more than as a translation in the strict sense. {cite:gloqur-hilali-khan-part-1} The 1977 and 1978 editions carry almost no Arabic glosses; later editions add many, together with explanation in brackets, until the line between translation and commentary blurs, and the first editions' appendix on jihad was replaced by appendices on doctrine. {cite:gloqur-hilali-khan-part-2}

## Reception
Yakubovych writes that the translation was warmly received by many readers, remains a main reference for many English-speaking Muslims, and helped bring traditional tafsir into translation; and that over recent decades many voices, Muslim scholars above all, have criticised its style, its English and the ideas in its commentary. {cite:gloqur-hilali-khan-part-1} {cite:gloqur-hilali-khan-part-2}
