## Overview
"Al-Jami al-Musnad al-Sahih al-Mukhtasar min Umur Rasul Allah wa Sunanihi wa Ayyamihi", by Muhammad ibn Isma'il [[person/al-bukhari|al-Bukhari]] al-Ju'fi. {cite:sahih-al-bukhari-bugha p.1}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls it al-Bukhari's most famous work and describes it as a collection of reports of the Prophet arranged by subject, which claims to contain only reports of the highest authority, the kind called [[concept/al-hadith-al-sahih|sahih]]. It was put together over sixteen years. Among Sunnis it came to rank, together with the Sahih of [[person/muslim-ibn-al-hajjaj|Muslim]], at the head of the collections of Sunni tradition, and most Sunnis regard it as the most important book after the Qur'an. This page follows the encyclopaedia's articles on al-Bukhari and on Hadith, and reports their judgements as theirs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1360} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1361}

## The compiler
The article gives al-Bukhari's dates as 194/810 to 256/870. His nisba al-Ju'fi comes from his great-grandfather al-Mughira, a client of the governor of [[place/bukhara|Bukhara]], at whose hands he accepted Islam. He began to learn traditions by heart at ten and was a precocious boy; at sixteen he made the pilgrimage with his mother and brother and then stayed for a time in the Hijaz. He travelled widely in search of traditions, from Khurasan to Egypt, and said he had heard them from more than a thousand teachers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1360}

In later life he met opposition at [[place/nishapur|Nishapur]], where, the article says, a rival was jealous of the crowds who came to hear him. Because he held that the Qur'an is uncreated but that this does not apply to its recitation, he was accused of unsound belief and left for Bukhara. There he refused the governor's request to bring his books to the governor's house, and refused to hold special sessions for the governor's children. He was expelled and went to [[place/khartank|Khartank]], a village near Samarkand, where he stayed with relatives. It is said that, worn down by the hostility he had met, he was heard praying one night that God would take him, and that he died within a month. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1360}

## Making of the book
The article says the Sahih took sixteen years to compile, that al-Bukhari is said to have chosen his reports from a mass of 600,000, and that he is said to have washed and prayed two cycles of prayer before putting any report into the book. These are reported as traditions about the work, not as established facts. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1360}

## Structure and size
The collection is arranged in 97 books with 3,450 chapters. By the article's count there are 7,397 reports with full chains of transmitters, or 2,762 once repetitions are left out. Sunnah.com instead describes it as containing over 7,500 hadith, counting repetitions, in 97 books. The English translation it presents is by Dr. M. Muhsin Khan {cite:sunnah-com bukhari}. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1360}

## Method and arrangement
The article places the Sahih among the classified works, which arrange their material by subject, as against the earlier works arranged by Companion. Because some reports touch more than one subject, the same report can appear under more than one chapter. In the main the book follows the divisions of [[concept/fiqh|jurisprudence]], but it also holds other material, among it the beginning of creation and accounts of paradise and hell. The article on Hadith adds that the Sahihs contain biographical material and Qur'an commentary beside the details of religious practice, law and conduct that fill the Sunan works. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1360} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1361} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33}

Al-Bukhari's chapter headings are meant to show the subject of the reports beneath them. The article notes that al-Subki counted him among the Shafi'i jurists, and judges this inexact, since he did not follow the doctrine of any one school consistently. It also records that the headings have sometimes been criticised because a report does not always seem relevant to its title, and that some chapters carry a heading but no reports. The article suggests this may mean that he drew up his plan first and left blanks where he had no sound report, hoping to find one. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1360} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1361}

The article on Hadith notes how the book handles the forms of transmission. Al-Bukhari has chapters on reading material over to a teacher and on handing a copy to a student and on writing to him, and the preposition "an", on the authority of, appears often in his chains; it is generally agreed to be allowable when the transmitter is reliable. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 36} Among his other works the article mentions his Ta'rikh, a biographical work on the men named in chains of transmitters, which he is said to have written as a young man on moonlit nights at the Prophet's tomb. It describes him as an outstanding traditionist, known for his minute knowledge of detail and his skill in detecting defects in reports. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1361}

## Standing among Sunni scholars
The article says that in al-Bukhari's own time the Sahih was seen as one work among others, but was soon recognised as outstanding, and by the fourth century AH it stood with Muslim's Sahih at the head of Sunni collections. In time, though criticisms were made on matters of detail, most Sunnis held it to be the most important book after the Qur'an. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1361}

The article on Hadith places the two Sahihs first among the six books, followed by the Sunan of [[person/abu-dawud-al-sijistani|Abu Dawud]], [[person/al-tirmidhi-muhammad-ibn-isa|al-Tirmidhi]], [[person/al-nasai|al-Nasa'i]] and [[person/ibn-majah|Ibn Maja]], and says there was no official body to commission such books, so each made its own appeal to the community; by the fourth/tenth century the collections of al-Bukhari and Muslim were fairly generally recognised. It adds that, in theory, the reports in the two books are of varying degrees of worth and that criticism has been made even of some of them. The six are treated in the record on the [[concept/kutub-al-sitta|six books]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33}

In the grading of sound reports described there, the highest grade is a report given by both al-Bukhari and Muslim, the second a report given by al-Bukhari alone, and the third one given by Muslim alone. Below these come reports not in either book that meet al-Bukhari's conditions and Muslim's conditions, and last those held sound by other authorities. The lower grades thus take the two books as the measure of soundness. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 34}

## Commentaries
The article says there have been many commentaries on the whole or on parts of the Sahih, and names as notable those of [[person/badr-al-din-al-ayni|al-Ayni]] and [[person/ibn-hajar-al-asqalani|Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani]], and one by al-Qastallani. The commentary of Ibn Hajar is the one the museum records as [[work/fath-al-bari|Fath al-Bari]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1361}

## Academic views
The article on Hadith reports that Western scholars have tended to argue that the traditional criticism of reports did not go far enough. It names Goldziher, who showed that Hadith rests on less firm ground than the conventional doctrine supposes, and Schacht, who argued that chains of transmitters grew over time, so that legal reports of a later period were traced back to the Prophet. It also lists features that make some material hard to accept as the Prophet's own, such as phrases from earlier scriptures, references to later parties and rulers, and detailed descriptions of the last things. The article does not explain the whole body of Tradition away on these lines, but concludes that much later material has been attributed to the Prophet and that it is hard to find a satisfactory test of what is genuine. These views are general to Hadith and are not aimed at the Sahih of al-Bukhari alone. They stand beside the Sunni scholarly position above, and the museum gives no verdict between them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 35} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 36}

## In «Asdaf al-Lu'lu'» by Abu Ishaq al-Heweny
[[person/abu-ishaq-al-heweny|Abu Ishaq al-Heweny]] shows viewers volumes of the Sultaniyya edition of Sahih al-Bukhari, which he says was printed in 1314 or 1315 by a team of about fifteen scholars, records manuscript variants in its margins, and sets out its method in the introduction to the first volume. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep11 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep11 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep11 00:02:30} He says al-Bukhari's fiqh lies in his chapter headings, and reads an example from Ibn Jama'a's book on the relevance of al-Bukhari's headings. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep11 00:06:00} He reads a sheet he wrote the day he began teaching Sahih al-Bukhari at the mosque of Ibn Shihab al-Zuhri, Wednesday 30 Safar 1406, corresponding to 13/11/1985, with notes on the forms of al-Bukhari's suspended reports, on his three-link chains, and on thirty-seven narrators from whom al-Bukhari narrates both directly and through an intermediary. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep12 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep12 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep12 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep12 00:04:30}

Among his unfinished books is «Kiswat al-Ari bi-Bayan Illat al-Hadhf ind al-Bukhari», on the places where al-Bukhari leaves out part of a hadith; his example is the hadith on intention at the opening of the Sahih, which al-Bukhari narrates from al-Humaydi with a clause left out, though al-Humaydi gives it in full in his Musnad, and which al-Bukhari gives in full in six other places. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep13 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep23 00:29:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep23 00:29:30} He says the idea arose while he was doing the takhrij of the hadiths of Ibn Kathir's tafsir; he then read all of «Fath al-Bari» for Ibn Hajar's remarks and found close to fifty places. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep23 00:30:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep23 00:30:30} He says he thought of changing the title from "the reason for the omission" to "the place of the omission" so as to finish quickly, then rejected it, because listing places adds nothing. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep24 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep24 00:10:00} Among his lessons that have been transcribed are «Fakk al-Withaq bi-Sharh Kitab al-Riqaq», given on al-Nas channel, and «Barq al-Kalim bi-Sharh Kitab al-Ilm» from Sahih al-Bukhari; he says he does not publish what he said aloud until he has revised it, because the pen weighs more than the tongue. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep23 00:18:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep23 00:16:30}
