## Overview
The oldest surviving continuous biography of the Prophet. It was written by [[person/ibn-ishaq|Muhammad ibn Ishaq]] (d. 151 AH). It was then refined and abridged by [[person/ibn-hisham|Abd al-Malik ibn Hisham]] (d. 213 or 218 AH), and became known as "The Prophetic Biography of Ibn Hisham". {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa}

In the Saqqa and Halabi edition it runs to four volumes. The fourth volume covers the period from the return of Ja'far from Abyssinia and Khaybar to the death and burial of the Prophet. {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam describes Ibn Hisham's edition of Ibn Ishaq's Sira as the basic work on the life of Muhammad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 832} This page follows that reference work for the author, the recension, the way the text reached us and the judgements passed on it.

## Two names for the genre
Two words have been used for the traditional account of the Prophet's life and background: maghazi, literally the campaigns, and sira. One scholar, Martin Hinds, argued that during the first two centuries the biographical material circulated only under the name maghazi, and that sira came into use with Ibn Hisham. Another, Maher Jarrar, replied that maghazi is only one part of the sira, used at times for the whole, and that [[person/muhammad-ibn-shihab-al-zuhri|al-Zuhri]], who died in 124/742, already called the biography sira. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 679}

## Ibn Ishaq, the author
According to the article, Ibn Ishaq is counted with Musa ibn Uqba and [[person/al-waqidi|al-Waqidi]] among the main authorities on the Prophet's biography. He was born in [[place/medina|Medina]] about 85/704. Most sources say he died in [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] in 150/767, while others give 151 or 153, and one gives 144/761-2. He was buried in the Khayzuran cemetery, near the grave of [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 842}

His grandfather Yasar was among the captives taken at Ayn al-Tamr in 12/633-4. Yasar became the slave and then the freedman of Qays ibn Makhrama, which gave the family the attribution al-Muttalibi. His three sons, Musa, Abd al-Rahman and Ishaq, were all known as transmitters of reports. The article notes that no details of Ibn Ishaq's early life survive, but that it was natural for him to follow his father and uncles into this field. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 842}

Al-Zuhri is reported to have called him the most knowledgeable of people in maghazi. In 119/737 he went to [[place/alexandria|Alexandria]] and studied under [[person/yazid-ibn-abi-habib|Yazid ibn Abi Habib]]. He then left Medina for Iraq. The reasons given in the sources are disputed. Hisham ibn Urwa is said to have objected to Ibn Ishaq relating traditions from his wife. Malik ibn Anas is also named as an opponent, and the sources suggest several grounds: professional rivalry, differences over Ibn Ishaq's lost book of Sunan, and objection to material on the campaigns obtained through the sons of Jews who had become Muslims. Malik is also reported to have objected to him as a Shi'i and a Qadari, a charge that was levelled against others too. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 842} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 843}

He went first to al-Abbas ibn Muhammad, governor of al-Jazira, then to the caliph [[person/al-mansur|Abu Ja'far al-Mansur]] at al-Hira, and finally settled in Baghdad. Besides the Sira he is credited with a Kitab al-Khulafa and a book of Sunan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 843}

## Ibn Hisham and his recension
Abu Muhammad Abd al-Malik ibn Hisham was said to come from a family of Himyarite origin that had moved from [[place/basra|Basra]] to Egypt, where he was born and lived. He was outstanding in genealogy and grammar. He died in Egypt on 13 Rabi II 218/8 May 833, or in 213/828. His Kitab al-Tijan, on South Arabian antiquities, survives. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 832}

He is chiefly famous for his edition of Ibn Ishaq's Sira. The article says Ibn Hisham left out material that was mostly not directly relevant to the career of Muhammad, gave more accurate versions of some of the poems, and explained difficult words and phrases, and it connects these changes with the great popularity of his edition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 832}

## How the text was transmitted
Ibn Ishaq's Sira does not survive as a single independent work. Passages that Ibn Hisham omitted are preserved in historians such as [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]] and [[person/al-azraqi-muhammad-ibn-abd-allah|al-Azraqi]]. The additions made by Ibn Hisham can be studied in A. Guillaume's English translation, where they are set apart from the main text. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 832} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 833}

Ibn Hisham took his knowledge of Ibn Ishaq's work from [[person/ziyad-al-bakkai|Ziyad al-Bakka'i]], who died in 183/799 and lived mostly in [[place/kufa|Kufa]]. The chief transmitter of Ibn Hisham's own work was his pupil Ibn al-Barqi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 833}

Ibn Ishaq's travels are reflected in the different versions, called riwayas, of his Sira. The article counts some fifteen, with Kufa, Rayy and Basra the most prominent and only a single riwaya from Medina; it cites J. Fück for this. He also had only one transmitter among the people of Medina, Ibrahim ibn Sa'd. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 842} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 843}

## Muslim scholarly assessments of Ibn Ishaq
The critics of narrators gave sharply different judgements of Ibn Ishaq. These are the views of the early Muslim scholars as the article reports them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 843}

- In favour: al-Zuhri's praise, noted above. Asim ibn Umar ibn Qatada said knowledge would remain among them while Ibn Ishaq lived. Shu'ba called him a commander of the believers in tradition. Abu Zur'a, al-Madini, Ibn Ma'in and Ibn Sa'd regarded him as sound in tradition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 843}
- Against: [[person/al-nasai|al-Nasa'i]] and [[person/yahya-ibn-said-al-qattan|Yahya ibn Sa'id al-Qattan]] did not accept him in matters of hadith. Al-Athram, Sulayman al-Taymi and Wuhayb ibn Khalid called him a liar, a charge the article says concerns hadith and is separate from the accusation, found in al-Jumahi, [[person/ibn-al-nadim|Ibn al-Nadim]] and Yaqut, that he included verses in the Sira knowing them to be forged. [[person/al-bukhari|Al-Bukhari]] and Ibn Numayr were not satisfied with his transmission. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 843}
- A middle position: [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]] accepted him on maghazi but did not draw on him for hadith, because he disliked Ibn Ishaq's use of a collective isnad, one chain covering several narrators without separating their words. The article's author adds the judgement that singling Ibn Ishaq out on this point is unfair, since early authorities often did the same. Ibn Hanbal and later authorities also did not accept him on legal matters, although Ibn Ishaq himself claimed authority in fiqh. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 843}

## Modern scholarly views
The article on the sira in the same reference work says that, for Muslims, the sira gradually became almost a holy text whose reliability was accepted with little questioning. It adds that some Muslims, reacting to Western historical criticism, defended its truth firmly, and it names Muhammad Husayn Haykal's Life of Muhammad of 1935 as an example of this apologetic writing. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 682}

On the academic side, its author lists reasons some scholars give against the authenticity of the material, among them that non-Islamic sources often differ from Islamic ones, and judges that pieces of salvation history and elaborations of Qur'anic passages are unfit as sources for scientific historiography, though valuable for the history of the Prophet's image in his community's belief. The same article says that the Document of Medina is generally considered authentic, while scholars disagree about the unity of the text. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 682}

The museum states no verdict between these positions.
