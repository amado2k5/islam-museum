## Summary

*Al-Ruh wa-l-Jasad* ("The Spirit and the Body") is a collection of essays by the Egyptian writer [[person/mostafa-mahmoud|Mostafa Mahmoud]]. Its first edition came from Dar al-Nahda al-Arabiyya in Cairo, its rights page giving the year 1394 AH {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-nahda-ed1 p. 6}; the copy used here is the seventh edition from Dar al-Ma'arif {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 5} {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 114}. What follows are the author's views, attributed to him.

## Words as a veil

The author opens by saying that "letters veil and do not reveal", that the word "I love you" has become "a worn-out coin", and that a silence full of feeling may say more {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 7} {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 8}. Divine realities, he holds, are "even more impossible for words", and he quotes from the addresses of "the righteous Sufi Muhammad ibn Abd al-Jabbar", [[person/al-niffari|al-Niffari]], that letters do not point to God {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 10}.

## The age of shouting, and love

In "The Age of Shouting" he describes today's civilisation as "a civilisation of shouting": in love, marriage, society and politics {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 13}. He quotes al-Niffari that whoever has not witnessed "what cannot be said" is scattered by what can be said, and that one should attach oneself only to God {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 16}; he notes that [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]] held the same view of ordinary love, and that the Qur'an describes the bond between spouses as affection and mercy {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 17}.

## Happiness, Paradise and Hell

In "On the Spirit and the Body" he holds that one secret of happiness is "harmony of the outer and the inner" {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 21}. In "Hell" he answers those who say a merciful God will punish no one: this, he argues, imagines a God who "treats criminals and their victims alike in requital", and is "the height of thinking ill of God" {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 29} {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 30}. A chapter on Paradise follows {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 35}.

## Water, the self and the idols of the age

In "The Magic Liquid" he reflects on the properties of water {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 41}, and in "The World of Secrets" on the self and its ranks {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 49}. In another chapter he says we claim to have left the jahiliyya behind, "but I say rather that we are idol-worshippers" whose idols have only changed their names {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 57}. Further chapters deal with companionship, discontent, "A Crack in the Wall", and truth and lies {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 63} {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 71} {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 81} {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 87}.

## Did we exist before we were born?

In the last chapter the author describes a feeling that has stayed with him that he "has always existed" and is not something that began at birth {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 97}. He holds that this feeling is supported by the religious truths of reckoning, the barzakh and eternity {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 98}. He reads [[passage/quran-95-4|al-Tin, verses 4-6]] as pointing to "a first creation in the finest form" that came before our worldly creation {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 98}, and cites [[passage/quran-7-27|al-A'raf, verse 27]] and [[passage/quran-90-4|al-Balad, verse 4]] {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 99}, and the words of Jesus in [[passage/quran-19-30|Maryam]] {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 108}.

:::perspectives

## For young visitors

Mostafa Mahmoud says a drop of water in a thirsty person's mouth is worth more than all the gold on earth, and invites us to look closely at this simple thing we drink every day {cite:mm-ruh-wal-jasad-maarif-ed7 p. 41}.
