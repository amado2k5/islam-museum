## Summary
"Al-Zij al-Sabi" by [[person/al-battani|al-Battani]] is a zij on [[concept/astronomy|the reckoning of the stars, the zodiac]] and the positions of the planets. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.1} It holds its author's observations of the Sun and Moon and his correction of their motions as [[person/batlamus|Ptolemy]] had set them in the [[work/al-majisti|Almagest]], with the motions of the five planets as his corrections led him, and all the other celestial computations required. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17}

## Its name
The Escorial manuscript has no title, and the Latin titles "On the motion of the stars", "On the science of the stars" and "On the numbers of the stars" are taken from the book's opening. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40} Nallino thinks the author called it simply a "[[concept/zij|zij]]"; early authors knew it as "Zij al-Battani", and later bibliographers — among them Ibn Khallikan, Abu al-Fida and Hajji Khalifa — named it "the Sabian Zij" because they took its author for a Sabian. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 41}

## Two recensions and their date
Al-Battani is said to have issued his Zij in two copies, the second better than the first. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 41} Nallino doubts the second changed the first greatly, since the roots of the mean motions and the great star catalogue are set for 1 Adhar 1191 of Alexander (1 March 880 CE) and must already have stood in the first. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 41} The first appeared certainly before 288 AH, because [[person/thabit-ibn-qurra|Thabit ibn Qurra]], who died in February 901, cites its chapter 52; the text of the Escorial manuscript and of Plato of Tivoli's version is the second, since it records two basic observations made at Antioch in January and August 901. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 41}

## Method and sources
Al-Battani modestly says he follows Ptolemy's path while changing some of his elements on the strength of new observations, and that he wished to smooth the way for students of astronomy. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 50} He knew the Almagest only in Arabic translations and collated copies whose numbers disagreed; he used the Handy Tables of Theon of Alexandria, from which he took the parallax tables for every clime. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 50} The Zij differs from the Almagest in aim: the Almagest tries to embrace all of astronomy, while the zij is written for practitioners rather than beginners, and it includes problems of spherical astronomy needed for astrological computation that Ptolemy and Theon leave out — fixing the houses, the revolutions of years, the projection of rays and the ascensions between the cardines. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 53} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 54}

## Corrections of Ptolemy
Al-Battani mentions some of his corrections without boasting: in the obliquity of the ecliptic; in the lengths of the seasons, the Sun's motion and the eccentricity of its orbit; in the angular diameters of Sun, Moon and shadow, where he found that the Sun's apparent diameter varies and so allows annular eclipses; in the lunar tables; in the planets' motions and apogees; and in using sines in place of Ptolemy's chords. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 51} He passes over many other departures in silence, as if fearing to seem irreverent towards Ptolemy: the equation of time, the parallax of the inferior planets, a new method for the magnitude of lunar eclipses, a more exact parallax at conjunctions, the construction of a new instrument, and the tacit correction of Ptolemy's error of an immobile solar apogee. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 51} Nallino puts this silence down to excessive reverence for Ptolemy, recalling that the Harranians exalted Greek learning at a time when the Shu'ubiyya were vaunting the glories of the ancient nations. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 52} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 53}

## Its trigonometry
Al-Battani uses the sine, the cosine and the versed sine, referred in the Greek manner to a radius of 60 parts, following earlier Arab authors who had fused Greek and Indian notions. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 56} He built his sine table by halving Ptolemy's chords of double arcs, and used tangents and cotangents only for sundials, referred in the Indian manner to a gnomon of 12 parts. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 56} Nallino thinks he knew the sine rule for right spherical triangles, and that he solved oblique spherical triangles by orthographic projection (the analemma) rather than Menelaus' theorem, save in one place, which gave him formulas easier for his time. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 57} Nallino reports von Braunmühl's finding that Regiomontanus learned the use of the versed sine and of orthographic projection in spherical trigonometry from al-Battani. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 47}

## Instruments
Al-Battani describes the instruments he used: a tall gnomon divided into twelve parts; a celestial globe with five rings, apparently his own invention; a triquetrum (parallactic rulers) long enough that its readings come from a circle 10 cubits, about 5 metres, across; a mural quadrant with a side of no less than a metre; and horizontal and vertical sundials to measure the seasonal hours. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 55} He urges the observer to perseverance and care, saying that truth in celestial matters is reached only through the labours of many successive generations, and he refrains from asserting what is not established: he found the obliquity smaller than Ptolemy's and the annual precession larger, but never claimed these variations real and continuous, and so declined the theory of trepidation. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 55} He leaves aside philosophical inquiries into the system of the world and the nature of the spheres, as if he did not count them as astronomy. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 55}

## Its language
Nallino finds al-Battani's Arabic easy at first sight but often obscure and involved on closer look, with long periods made darker by the manuscripts' lack of vowels and punctuation; his contemporary Habash is much clearer. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 54} Al-Battani avoided the Persian terms common among astronomers of his day, not out of Arabic purism but because they were absent from the Almagest translations. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 52}

## Contents
The book opens with the division of the celestial circle and the computation of chords, then treats the obliquity of the ecliptic, the risings of the signs in each city, finding latitudes by observation, shadows and altitudes, the meridian line, and the distances of the stars from the equator and from one another. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.5} {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.6} {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.7} Plato of Tivoli's version gives it fifty-seven chapters, the last on making the armillary globe, the quadrant and the parallactic rulers. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 61}

## Reception among Muslims
Arab authors praised it with one voice, even [[person/abd-al-rahman-al-sufi|Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi]] and Ibn Yunus, who found small faults in it. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 41} [[person/maslama-al-majriti|Maslama al-Majriti]] abridged its tables of the equations of the Sun, Moon and five planets, and [[person/kushyar-ibn-labban|Kushyar ibn Labban]] took its solar equation tables into his al-Zij al-Jami'. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 42} Ali ibn Ahmad al-Nasawi judged it the most perfect of observational zijes and the nearest to truth, though built on the Roman and Hijri eras and so hard to adapt to the Persian. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 42} [[person/al-biruni-muhammad-ibn-ahmad|Al-Biruni]] wrote a book entitled [[work/jala-al-adhhan-fi-zij-al-battani|Jala' al-adhhan fi zij al-Battani]], and [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]] counted the zijes of al-Battani and Ibn al-Kammad among the best books of astronomy. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 42}

## Reception among Jews and Europeans
Abraham ibn Ezra cited it and recommended its tables, Maimonides tacitly followed it for the Sun's mean motion in the Mishneh Torah, and other Jewish scholars built tables upon it. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 43} In Europe [[person/robert-of-ketton|Robert]] composed canons for the meridian of London from its tables, Albertus Magnus commended it, and Alfonso X of Castile gathered works resting on its authority in his Libros del saber de astronomía. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 43} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 45} Regiomontanus studied it closely; [[person/plato-of-tivoli|Plato of Tivoli]]'s translation was first printed at Nuremberg in 1537 from a copy he had annotated; Copernicus and Tycho Brahe used its observations; and Halley held that they deserved to be kept among the most precious treasures of astronomy. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 46} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 47} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 48}

## Translations
There are three versions: a Latin one by Robert, of which nothing survives; the [[work/de-motu-stellarum-plato-tiburtinus|Latin of Plato of Tivoli]], twice printed; and a Castilian one by an unknown translator, ordered by Alfonso X, surviving in an incomplete manuscript in the Bibliothèque de l'Arsenal, Paris. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 58} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 66} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 67} Nallino concludes that the Escorial manuscript and Plato's exemplar descend from one archetype, since they share the same corruptions and interpolations. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 71}

## Manuscript and editions
The Escorial manuscript no. 908 (formerly 903) has 245 folios, 26.5 cm by 19 cm, 19 lines a page, on thick cotton paper, in a Maghribi hand closer to naskh, with chapter titles in red; it has no title or colophon, hadith in another hand at the end, and Nallino dates it to the end of the eleventh or start of the twelfth century CE. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 70} Nallino published the Arabic text from it at Milan in 1899, then the Latin version of the chapters with notes, then that of the tables in 1907. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 12} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 420} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 870} Dar Byblion reissued the text in Nallino's edition in 2005. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.2} {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.4}
