## Summary

The third session continues the argument that the period before the prophetic mission matters for more than proving the Prophet's greatness. He gives examples where knowing pre-Islamic practice explains Qur'anic verses and reports, shows how Islam corrected earlier customs, and argues that the Qur'an's content could not have come from the Arabs' own setting or from the People of the Book. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}

### Why the pre-prophetic period is usually treated too narrowly

- He says that this is the third session of the series and that earlier sessions asked whether anything is left to say about the biography and whether the years before prophethood matter. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He relates that many biographers treat the pre-prophetic years from two angles only: the portents that preceded the mission and the Prophet's moral distance from the ways of the pre-Islamic Arabs. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He holds that the account of the shaking of the Persian king's palace and the going out of the Persians' fire is not established, and he calls both of those angles valid but not enough on their own. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}

This is the speaker's view; the museum reports it as his and does not adopt it.

### Safa and Marwa and the trade markets of the pilgrimage

- He says that some verses cannot be understood well without knowing what happened in pre-Islamic times, and that the verse on walking between Safa and Marwa is one case. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He relates that Urwa found the wording of that verse puzzling and asked Aisha, who explained that people had disliked the rite because of its pre-Islamic associations until the verse removed the worry. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He relates from Sahih al-Bukhari that Ibn Abbas said Dhu al-Majaz and Ukaz were markets of the pre-Islamic Arabs, that some Muslims disliked trading during the pilgrimage, and that a verse then permitted seeking God's bounty. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}

### The Hums, standing at Arafat and naked circling of the Kaaba

- He says that the verse telling pilgrims to depart from where the people depart refers to a pre-Islamic arrangement, and that these explanations are in Sahih al-Bukhari. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He relates that Quraysh were called the Hums, had special pilgrimage rules, and left from Muzdalifa instead of going to Arafat, while the other people left from Arafat. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He relates a report that Jubayr ibn Mut'im saw the Prophet standing at Arafat before Islam and wondered at it, because the Prophet belonged to the Hums; he is unsure of the exact timing of the report. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He says that people used to circle the Kaaba unclothed, that Quraysh provided them with covering, and that a verse about wearing adornment at every place of prayer is reported to relate to this. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}

### Pre-Islamic beliefs show what Islam changed

- He holds that knowing the beliefs and customs of the pre-Islamic Arabs shows how distinct Islam was and how much it corrected, so that its rulings are seen both for their own beauty and by contrast with what came before. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He relates that the Prophet answered talk about the length of a widow's waiting period by saying it was now four months and ten days, whereas in earlier times a woman stayed a full year and then marked the end by a ritual with dung. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He relates from Sahih al-Bukhari that al-Mughira ibn Shu'ba, speaking to a Persian commander before a battle with the army of al-Nu'man ibn Muqarrin, described the Arabs as having lived in hunger and hardship and idol worship until a prophet from among them was sent. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He relates from Sahih Muslim that Jabir ibn Samura, asked whether he sat with the Prophet, said that after the dawn prayer the Companions would speak of pre-Islamic times, laugh, and the Prophet would smile, and he reads this as recalling God's favour on them. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}

### Aside on sitting after dawn until sunrise

- He says that the same report shows the Prophet used to stay seated in his place of prayer until the sun rose and then leave. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He holds that this is sounder than the report promising the reward of a complete pilgrimage and lesser pilgrimage for praying two units after sunrise, which he calls weak in its chain, and he adds that the forenoon prayer is established for after the sun has risen higher. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}

This is the speaker's view; the museum reports it as his and does not adopt it.

### Pre-Islamic customs the Prophet forbade, and mourning

- He says that the Prophet forbade his followers certain pre-Islamic practices, and that some verses and reports are completed only by knowing the custom they address. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He relates from Sahih al-Bukhari a saying disowning those who strike their cheeks, tear their garments and cry out as in pre-Islamic times, and he explains, following some commentators, that the last phrase means wailing and calling down woe over the dead. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He says that the report about the dead being punished for their families' weeping troubled the Companions, that some thought it wrongly transmitted and in conflict with a Qur'anic principle of individual responsibility, and that one way of reconciling it rests on the pre-Islamic habit of a dying person asking to be mourned. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He relates that on this reading the punishment concerns weeping the dead person had encouraged, and that some scholars say it applies only when he had not corrected his household's expectation. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}

This is the speaker's view; the museum reports it as his and does not adopt it.

### The wealth of knowledge in the two Sahih collections

- He says that all the reports he has cited so far come from Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He holds that the knowledge in the two collections exceeds what many students and writers on Islamic thought imagine, and that al-Bukhari's compilation is the most important gathering of the Prophet's Sunna, followed by Muslim's. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He concludes that the reports cited show that parts of the religion are fully understood only through what existed before Islam. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}

### The Qur'an could not have come from the Arabs' own setting

- He holds that a study of the Arabs' condition before the mission shows that their knowledge, even if their leaders had pooled it, could not have produced the content of the Qur'an, and that this strengthens belief in its divine origin. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He says that the Qur'an itself points to this when it tells stories of the unseen, such as the end of the story of Noah in Surat Hud, where Muhammad and his people are told they did not know these accounts before. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He says that Surat al-Qasas repeats the point several times, in the passages about Musa and the Prophet's absence from those events, and again near its end about not expecting the Book to be given to him. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He relates that the Qur'an then challenged the Arabs openly to produce something like it. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}

### The Qur'an and the People of the Book

- He says that the Arabs regarded the People of the Book as the learned people, and that after the mission the Arabs sometimes sought their help and put questions to the Prophet at their prompting. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He holds that the Qur'an does more than debate them: it corrects a central belief, that the Messiah was crucified, by denying the killing and the crucifixion, and he argues that no Arab of that setting could have dared this by himself. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He says that the Qur'an argues from their own scriptures and reports that they kept from ordinary people. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He holds that some commentators who came generations later overlooked what the Qur'an had already told, and that this appears in how they treated the verse saying the righteous servants inherit the earth, which echoes a line in the Psalms about the righteous inheriting the land. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}

### Pre-Islamic marriage forms and Ibn Abbas on the Arabs' ignorance

- He says that Aisha described the corrupt forms of marriage before Islam and how Islam abolished them and kept one form, in which a woman is asked for from her guardian. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He holds that dropping such material would lose a large share of the knowledge tied to the Qur'an and the Sunna. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He relates from Sahih al-Bukhari that Ibn Abbas advised those who want to know the Arabs' ignorance to read a stretch of Surat al-An'am, and that al-Bukhari titled the chapter on the ignorance of the Arabs. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}

### What was good in pre-Islamic Arabia, and the conclusion

- He says that the Prophet affirmed some good in the earlier period, relating from Sahih al-Bukhari a saying that people are like mines and the best of them before Islam are the best after it if they gain understanding. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He says that the Arab custom of granting protection, in which the Arabs took pride, benefited the Prophet and his Companions after the mission began. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
- He concludes that a biography should begin with the period before the mission, and that many writers treat it only to show Islam's general merit over the earlier age or to show the portents and the Prophet's standing, whereas the subject is much wider. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-lilmuslihin/ep3}
