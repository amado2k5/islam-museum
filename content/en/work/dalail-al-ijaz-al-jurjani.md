## Summary
Dala'il al-I'jaz fi Ilm al-Ma'ani is the book of [[person/abd-al-qahir-al-jurjani|Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani]] in which he says that nazm is working by the meanings of grammar {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992 1 p.81}. This page sets out what was read of the book at specific places: what he says on why knowing eloquence needs detail, on the nature of rhetoric and eloquence, on nazm and nahw, on the meaning of the challenge, and on why eloquence cannot be ascribed to the single word. For a comparison of the books on inimitability see [[concept/nazariyyat-al-ijaz-wal-nazm|the books on inimitability]].

## A general knowledge is not enough
At the entrance to his discussion of the inimitability of the Qur'an, al-Jurjani says that in no craft do you know anything until you can tell its error from its right and weigh one excellence against another and know the ranks of the excellent, and that for the science of eloquence it is not enough to set up some measure and describe it in general; you must detail the speech, put a hand on the properties that arise in the nazm of words, count them one by one and name them one by one, and be patient in contemplation. The aim, he says, is to know God's proof from the aspect that is clearest for it {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992 1 p.37}. So the book announces from its start that it is a detailing of properties and not a general verdict.

## Rhetoric, eloquence and clear expression
In the section on verifying what is said about balagha, fasaha, bayan and bara'a, he says these are terms by which some speakers are said to excel over others in how they spoke and told listeners of their aims. He holds that they mean nothing except describing speech by good and complete signifying of what it signifies and then its appearing in a fairer and more adorned form, and that there is no way to this but to bring the meaning from the direction most sound for conveying it and to choose for it the word most proper to it and most revealing of it {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992 1 p.43}.

## Nazm is the meanings of nahw
al-Jurjani prepares for nazm by saying that scholars agreed on exalting it, that there is no merit without it and no worth in speech that does not hold to it, and that it is the pole on which the matter turns {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992 1 p.80}. He then says: "nazm is nothing but that you place your speech as the science of nahw requires", work by its rules and principles, know its methods and break none of its rules. By his account the arranger seeks only to look at the forms and distinctions of each chapter: in the predicate, the forms seen in "Zayd is going", "Zayd goes", "goes Zayd" and "Zayd is the one going", and in condition and result the forms of "if you go out I go out" and "if you go out, I am going out" {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992 1 p.81}. Here the book touches the page [[concept/ilm-al-nahw|Arabic grammar (nahw)]], and beside it stands [[work/al-khasais-ibn-jinni|al-Khasa'is (Ibn Jinni)]] on the definition of nahw and i'rab {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.35-36}.

## The meaning of the challenge
In the section on verifying what is said about inimitability, al-Jurjani calls to mind two verses his addressees recite: one on humans and jinn gathering to bring the like of the Qur'an, [[passage/quran-17-88|al-Isra 88]], the other on their being called to bring a sura like it, [[passage/quran-2-23|al-Baqara 23]]. He asks whether God may have commanded His Prophet to challenge the Arabs to oppose the Qur'an with its like without their knowing the attribute which, if they brought speech of that attribute, would mean they had brought its like. He says that if they answer that it may, they void the challenge, since the challenge is a demand to bring speech of an attribute and such a demand is not valid unless the attribute is known to the one asked, and the claim of inimitability is voided by it too {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992 1 p.385}. In the Shamela text read, the quotation of the first verse is followed by a reference to Hud 13, which does not match the words quoted; they are the words of al-Isra 88 as shown in its record here. A research summary supplied to the museum (the museum did not open the pages it names; the founder approved its use on 21 November 2026) accounts for this. It says that in Mahmud Muhammad Shakir's edition the Quranic index pairs [[passage/quran-11-13|Hud 13]] with the verse on ten invented suras (pp. 385, 606, 617) {cite:ablibrary-dalail-shakir-index-p634 p.634}, and that al-Jurjani's al-Risala al-Shafiya pairs the verse on humans and jinn gathering with [[passage/quran-17-88|al-Isra 88]] {cite:islamweb-jurjani-risala-shafiya-sarfa}; it found no pairing of al-Isra 88 with Hud 13 in Shakir's digitised edition. On that account the Hud 13 label seen on this verse is a fault of a secondary reprint or a transcription, not Shakir's own reference. The museum has not checked this itself; the point is noted here and the text read is not corrected.

## Eloquence belongs to meanings, not the single word
In another section al-Jurjani gives a proof that eloquence cannot be an attribute of the word as word: it must be either a sensed attribute known by hearing or an intelligible attribute known by the heart; if it were sensed, listeners would equally know a word to be eloquent; if intelligible, no attribute of a word is known by the mind and not by the senses except its signifying a meaning, so describing a word as eloquent describes it from the side of its meaning {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992 1 p.407}. He gives a second proof: a reader of the verse [[passage/quran-19-4|Maryam 4]] does not find the eloquence until the sentence reaches its end; if it were an attribute of the single word in it, he would feel it as he uttered it {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992 1 p.407}. This is al-Jurjani's argument and is ascribed to him; others have other sayings on the aspect of inimitability, on their own page.

## What this page did not reach
Only the places named were read, few in proportion to the book's size; the sections on advancing and delaying, omission, separation and joining, restriction, metonymy and metaphor, which fill most of it, were not read, nor were the letters appended to it. No date of composition was found in the pages read, so none is stated. Page numbers are those of the Shamela copy of Mahmud Muhammad Shakir's edition and were not compared with a paper copy. Some section titles in that edition were added by the editor, as he notes in one of his footnotes {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992 1 p.80}, and are not ascribed to the author.

## Place in the canon
This book appears in a research summary supplied to the museum among sixty of the most-cited classical and modern Arabic works across five sciences, twelve for each; it stands here in the section on rhetoric (balagha), and its author, according to the summary, is [[person/abd-al-qahir-al-jurjani|Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani]]. The file gives the author's death as 471 AH / 1078 CE; this is the date of the author's death, not of the book's composition. The Shamela links are bibliographic pointers only. {cite:wikipedia-en-abd-al-qahir-al-jurjani} {cite:shamela-ws-book-151016} The museum's own record of Dala'il al-I'jaz cites a Shamela copy (book 12055) from which the museum has read pages; so a Shamela text of the book does exist, whatever the file reported, and both statements are kept as they stand. {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992} The museum did not open the pages the summary draws on, and the founder approved using the summary without re-verification on 21 November 2026; this section does not alter what was read of the book in the sections above.
