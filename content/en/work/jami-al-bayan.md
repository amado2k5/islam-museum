## Summary
Ibn Jarir al-Tabari's tafsir, Jami' al-Bayan 'an Ta'wil Ay al-Qur'an, runs to thirty volumes; Abu Hamid al-Isfarayini said of it that a man travelling to China to obtain it would not have gone too far, and Goldziher called it a rich encyclopaedia of tradition-based exegesis {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim p.351}.

## Its method
Al-Tabari arranges his commentary verse by verse, building it on reports transmitted from accredited scholars alone and supporting them with variant isnads, applying to the transmitters the hadith critics' jarh and ta'dil — so that when a report proves untrustworthy he says so plainly, taking a frank, independent stance even toward the opinions of Ibn Abbas {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim p.351}. He attends to the plain meaning that cannot be abandoned, and where the text calls for another explanation he returns to the words of the salaf — Companions, Followers and the scholars of the community — while also drawing on the lore handed down from the People of the Book through figures like Ka'b al-Ahbar and Wahb ibn Munabbih {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim p.351}. He is counted the first commentator to make systematic use of ancient poetry, and his tafsir carries extensive linguistic and grammatical discussion, including some of the earliest documentation of the Basran-Kufan grammatical controversy {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim p.352}. For all this, Goldziher concludes, the great tafsir of al-Tabari is the heart of tradition-based exegesis and the summit that school reached {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim p.352}.

## For young visitors
Al-Tabari's commentary is among the greatest early books of tafsir; in it he gathered, verse by verse, what the first Muslims said about the meanings of the Qur'an.

## Readings, language and grammar
In a doctoral thesis at Umm al-Qura University (1402–1403 AH), Ahmad Khalid Babakr studied [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]]'s work in language and grammar through his handling of the [[concept/qiraat|readings]] he cites in Jami' al-Bayan. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.1} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.7} He concludes that al-Tabari stops at the places where the readers differ, discusses them and accounts for them, and that his commentary is an encyclopaedia of applied language and grammar, its discussions centred on words and constructions in order to draw out the possible meanings. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.1443} He finds al-Tabari independent in judgement, partisan neither for the Basrans nor for the Kufans: he discussed the scholars of both schools, weighed them and chose what he thought soundest in rule and strongest in argument, so that he belongs, in the thesis's view, to the Baghdad trend that blended the two schools. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.1443} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.1444} He notes that al-Tabari drew on [[person/al-kisai|al-Kisa'i]], [[person/al-farra|al-Farra']], [[person/qutrub|Qutrub]], [[person/ibn-qutayba|Ibn Qutayba]] and [[person/al-akhfash-al-awsat-said|al-Akhfash al-Awsat]], that he often cites a view by the city of its holder — "one of the grammarians of Basra said" when it comes from Abu Ubayda or al-Akhfash, "one of the grammarians of Kufa said" when it comes from al-Farra' — and that most of his reliance in language and grammar was on al-Farra'. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.1444}

## Its sources in exegesis and the chains to them
Ahmad Khalid Babakr holds that [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]] gathered his material in two ways: hearing his teachers on his journey from Tabaristan to Iraq, Syria and Egypt, and reading the works of those before him. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.47} He cites al-Dawudi's report of Abd Allah ibn Ahmad al-Farghani: the book explains the rulings, the abrogating and abrogated, the difficult and the rare, the meanings, the disagreement of the interpreters, the grammatical analysis of the words, the stories and the histories of nations, so that a scholar could make ten books of single sciences out of it. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.46} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.47}

Following Fuat Sezgin, the thesis names the written commentaries al-Tabari quotes through fixed chains: {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.47}
- the commentary of [[person/mujahid-ibn-jabr|Mujahid ibn Jabr]], by the chain Muhammad ibn Amr al-Bahili, from Abu Asim, from Isa ibn Maymun, from Ibn Abi Najih, from Mujahid; {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.47}
- that of [[person/al-dahhak-ibn-muzahim|al-Dahhak ibn Muzahim]], by two chains: "I was told from al-Minjab ibn al-Harith", from Bishr ibn Umara, from Abu Rawq, from al-Dahhak; and Abu Kurayb, from Uthman ibn Sa'id, from Bishr ibn Umara, from Abu Rawq; {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.48}
- that of [[person/atiyya-al-awfi|Atiyya al-Awfi]] from [[person/abdullah-ibn-abbas|Ibn Abbas]], in some 1,560 places, by the chain Muhammad ibn Sa'd, from his father, from his uncle, from al-Husayn ibn al-Hasan, from his father, from his grandfather Atiyya; {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.48}
- that of [[person/ata-ibn-abi-rabah|Ata ibn Abi Rabah]], from al-Qasim ibn al-Hasan al-Hamadani (d. 272), from al-Hasan ibn Dawud al-Massisi (d. 226; so in the thesis), from Hajjaj ibn Muhammad al-Massisi (d. 206), from [[person/ibn-jurayj|Ibn Jurayj]] (d. 150); {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.48} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.49}
- that of [[person/qatada-ibn-diama|Qatada ibn Di'ama]], in more than three thousand places, by the chain Bishr ibn Mu'adh, from Yazid ibn Zuray', from Sa'id, from Qatada; {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.49}
- that of [[person/al-rabi-ibn-anas|al-Rabi' ibn Anas]], from Ammar ibn al-Hasan, from Abd Allah ibn Abi Ja'far, from his father, from al-Rabi'; {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.49}
- and also the commentary of Muqatil ibn Sulayman al-Balkhi, that of [[person/hushaym-ibn-bashir|Hushaym ibn Bashir]] as transmitted by Ya'qub ibn Ibrahim al-Dawraqi (d. 252), and many passages from that of [[person/yazid-ibn-harun-al-wasiti|Yazid ibn Harun]] as transmitted by Mujahid ibn Musa (d. 244). {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.50}

## Its sources in language and grammar
The thesis says al-Tabari drew on [[person/abu-amr-ibn-al-ala|Abu Amr ibn al-Ala]], [[person/al-kisai|al-Kisa'i]], [[person/qutrub|Qutrub]], [[person/al-farra|al-Farra]], Abu Ubayda Ma'mar ibn al-Muthanna, [[person/al-akhfash-al-awsat-said|al-Akhfash al-Awsat]] and [[person/ibn-qutayba|Ibn Qutayba]], and that his linguistic and grammatical discussions are almost a discipline of their own inside the commentary. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.50} It cites Yaqut: al-Tabari set down the sum of what al-Kisa'i said and of the books of al-Farra, al-Akhfash and Qutrub, sometimes without naming them. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.50} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.51} He leaned heavily on al-Farra's Ma'ani al-Qur'an; Ahmad Makki al-Ansari notes that he often takes its very wording and verse-evidence and seldom names him. He attributed views to the scholars of a city — "one of the Basran scholars of Arabic said" when quoting al-Akhfash, "one of the Kufans learned in Arabic" when quoting al-Farra. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.51} He used Ibn Qutayba's [[work/tafsir-gharib-al-quran|Tafsir Gharib al-Qur'an]]; its editor al-Sayyid Ahmad Saqr says he sometimes copied its words without acknowledgement. The thesis offers the excuse that the two were contemporaries working from similar sources, and stresses that al-Tabari was no mere copyist: he criticised some of these views and rejected others. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.51} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.52}

## The readings in it
Al-Tabari stops at a plurality of readings in 854 places of the Qur'an, explains the variants and gives his view: sometimes he agrees with the readers and gives his reasons, sometimes he differs from some of them and gives his reasons, and in some places he only records the disagreement. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.1365} The thesis concludes that his judgements of accepting or rejecting a reading rest on three pillars: agreement with the script of the codex, the agreement of most readers, and conformity with the most eloquent speech of the Arabs. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.1381} The dispute over this is set out in [[question/did-al-tabari-reject-established-readings|Did al-Tabari reject established readings?]].

## Its influence on later scholars
The thesis cites Goldziher quoting Nöldeke, before the book was found, that if it were recovered one could do without the later commentaries. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.53} It notes that [[work/al-jami-li-ahkam-al-quran|al-Qurtubi]] quotes it in several places in his commentary on al-Fatiha and al-Baqara, that [[work/tafsir-ibn-kathir|Ibn Kathir]] quotes whole passages from it under al-Tabari's name, and that [[work/fath-al-qadir|al-Shawkani]] hardly discusses a verse without quotations from al-Tabari. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.53} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.54} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.55} In the readings, [[person/makki-ibn-abi-talib|Makki ibn Abi Talib]] gives some of his views on the seven ahruf in [[work/al-ibana-maani-al-qiraat|al-Ibana an Ma'ani al-Qira'at]], and Abu Zur'a Ibn Zanjala reproduces whole passages with their evidence in Hujjat al-Qira'at; in grammatical analysis Abu Ja'far al-Nahhas quotes him in about eighteen places of I'rab al-Qur'an, according to its editor Zuhayr Ghazi Zahid. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.55} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.56}

## The edition used in this harvest

The copy used here is the second edition by Ahmad Muhammad Shakir and Mahmud Muhammad Shakir, published by Maktabat Ibn Taymiyya in Cairo in sixteen volumes {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 1} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 1}. Its first page records that the text was read aloud to al-Tabari himself in the year 306 AH, a note of transmission at the head of the book {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 3}. The printing year is not read on the pages used here, so it is not given. Each volume is cited as its own source record.

## The introduction in the first volume

The first volume opens with praise of God and of the Prophet, and then al-Tabari states his aim: a book that covers, as fully as possible, everything people need of the knowledge of the Qur'an {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 6}. The index at the end of the volume lists the parts of the introduction. It begins with the "preface to the tafsir": the sameness of meaning between the Qur'an's wording and the speech of the Arabs, the degrees of eloquence, the miraculous quality of the Qur'an, and the words it shares with other languages {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 619}. It then takes up the language in which the Qur'an was revealed, and the hadith that it came down in seven ahruf, with its many versions {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 619}. The index gives al-Tabari's own summary: the seven ahruf are seven dialects of one word, differing in wording and agreeing in meaning, and the recitation in them was a permission, not an obligation {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 620}. Later chapters cover the collection of the Qur'an under Abu Bakr and its single written copy under Uthman, and the hadith of the seven gates of Paradise {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 620} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 621}. These are the index's summaries; the chapters themselves are read in a later pass.

## The introduction of volume 1 in detail (printed pp. 3-106)

The introduction opens with a statement that it was read to Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Jarir al-Tabari in 306 AH, then with praise of God for the wonders of His wisdom and the marvels of His making, and the witness of everything that exists to His oneness and lordship through the marks of making He set on them, lack and increase, weakness and need, so that His proof is complete; for this he cites [[passage/quran-13-15|13:15]] on those in the heavens and the earth bowing willingly or unwillingly. He says God followed His signs by sending messengers to whom He willed, so that people have no plea against God after the messengers, as in [[passage/quran-4-165|4:165]]. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 3} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 4}
God strengthened the messengers with His help and set them apart from the rest of creation by proofs and miracles so that none could say they were human like themselves, as in [[passage/quran-23-33|23:33-34]], and made them envoys between Himself and His creation and trustees of His revelation, then ranked them: some He honoured with speech, some He strengthened with the Holy Spirit, and He raised our Prophet Muhammad to the highest degree with the universal message and the complete call, protecting him from every stubborn tyrant and rebellious devil until through him the religion was made to prevail, the way made clear and the signposts of idolatry wiped out. He says the sign of his prophethood remains over the days, unlike the signs of earlier messengers who were overpowered by tyrants, so that their traces faded, or who were sent to one people and not another. The editor adds notes on the wording of the manuscript against the printed text. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 4} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 5}
Among the greatest merits God gave the community of Muhammad is His guarding of His revelation for them, made a sign of the truth of their Prophet's mission and a conclusive proof that divides the truthful from the false; if all jinn and humans united to bring a surah like it they could not, though they backed one another, and the editor points to [[passage/quran-2-23|2:23]], [[passage/quran-10-38|10:38]] and [[passage/quran-17-88|17:88]]. It is for them a light in the dark and a guide on the roads, guiding those who follow God's pleasure to the ways of peace as in [[passage/quran-5-16|5:16]], guarded by an eye that does not sleep; whoever follows it succeeds and whoever turns from it goes astray, and it is their refuge in dispute, their fortress against the whispers of Satan and the decision of His judgement among them. He then prays that God grant him success in reaching the right in its clear and its ambiguous verses, its lawful and unlawful, general and particular, summarised and explained, abrogating and abrogated, outer and inner, and the grace to hold to its clear verses and submit to its ambiguous ones. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 5} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 6}
Addressing the readers, he says that what most deserves attention is the knowledge in which God is pleased and which guides its bearer, and the most comprehensive of it is the Book of God in which there is no doubt, quoting the sense of [[passage/quran-41-42|41:42]]. He states his aim: to compose, in the explanation of its interpretation and its meanings, a comprehensive book of all that people need of its knowledge, sufficient in place of other books, reporting the agreement of the authorities where they agree and their differences where they differ, explaining the justification of each school, making clear what is sound in his view as briefly as he can, and asking God for help and success. He announces that he begins with the causes which are best put first, namely the account of what in the verses of the Qur'an confuses anyone who has not practised the Arabic sciences and has no firm knowledge of the turns of speech in natural tongues. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 6} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 7}
The first chapter of the introduction is headed "the account of the agreement of the meanings of the verses of the Qur'an with the meanings of the speech of those in whose tongue it came, as to clear expression, and the proof that this is from God as far-reaching wisdom, with the account of the excellence by which the Qur'an stands apart from all other speech". He says one of God's greatest favours is the gift of clear speech by which people express what is in their hearts, which loosened tongues and eased what was hard; and then He ranked them in it, from the fluent orator and the swift-tongued to the one struck dumb who cannot express himself, the highest being the one who is most eloquent in what he wants and clearest about himself. He cites [[passage/quran-43-18|43:18]] as telling them of the excellence of the speech He gave them over those who cannot express themselves. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 8} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 9}
He concludes that the highest rank of speech is the one that most fully conveys what the speaker needs to say, is clearest on his intent and nearest to the listener's understanding; if it exceeds that measure and passes the capacity of mankind so that all servants cannot bring its like, it is a proof and a sign for the messengers of the One, the Almighty, as raising the dead and healing the leper and the blind were signs for them, being beyond the highest degrees of medicine, and as a journey of two months in a single night was; so there is no clearer speech, no more far-reaching wisdom, nor nobler word than the speech with which He challenged creation. The editor adds linguistic notes on words such as "fluent", "swift-tongued" and "struck dumb". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 9}
Al-Tabari completes the chapter on clear speech: God challenged with the Qur'an a people who in their day were the masters of the craft of oratory, eloquence, poetry, rhymed prose and divination; He called their reasoning foolish, disowned their religion, and called them all to follow and believe him, telling them that the proof of his claim was what he brought of clear speech, wisdom and the criterion, in a tongue like theirs with a meaning matching the meaning of their speech; then He told them they could not bring the like of any part of it, and all admitted their inability and submitted in belief, except one who feigned ignorance and grew proud, attempted what he knew he could not do and produced what a weak clumsy man could, which he describes as strained rhymed nonsense ascribed to Musaylima the liar. The editor only refers to al-Tabari's History. From this he concludes that the clearest speech is His and the best word is His. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 10} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 11}
Next, God cannot address any creature except in what the one addressed understands, nor send a messenger except in a tongue the recipient understands, since if the one addressed does not understand, his state before and after the address is the same, and that is the act of deficient and idle people, from which God is exalted. He cites [[passage/quran-14-4|14:4]], that no messenger was sent except in the tongue of his people to make things clear, and [[passage/quran-16-64|16:64]] on explaining what they differed over. Every messenger was sent in the tongue of his people and every book sent down in the tongue of the one it came to, so the Qur'an is in the tongue of Muhammad, which is Arabic, and so [[passage/quran-12-2|12:2]] and [[passage/quran-26-193|26:193-195]] say. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 11} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 12}
He says the meanings of the Book of God agree with the meanings of Arab speech and its outward sense fits the outward sense of their language, although it surpasses it by the excellence by which it is above all other speech; whatever is found in Arab speech of brevity and economy, of leaving out rather than stating, of lengthening and repeating, of naming rather than using a pronoun, of the particular meant by the general and the general by the particular, of the quality meant for the thing described and the reverse, of putting forward what is later in sense and delaying what is earlier, of relying on part for the whole, has its likes in the Book of God. He promises to show all this in its places; the editor remarks on the length of the sentence, a habit of al-Tabari's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 12}
A second chapter begins, headed "the account of the words in which the words of the Arabs and of other kinds of nations agree". An inquirer says: you wrote that God cannot address anyone except in what he understands, so what do you say to what is reported from Abu Musa that "kiflayn" in [[passage/quran-57-28|57:28]] means two portions in the Abyssinian tongue, from Ibn Abbas that "nashi'at al-layl" in [[passage/quran-73-6|73:6]] means rising at night in Abyssinian, from Abu Maysara that "awwibi" in [[passage/quran-34-10|34:10]] means "glorify" in Abyssinian, from Ibn Abbas that "qaswara" in [[passage/quran-74-51|74:51]] is lion in Arabic, "shar" in Persian, "arya" in Nabataean and "qaswara" in Abyssinian, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr that Quraysh asked why this Qur'an was not sent in a foreign tongue as well as Arabic and [[passage/quran-41-44|41:44]] came, after which every tongue was sent down in it, including "sijjil" as Arabised Persian, and from Abu Maysara that in the Qur'an are words from every tongue? {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 13} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 14}
He answers that those who said so did not say these words were not Arabic speech before the Qur'an came; they said that this word in the Abyssinian tongue means this, and he finds nothing strange in speech in which the words of different nations agree with one meaning, as with dirham, dinar, inkwell, pen and paper, which Persian and Arabic share in word and meaning. If someone said all this is Persian and not Arabic, or the reverse, or that the origin came from the Arabs to the non-Arabs or the other way, he would be taken for ignorant, since the Arabs are no likelier to be the source than the others, and whoever claims one direction claims what can be reached only by a report that gives knowledge. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 14} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 15}
Rather, in his view, such a word should be called Arab-foreign or Abyssinian-Arab, since both peoples use it in their speech as they use the rest of their language, and the way to ascribe any word shared in sound and sense is by joining and pairing. This is what those mean who ascribed words to Abyssinian, Persian or Greek: by that ascription they did not deny it was Arabic, nor do those who called it Arabic deny it belongs to the others; for an affirmation implies a denial only where the two cannot be together, as "so and so is standing" shows he is not sitting, but not so for "he is standing and speaking to so and so". So a word may be both Arabic and foreign, unlike descent from Adam's children, which is limited to one side as [[passage/quran-33-5|33:5]] says, because speech is ascribed to those known to use it, like a land between plain and mountain, called both plain and mountain land; and this, he says, is the sense of the saying that the Qur'an has words of every tongue, meaning words in which Arabic and another nation's tongue agree. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 16} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 17}

We stop here at the foot of p. 17, after the example of a land between plain and mountain; the next batch begins at p. 18. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 17}
