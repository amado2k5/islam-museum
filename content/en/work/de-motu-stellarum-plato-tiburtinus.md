## Summary
This is the Latin translation of [[work/al-zij-al-sabi|al-Battani's Zij]] made by [[person/plato-of-tivoli|Plato of Tivoli]], the only one of two Latin versions to survive. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 58} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59} The titles it bears in print — On the motion of the stars, On the science of the stars, On the numbers of the stars — are taken from the book's opening. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40}

## First printing
It was first printed at Nuremberg in 1537 together with Alfraganus' Rudimenta, under a title naming "Albategnius, most skilled astronomer, on the motion of the stars from his own and Ptolemy's observations, all with geometrical demonstrations and the additions of Johannes Regiomontanus"; Alfraganus takes 26 folios and Albategnius 90, separately numbered. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59} It was printed from a copy Regiomontanus had annotated in his own hand. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 46} Plato's preface comes first, calling the science of the stars the prince of the liberal arts. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59}

## Opening and chapters
The version opens "in the name of the Lord" and announces the book of Muhammad son of Jabir son of Sinan, called Albategni, on the numbers of the stars and the places of their motions grasped by experiment, in fifty-seven chapters. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59} In some Latin manuscripts, such as those of Venice and the Vatican, chapter 57 is headed as the completion of the book and the making of the instrument in the likeness of the heavens and of two sighting instruments. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 61}

## Manuscripts
Manuscripts exist at Erfurt, one breaking off in chapter 55, another holding multiplication and division tables drawn from chapter 2. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 60}

## Its value
Halley and Nallino find it obscure and barbarous, senseless in many places, and with more numerical errors than the Arabic. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 65} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 71} Yet it witnesses an archetype shared with the Escorial manuscript and preserves a few words lost from it. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 71}
