## Overview
"The Thousand and One Nights", a book of stories. This entry records the earliest mention of it in the sources held here, not its text.

## In al-Mas'udi
In the year 332 AH, al-Mas'udi mentioned books translated from Persian, among them "Hazar Afsana". Its Arabic meaning is "A Thousand Fables", since a fable in Persian is an "afsana". He said that people call it "The Thousand and One Nights". {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.550}

As he describes it, the book tells the story of a king, his vizier, the vizier's daughter and her maid, who are named Shirzad and Dinazad. He mentions alongside it the book of Farzah and Simas, and the book of Sindbad. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.550}

## In the Fihrist
Ibn al-Nadim reports that Muhammad ibn Abdus al-Jahshiyari, the author of "Kitab al-Wuzara", began to compose a book in which he chose a thousand tales from the tales of the Arabs, the Persians and the Byzantines. He gathered four hundred and eighty nights of them before death overtook him. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.423}

Ibn al-Nadim saw several parts of it in the hand of Abu al-Tayyib, the brother of al-Shafi'i. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.423}

## Encyclopaedia of Islam account: origins and Galland
The Encyclopaedia of Islam (2nd ed.) describes Alf layla wa-layla, "A thousand nights and one night", as the most famous Arabian collection of fairy-tales and other stories. The fairy-tales are its most striking part. The Arabs, like other Orientals, enjoyed imaginative stories from the earliest times, but since their intellectual horizon before Islam was narrow, much of the material was borrowed from Persia and India, as the account of the merchant al-Nadr, a rival of the Prophet, suggests. Later, when Arab civilization was richer, foreign influence was far stronger. The stories range from King Solomon and the kings of ancient Persia to Alexander, the caliphs and anachronisms such as guns and coffee, and the whole work is enclosed in a frame-story. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 380-386}

The European discovery of the Nights is the work of Antoine Galland. He first translated Sindbad the Sailor from an unidentified manuscript, then learned that it belonged to a great collection, and then received from Syria four volumes of a manuscript that, apart from a small fragment found by Nabia Abbott, is the oldest known and contains the best surviving text; the first three of these volumes are still in the Bibliotheque nationale, and the fourth is lost. Galland translated the first seven volumes from the three Arabic volumes, adding Sindbad and Camalzaman (Kamar al-Zaman) from unidentified manuscripts. For lack of material he stopped for three years, until his publisher, without authority, issued an eighth volume containing Ganem (Ghanim), translated by Galland, and two stories, Zayn al-Asnam and Khudadad, translated by Petis de la Croix for his Mille et un jours. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 380-386}

In 1709 Galland met the Maronite Hanna of Aleppo, whom the traveller Paul Lucas had brought to Paris. Recognizing an oral source of the story material, he took down Hanna's tales in Arabic and inserted abstracts of them in his Journal, and Hanna also gave him transcripts, which filled the last four volumes of Galland's translation. Hanna's transcripts have vanished, but two Arabic manuscripts of Aladdin and one of Ali Baba have since come to light; Zotenberg's study of Aladdin (1888) is the main guide to them. For more than a century Galland's French version was the Nights for Europe. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 380-386}

## Later supplements
Other manuscripts connected with the Nights were used for supplements, which translators attached to Galland's text or published separately. Denis Chavis's tales from the Arabic appeared in 1788 as a supplement to the Cabinet des Fees (vols 38-41), and three separate English translations of it appeared in 1792-94. William Beloe published Arabic stories that Patrick Russell had translated for him orally (1795). Jonathan Scott translated stories from a manuscript brought from India by James Anderson (1800), and added a volume from the Wortley Montague manuscript, now in Oxford, to his English Galland (1811). Caussin de Perceval added two supplementary volumes to his Galland in 1806, and Edouard Gauttier, in his Galland of 1822-25, went much further and freely inserted tales of all kinds. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 380-386}

Von Hammer, whose firmer foundation was a true recension, had acquired in Egypt a manuscript of the recension now called Zotenberg's Egyptian Recension, which became through many editions the Vulgate text of the Nights. His French translation of stories absent from Galland is lost, but Zinserling translated it into German (1823), Lamb into English (1826), and Trebutien into French (1828). {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 380-386}
