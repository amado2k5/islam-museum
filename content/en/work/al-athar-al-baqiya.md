## Summary
Al-Athar al-Baqiya 'an al-Qurun al-Khaliya ("The Remaining Traces of Past Centuries") is [[person/al-biruni-muhammad-ibn-ahmad|Abu Rayhan Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Biruni of Khwarazm]]'s work on eras, calendars and festivals, written for Shams al-Ma'ali Qabus. {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179} {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 3} Hajji Khalifa calls it a useful book setting out the eras the nations use and the disagreement over their principles. {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}

## Occasion and method
Al-Biruni says a man of letters asked him about the eras the nations use and how they differ in their principles - their starting points - and branches - their months and years - and the reasons behind them, and about the famous feasts and days kept for seasons and works, asking him to explain all this as clearly as possible so as to spare him searching scattered books. {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 4} He says he found it a hard undertaking but resolved to spend his utmost on it, as far as his knowledge reached, "by hearing, by observation, or by inference". {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 4} He warns against the falsehoods that enter reports, holding that whatever is within the bounds of the possible is treated as a true report unless other evidence shows it false, and that a human life is not enough to know the history of a single nation thoroughly, let alone all of them. {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 5}

## The day and its beginning
Al-Biruni defines the day with its night as the sun's return, through the turning of the whole, to a circle chosen as its beginning, and notes that the Arabs made sunset the beginning of the day with its night, because their months follow the course of the moon and are fixed by sighting the new crescent rather than by calculation, and the crescent is seen at sunset - so for them the night comes before the day. {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 6}

## Intercalation and nasi'
Al-Biruni explains how the nations differ in intercalation: some intercalate their solar year, some ignore the fractions, and the Jews, Sabians and Harranians take their year from the sun and their months from the moon, intercalating seven months in every nineteen lunar years. {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 13} He says that the pre-Islamic Arabs added a month whenever the difference between their year and the solar year amounted to one, that this was done by the intercalators of Kinana known as the Qalamis, that they took it from the Jews about two hundred years before Islam, and that it lasted until the prohibition of [[concept/nasi|nasi']] at the Farewell Pilgrimage. {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 13} {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 14}

## The chapters on festivals
Al-Biruni devotes chapters to the feasts and days in the months of the nations: the Persians (p. 256), the Sogdians (p. 280), the people of Khwarazm (p. 283), the Byzantines (p. 292), the Jews (p. 336), the Melkite Christians (p. 352), the Christian fasts (p. 374), the Nestorians (p. 381), and the ancient Magians and the Sabians (p. 392). {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 256} {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 336} {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 392}

## The Arabs and the Muslims
Al-Biruni says the Arab months are twelve, four of them sacred - one standing alone, Rajab, and three in succession, Dhu al-Qa'da, Dhu al-Hijja and Muharram - and reports what was said of the meanings of their names, such as Safar for an epidemic that turned their faces yellow, Ramadan because it came when the ground grew scorching, and Shawwal for the camels raising their tails. {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 401} {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 402} He says the Muslims used the Arab months without intercalation, kept the four sacred months, called Shawwal, Dhu al-Qa'da and the first ten days of Dhu al-Hijja the months of pilgrimage, and then lists the days honoured in each month, among them Ashura'. {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 407} See: [[concept/hijri-calendar|The Hijri calendar]].

## The lunar mansions
Al-Biruni ends the book with a chapter on the [[concept/manazil-al-qamar|lunar mansions]], their risings, settings and figures. {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 p. 416}

## Not covered here
