## Summary

*The Art and Architecture of Islam, 1250–1800* by Sheila S. Blair and Jonathan M. Bloom surveys the architecture and arts of the traditional Islamic lands from the Mongol conquests of the early thirteenth century to the advent of European colonial rule in the nineteenth. {cite:openlibrary-art-and-architecture-of-islam-1250-1800} The publisher's description notes that virtually all the masterpieces of Islamic art, such as the Alhambra, the Taj Mahal and the Shahnama of Shah Tahmasp, were produced in this period. {cite:openlibrary-art-and-architecture-of-islam-1250-1800} It was conceived as a sequel to [[work/art-and-architecture-of-islam-650-1250|*The Art and Architecture of Islam, 650–1250*]] by Richard Ettinghausen and Oleg Grabar, and follows its format: chronological and regional divisions, with architecture treated separately from the other arts. {cite:openlibrary-art-and-architecture-of-islam-1250-1800} The authors describe over two hundred works of the period and look at their social and economic contexts, including function, patronage and meaning. {cite:openlibrary-art-and-architecture-of-islam-1250-1800}

The book has two parts. Part I, 1250–1500, covers Iran and Central Asia under the Ilkhanids and then the Timurids, Egypt and Syria under the Mamluks, the Maghrib under the Hafsids, Marinids and Nasrids, Anatolia under the Beyliks and early Ottomans, and India under the Sultanates. {cite:uwmadison-art-and-architecture-of-islam-1250-1800} Part II, 1500–1800, covers Iran under the Safavids and Zands, Central Asia under the Uzbeks, the Ottomans after the conquest of Constantinople, Egypt and North Africa, and India under the Mughals and their contemporaries in the Deccan. {cite:uwmadison-art-and-architecture-of-islam-1250-1800} According to the publisher's description, the authors argue that Iranian forms, techniques and motifs dominated artistic life in most of the Muslim world after the Mongol invasions, the Maghrib excepted, and that by the sixteenth century the Ottomans and Mughals had developed distinct regional and imperial styles. {cite:openlibrary-art-and-architecture-of-islam-1250-1800} The book closes with an essay on the legacies of later Islamic art in Europe and the Islamic lands in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. {cite:openlibrary-art-and-architecture-of-islam-1250-1800} {cite:uwmadison-art-and-architecture-of-islam-1250-1800}

## Publication and editions

Yale University Press published the book in New Haven in 1994, in 348 pages, in its Pelican History of Art series. {cite:openlibrary-art-and-architecture-of-islam-1250-1800} The University of Wisconsin–Madison catalogue records the illustrated 1995 printing, “reprinted with corrections”, with a bibliography on pages 333–339. {cite:uwmadison-art-and-architecture-of-islam-1250-1800}
