## Summary
[[person/mark-zebrowski|Mark Zebrowski]]'s Deccani Painting argues that a surprisingly large share of the masterpieces of Indian painting was made for the sultans of the Deccan in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and that at least three kingdoms — Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and Golconda — patronised outstanding painting long confused with Persian, Turkish or Mughal art because of their strong links with Safavid Iran. {cite:zebrowski-deccani-painting-1983 p. 1} {cite:zebrowski-deccani-painting-1983 p. 7}

## Structure
Twelve chapters: [[concept/ahmadnagar-painting|Ahmadnagar]], with an appendix on [[person/malik-ambar|Malik Ambar]]; the northern Deccan; three chapters on [[concept/bijapur-painting|Bijapur]] under Ali Adil Shah I, [[person/ibrahim-adil-shah-ii|Ibrahim Adil Shah II]] and Muhammad Adil Shah, with an appendix on [[practice/deccani-marbled-drawings|marbled drawings]]; one on Ali II and Sikandar; three on [[concept/golconda-painting|Golconda]]; then Mughal hegemony (1687–1724) and Hyderabad and the provinces, with a table of rulers and an index of artists. {cite:zebrowski-deccani-painting-1983 p. 5}

## Argument
For the author, Deccani art reached a character of its own, owing as much to the sensuality of South Indian art as to Persianate traditions, with dense compositions, intense colour and a lyrical romanticism alien to Mughal sobriety and Iranian calligraphic subtlety. He rejects the common view that Deccani painting declined after the Mughal conquest: it was entering a new phase that the conquest cut short, and creativity continued under Mughal hegemony. {cite:zebrowski-deccani-painting-1983 p. 1} {cite:zebrowski-deccani-painting-1983 p. 13} {cite:zebrowski-deccani-painting-1983 p. 206}
