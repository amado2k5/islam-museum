## Overview
*Al-Asbila fi al-'imara al-islamiyya bi-Makka al-Mukarrama wa-l-Madina al-Munawwara: dirasa tarikhiyya athariyya* is a book by [[person/muhammad-hamza-ismail-al-haddad|Muhammad Hamza Isma'il al-Haddad]], professor of Islamic architecture and archaeology at Cairo University, the fourth part of his series Islamic Architecture in the Arabian Peninsula, first published by Maktabat Zahra' al-Sharq in Cairo in 2004. {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 title page} {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 imprint}

## Structure
- Introduction: on the meaning and derivation of the word [[concept/sabil|sabil]] and on charitable foundations generally. {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.7} {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.10}
- Chapter 1, historical study: water in the Hijaz and the Mecca-Medina road, then the sabils of Mecca from the Abbasids to King Abd al-Aziz, and those of Medina from the Mamluks to Muhammad Ali. {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.5} {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.13}
- Chapter 2, archaeological study: the [[building/siqayat-al-abbas|Siqaya of al-Abbas]], [[building/sabil-of-al-muayyad-shaykh-mecca|al-Mu'ayyad Shaykh's sabil]] at the Haram, [[building/sabil-of-qaytbay-mina|Qaytbay's sabil]] at Masjid al-Khayf, then Ottoman sabils and those of King Abd al-Aziz. {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.6} {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.37}
- Conclusion, notes, figures and plates, sources. {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.6}

## Method
The author notes that almost none of the sabils of the two cities survive, so he relies on local histories such as al-Azraqi's and al-Fakihi's Akhbar Makka and al-Fasi's Shifa' al-gharam, on travellers' accounts, documents and biographies, and on surviving inscriptions and photographs to recover their types and elements. {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.10} {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.37} He compares the Hijazi examples with the Mamluk sabils of Cairo and discusses modern scholars such as al-Harithi, Ghabbashi and Nuwaysir. {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.41} {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.43} {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.47}

## Main conclusions
The author concludes that siqayas were known from early Islam, that by the last quarter of the third century AH Mecca alone had about a hundred, and that they spread under the name sabil from the Ayyubid period. {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.49} He holds that al-Mu'ayyad Shaykh's sabil beside Zamzam is the earliest known three-window sabil, that Qaytbay's four-window sabil at Masjid al-Khayf is a rare example, that Ottoman sabils added the "çeşme" (tap fountain) for drinking and ablution, and that the Mina sabil of 1340 AH was a splendid finale to sabil architecture. {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.50} {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.51}
