## Overview
Sirat al-Hadi ila al-Haqq is a biography of Imam Yahya ibn al-Husayn ibn al-Qasim al-Rassi, founder of the Zaydi imamate in Yemen. Its first page attributes the narration to two men, Muhammad ibn Sulayman al-Kufi and Ali ibn Muhammad ibn Ubaydallah al-Abbasi al-Alawi. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.15} It was edited by Suhayl Zakkar and published in Beirut by Dar al-Fikr, and his preface is dated 15 Sha'ban 1392 AH. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.10} It is a Zaydi source that speaks in the voice of the imam's followers: its descriptions of the imam, his merits and the marvels related of him are attributed to its narrators and are not the museum's own judgement.

## The two narrators
The first leaf of the Ali Amiri manuscript says the book is narrated by Muhammad ibn Sulayman al-Kufi and Ali ibn Muhammad ibn Ubaydallah al-Abbasi al-Alawi. The editor says he searched the sources available to him for biographies of the two men and found none, but that the biography itself gives enough to identify them. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.7}

Al-Kufi was a companion of al-Hadi before the imam went to Yemen, and perhaps joined him from Kufa after hearing of him; he travelled to Yemen some fifty days before the imam. In Yemen he stayed close to al-Hadi in what could be called the position of a vizier: the imam gave him missions, had him carry out orders and put some provincial and tax-collecting duties in his hands. The editor therefore treats his reports as documentary in character, although they relate events from one side only. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.7} Yet in the editor's judgement al-Kufi was not one of the compilers of the book: a study of the text shows that its author and narrator is Ali ibn Muhammad. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.7}

The material Ali provided falls into two parts: a large part, most of which he relates from his father Muhammad ibn Ubaydallah, from al-Kufi and from others who were with al-Hadi, and a part consisting of events he witnessed and took part in. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.8} His father was among the first to receive al-Hadi's call before he left for Yemen; he went with him there and the imam entrusted him with weighty duties until he was killed carrying them out. When the father resolved to emigrate he told his son to follow, and Ali was then a boy on whom no obligation before God had yet fallen, that is, one who had not yet come of age; he went to al-Hadi in Dhu al-Hijja 285 AH and stayed with him until his own death. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.8} The heading of the book presents Ali as one of the noble young men of al-Hadi's days and among the martyrs at Najran, carried alive from the battle to Khiwan, where he died and where his tomb is well known and visited. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.15}

## The manuscript and the edition
Suhayl Zakkar found the manuscript in the Ali Amiri library at the Fatih in Istanbul in autumn 1969: number 2469, ninety-four large leaves, more than thirty-five lines to the page and about fifteen words to the line. It was copied in Yemen in 1086 AH and collated against what was called the mother copy in 1087 AH. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.5} {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.6} When he read the photographs he found them hard to read but rich in information, and decided to transcribe and then edit the text. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.6}

He learned of another copy in the library of the Great Mosque of Sana'a and obtained a photograph of it through his colleague Dr Hassanayn Rabi' of Cairo University. Comparison showed that both copies derive from a single original, and that the Ali Amiri copy is the more complete, sounder and better pointed, although later in date than the Sana'a copy, which has many omissions, misreadings and grammatical and spelling errors; he therefore took the Ali Amiri copy as his base. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.6} He recorded only some of the differences between the two copies and did not multiply notes. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.7} A table of printing errors is appended to the book. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.10}

## Structure of the book
In the editor's view the book consists of a main part and two appendices: the first closely tied to the author and narrator, the second added later and dealing with the sons of al-Hadi after him. He thinks the main part has been somewhat arranged and added to, perhaps including the opening on the imamate and the need for an imam, and the verse scattered through the book, especially near the end. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.8} He judges all the headings in the book to be later insertions, not original, but left them unchanged. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.9} {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.10}

The editor also observes that the biography is modelled on the prophetic sira: it opens with the imamate and the need for an imam as that opens with prophethood, and al-Hadi in it is the heir of the Prophet, with marvels and distinctions like his. As the cave belongs to the emigration from Mecca to Medina, so a small well of water belongs to al-Hadi's emigration to Yemen; and as the Prophet met communities of the People of the Book after reaching Medina, al-Hadi met the like after reaching Najran. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.9} This is the editor's observation, not a judgement of the museum.

## Its historical value
The editor says that, although it is the biography of one of the great imams of the Shia, the text is among the most important historical texts and the richest in information about a large part of Arabia running from Najran across almost all of Yemen, with unique reports on the condition and movements of Arab tribes and on the movements of the Qarmatians in the early fourth century AH, tenth century CE. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.9}

## How the narrative opens
The book opens with al-Hadi's descent: Yahya ibn al-Husayn ibn al-Qasim ibn Ibrahim ibn Ismail ibn Ibrahim ibn al-Hasan ibn al-Hasan ibn Ali ibn Abi Talib. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.15} The narrative then begins with his being summoned from al-Rass: he went to Sa'da, Najran and the districts of Khawlan, calling people to the Book of God and the sunna of his Messenger, applying the penalties, enjoining the good and forbidding the wrong, and the Friday address was given in his name in the districts of Najran and Khawlan. The man who held the affairs of Sana'a, known as Abu al-Atahiya, corresponded with him and supplied him with troops and money, then invited him to Sana'a, met him, handed over what he held and swore allegiance to him together with the commanders of Yemen who were with him. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.17} Al-Hadi entered Sana'a on the night of Friday, 21 Muharram 288 AH; the address from the pulpits was given in his name as imam, his name was put on the coinage and the tiraz, and he appointed as judge Muhammad ibn Ahmad ibn Zurayq al-A'sam, a client of the Banu al-Abbas, who judged according to his own school. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.18} In a footnote on p. 20 the editor says all the preceding pages appear to have been added to the original, that Muhammad ibn Sulayman al-Kufi's account begins after them, and that the complete Great Mosque manuscript of Sana'a begins from that point. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.20} {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.21}

## The doctrinal preface: the imamate and the reports of the man of Yemen, pp. 21 to 35
The account of [[person/muhammad-ibn-sulayman-al-kufi|Muhammad ibn Sulayman al-Kufi]] begins with praise of God and then a doctrinal preface on the duty of jihad and enjoining good, on the imamate in the line of al-Hasan and al-Husayn, and on the qualities of the one whose obedience is due. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.21} {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.22} {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.27} It is followed by reports and verses attributed to Ali, Muhammad al-Baqir, Zayd ibn Ali and others about the rising of a man of the family in Yemen after 280 AH, from which the narrators conclude that it is al-Hadi, after looking at those of the family who rose before and not finding the qualities in them. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.29} {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.33} {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.34} {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.35} Two records set this out: [[concept/imamate-conditions-in-the-preface-of-sirat-al-hadi]] and [[concept/reports-foretelling-the-man-of-yemen-in-sirat-al-hadi]]. This is among the most contested parts of the book; the Zaydi position is shown as attributed to its narrators, with the Sunni position in the general record [[concept/imama]].

## Al-Hadi's letters to Medina and his first journey, pp. 35 to 37
Ali ibn Muhammad relates that al-Hadi had gone to Yemen in 280 AH as far as al-Sharfa near Sana'a and returned, {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.36} [[event/al-hadi-first-journey-to-yemen-280]], that in Dhu al-Qa'da 283 AH his letters reached a group of the Banu Abi Talib at Medina, {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.35} {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.36} [[event/al-hadi-letters-to-medina-283]], and that his father Muhammad ibn Ubaydallah left with Yahya ibn al-Husayn ibn Yahya at the new moon of Dhu al-Hijja for [[place/al-fur-medina|al-Fur']], where they met al-Hadi, his father al-Husayn ibn al-Qasim and his two uncles Muhammad and al-Hasan. {cite:sira-hadi-ila-al-haqq p.37}

## The journey to Yemen and the arrival at Sa'da, pp. 37 to 44
