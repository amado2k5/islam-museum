## Overview
*Sahih Ibn Hibban* is the collection of sound hadith of [[person/ibn-hibban|Abu Hatim Muhammad ibn Hibban al-Tamimi al-Busti]] (d. 354 AH). The title found on the first surviving fragment of the original is "The Sound Musnad on Divisions and Types, with no Break in its Chains and no Established Discreditation of its Transmitters". Works on hadith terminology and biography call it *al-Taqasim wa-al-Anwa'* ("Divisions and Types"), while hadith scholars, jurists, referencers and the public at large call it *Sahih Ibn Hibban*. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 6-9}

[[person/ahmad-muhammad-shakir|Ahmad Muhammad Shakir]] holds that this full title is the one its author gave it, citing the fact that Abu Sa'd al-Idrisi, the hadith scholar of Samarqand and Ibn Hibban's contemporary, said he wrote "al-Musnad al-Sahih", and that "al-Taqasim wa-al-Anwa'" is simply another part of the same title. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 9-10}

## Its place among the collections of sound hadith
Shakir explains that Ibn Hibban followed his teacher [[person/ibn-khuzayma|Ibn Khuzayma]] in compiling a book limited to sound hadith, and that his own student [[person/al-hakim-al-nisaburi|al-Hakim]] followed him with [[work/al-mustadrak-ala-al-sahihayn|*al-Mustadrak*]]; these three are the most important books of "sound hadith only" after [[work/sahih-al-bukhari|al-Bukhari's]] and [[work/sahih-muslim|Muslim's]] *Sahih*s. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 6-7}

He reports that critics ranked them, by how strictly each kept to sound hadith, as Ibn Khuzayma's, then Ibn Hibban's, then the *Mustadrak*. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 11} His introduction gathers their statements: [[person/ibn-al-salah|Ibn al-Salah]] said Ibn Hibban's *Sahih* "comes close" to the *Mustadrak* in its standing; [[person/abd-al-rahim-al-iraqi|al-'Iraqi]] explained that he meant closeness in laxity, al-Hakim being the laxer; [[person/al-hazimi-muhammad|al-Hazimi]] said "Ibn Hibban is more capable in hadith than al-Hakim"; and [[person/ibn-kathir|Ibn Kathir]] said Ibn Khuzayma and Ibn Hibban "are far better than the *Mustadrak*, and cleaner in chains and texts". {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 12-13} The debate is set out on the card [[question/tasahul-ibn-hibban|Was Ibn Hibban lax in authenticating hadith?]].

He also quotes [[person/al-suyuti|al-Suyuti]] that Ibn Khuzayma's *Sahih* ranks higher because of his extreme caution, Ibn al-Mulaqqin in *al-Badr al-Munir* that most of Ibn Hibban's *Sahih* is drawn from his teacher Ibn Khuzayma's, and *al-Shadharat* that most hadith critics consider it sounder than the *Sunan* of Ibn Majah. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 13, 15} Shakir's own view is that Ibn Hibban produced an independent book, not built on the two *Sahih*s or on anything else. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 15}

## An arrangement of his own: five divisions and four hundred types
In his preface Ibn Hibban says that he saw the routes of reports multiplying while people's knowledge of the sound ones dwindled, and that the compilers of the Sunna had repeated chains so often that learners leaned on the book and gave up memorising. So he studied the sound reports to make them easier to memorise, and found that they fall into five divisions: the commands God gave His servants; the prohibitions He laid on them; His reports of what people need to know; the permissions of what may be done; and the acts the Prophet did that were his alone. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 58-59}

He then split each division into types: the commands come to one hundred and ten types {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 60, 74}, the prohibitions to one hundred and ten {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 75}, the reports to eighty {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 88}, the permissions to fifty {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 98} and the acts to fifty {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 103}; "so all the types of the Sunna are four hundred". {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 109}

He explains that the purpose of classifying the Sunna was to bring two things to light: reports over which the imams disagreed in substance or interpretation, and general wordings whose meaning most people found hard to grasp. He modelled the layout on the Qur'an: the divisions stand for its parts, the types for its suras and the hadiths for its verses; Shakir takes "parts" here to mean the early division into portions reported in the hadith of Aws ibn Hudhayfa, not the thirty juz'. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 109-110} Whoever memorises the book's divisions, he says, will command all of it, just as one who knows the Qur'an by heart knows where every verse is; this "device" was meant to make people memorise the Sunna and resort to writing only when needed. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 111}

At the close of each division he notes that some of its hadiths were scattered through the other divisions, and that no type is exhaustive: he gave only the most prominent of each, so that the book would not become repetitive. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 124-126}

Shakir quotes al-Suyuti that the arrangement is "invented, neither by chapters nor by Companion", that finding anything in it is very hard, and that al-Suyuti put this down to Ibn Hibban's knowledge of theology, grammar and philosophy; Shakir himself judges that the author's aim was never achieved and readers were left with the difficulty instead. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 15-16}

## His condition for narrators
Ibn Hibban says that he relied in the book only on hadiths in which every shaykh of the chain combined five things: probity in religion, shown by a good outward record; truthfulness in hadith, shown by repute in it; understanding of what he narrates; knowledge of what can distort the meaning of what he narrates; and a report free of [[concept/tadlis|concealment (tadlis)]]. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 112}

He then defines each. The upright person is one whose outward conduct is mostly obedience to God, for if probity required no sin at all there would be no upright person in the world. Understanding means knowing enough language not to shift the meanings of reports, and enough of the craft of hadith not to give a halted report a chain, raise a loose report to the Prophet, or garble a name. Knowledge means knowing enough law that when he passes on, recalls or shortens a report he does not turn it from its meaning. Freedom from tadlis means that he hears it from someone of the same description, and so on up to the Prophet. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 112-113}

He says that he wrote from more than two thousand shaykhs, from Isbijab to Alexandria, but narrated in this book from only about a hundred and fifty, and that it rests mainly on some twenty shaykhs around whom he organised the Sunna. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 113-114}

## Disputed narrators and the method of corroboration
Ibn Hibban states openly that he sometimes relied on shaykhs criticised by some imams, such as [[person/simak-ibn-harb|Simak ibn Harb]], [[person/dawud-ibn-abi-hind|Dawud ibn Abi Hind]], [[person/ibn-ishaq|Muhammad ibn Ishaq]], [[person/hammad-ibn-salama|Hammad ibn Salama]] and Abu Bakr ibn 'Ayyash: whoever he found by clear proofs to be trustworthy he relied on, paying no attention to the critic, and whoever he found not upright he did not use, even if some had vouched for him. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 114} He takes Hammad ibn Salama as his example and answers, one by one, the objections raised against him. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 114-117} Shakir reads this as a veiled reproach of al-Bukhari, who did not use Hammad as a proof. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 115-116}

He describes the method of *i'tibar* (corroboration): when a trustworthy narrator gives a report no one else gives, one first asks whether his own students transmit it from him, then whether another reliable narrator has it from his teacher's teacher, then from the Companion through someone other than the Successor, then from the Prophet through another Companion; if all of this is lacking and the report contradicts the three principles, it is known to be fabricated and the one who alone transmits it to be its fabricator. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 117-118} Those he found discredited he placed in [[work/al-majruhin-min-al-muhaddithin|*al-Majruhin min al-Muhaddithin*]] under one of the twenty grounds of discreditation set out at its start; those he relied on despite criticism are explained in [[work/al-thiqat-ibn-hibban|"the abridged book of the history of the trustworthy"]]. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 118, 126}

## Solitary reports and additions
Ibn Hibban holds that all reports are [[concept/khabar-al-ahad|solitary reports (ahad)]], since no report exists that two upright men took from two upright men all the way back to the Prophet, and that whoever refuses solitary reports has in effect abandoned the entire Sunna. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 118} When one upright narrator gives a report without its Companion link and another gives it with a full chain, he accepts the full chain because it preserves something extra; when those who omit it are many, he looks at the transmission above the point of disagreement. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 119}

## Al-Ihsan: Ibn Balban's rearrangement
Because the original was so hard to search, [[person/ibn-balban-ala-al-din-ali|al-Amir 'Ala' al-Din 'Ali ibn Balban al-Farisi]] (d. 739 AH) rearranged it by books and chapters and called it [[work/al-ihsan-fi-taqrib-sahih-ibn-hibban|*al-Ihsan fi Taqrib Sahih Ibn Hibban*]]. Shakir says that he did nothing but rearrange it, dropping not a word, and kept the preface and the closing words of each division in his introduction. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 10, 49-50} Its chapters open with beginning with God's praise and holding fast to the Sunna, followed by the books of the Night Journey, Revelation, Knowledge, Faith and Excellence (ihsan). {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 127}

## Manuscripts
Shakir explains that the original in Ibn Hibban's own arrangement has not survived complete, and that he found fragments of it: the first part, in the Egyptian National Library, containing the preface; volumes two and three in the hand of Ahmad ibn Yahya ibn 'Asakir dated 739 AH, photographed from Istanbul; and volume three of another copy written in 601 AH. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 7, 48} The Ibn 'Asakir copyist describes his copy as a division into four parts. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 32}

The manuscript of *al-Ihsan* in the Egyptian National Library fills nine volumes in eighth-century hands; Shakir thinks eight of them are the author's own copy made from his draft, because whole hadiths and chapters are struck through in place. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 41-42}
