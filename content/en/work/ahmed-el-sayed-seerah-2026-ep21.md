## Summary

The lecturer opens with a biographical portrait of Jafar ibn Abi Talib, including how he judges the reports of al-Waqidi, then turns to the legal rulings drawn from the migration to Abyssinia: when emigration from a land of unbelief is required, the three kinds of territory, and the case of the Negus. He closes with a survey of the themes of Surat al-Nahl against the objections of the Quraysh. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}

### Jafar ibn Abi Talib: reports of his generosity and his title

- He relates that the series proceeds by a biography of an early Muslim at the start of each session, and that for Abyssinia he chooses Jafar ibn Abi Talib, noting that books limited to Badr veterans omit some of the early emigrants. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He cites a saying of Abu Hurayra, graded by al-Tirmidhi as good, sound and singular, ranking Jafar highest among people after the Prophet in generosity, and he explains that it is a report stopping at Abu Hurayra and concerns giving and kindness, not an absolute ranking over the first caliph. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates from Sahih al-Bukhari that Abu Hurayra described Jafar as the kindest of people to the poor, feeding the hungry from whatever his house held. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that Abdullah ibn Umar greeted Abdullah ibn Jafar with the title of the one with two wings, and that Ibn Hajar links the title to reports, graded good, that Jafar flies among the angels, with wings given in place of the hands he lost at Mu'ta. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He holds that Jafar's presence in Abyssinia was like a reflection of the Prophet among the emigrants, since the Prophet said Jafar resembled him in build and character, and that Jafar was probably sent as leader of the Muslims there because his father Abu Talib kept him from being tortured. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}

### How to weigh the reports of al-Waqidi

- He relates that Ibn Sa'd transmits much of what is known about early Muslims from al-Waqidi, and he restates that al-Waqidi is weak in the view of most scholars, though about ten critics approved him against a heavier body who weakened him. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He holds that general statements of al-Waqidi on the campaigns, such as dating a raid, ordering raids, or naming the dead or the emigrants, are in principle acceptable and are weighed against other biographers. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He holds that a detailed chain-backed report from al-Waqidi is weak, more so when it rises to the Prophet and less so when it stops at a Companion or Successor, and that it is better still when Ibn Sa'd passes it on. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He holds that detailed accounts al-Waqidi gives without a chain, such as the stations on the road from Medina to Badr, are also usable for corroboration, and that most of his reports are singular, which calls for caution. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He says he will treat al-Waqidi at length in a later session, reading his entry in Siyar A'lam al-Nubala. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}

This is the speaker's view; the museum reports it as his and does not adopt it.

### Biography of Jafar: conversion, emigration, return and death at Mu'ta

- He relates from Ibn Sa'd, through al-Waqidi, that Jafar became Muslim before the Prophet entered the house of al-Arqam, which makes him one of the earliest Muslims. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that Jafar emigrated in the second migration to Abyssinia with his wife Asma bint Umays, that three sons were born to them there, that he was the emigrants' leader, and that he came to the Prophet at Khaybar in the seventh year, a point he says Ibn Ishaq also gives. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates from Ibn Sa'd that the Prophet embraced Jafar on his return and said he did not know whether Jafar's arrival or the conquest of Khaybar gave him more joy. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the Prophet appointed Jafar as one of the commanders at Mu'ta, that he dismounted from his horse, hamstrung it and fought until killed, and that Ibn Sa'd reports ninety wounds on his body from one source and seventy-two from another. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He mentions a book devoted to Jafar, Abaq al-Rayahin by Nayif Faris, for readers who want more. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}

### Two legal questions from the Abyssinian migration; al-Tarifi's book

- He relates that the series alternates between narrative, verses, legal rulings and method, and that this session draws rulings from the Abyssinian migration in two areas, the rules of emigration and the policy of the ruler, the latter tied to the Negus. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He recommends the book al-Tafsir wa al-Bayan li Ahkam al-Qur'an by Shaykh al-Tarifi as among the best on emigration and the law of territories, and says it is a set text in his programme. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He says he will read from the shaykh's discussion on Surat al-Nisa and the verse about those who wronged themselves by not emigrating, rather than summarise it. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}

### Those who stayed with the polytheists and swelled their numbers

- He relates, following al-Tarifi, that al-Bukhari reports from Ibn Abbas that some Muslims who stayed in Mecca were brought out with the polytheists at Badr and killed by arrows, and that a verse in Surat al-Nisa came down about them. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the author reasons that they were forced to fight, yet had caused the compulsion by not emigrating when they could, and that emigration from Mecca to Medina was binding on all who were able, by agreement of the Muslims. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates the author's inference that gathering around the Prophet and swelling his ranks was itself a great merit of the Companions. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the author says the Prophet treated hypocrites in Mecca by the rules of war and those in Medina by the rules of Islam, and that Muslims captured in the polytheist ranks at Badr, among them al-Abbas, were taken as prisoners and asked to pay ransom. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the author, citing al-Shafi'i in al-Umm, holds that merely staying in a land of war is not unbelief in itself, and that mixing with a non-warring polytheist for trade, farming or kinship does not make one like him, treating the reports that say otherwise as unsound. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}

### The vulnerable, who must emigrate, and the three kinds of territory

- He relates that the author separates those who claim weakness to excuse themselves from the truly vulnerable, whom God excuses, and that Ibn Abbas counted himself and his mother among the genuinely vulnerable. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the Prophet used to pray for named vulnerable believers still in Mecca, among them Ayyash ibn Abi Rabia, Salama ibn Hisham and al-Walid ibn al-Walid. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the author sets three conditions that make emigration binding: bodily ability, a refuge from the Quraysh, and knowledge of a safe road with provisions, and that lacking any of them makes a person vulnerable. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the author holds that whether a country is Muslim depends on its people and not on its ruler, giving examples of Muslim lands under Buwayhid and Fatimid rulers, and that the scholars of Qayrawan silenced Abu Jafar al-Dawudi when he urged emigration. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates the author's second case, a Muslim ruler over a mostly unbelieving population, as with the Negus in Abyssinia, which is counted a land of unbelief unless Muslims hold dominant power, as with Khaybar under the Prophet. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that some scholars add a third category beside the abode of Islam and the abode of war, and that Ibn Taymiyya's famous ruling on Mardin treated it as a mixed case. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He explains that in Mecca, Abyssinia and Medina the museum can see three kinds of land: a hostile land of unbelief, a peaceful and supportive one, and the abode of Islam. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}

### Why the Companions went to Abyssinia: protecting religious practice

- He relates from al-Tarifi that a Muslim may move from a hostile land of unbelief to a peaceful one when he cannot reach a Muslim land, that the Prophet called those who went to Abyssinia emigrants, and that Qatada held a verse in Surat al-Nahl was revealed about them. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the author separates two reasons for emigration, the practice of religion and the character of the land, and holds that the Abyssinian migrations were for the sake of practice, not because Abyssinia was a Muslim land, whereas the move to Medina served both. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates the author's view that preserving the ability to practise outweighs living in a virtuous place, since the merit of deeds exceeds the merit of lands, unless the matter is jihad. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}

### When emigration is obligatory, permitted or forbidden

- He relates from al-Tarifi that jurists differ on emigration from a land of unbelief where religion can be practised, yet agree on certain cases: emigration is required in time of war between Muslims and unbelievers even if practice is allowed, and also when the essential rites cannot be performed. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that staying is agreed to be permitted for calling people to God, following the prophets. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that moving from an Islamic land to a land where Islam is fought, merely to protect worldly interests, is forbidden by agreement, and that the lecturer adds that danger to one's children is also a reason against moving. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that a person oppressed by a Muslim tyrant may move to an unbelieving land if he intends to return, with al-Zuhri's plan to go to the Byzantine lands to escape al-Walid ibn Yazid as the example. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates the author's final view that the ruling depends on the case: Muslims in cities and regions with their own strength need not emigrate, while scattered individuals and families in a hostile land must, because small groups dissolve and their descendants are lost. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}

### Lessons from Abyssinia: jihad, protection and man-made laws

- He relates that the author draws from the emigrants in Abyssinia that they were not ordered to fight while Medina's Muslims were, because they were few and weak, and that the Prophet kept them in Abyssinia for years after the verses on fighting came down. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the author allows seeking the protection of a non-Muslim when attackers cannot be repelled otherwise. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the author treats lands whose laws depart from God's in two cases: where the departure is not widespread, emigration is not required if the faith can be practised, and where it is imposed on everyone, leaving is binding. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the author holds that the Prophet did not call his companions back from Abyssinia though penal verses were being revealed, and did not rebuke Jafar's party when they arrived after Khaybar. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the author holds that a ruler who makes lawful what God forbade is not a legitimate ruler and must be removed if people can, though they need not leave their land for his sake as long as they practise their religion. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}

### The first and second emigrations to Abyssinia and the Prophet's reasons

- He relates from al-Tarifi that emigration may be binding on a particular group singled out for harm, as when the Prophet ordered some of his followers to go to Abyssinia while he stayed under the protection of the Banu Hashim. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the first group left in the month of Rajab, five years after the call began, led by Uthman ibn Affan with his wife Ruqayya, daughter of the Prophet, about a dozen men and women, and that the Quraysh pursued them to the sea. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates the saying of Umm Salama that in Abyssinia they lived safely under the Negus and worshipped freely, and a saying of Ibn Mas'ud, from al-Tabarani and Ibn Sa'd, that the conversion of Umar brought relief to the Muslims at the Kaaba. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the first group came back to Mecca in Shawwal, that pressure grew until the Banu Hashim were blockaded in the ravine of Abu Talib, and that over eighty men and women then went again, with the Quraysh sending envoys to the Negus who refused to hand them over. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates the author's explanation that the Prophet stayed because God had promised him protection and the order of the faith in Mecca depended on him, and that the emigrants returned in the seventh year after the Muslims grew strong following Badr, Uhud, al-Hudaybiyya and Khaybar. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}

### The Negus: his faith, his limits and the funeral prayer

- He relates that the author, citing the Prophet's foretelling, notes that the Negus became Muslim and died before the conquest of Mecca, and counts him a supportive non-Muslim state at the time of weakness. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates from the Fatawa of Ibn Taymiyya that a person who believed but could not emigrate or learn all the rules is a believer who will enter Paradise, like the believer of Pharaoh's household, Pharaoh's wife and Yusuf among the Egyptians, and that the Negus belonged with them. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that Ibn Taymiyya reasons that the Negus's people did not follow him, so none was left to pray over him when he died, and that the Prophet prayed for him in absentia in Medina. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He holds that this prayer is the only one in the Prophet's practice, that scholars differ on funeral prayer in absence, and that the likelier view is that it applies to someone over whom no prayer has been said. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He holds that Ibn Taymiyya's claim that the Negus never prayed, fasted or performed the pilgrimage is open to question, since the emigrants had left before the Night Journey and many scholars say prayers were then two a day. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that Ibn Taymiyya says the Negus could not rule by the Qur'an because his people would not allow it, comparing officials among the Tatars who wished to do justice but were prevented, and Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz who was harmed for his justice. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that Ibn Taymiyya cites Ibn Abbas and others that a verse about People of the Book who believe was revealed about the Negus, and he says the Negus's inability is tied to the episode in which he refused the Quraysh envoys' view of Jesus after Jafar's reply and his officers snorted. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that Ibn Taymiyya concludes that an obligation falls only as far as one is able, and that one who did not know a duty need not make it up, a position he says Abu Hanifa and the Zahiris also hold. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}

### Surat al-Nahl and the objections of the Quraysh

- He says he would like to read Meccan chapters watching what the polytheists said against the Prophet and how God answered them, and chooses Surat al-Nahl because Qatada tied one of its verses to Abyssinia. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He holds that the Meccan verses repeatedly cite earlier peoples destroyed for treating their prophets as the Quraysh treated Muhammad, and that the repetition shows how hard the Prophet's situation was. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the chapter opens by answering the demand to hasten the punishment, then refutes the claim that God has daughters, and its main method is reminding people of God's gifts, which is why it is also called the chapter of blessings. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the speaker follows Ibn Ishaq in attributing the charge that the Qur'an is tales of the ancients to al-Nadr ibn al-Harith. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the chapter answers further sayings: that God would have prevented their worship if he wished, that there is no resurrection, and that a prophet should be an angel, the last answered by pointing to Moses and earlier messengers being men. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He relates that the chapter speaks of those who emigrated after being persecuted, of the compelled who spoke words of unbelief, and of Ibrahim as the model of thankfulness whom the Prophet is told to follow, and ends with a command to call with wisdom and argue kindly. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
- He says that the plan to continue with the names of the emigrants to Abyssinia, eighty-two men and a number of women, is deferred to the next session. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep21}
