## Summary
Al-Muwafaqat fi Usul al-Shari'a is a work on legal theory and the objectives of the law by Abu Ishaq [[person/abu-ishaq-al-shatibi|Ibrahim ibn Musa al-Lakhmi al-Gharnati al-Shatibi]], a Maliki (d. 790 AH). {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 1} {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 4} Al-Zirikli, as quoted by the publisher of the Dar al-Kutub al-Ilmiyya edition, counts it in four volumes. {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 4}

## Its five parts
In his preface al-Shatibi says he kept recording the rare and scattered points of this science, relying on "comprehensive inductions" rather than isolated particulars and supporting their transmitted bases with rational considerations, and then arranged them under headings that return them to their principles, so that the book fell into five parts: {cite:muwafaqat-mashhur-ibn-affan-v1 p. 9}
1. the preliminary premises needed to lay the groundwork; {cite:muwafaqat-mashhur-ibn-affan-v1 p. 10}
2. rulings, whether of declaration (wad') or of obligation (taklif); {cite:muwafaqat-mashhur-ibn-affan-v1 p. 10}
3. the objectives of the law and the rulings attached to them; {cite:muwafaqat-mashhur-ibn-affan-v1 p. 10}
4. the enumeration of the legal proofs, their sources and how they are applied to human acts; {cite:muwafaqat-mashhur-ibn-affan-v1 p. 10}
5. independent reasoning and imitation, conflict and preference, question and answer. {cite:muwafaqat-mashhur-ibn-affan-v1 p. 10}
In the Draz edition Part 1 begins on p. 18, the book of rulings on p. 63, the book of objectives on p. 219, the book of proofs on p. 469 and the book of ijtihad on p. 774. {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 18} {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 63} {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 219} {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 469} {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 774}

## Its title
Al-Shatibi says he first named the book 'Unwan al-Ta'rif bi-Asrar al-Taklif ("Introduction to the Secrets of Legal Obligation") for the secrets it contains, then gave up that name because one of his teachers told him he had seen him in a dream holding a book he had written called al-Muwafaqat ("The Reconciliations"), and on asking about the name was told that it reconciled the schools of [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-al-qasim|Ibn al-Qasim]] and [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]]; al-Shatibi says he had in fact begun writing the book. {cite:muwafaqat-mashhur-ibn-affan-v1 p. 10} {cite:muwafaqat-mashhur-ibn-affan-v1 p. 11} He advises the reader not to make the book his authority in every question, to rise from imitation to insight, and warns against partisanship. {cite:muwafaqat-mashhur-ibn-affan-v1 p. 11} {cite:muwafaqat-mashhur-ibn-affan-v1 p. 12}

## The book of objectives
Al-Shatibi divides objectives into two: those of the Lawgiver and those of the legally responsible person; the former are of four kinds - the Lawgiver's purpose in instituting the law, in making it understood, in imposing obligations by it, and in bringing the responsible person under its rule. {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 219} He prefaces this by stating that laws were instituted for people's welfare in this world and the next together, relying for proof on induction. {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 220} He then holds that the obligations of the law serve to preserve three kinds of purpose - necessities, needs and refinements - each with its complements. {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 221} {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 223} See: [[concept/maqasid-al-shariah|Objectives of the Shari'a]] and [[concept/al-daruriyyat-al-khams|The five necessities]].

## Its standing as Draz describes it
In his introduction Abd Allah Draz holds that deriving rulings rests on two pillars - knowledge of the Arabic language and knowledge of the secrets and objectives of the law - and that legal theorists attended to the first and neglected the second, apart from a hint in the chapter on analogy where the cause is divided into necessities, needs and refinements. {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 6} He says the discipline had stood still since the fifth century at what had formed in the first half, until al-Shatibi came in the eighth century, analysed the objectives into four kinds, added the objectives of the responsible person, and set this out in sixty-two questions and forty-nine sections. {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 7} He notes that the book opens with thirteen premises and five sections, that about a quarter of it is on rulings, dwelling on the permitted, cause, condition, strictness and dispensation. {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 7} From the part on proofs he summarises that the Meccan revelation contains the universals of the law and the Medinan details them, that abrogation did not touch the universals but only a few particulars, and that the sunna does not go beyond the Qur'an's universals in its rulings. {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 8} From the part on ijtihad: that the law returns, in every ruling, to a single view however many the disagreements. {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 8}

## Why it was little known
Draz explains the book's limited circulation by two things: its topics were new, arriving in the eighth century after scholars had come to regard the other half as the whole of legal theory; and al-Shatibi's style, where he argues from the sources, makes the reader move from word to word "as though walking on the teeth of a comb". {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 10} He says he had heard [[person/muhammad-abduh|Shaykh Muhammad Abduh]] urging students to study the book, and could find only a copy in Maghribi script borrowed from a student. {cite:muwafaqat-draz-ilmiyya p. 10}
