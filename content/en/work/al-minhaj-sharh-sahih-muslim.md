## Summary
*Al-Minhaj* is [[person/al-nawawi|Muhyi al-Din al-Nawawi's]] commentary on [[work/sahih-muslim|Sahih Muslim]], printed by al-Matba'a al-Misriyya bi-l-Azhar together with the text of the Sahih as *Sahih Muslim bi-Sharh al-Nawawi*, its first volume appearing in 1347 AH/1929 CE. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 1} Al-Nawawi opens with praise of the science of isnad, which he holds God granted to this community alone. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 2}

## Why and how he wrote it
Al-Nawawi argues that the study of hadith is among the noblest sciences, since most rulings of fiqh rest on the Sunna and scholars require a mujtahid judge or mufti to know the legal hadiths; he laments that sessions of hadith, once attended by thousands, have dwindled. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 4} He holds the two Sahihs to be the soundest books in hadith — indeed in learning at large — and says he had gathered much for a commentary on al-Bukhari and was working to finish it. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 4}

He meant the Muslim commentary to sit between abridgements and long works, saying that were it not for weak resolve and few readers he would have expanded it to more than a hundred volumes. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 4-5} He lists what it contains: rulings of principles and branches, ethics and points of renunciation, foundational legal maxims, the meanings of words, narrators' names and the fixing of difficult readings, the names of those known by kunya and those left unnamed, subtleties of hadith science, and the reconciliation of hadiths that only someone not versed in both hadith and fiqh would think contradictory. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 5} His method includes not attributing well-known points to their many authors while attributing the unusual ones, and explaining a matter fully at its first occurrence and cross-referring afterwards. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 5}

## His chain to Muslim
Al-Nawawi transmits the Sahih from his teacher Abu Ishaq Ibrahim b. 'Umar al-Wasiti in the great mosque of Damascus, from Mansur b. 'Abd al-Mun'im al-Furawi, from Muhammad b. al-Fadl al-Furawi, from 'Abd al-Ghafir al-Farisi, from Abu Ahmad al-Juludi, from Abu Ishaq Ibrahim b. Muhammad b. Sufyan, from Muslim — six links between him and Muslim. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 6} He says the book is mass-transmitted from Muslim as a whole, while the connected chain of transmission had narrowed in his day to Ibn Sufyan's route, with that of Abu Muhammad al-Qalanisi also current in the Maghrib; he quotes [[person/ibn-al-salah|Ibn al-Salah]] that al-Qalanisi's transmission reached the Maghrib through Ibn al-Hadhdha' of Cordoba and others who heard it in Egypt from Ibn Mahan. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 11-12}

## An introduction on the Sahih's standing and condition
- **The soundest books:** he reports the scholars' agreement that the soundest books after the Qur'an are the two Sahihs, and chooses — with the great majority — al-Bukhari's as the sounder, noting the view of Abu 'Ali al-Naysaburi and some Maghribi scholars that Muslim's comes first. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 14} He credits Muslim with a merit of his own: he gave each hadith a single place gathering its routes and wordings, making it easy for the student to compare them. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 14-15} See [[concept/shart-al-shaykhayn|the condition of the two Shaykhs]].
- **What Muslim said of his book:** he relays from Makki b. 'Abdan Muslim's statement that he showed his book to [[person/abu-zura-al-razi|Abu Zur'a al-Razi]] and left out everything Abu Zur'a flagged as defective, and from al-Khatib al-Baghdadi Muslim's statement that he compiled it from three hundred thousand hadiths he had heard. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 15}
- **Al-Daraqutni's "obligations":** he notes that [[person/al-daraqutni|al-Daraqutni]] and others [[work/al-ilzamat-ala-sahih-al-bukhari|"obliged"]] the two Shaykhs to include hadiths meeting their condition, and holds the obligation not binding, since they never undertook to include all sound hadith. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 24}
- **His use of weak narrators:** he answers those who faulted Muslim for narrating from weak men with the replies Ibn al-Salah gave: the narrator may be weak to others but reliable to Muslim; the report may be a corroboration rather than a main report; the weakness may have come later through mental decline after Muslim took from him; or the weak man's chain was shorter while Muslim had it from reliable men with a longer chain. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 24-25}
- **Books extracted on it:** he lists the [[concept/mustakhraj|mustakhrajat]] on Sahih Muslim. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 26-27}

He then devotes chapters to sound, good and weak hadith, the hadith scholars' terms, chains linked by "from" ('an), [[concept/tadlis|tadlis]], narrators who became confused, abrogation, and transmission by meaning. {cite:sharh-nawawi-misriyya-1347-v1 p. 27-36}
