## Overview
A book by [[person/malik-ibn-anas|Malik ibn Anas]], Imam of the City of the Migration (d. 179 AH), compiled in Medina. It is one of the earliest works of hadith and jurisprudence arranged by chapters. {cite:muwatta-maruf-1997}

The best-known of its transmissions is that of Yahya ibn Yahya al-Laythi. Its books range from prayer to the aqiqa. {cite:muwatta-maruf-1997}

## Purpose and character
The entry on Malik in the Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, describes the Muwatta as the earliest surviving Muslim law-book, setting aside the legal corpus of [[person/zayd-ibn-ali|Zayd ibn Ali]]. Its aim, the article says, was to survey law and justice, and the ritual and religious practice that rested on the consensus and the usual sunna of [[place/medina|Medina]]. It also sought to offer a theoretical standard for matters on which neither consensus nor sunna had settled anything. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284}

The article connects the book with its time. Under the early Abbasids, canon law was gaining recognition, and there was a practical wish for a smoothed path, which is roughly what the title means, through wide disagreement even on elementary questions. Malik, it says, wished to serve that wish on the basis of practice in the Hijaz and to codify and systematise the customary law of Medina. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284} The article also notes that Malik treats tradition as a means and not an end, so earlier jurists are hardly ever quoted except as authorities for Malik himself. Because he was concerned with documenting the sunna and not with criticising its form, the article judges him very careless about the order of his traditions, and it reads the book as a transition from the simple fiqh of the earliest period to the pure science of hadith of the later period. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284}

## A book among similar books
The article says Malik was not alone in composing such a work. [[person/al-majishun|Al-Majishun]], who died in 164 AH, 781 CE, is said to have dealt with the consensus of the scholars of Medina without quoting traditions, and several other Medinan scholars of the same time are recorded as writing in the style of the Muwatta. None of those works survives. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284} The article attributes the success of Malik's book to its always taking an average view on disputed points. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284}

## Recensions
The article reports that Malik did not fix one definitive text, whether oral or by handing over a copy, to be disseminated, so the different recensions differ in places very much. It offers two reasons: little stress was laid in that period on exact literal repetition, and transmitters took great liberty; and Malik probably did not always give his spoken Muwatta exactly the same form. It adds that the core which goes back to Malik himself is found in every recension, and that this is a guarantee that he wished to create a work in the later sense of the word. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284}

Fifteen recensions are known. Only two survived in full, about five were studied in Spain in the third and fourth Islamic centuries, and twelve were still available to al-Rudani, who died in 1094 AH, 1693 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284} The article singles out three.

- The vulgate, transmitted by [[person/yahya-ibn-yahya-al-laythi|Yahya ibn Yahya]] al-Masmudi, who died in 234 AH, 848 or 849 CE. It has been printed often, in places such as Delhi, Cairo with the commentary of al-Zurqani, Lahore and Tunis, and has produced many commentaries, editions and synopses. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284}
- The recension of [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-hasan-al-shaybani|Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Shaybani]], who died in 189 AH, 805 CE. The article calls it an edition and critical development of Malik's work, because at the end of most chapters al-Shaybani gives his own views and those of [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]] on the questions discussed, sometimes with full reasoning. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284}
- The recension of [[person/abdallah-ibn-wahb-al-misri|Abdallah ibn Wahb]], who died in 197 AH, 813 CE. Its quotations are preserved in fragments of [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]]'s book on the disagreements of the jurists, are fairly comprehensive, and follow Yahya's recension quite closely. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 285}

## Other works ascribed to Malik
The article doubts that Malik composed any other book. It says the statements in the Fihrist that speak of several works are vague and uncertain. The legal titles ascribed to him include a book of sunan, a book of pilgrimage rites, a book of sessions, a letter on judgements and a letter on fatwa, and it judges the authenticity of all of them uncertain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284} Titles such as a commentary on the Qur'an, a work on destiny and a book of the stars are, in its words, in the usual style of apocryphal literature. It also regards as suspect the letter of advice to the caliph [[person/harun-al-rashid|al-Rashid]], mentioned already in the Fihrist, which it compares to the Kitab al-Kharaj of [[person/abu-yusuf-al-qadi|Abu Yusuf]]. It notes that even al-Suyuti doubted it, though for reasons the article does not find convincing. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284}

## Content and sources of the doctrine
The article on the Malikiyya adds that the originality of Malik's teaching lies in the Muwatta's recognition of the amal, the effective and unanimous practice of Medina, which he established as an organised judicial system. It describes the book as a treatise of fiqh based on hadith, serving as legal argument, with two concerns: worship and general law, meaning transactions. It says most of the traditions go back to Abdallah ibn Umar, and that all the individuals named are Medinans or scholars who frequented Mecca or Medina. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 298} The numbers of hadiths, mursal reports and opinions given for the final edition are unreadable in the available scan, so they are not repeated here. See also [[concept/amal-ahl-madina|the practice of the people of Medina]].

The second main source for Malik's teaching, the article on Malik says, is the [[work/al-mudawwana-al-kubra|Mudawwana]] of [[person/sahnun-ibn-said|Sahnun]], who died in 240 AH, 854 CE. It holds replies by [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-al-qasim|Ibn al-Qasim]], according to the school of Malik or to his own opinion, together with traditions and opinions of Ibn Wahb. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 285} The Malikiyya article adds that it illustrates the links between religion and trade and describes merchants' practices. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 298} Al-Tabari also quotes Malik's traditions and opinions often in his commentary on the legal verses. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 285}

## Place in the history of fiqh
In the article's reading, Malik stands at a stage when legal reasoning was still occasional and not systematic, in the town where the foundations of Muslim law were laid. A main feature of the juristic thought in the Muwatta, it says, is the permeation of all legal life by religious and moral ideas, working on the customary law of Medina, which was adapted to a highly developed trading community. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 285}

The article then offers an interpretation that it presents as its own judgement. Since the Islamisation of law was largely complete before Malik, and later generations did the work of systematisation, his own achievement can only have lain in developing a system, and how great his share was cannot be settled for lack of comparison material. The success of the Muwatta among similar works would be explained, in this view, by its recording the usual consensus of Medina with little work of the author's own, so that it is better evidence of the stage reached by the law of his time than of his individual activity. The article adds that this average character may be just what Malik aimed at. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 285} It further holds that the high regard for Malik in older sources rests on his strict criticism of hadiths, and that even this only means he stayed within the later consensus. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 285}

On method, the article says hadith is not the highest or only court of appeal for Malik. Where traditions differ he preferred the actual undoubted practice of Medina, and where neither Medinan tradition nor Medinan consensus existed he laid down the law independently, using his own reasoning to the point that he was occasionally reproached with agreeing with the Iraqis. It mentions a later legend, directed against reasoning, that he repented of this on his deathbed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 285}

## Later reception and legend
The article reports that in later times many regarded the Muwatta as canonical, and that numerous legends deal with its origin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284} One, which it calls quite fictitious, says that a caliph wanted to make the Muwatta canonical and gave up the intention at Malik's urging. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 283} The article on the Malikiyya sets a related account in the second Islamic century. It says the Abbasid caliph [[person/al-mansur|al-Mansur]] approached the Medinan jurist with a plan for a judicial system to unify the methods in force in different Islamic lands. It gives as reasons for choosing Malik that he represented Medina and that his teaching had already been spread by his pupils in the Maghrib and Spain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 298} This page reports both articles without choosing between them.

## For Sprouts
The Muwatta is a book collected by the scholar Malik ibn Anas in Medina more than twelve hundred years ago, containing the Prophet's hadiths and the sayings and rulings of the people of Medina. Muslims count it among the first great books of the religion, and it is still read and taught.

## For Explorers
The Muwatta is Imam Malik ibn Anas's work of hadith and fiqh, composed in Medina in the second Islamic century and refined over years. In it he gathered the Prophet's hadiths, reports of the Companions, rulings of the Successors and his own opinions, arranged on the doors of fiqh from purity to the 'compendium', and it is famous for relying on the practice of the people of Medina as evidence. It circulated through his students in many recensions; the best known is Yahya ibn Yahya al-Laythi's. Scholars describe it as the first comprehensive composed book in Islam, and al-Shafi'i said there is no book after God's book sounder than Malik's book.

## For Scholars
The Muwatta is the work of Malik ibn Anas (d. 179 AH) in hadith and Medinan fiqh, the oldest surviving comprehensive book in the Islamic tradition. Malik composed it in Medina on the doors of fiqh from purity to the compendium, refining its contents over some forty years until it held about two thousand hadiths and reports. It contains mursal reports of the Prophet, sayings of the Companions, rulings of the Successors and Malik's own fatwas, and is famous for relying on the practice of Medina's people as a juridical proof built on continuity of dealing since the Prophet's era. Its recensions through his students — Yahya ibn Yahya al-Laythi, Ibn al-Qasim, Asbagh and others — are many, with Yahya's the most widespread, reaching about nine hundred sections. Al-Shafi'i called it the soundest book after God's book; al-Suyuti, al-Zurqani and Ibn Abd al-Barr commented on it, and modern studies debate its genesis and sources.
