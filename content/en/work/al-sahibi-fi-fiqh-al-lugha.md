## Summary
al-Sahibi is [[person/ibn-faris|Ahmad ibn Faris]]'s book on the jurisprudence of the Arabic language, its questions and the customs of the Arabs in their speech. This page sets out what was read of its editor's introduction and of the chapters on the origin of language, the merit and range of Arabic, the language of the Qur'an and how much of Arab speech has reached us. Each saying is ascribed to its holder, and what the reading did not reach is stated at the end.

## The author and the book
The editor's introduction says Ibn Faris was Abu al-Husayn Ahmad ibn Faris ibn Zakariyya al-Razi, born in Qazvin and raised in Hamadhan, called to Rayy by the Buyid Fakhr al-Dawla to tutor his son; a Shafi'i jurist who upheld the school of Malik, following the Kufan method in grammar, and among his pupils were al-Sahib ibn Abbad and Badi' al-Zaman al-Hamadhani {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.5}. He is said to have died at Rayy in 395 AH, other years also being reported {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.6}. All this is the editor's account, not part of the book's text. On the title, the editor says it was called al-Sahibi after [[person/sahib-ibn-abbad|al-Sahib ibn Abbad]], to whom Ibn Faris presented it and who put it in his library, and that its content turns on Arabic, its priority and origin, then the Arabs' ways of address, literal and figurative speech {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.8}. The editor's introduction is dated 21 Sha'ban 1417 AH {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.9}.

## Language is tawqif
Ibn Faris opens the book by saying that the language of the Arabs is tawqif, taught. He argues from the verse [[passage/quran-2-31|al-Baqara 31]] and from what is reported from Ibn Abbas, that God taught Adam all the names, such as people know them: beast, earth, plain, mountain, donkey. He mentions Mujahid's saying, notes that others said they were the names of the angels or of the offspring, and chooses Ibn Abbas's view. He says words like sayf, husam and adb are likewise tawqif, and his proof is the scholars' consensus on citing the speech of the people and their poetry as evidence: if language were agreement, they would be no better to cite than we would be had we agreed on today's language {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.13}. al-Suyuti copied these chapters in al-Muzhir and set Ibn Jinni's view after them, as on the page [[work/al-khasais-ibn-jinni|al-Khasa'is]] {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.12-14}.

## The merit and range of Arabic
In the chapter that Arabic is the best and widest of languages, Ibn Faris cites the verse [[passage/quran-26-195|al-Shu'ara 195]] and says God described the Qur'an by clear expression (bayan); when He singled out the Arabic tongue for it, it is known that other languages fall short of it. To those who say clear expression can occur in other languages he answers that this is the lowest rank of expression, and that if we had to speak of the sword and its attributes in Persian we could do it only with one name, while in Arabic the sword has many attributes {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.19}. As examples of what cannot be carried into another language he gives the verses [[passage/quran-8-58|al-Anfal 58]] and [[passage/quran-18-11|al-Kahf 11]], holding that the first can be moved only by spreading out its words; he then says God's speech is too high to be set against any speech, and that poets sometimes hint, as Imru' al-Qays does in a line that takes long to put into other words {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.20}. He then lists as features of Arabic the transposition of letters, assimilation, avoiding two consecutive silent letters and the suppression of verbs {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.21}. This is Ibn Faris's statement; those who speak of other languages say otherwise, and the museum does not judge between them.

## The language of the Qur'an
In the chapter on the language in which the Qur'an came down, Ibn Faris reports from Ibn Abbas that it was sent down in seven ahruf or seven dialects, five of them the speech of the 'ajz of Hawazin, and gives Abu Ubayd's saying that the most eloquent of them were Banu Sa'd ibn Bakr {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.32}. He then sets out a disagreement between the jurists, who said the Qur'an holds some words of non-Arabic origin such as araik and hayta, and the people of Arabic, who said it is wholly in an Arabic tongue and cited the verse [[passage/quran-43-3|al-Zukhruf 3]]. He prefers Abu Ubayd's reconciliation: these words were of foreign origin, fell to the Arabs, who Arabised them with their tongues, so they became Arabic. Ibn Faris explains his preference: if the Qur'an held anything of another language, someone might suppose the Arabs failed to match it because it came with languages they did not know, and then there would be no ground for allowing the Qur'an to be recited in prayer in Persian, since the Persian is a translation that is not inimitable {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.33}. The matter touches the inimitability of the Qur'an; see [[concept/nazariyyat-al-ijaz-wal-nazm|the theory of inimitability and nazm]] and [[concept/al-arabiyya-lughat-al-quran|Arabic, the language of the Qur'an]].

## Has anyone encompassed Arab speech
He reports from some jurists that Arab speech is encompassed only by a prophet, and thinks that likely to be true, and finds it unlikely that al-Khalil claimed to have kept all the language {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.24}. In a later chapter he says what has reached us of the speech of the Arabs is the lesser part, and that if all of it had come we would have had much poetry and much speech {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.36}.

## What this page did not reach
The last chapters of the book, on particles, the meanings of speech, literal and figurative language and the customs of the Arabs, which are most of the text, were not read, nor was the letter of Ibn Faris appended at its end. Page numbers are those the Shamela copy shows. The introduction of this copy is signed by Ahmad Hasan Basaj {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.9}, while the museum's source record names the publisher Muhammad Ali Baydun; this was not checked against a paper copy.
