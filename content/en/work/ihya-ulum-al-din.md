## Summary
Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din ("The Revival of the Religious Sciences") is the work of [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|Abu Hamid al-Ghazali]], who died at Tus in 505 AH, in forty books arranged in four quarters: acts of worship, customs of daily life, things that destroy, and things that save {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 221-223} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 9}. In his preface al-Ghazali says he wrote it "as a revival of the sciences of religion", having seen that knowledge of the path to the hereafter had become forgotten, and that he confined the book to the "knowledge of conduct" (mu'amala), leaving aside the "knowledge of unveiling" (mukashafa) {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 9} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 16}. He put the Book of Knowledge first, then gave each quarter ten books {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 10} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 11} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 12}. In the quarter of destructive things he gives each blameworthy trait its definition, cause, harms, signs and treatment {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 13} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 14}. He says he cast the book in the mould of jurisprudence, itself in four quarters, "as a gentle way of drawing hearts in" {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 17} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 18}.

Scholars differed over it: [[person/katip-celebi|Hajji Khalifa]] reports from [[person/al-nawawi|al-Nawawi]] that if the books of Islam were lost and the Ihya' remained it would suffice for what was lost, that [[person/ibn-al-jawzi|Ibn al-Jawzi]] collected its errors in a book, and that the hadith master [[person/abd-al-rahim-al-iraqi|al-Iraqi]] traced its hadith {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 221-223}. [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] holds that it has much benefit, that it contains blameworthy matter from the philosophers and weak and fabricated hadith, and that the shaykhs' words in it that agree with the Qur'an and Sunna outweigh what is rejected, "and for this reason people's judgement on it differed and they disputed over it" {cite:majmu-fatawa-qasim-v10 p. 551} {cite:majmu-fatawa-qasim-v10 p. 552}.

## Why he wrote it: the author's preface
Al-Ghazali opens the book by addressing a critic "excessive in reproach" who objected to his change of course, and says it was this reproach that loosened the knot of his silence. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 3} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 7} He describes himself as having turned a little from "keeping to convention" towards acting on what knowledge requires, seeking the purification of the soul and the reform of the heart, and making up for wasted years. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 7}

He holds that the guides of the path are the scholars, heirs of the prophets, and that his age had emptied of them except for "those who keep the outward forms". People, he says, had come to think that knowledge was only three things: legal opinions that help judges settle disputes, debate pursued for victory and silencing an opponent, and rhymed preaching that draws in the common people. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 8} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 9} The knowledge of the path to the hereafter, which the Qur'an calls understanding, wisdom, light and guidance, had in his words become "folded away" among people, so he judged writing this book important in order to revive it. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 9}

## Structure: four quarters, forty books
Al-Ghazali put the Book of Knowledge at the head of the whole, because he wanted first to set out the knowledge that God commands every individual to seek, and to separate useful knowledge from harmful knowledge. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 10} Each quarter then has ten books: {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 10} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 11} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 12}

- **Quarter of acts of worship:** knowledge; the foundations of the creed; the inner meanings of purification, prayer, zakat, fasting and pilgrimage; the etiquette of reciting the Qur'an; remembrance and supplication; the ordering of devotions through the day. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 10}
- **Quarter of customs:** the etiquette of eating; marriage; earning a living; the lawful and the unlawful; companionship and social life; seclusion; travel; listening to music and ecstasy; enjoining good and forbidding wrong; the conduct of daily life and the character of prophethood. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 11} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 12}
- **Quarter of things that destroy:** the marvels of the heart; disciplining the soul; the dangers of the two appetites; the dangers of the tongue; anger, rancour and envy; the censure of the world; of wealth and avarice; of status and showing off; of pride and self-admiration; of self-delusion. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 12}
- **Quarter of things that save:** repentance; patience and gratitude; fear and hope; poverty and renunciation; divine unity and trust; love, longing, intimacy and contentment; intention, sincerity and truthfulness; vigilance and self-examination; contemplation; the remembrance of death. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 12} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 13}

In the Shamela file of the Dar al-Ma'rifa edition, part 3 opens with the Book of the Marvels of the Heart and part 4 with the Book of Repentance. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 4131} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 6250} Openings of individual books in that file include the foundations of the creed, purification, eating, the lawful and unlawful, seclusion, travel, breaking the two appetites, the tongue, the world, status and showing off, pride, self-delusion, poverty and renunciation, love, intention and sincerity, vigilance, contemplation and the remembrance of death. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 467} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 660} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 1993} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 2478} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 3208} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 3332} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 4527} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 4666} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 5151} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 5526} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 5843} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 6065} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 7181} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 7703} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 8043} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 8204} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 8352} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 8478}

## Method within each quarter
Al-Ghazali says that in the quarter of worship he sets out the hidden etiquette, fine points and inner meanings of the acts, most of which had been neglected "in the craft of jurisprudence", and in the quarter of customs the secrets of dealings between people and the hidden points of scrupulousness in them. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 13} In the quarter of destructive things he gives for each blameworthy trait its definition and reality, then the cause from which it grows, the harms that follow from it, its signs, and the ways of treating it, joined with evidence from verses, hadith and reports. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 13} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 14} In the quarter of saving things he gives for each praiseworthy trait its definition, reality, cause, fruit, sign and merit. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 14}

He lists five ways in which he considers his book distinct from earlier writing on these themes: untying what others knotted and opening what they summarised; ordering what they scattered; shortening what they drew out; removing what they repeated; and establishing obscure matters never treated in books at all. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 14} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 15}

## The two motives for the fourfold division
The first motive, for al-Ghazali, is that knowledge directed towards the hereafter is of two kinds, [[concept/ilm-al-muamala-wal-mukashafa|the knowledge of conduct and the knowledge of unveiling]], and the book is confined to the first, because there is "no licence to set down" the knowledge of unveiling in books. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 15} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 16} The knowledge of conduct is in turn outward, concerning the limbs, and then either custom or worship; or inward, concerning the states of the heart, and then either blameworthy or praiseworthy. This yields four parts. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 16} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 17}

The second motive is that he saw students' eagerness was real for jurisprudence, which is arranged in four quarters, so he shaped his book in its form "as a gentle way of drawing hearts in". {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 17} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 18} He cites the author who laid out a book of medicine like an astronomical almanac, in tables, and called it "The Almanac of Health" so that rulers would feel at home with it, and holds that the medicine of hearts deserves such gentleness more than the medicine of bodies. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 18}

## The Book of Knowledge as an example
Al-Ghazali divided the Book of Knowledge into seven chapters: the merit of knowledge, teaching and learning; individual and collective obligations, with the definition of jurisprudence and theology; what common people count as religious knowledge when it is not; the dangers of disputation; the etiquette of teacher and student; the dangers of knowledge and the signs that separate scholars of this world from scholars of the hereafter; and the intellect, its merit and its kinds. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 18} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 19}

## Notable themes in the book
The book treats subjects that now have their own entries in the museum, among them [[concept/ikhlas|sincerity]] and the degrees of admixture that cloud it, {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 8131} [[concept/ghurur|self-delusion]] and the classes of the deluded among scholars, worshippers, Sufis and the wealthy, {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 6068} [[concept/hubb-al-jah|the love of status]] as "possession of hearts", {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 5548} [[concept/faqr|poverty]] and the five states of the poor man, {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 7186} and [[concept/rida-bil-qada|contentment with God's decree]] and why supplication does not contradict it. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 7991} It also contains books on [[concept/tawba|repentance]], [[concept/zuhd|renunciation]], [[concept/hubb-allah|love of God]], [[concept/riya|showing off]], [[concept/kibr|pride]], [[concept/uzla|seclusion]] and [[concept/sama|listening (sama')]]. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 12} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 13}

## The tracing of its hadith: al-Iraqi's al-Mughni
The Dar al-Ma'rifa edition carries in its footnotes [[work/al-mughni-an-haml-al-asfar|al-Mughni 'an Haml al-Asfar fi al-Asfar]] by the hadith master [[person/abd-al-rahim-al-iraqi|Zayn al-Din al-Iraqi]]. {cite:ihya-dar-al-marifa-1982-v3 p. 5} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 6} Al-Iraqi says in his introduction that he completed his discussion of the Ihya's hadith in the year fifty-one, put off the fair copy until sixty, and then abridged it "to the utmost", giving only the opening of the hadith, its Companion, its source and its grade. {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 4} {cite:ihya-shamela-dar-al-marifa p. 5} The closing note in the Kitab al-Sha'b edition says he finished the fair copy of this abridgement on Monday 12 Rabi' al-Awwal 790. {cite:ihya-kitab-al-shab-pts13-16 p. 712}

## Praise and criticism
[[person/katip-celebi|Hajji Khalifa]] reports from [[person/al-nawawi|al-Nawawi]] that if the books of Islam were lost and the Ihya' remained, it would suffice. He reports that some Maghribis rejected it when it first reached them, and that [[person/ibn-al-jawzi|Ibn al-Jawzi]] found fault with it and collected its errors in [[work/ilam-al-ahya-bi-aghlat-al-ihya|I'lam al-Ahya' bi-Aghlat al-Ihya']]. He reports a saying of Ibn al-Jawzi's grandson that al-Ghazali built it on the doctrines of the Sufis and set aside the rule of jurisprudence, so that the hadith in it that are not sound were objected to; and that the hadith master al-Iraqi traced its hadith in 751 AH. {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 221-223}

[[person/abd-al-qadir-ibn-shaykh-aydarus|Abd al-Qadir ibn Shaykh al-Aydarus]] gathered the words of its admirers in his treatise [[work/tarif-al-ahya-bi-fadail-al-ihya|Ta'rif al-Ahya' bi-Fada'il al-Ihya']]. He quotes [[person/abd-al-ghafir-al-farisi|Abd al-Ghafir al-Farisi]] that it is one of al-Ghazali's works without precedent; al-Nawawi: "the Ihya' was almost a Qur'an"; and Abu Muhammad al-Kazaruni that if all the sciences were erased they could be recovered from the Ihya'. {cite:ihya-kitab-al-shab-pts13-16 p. 782} He relates from [[person/abdullah-al-yafii|al-Yafi'i]] the story of the Maghribi jurist [[person/ali-ibn-hirzihim|Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Hirzihim]], who intended to burn copies of the Ihya' and then gave up his objection after a dream. {cite:ihya-kitab-al-shab-pts13-16 p. 779} {cite:ihya-kitab-al-shab-pts13-16 p. 780} And he reports that the sultan [[person/ali-ibn-yusuf-al-murabiti|Ali ibn Yusuf]] ordered al-Ghazali's books burned when they reached the Maghrib, an account with its own entry, [[event/burning-of-ghazali-books-maghrib|the burning of al-Ghazali's books in the Maghrib]]. {cite:ihya-kitab-al-shab-pts13-16 p. 788}

[[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]], in an answer in volume 10 of [[work/majmu-al-fatawa|Majmu' al-Fatawa]], assesses the Ihya' as following [[work/qut-al-qulub|Qut al-Qulub]] in what it says on the works of the heart – patience, gratitude, love, reliance – and holds that Abu Talib al-Makki knew hadith and reports better and that his words are sounder and further from innovation; he adds that most of what the Ihya' says on "the destroyers", such as pride, vanity, ostentation and envy, is taken from al-Harith al-Muhasibi's *al-Ri'aya*. {cite:majmu-fatawa-qasim-v10 p. 551} He finds in the Ihya' much benefit, but also blameworthy material from the philosophers on tawhid, prophecy and the return, weak and even fabricated hadiths, and some Sufi errors, and he quotes imams who objected that "the *Shifa'* made him ill", meaning Ibn Sina's *Shifa'*; then he says that what it contains of the upright shaykhs' words on the works of the heart in agreement with the Qur'an and Sunna outweighs what is to be rejected, "and for this reason people's judgement on it differed and they disputed over it". {cite:majmu-fatawa-qasim-v10 p. 551} {cite:majmu-fatawa-qasim-v10 p. 552}

## Composition and setting, according to the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The article on al-Ghazali in the second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam, by W. Montgomery Watt, dates the writing of the Ihya' to the years after al-Ghazali gave up teaching. A nervous illness had made lecturing physically impossible for him; in Dhu al-Qa'da 488/November 1095 he left [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]], where he had held the chair at the [[building/al-madrasa-al-nizamiyya-baghdad|Nizamiyya madrasa]], on the pretext of making the pilgrimage, and in reality abandoned his professorship and his career as a jurist and theologian. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088} He spent time in [[place/damascus|Damascus]], travelled by [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]] and Hebron to [[place/medina|Medina]] and [[place/mecca|Mecca]] for the pilgrimage of 489/1096, and then returned to his home at [[place/tus|Tus]]. The article insists that he was only about two years in Syria, correcting the older statement that he spent ten years there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088}

In this retirement at Damascus and Tus, the article says, he lived as a poor Sufi, often alone, given to meditation and spiritual exercises, and it was then that he composed his greatest work, the Ihya', which he may also have taught to select audiences. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088} By the end of the period he had advanced far along the mystic path and was convinced that it was the highest way of life. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088} In 499/1106, pressed by Fakhr al-Mulk, son of [[person/nizam-al-mulk|Nizam al-Mulk]], he began to lecture at the Nizamiyya of [[place/nishapur|Nishapur]], and he retired again to Tus in 505/1111, where he had a khanqah, a hermitage in which he trained young disciples in the theory and practice of Sufism. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088}

The article reviews the reasons given for his renunciation. Al-Ghazali himself says he feared going to Hell, and he criticises the corruption of the scholars of his time, for instance in the first volume of the Ihya'; the author judges that he may have felt the organised legal profession so corrupt that an upright life required leaving it. A suggestion by F. Jabre that he feared the Ismailis is described as emphasising what can at most have been one factor, and a suggestion by D. B. Macdonald points to the political events of the time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088}

## The structure as the article describes it
The article calls the Ihya' al-Ghazali's greatest work in size and in the importance of its contents, in four volumes. It is divided into four quarters: acts of worship, social customs, vices or faults of character leading to perdition, and virtues or qualities leading to salvation, each quarter having ten books. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089} In the author's words it is a complete guide for the devout Muslim to every aspect of religious life: worship and devotional practice, conduct in daily life, purification of the heart and advance along the mystic way. The first two books, he adds, deal with the necessary minimum of intellectual knowledge. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089}

The author explains the undertaking as arising from al-Ghazali's feeling that in the hands of the scholars of his day religious knowledge had become a means of worldly advancement, whereas he was convinced that it was essentially for attaining salvation in the world to come. He therefore describes the prescriptions of the Shari'a in some detail and tries to show how they contribute to a person's final salvation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1090}

## Its place among al-Ghazali's writings
The article treats the Ihya' as the chief work of al-Ghazali's Sufi practice. It notes that he finished the [[work/tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahafut al-Falasifa]] at the beginning of 488/1095, just before the crisis that made him leave Baghdad, and that the Tahafut sets out inconsistencies without arguing positive views of its own; the accusation that he remained something of a sceptic is answered, in the author's judgement, by the fact that when he wrote the Ihya' he was in no doubt about what he believed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089}

On theology, the article holds that al-Ghazali never ceased to be an [[concept/ashariyya|Ash'ari]] in dogmatics. It cites his [[work/al-iqtisad-fil-itiqad|al-Iqtisad]] in support: in the Kitab al-Arba'in, written after the Ihya', he says the Iqtisad is more likely than the usual works of dogmatics to prepare for the gnosis of the Sufi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089} The article also states that [[work/iljam-al-awwam|Iljam al-'Awamm]], apparently his last work, takes a position like that of the Ihya' and was finished a few days before his death; this, it says, rules out the supposition that he adopted extreme philosophical forms of Sufism in his last years. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088} It records Ibn Tufayl's suggestion that al-Ghazali wrote differently for ordinary people and for the elite, and adds that there is no reason to think the higher levels ever directly contradicted the lower. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088}

## Companion and derived works
The article lists several works connected to the Ihya'. [[work/bidayat-al-hidaya|Bidayat al-Hidaya]] is a brief rule of daily life for the devout Muslim with advice on avoiding sins. The [[work/al-arbain-fi-usul-al-din-ghazali|Kitab al-Arba'in]] is a short summary of the Ihya', though its forty sections do not wholly correspond to the forty books. Al-Maqsad al-Asna discusses in what sense people may imitate the names or attributes of God. [[work/kimiya-i-saadat|Kimiya-yi Sa'adat]] is mainly a Persian abridgement of the Ihya', translated in whole or part into Urdu, Arabic and other languages, with some differences that have not been fully investigated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1090} A short work, [[work/al-imla-ala-mushkil-al-ihya|al-Imla']], answers criticisms made of the Ihya'. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089} The article adds that al-Ghazali's brother [[person/ahmad-al-ghazali|Ahmad al-Ghazali]] wrote an abridgement of the book which, it says, has not survived. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1090}

## Influence: the article's provisional assessment
The article cautions that a balanced account of al-Ghazali's influence is not yet possible, because the religious movements of the following centuries need much more study, and it presents its assessments as provisional. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1090} It judges that he undoubtedly performed a great service for devout Muslims of every level of education by presenting obedience to the Shari'a as a meaningful way of life. It compares his khanqah at Tus, where he and his disciples lived together, to a Christian monastery, and says he may have given an impetus to the movement out of which the dervish orders came, but adds that this requires further investigation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1090} It also suggests that his example may have encouraged forms of [[concept/tasawwuf|Sufism]] close to Sunnism or entirely Sunni, while noting that before him there had been much more Sufism among Sunni scholars than is commonly realised, and that his influence on the Sufi movement as a whole needs careful study. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1090}

## For young visitors
Al-Ghazali wrote the Ihya' to teach people how worship and everyday habits can be done with a present heart. He divided it into four parts: acts of worship; daily customs such as eating, travel and friendship; the traits that ruin a person, such as pride and self-delusion; and the traits that save, such as repentance, patience and sincerity.

## Notes for researchers
- Page numbers of the Shamela file are pages of the file (8,992 pages), not of the printed book, even though the file says its numbering follows the print.
- The verses in the Shamela file have scrambled letters, so no Qur'anic text is taken from it.
- Badawi Tabana's introduction was not read, because part 1 of his edition is not in this harvest.
