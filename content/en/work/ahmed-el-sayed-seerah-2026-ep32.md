## Summary

He reads from Ibn Hisham the account of the five Quraysh men who tore up the boycott document and Abu Talib's poem about them, then draws lessons on accepting help from non-Muslims in times of weakness. He treats the Taif journey as the hardest experience of the Prophet and then turns to the story of al-Tufayl ibn Amr al-Dawsi, weighing its chain against the sound reports. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}

### The tenth year and the three years in the glen

- He says the three years in the glen were not empty of other events, and that some biographers miss this because they list only the headline events. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He holds that Ibn Ishaq filled this period with exchanges and debates with the polytheists rather than fixing events to a particular year, and he counts this as a sign of Ibn Ishaq's insight. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that al-Shafi'i is reported to have said that those who want the biography depend on Ibn Ishaq. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He estimates that there were roughly 150 to 200 Muslims by the tenth year, many of them in Abyssinia, where about seventy-two men and a dozen or so women had gone in the second migration. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He says Ibn Sa'd's Tabaqat is the main reference for the lives of those emigrants, and that Ibn Ishaq places their return to Mecca close to the tenth year, though this is disputed. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He describes the tenth year as full of events and as the point where the Prophet is close to leaving Mecca and seeking supporters among the tribes. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}

### The five men who worked to cancel the boycott document

- He relates, following Ibn Hisham, that five men worked to cancel the document: Hisham ibn Amr, Zuhayr ibn Abi Umayya, al-Mut'im ibn Adi, Abu al-Bakhtari ibn Hisham and Zam'a ibn al-Aswad. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that Hisham ibn Amr, linked to Banu Hashim through his mother's side, used to bring camels loaded with food into the glen at night and send them in. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that Hisham approached Zuhayr, whose mother was Atika bint Abd al-Muttalib, and appealed to kinship; Zuhayr is also named as the father of Umm Salama. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that Hisham asked for a second man, then recruited al-Mut'im, who belonged to Banu Nawfal, a branch of the sons of Abd Manaf equal in rank to Banu al-Muttalib. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He says Banu Makhzum were the clan that rivalled the sons of Abd Manaf, and connects this to a remark attributed to Abu Jahl about the two sides being like two racehorses. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that the group recruited Abu al-Bakhtari and then Zam'a, and that they agreed to meet at night at the upper part of Mecca near al-Hajun and swore to act until the document was torn. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He holds that the episode shows the value of keeping family ties even with non-Muslims where they serve the cause without compromise, noting that some non-Muslims of Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib had entered the glen out of loyalty. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}

### The scene at the Kaaba and the tearing of the document

- He relates that Zuhayr circled the Kaaba and publicly demanded that the document be torn, and that Abu Jahl contradicted him from a corner of the mosque. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that Zam'a, Abu al-Bakhtari, al-Mut'im and Hisham each sided with Zuhayr, and that Abu Jahl said the matter had been decided by night elsewhere. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He says Abu Jahl plays the role of one who blocks the way to the call, and even opposes fellow wrongdoers who lean toward the truth. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He says the siege was not a prison or a physical blockade but economic and social sanctions with a security element, and that entry into the glen was in some reports voluntary; he adds that Abu Talib could therefore sit in the mosque. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that al-Mut'im went to tear the document and found the earth had eaten it except for the name of God, and that its scribe was said to have had a withered hand. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates Ibn Hisham's further report that the Prophet told Abu Talib of this and that Abu Talib put it to Quraysh as a test, and he judges this report to be a loosely attached one without a strong chain. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}

### Abu Talib's poem and Hassan's lament for al-Mut'im

- He says Abu Talib composed a poem praising those who tore the document, and that his poems work like media statements with political content and a dialogue with the polytheists. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He says he thinks this is the hardest of Abu Talib's poems met so far in the series. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He explains that the Abyssinia reference in its opening addresses those who had emigrated there, and that the poem's mention of a group at al-Hajun means the men who tore up the document. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that Hassan ibn Thabit later mourned al-Mut'im ibn Adi in verse, and links this to the Prophet's remark after Badr about what he would have done had al-Mut'im been alive and spoken for the captives. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates Ibn Hisham's note on the Prophet's return from Taif: he went to Hira, asked al-Akhnas ibn Shariq and then Suhayl ibn Amr for protection, both declined, and al-Mut'im agreed, armed himself and his household, and brought the Prophet into the mosque to circle the house and pray. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}

### Relief through non-Muslims and the ethics of weakness

- He holds that relief for the Prophet came through polytheists who worked to cancel the document, and that this shows relief need not come from believers. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He compares this to the relief that came to Yusuf through a dream of a non-believing king, and says Yusuf's refusal to leave prison was about clearing his name, not about who brought the relief. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He holds that in times of weakness a movement must accept some compromise and distinguish a hostile fighter from a peaceful polytheist, whereas once strong it may raise its conditions. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He cites the Prophet's later refusal, after strength was established, to accept help from a polytheist who joined a campaign, and notes that scholars differ on whether this forbids such help. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He lists as precedents the document's cancellation by polytheists, the migration to Abyssinia under a ruler who was then not Muslim but just, al-Mut'im's protection, Abu Talib's long protection and a non-Muslim guide on the hijra. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He holds that the Companions were trained both in clarity of principle and in realism, giving the example of the Muslims' answer to the Negus about Isa and the rejection of flattery there. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates the scholarly dispute over whether the sword verse abrogates the verses of forbearance, saying many held abrogation, and cites al-Zarkashi in al-Burhan as arguing that these are postponed commands tied to a condition, not abrogated. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}

This is the speaker's view; the museum reports it as his and does not adopt it.

### Kinds of trial in the Meccan period

- He counts the kinds of trial endured so far: bodily torture, verbal abuse, exile through migration, killing as in the case of Sumayya, and economic and social boycott. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He holds that this year and those around it were probably the hardest for the Prophet, coming just before the death of Abu Talib and the death of Khadija. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}

### Why Taif was the hardest day

- He says the Prophet himself named his approach to the leader of Taif, Ibn Abd Yalil, as the hardest thing he met, and that reports speak of stones thrown and his being driven out. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He offers his own reflection that it was hardest because it came after ten years of denial and was the last visible hope, with Abu Talib dead and only Mecca and Taif, the two towns mentioned in Surat al-Zukhruf, nearby. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that Aisha asked the Prophet whether any day was harder than Uhud, and that his answer pointed to Taif. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He holds that trials in a time of weakness, with no refuge or protector, are heavier than losses in battle once a refuge such as Medina exists, citing the Prophet's dream at Uhud about a strong coat of mail interpreted as Medina. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He distinguishes two kinds of protection for the Prophet, the protection of Abu Talib without military support and later the support of Medina, and says the worst harm came after Abu Talib died. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He compares Taif with Uhud, where seventy Companions were killed, the Prophet's tooth was broken, his face wounded and Ali and Fatima tended the wound, and holds that even so Taif was harder. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}

### Ibn Ishaq's account of al-Tufayl and its chain

- He says Ibn Ishaq tells the story of al-Tufayl ibn Amr al-Dawsi at length without a chain, attributing it directly to al-Tufayl. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates from the editor's note by Hammam Sa'id that Ibn Ishaq did not give a chain in the Ziyad al-Bakka'i transmission, and that Ibn Abd al-Barr cites a mursal chain through Uthman ibn Huwayrith and Salih ibn Kaysan, of whom the first has no known biography. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He concludes that the long story has no standing chain, not even a sound mursal one, so it should be used only for general illustration and not as a basis. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}

### What the sound reports say about al-Tufayl

- He relates a report in al-Bukhari, through Sufyan ibn Uyayna and then Abu al-Zinad, al-A'raj and Abu Hurayra, that al-Tufayl told the Prophet that Daws had refused, and that the Prophet asked for guidance for them. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates from Sahih Muslim, through Jabir, that al-Tufayl offered the Prophet a fortress belonging to Daws and the Prophet declined it because God had reserved that for the Ansar, which he takes to mean al-Tufayl had become Muslim in Mecca before the hijra. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates the same report's account of a man from al-Tufayl's people who fell ill after migrating to Medina and injured his own hands, was seen in a dream, and was told his hands would not be mended though he was forgiven for his migration; the Prophet then asked forgiveness for his hands. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He notes that a version in Ibn Hibban has the Prophet repeating the request three times, and that al-Hakim in al-Mustadrak places al-Tufayl's migration after Badr, Uhud and the Trench, when the Prophet was at Khaybar. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He summarises that al-Tufayl became Muslim in Mecca, invited the Prophet to his people's fortress in the region near today's al-Baha, later migrated, and complained of his people's refusal, and that the Prophet prayed for them instead of against them. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}

### Al-Tufayl's conversion story as told by Ibn Ishaq

- He reads the account, with the caution that it is used only for illustration, that al-Tufayl was a noble poet who came to Mecca and that Quraysh men warned him against listening to the Prophet. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that al-Tufayl plugged his ears with cotton, heard part of the Prophet's prayer anyway, judged what he heard to be good, followed him home, was offered Islam and accepted it. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that al-Tufayl asked for a sign to help him with his people, that a light appeared on his forehead and moved to the end of his whip, and that his father and then his wife became Muslim after washing and purifying themselves. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that Daws was slow to respond, that al-Tufayl asked the Prophet to pray against them, and that the Prophet instead prayed for their guidance and told him to return and treat them gently. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that al-Tufayl arrived in Medina with seventy or eighty households of Daws while the Prophet was at Khaybar and was given a share with the Muslims. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that after the conquest of Mecca al-Tufayl asked to be sent to burn the idol of Dhu al-Kaffayn, and did so. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He notes a textual doubt over the word for the vice of Daws mentioned in the story and a possible conflict with the Muslim report about whether he came twice, and says these will be examined when Khaybar is reached. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}

### Al-Tufayl's death at al-Yamama and the Companions lost there

- He relates from Ibn Ishaq that during the wars against those who left Islam al-Tufayl fought with the Muslims against Tulayha and then went to al-Yamama with his son Amr. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He relates that al-Tufayl told his companions a dream and interpreted it as his death, and that he was killed as a martyr at al-Yamama while his son was badly wounded, recovered, and was later killed at the Yarmuk in the time of Umar. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He says that among the early Meccan Companions covered in the series, about thirty-one were killed at al-Yamama, naming among them Zayd ibn al-Khattab, Abu Hudhayfa ibn Utba, Salim the freed slave of Abu Hudhayfa, Shuja ibn Wahb and Abbad ibn Bishr. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
- He holds that al-Yamama and the Yarmuk and Ajnadayn left few of the early Companions alive. {cite:podcast-ahmed-el-sayed seerah-2026/ep32}
