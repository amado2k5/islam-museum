## Summary
The "Book of al-Jabr and al-Muqabala" is a concise book of reckoning by [[person/al-khwarizmi|Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi]]. In the London edition it carries the title "al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala" and is attributed to "al-Shaykh al-Ajall Abu Abd Allah Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi". {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-rosen-1831 p.121} In his preface the author says he wrote it with the encouragement of the caliph [[person/abd-allah-al-mamun|al-Ma'mun]], whose love of learning and closeness to scholars he praises, {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.29} and that he made it "comprehensive of the fine and the weighty in reckoning" for what people need in their inheritances, bequests, partitions, judgements and trade, in measuring land, digging canals, geometry and the like. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.30} For the editors of the Cairo edition it is the book with which al-Khwarizmi founded [[concept/algebra|algebra]], and from it the name of the science passed into the world's languages. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.24} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.25}

## Author and date
The book states no year of composition, but the mention of al-Ma'mun in the preface shows it was written in his reign; the editors give that reign as 813 to 833 CE and note that Ibn al-Nadim describes al-Khwarizmi as attached to al-Ma'mun's "Treasury of Wisdom". {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.22} They observe that Ibn al-Nadim does not list this book among al-Khwarizmi's works, although he names three commentators on it: Sinan ibn al-Fath, Abd Allah ibn al-Hasan, and [[person/abu-al-wafa-al-buzjani|Abu al-Wafa al-Buzjani]]. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.23}

## Structure
The book opens with a sermon praising God and blessing the Prophet, then reflects on those who write learning for later generations: a man who reaches what no one had drawn out before him, a man who explains what the ancients left obscure, or a man who finds a flaw in a book, repairs it and thinks well of its author. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.29} The chapters then follow in this order in the Cairo edition:

- **Numbers and the six types** (scan pp. 30-35): all numbers are composed of the unit, and reckoning by al-jabr needs three kinds: roots, squares (*mal*) and simple number; he then lists [[concept/al-durub-al-sitta|the six types]] of equation and solves each with numerical examples. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.30} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.35}
- **The causes of the compound types** (pp. 35-43): each of the three types that require halving the roots has a geometric "figure" from which its cause is shown, a proof by squares and rectangles. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.35} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.36} The section ends with the claim that everything worked by al-jabr and al-muqabala "must bring you to one of the six chapters". {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.43}
- **Chapter of multiplication** (pp. 43-46): how "things", that is roots, are multiplied together alone or with a number added or subtracted, with the sign rule: if the units are both added or both subtracted the fourth product is added, and if they differ it is subtracted. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.43}
- **Chapter of addition and subtraction** (pp. 46-50): adding and subtracting roots, including irrational (*asamm*) roots, with expressions that have no "figure" because they combine three different kinds, so their necessity is shown "in words". {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.50}
- **Chapter of the six problems** (pp. 50-54): six problems serving as examples of the six types, the first being: ten divided into two parts, one part multiplied by the other and one part multiplied by itself. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.50}
- **Chapter of miscellaneous problems** (pp. 54-69): varied problems reduced to the six types. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.54} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.69}
- **Chapter of transactions** (pp. 69-70): buying, selling, exchange and hire by four numbers, the rated quantity, the rate, the price and the priced quantity, three known and one unknown. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.69} See [[concept/rule-of-three-muamalat|the rule of transactions]].
- **Chapter of mensuration** (pp. 70-82): areas of squares, triangles, the rhombus, the circle and its segments, the cone and the truncated pyramid, and the square inscribed in a triangle. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.70} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.81} See [[concept/ilm-al-misaha|mensuration]].
- **Book of bequests** (pp. 83-122): problems of dividing estates with bequests, in chapters on cash and debt, further bequest chapters, the bequest of a dirham, the "completion" (*takmila*), and then "circular reckoning" (*hisab al-dawr*) with marriage in final illness, manumission in final illness, acknowledgement, and forward sale (*salam*) in final illness. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.83} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.105} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.108} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.110} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.118} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.121} See [[concept/hisab-al-dawr|circular reckoning]].

## Method
Al-Khwarizmi expresses everything in words, not symbols: the unknown is a "thing" or "root", its square a *mal*, and the constant a "number" or "dirhams". {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.31} He uses the two operations the book is named after: *al-jabr*, restoring a quantity that has a deduction by adding its like to both sides, as in "restore the thirty by the thing and add it to the two things"; {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.121} and *al-muqabala*, cancelling like terms on the two sides, as in "set it against... so you cast away a square by a square". {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.69} He restricts solutions to positive numbers and reduces any number of squares to a single square before solving, {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.30} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.33} and pairs each verbal rule with a geometric proof by areas. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.36} The editors point out that he called the case with no root "impossible" (*mustahila*), a name that remained current among mathematicians until the late eighteenth century. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.35}

## Well-known problems
The problem "a square and ten of its roots equal thirty-nine dirhams" is the book's most famous example: halve the roots to get five, multiply it by itself to get twenty-five, add it to thirty-nine to get sixty-four, take its root, eight, and subtract five, leaving three, which is the root of the square; the square is nine. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.32} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.33} The editors say that his equations were repeated in algebraic works from his time to the beginning of the modern age, and that some still appear in algebra textbooks today. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.24}

In the chapter on mensuration he takes the circumference of a circle as the diameter times three and a seventh, calling it "a convention among people, not a necessity", and gives two other views of the geometers: the root of ten times the square of the diameter, and the astronomers' rule of multiplying the diameter by 62,832 and dividing by 20,000. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.71} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.72} A marginal gloss of the manuscript, which the editors quote, calls this "an approximation, not an exact determination". {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.71}

## Glosses in the manuscript
The manuscript's margins carry glosses that the editors take to be explanations added when the book was taught, and they did not treat them as part of the text. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.11} Among them, in the chapter on transactions, are two lines of verse on the rule of dividing by the "middle" and the "first" term in measures and weights, {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.69} and verbal corrections written above the original word by the copyist or a reader. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.34}

## Manuscript and editions
The Cairo edition is based on a manuscript in the Bodleian Library, Oxford, written in Cairo and finished on Sunday 19 Muharram 743 AH, about five hundred years after the author's death; to the editors' knowledge it is the only surviving copy. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.26} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.122} They say they corrected obvious copying errors without notice. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.12} The Arabic text was first published in London in 1831 by Friedrich Rosen, with an English translation and commentary; {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.26} its Arabic title page says it was printed "in the Christian year 1830". {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-rosen-1831 p.121} Marre published a French translation of the mensuration chapter from Rosen's text, and in 1915 Karpinski published a translation of the Latin version made from the Arabic by Robert of Chester; the editors say the Latin differs from the Arabic in many places. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.26} The Cairo edition is publication no. 2 of the Faculty of Science of the Egyptian University, introduced and annotated by [[person/ali-mustafa-musharrafa|Ali Mustafa Mosharafa]] and [[person/muhammad-mursi-ahmad|Muhammad Mursi Ahmad]], with notes that write each problem in modern algebraic symbols. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.1} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.30}

## Standing and influence
Before the text the editors place a chapter on "algebra before al-Khwarizmi" surveying what was known of equations among the ancient Egyptians, Babylonians, Greeks and Indians; in their view al-Khwarizmi stands out for studying the types of second-degree equation systematically, which Diophantus did not do. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.13} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.18} They report that [[person/abu-kamil-shuja-ibn-aslam|Abu Kamil Shuja ibn Aslam]] openly acknowledges al-Khwarizmi as his authority, that [[person/umar-khayyam|Umar al-Khayyam]] quotes him, that Ibn Khaldun names him as the first to write on algebra with Abu Kamil after him, and that [[person/al-qazwini|al-Qazwini]] says he was the first to translate algebra for the Muslims. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.24} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.25} They close by noting that al-jabr, the word he used as his book's title, became the name of the science in every language. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.25}
