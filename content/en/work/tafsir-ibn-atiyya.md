## Summary
*al-Muharrar al-Wajiz fi Tafsir al-Kitab al-'Aziz* ("The Precise and Concise in Interpreting the Mighty Book") is a commentary on the whole Qur'an by the judge Abu Muhammad [[person/ibn-atiyah-al-andalusi|Abd al-Haqq ibn Ghalib ibn Atiyya of al-Andalus]] (481–541 AH), known as Tafsir Ibn Atiyya. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.1} {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.2} He began it in the lifetime of his father, who died in 518 AH, when he was about thirty, worked on it for a long time, and his student Ibn Hubaysh read the whole of it with him at Almería. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.3}

## Purpose and method, in the author's preface
Ibn Atiyya says that when he wished to choose for himself a science whose lights he could store up against the darkness of his grave, he knew that a science is as noble as its object, and found the knowledge of God's Book the strongest of them, so he made it "the profit of my life". {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.14} Finding that the subtleties of interpretation outran his memory, he set about recording what he sifted out of it. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.14} He meant his book to be "comprehensive, concise and precise": telling only those stories without which a verse cannot be understood, and setting down the scholars' views on meanings attributed to them, in line with the sound Arabic sense in which the early generations received the Book; whenever a trusted scholar used an expression tending towards the aims of the allegorists and esotericists, he says, he pointed it out. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.14} He treats each verse in the order of its words — ruling, grammar, language, meaning or reading — following the words closely so as to avoid the "leaps" found in many commentaries; he considers al-Mahdawi's arrangement "a scattering of attention"; and he aims to give all the readings, accepted and irregular, and every possible sense of the words "with the utmost concision". {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.14} He calls the book "the fruit of my existence and the choice of my effort". {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.15}

## His introduction on the Qur'anic sciences
Ibn Atiyya prefaced his commentary with chapters on the merit of the Qur'an; the merit of interpreting it and of studying its language and grammar; what has been said about speaking on interpretation and the ranks of the commentators; the meaning of the hadith of [[concept/al-ahruf-al-sabaa|the seven ahruf]]; the collection of the Qur'an, its vowelling, dotting and division; words connected with non-Arab languages; inimitability; and the names of the Qur'an, of the sura and of the verse. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.4} {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.15} {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.18} {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.19} {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.21} The orientalist Arthur Jeffery published this introduction with another in Cairo in 1954 as *Muqaddimatan fi 'Ulum al-Qur'an*; Majd Makki considers his edition full of distortions and errors. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.4}

Among its best-known parts is his ranking of the commentators: first Ali ibn Abi Talib, then [[person/abdullah-ibn-abbas|Abd Allah ibn Abbas]], who "devoted himself to the task and perfected it" and was followed by Mujahid and Sa'id ibn Jubayr; then Ibn Mas'ud, Ubayy ibn Ka'b, Zayd ibn Thabit and Abd Allah ibn Amr; among the Successors al-Hasan, Mujahid, Sa'id ibn Jubayr and Alqama, then Ikrima and al-Dahhak; and he records al-Sha'bi's criticism of al-Suddi and Abu Salih. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.20} He then names those who wrote commentaries, such as Abd al-Razzaq, Ali ibn Abi Talha and al-Bukhari, says that [[person/al-tabari|Ibn Jarir al-Tabari]] "gathered the scattered parts of interpretation for the people", that the words of al-Zajjaj and Abu Ali al-Farisi are "well sifted", and that people found much to correct in al-Naqqash and al-Nahhas. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.20} On the hadith "whoever speaks about the Qur'an by his opinion", he holds that it concerns one who storms a meaning by his own opinion without regard to what scholars have said and the rules of the sciences, not one who reasons by the rules of grammar and legal theory; see [[concept/tafsir-bil-raay|tafsir by opinion]]. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.19} {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.20}

## Sources
Majd Makki names as his chief Eastern source al-Tabari's [[work/jami-al-bayan|Jami' al-Bayan]], from which he took transmitted material while dropping the chains, often disagreeing with and debating al-Tabari; also al-Naqqash's *Shifa' al-Sudur*; from North Africa al-Mahdawi's *al-Tahsil*; and from al-Andalus the commentary of Makki ibn Abi Talib. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.4} {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.5} In theology he drew on the works of al-Ash'ari, al-Baqillani and al-Juwayni. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.5}

## Characteristics
Majd Makki notes that he cut down on Israelite reports, reproached commentators for multiplying them and criticised many of them; that he built his commentary on language and grammar; that he gave and explained the accepted and irregular readings; and that in fiqh he did not accept the school of Dawud al-Zahiri. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.6} {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.7} A leaning towards the Mu'tazila has been attributed to him; the views are set out under [[question/was-ibn-atiyyas-tafsir-mutazili|Does Ibn Atiyya's commentary lean towards the Mu'tazila?]]. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.7}

## Standing and influence
Majd Makki gathers what scholars said of it: Ibn Amira al-Dabbi, that in it he "surpassed all who came before"; Ibn Juzayy in the preface to *al-Tashil*, that it is "the best and most balanced of works"; Abu Hayyan, who calls it and al-Kashshaf "the most eminent works composed in tafsir"; Ibn Khaldun, that he summarised the earlier commentaries and sought what was nearest to soundness; and Ibn Ashur, that the legal bent prevails in it, as the rhetorical bent prevails in al-Zamakhshari. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.7} {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.8} It shaped four books in particular: al-Qurtubi's commentary, which sometimes followed it in citing weak hadith; Ibn Juzayy's [[work/tafsir-ibn-juzayy|al-Tashil]], which quotes it and modelled its introduction on it; Abu Hayyan's *al-Bahr al-Muhit*, hardly a page of which lacks a discussion of it; and al-Tha'alibi's [[work/al-jawahir-al-hisan|al-Jawahir al-Hisan]], an abridgement of it. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.8} {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.9} Abu Hayyan's objections to it were collected by Ibn Maktum in *al-Durr al-Laqit*, and Yahya al-Shawi wrote *al-Muhakamat* between Abu Hayyan, Ibn Atiyya and al-Zamakhshari. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.9}

## Editions
The book remained in manuscript until the Moroccan Ministry of Awqaf published it, edited by the Scholarly Council of Fez, in 1395 AH in sixteen volumes; it was then printed in Qatar in 1398 AH in thirteen volumes, edited by al-Rahhali al-Faruqi, Abd Allah al-Ansari, al-Sayyid Abd al-Al al-Sayyid Ibrahim and Muhammad al-Shafi'i Sadiq al-Inani from six manuscripts; and two volumes, to Surat Al Imran, appeared in Egypt edited by Ahmad Sadiq al-Mallah. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.10} The museum used Dar Ibn Hazm's one-volume printing based on the Qatar edition. {cite:muharrar-wajiz-dar-ibn-hazm p.10}
