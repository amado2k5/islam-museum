## Summary
Zad al-Masir fi Ilm al-Tafsir ("Provisions for the Journey in the Science of Tafsir") is the commentary of [[person/ibn-al-jawzi|Abu al-Faraj Ibn al-Jawzi]] the Hanbali (d. 597 AH). {cite:zad-al-masir-dar-al-kitab-al-arabi-2001-v1 p.1} {cite:zad-al-masir-dar-al-kitab-al-arabi-2001-v1 p.7} In his preface he says he looked through the commentaries and found them either so large that the memoriser despairs of them, so small that they do not serve the whole purpose, or middling, of few benefits and no order, sometimes neglecting the difficult while explaining what is not obscure; so he brought "this easy compendium, holding abundant knowledge", and made its wording as brief as he could. {cite:zad-al-masir-maktab-islami-v1 p.3} {cite:zad-al-masir-maktab-islami-v1 p.4}

## Its aim
Ibn al-Jawzi says that most commentaries do not do the job on their own, so that for a single verse one must look in several books: one omits abrogation; if it has that, it lacks the occasions of revelation; if it has those, it does not say what is Meccan and what Medinan; if it does, it does not point to the verse's ruling; if it does, it does not answer the difficulty. He put all these into his book, avoided re-explaining a word already explained except by reference, and left out views unlikely to be sound. {cite:zad-al-masir-maktab-islami-v1 p.6} {cite:zad-al-masir-maktab-islami-v1 p.7} Whatever is not explained in the run of the verses, he says, has either been explained already or is clear enough to need no explanation. {cite:zad-al-masir-maktab-islami-v1 p.7}

## Its opening sections
Before the commentary he sets several sections: on the merit of tafsir, citing Ibn Mas'ud that they learned ten verses at a time and went no further until they knew the knowledge and practice in them, and Iyas ibn Mu'awiya's likeness of one who reads the Qur'an without knowing its meaning to people who receive a letter at night with no lamp; {cite:zad-al-masir-maktab-islami-v1 p.4} on tafsir and ta'wil, which those inclined to Arabic, the majority of early exegetes, take as one, and those inclined to law distinguish; {cite:zad-al-masir-maktab-islami-v1 p.4} on the span of revelation: from Ibn Abbas, sent down whole to the House of Glory on the Night of Decree and then over twenty years, as al-Sha'bi also said, and from al-Hasan eighteen years; {cite:zad-al-masir-maktab-islami-v1 p.5} and on the first and last revealed — see [[question/how-did-revelation-begin|How did revelation begin?]] and [[question/what-was-the-last-revelation|What was the last part of the Qur'an to be revealed?]]. {cite:zad-al-masir-maktab-islami-v1 p.5} {cite:zad-al-masir-maktab-islami-v1 p.6} Then come sections on the isti'adha and on the basmala, where he gives Ahmad's position and others'; see [[question/is-the-basmala-a-verse-of-al-fatiha|Is the basmala a verse of al-Fatiha?]]. {cite:zad-al-masir-maktab-islami-v1 p.7} {cite:zad-al-masir-maktab-islami-v1 p.8}

## Its sources in language
Abd al-Razzaq al-Mahdi says Ibn al-Jawzi names those about whom verses came down, gives the well-known and sometimes the irregular readings, stops at abrogated verses and those whose abrogation is disputed, and traces each view to its source, relying on lexicographers such as Ibn Qutayba, Abu Ubayda and al-Khalil, grammarians such as al-Farra', al-Zajjaj, al-Akhfash and al-Kisa'i, and readers such as al-Jahdari and Asim. {cite:zad-al-masir-dar-al-kitab-al-arabi-2001-v1 p.9}
