## Overview
The Hanafi school, or madhhab, is the school of religious law named after [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]], al-Nu'man b. Thabit. The Encyclopaedia of Islam article on the school says that it grew out of the main body of the ancient school of [[place/kufa|Kufa]] and also absorbed the ancient school of [[place/basra|Basra]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 172}

Ahmad Amin, writing in Duha al-Islam, calls it the widest of the schools in its use of personal opinion. If circles were drawn to show how much each school used opinion, the smallest would be the Zahiri circle, then the Hanbali, then the Maliki, then the [[group/shafii-madhhab|Shafi'i]], and the largest the Hanafi. {cite:duha-islam p.502}

## How a school took shape around Abu Hanifa
The article notes that already in the generation after Abu Hanifa, who died in 150/767, his pupil [[person/abu-yusuf-al-qadi|Abu Yusuf]], who died in 182/795, called him the prominent lawyer, and [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-hasan-al-shaybani|al-Shaybani]], who died in 189/805, spoke of the followers of Abu Hanifa. [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]], who died in 204/820, repeatedly treats those followers as one homogeneous group. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 172}

The article credits the writing of Abu Yusuf and above all of al-Shaybani with turning the old Kufan school into the Hanafi school. Three works of al-Shaybani, the Kitab al-Asl and the large and small Jami, became the standard texts. Abu Yusuf and al-Shaybani came to be seen as the two main companions of Abu Hanifa, and with him they form the triad of the highest authorities of the school, although others, such as [[person/zufar-ibn-al-hudhayl|Zufar b. al-Hudhayl]] and al-Hasan b. Ziyad al-Lu'lu'i, were just as prominent in their time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 172} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 173}

The three authorities often disagreed with one another. The article therefore says that the doctrine of the Hanafi school is less uniform than that of the other schools, and it points to a work of [[person/abu-l-layth-al-samarqandi|Abu al-Layth al-Samarkandi]] that lists their differing opinions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 173}

## Opinion, istihsan and the Traditionists
The article states that, for reasons it calls adventitious, Abu Hanifa and his school became the main target of the Traditionists, the [[concept/ahl-al-hadith|Ahl al-Hadith]], in their attack on the use of subjective opinion, ra'y, in religious law. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 173}

Ahmad Amin describes the Hanafi position in his own terms. The Hanafis upheld [[concept/istihsan|istihsan]], juristic preference. As he explains it, the jurist leaves analogy in favour of judging a matter by what justice requires, seeking absolute justice. The Shafi'is rejected it, and a saying of al-Shafi'i in disapproval is transmitted: "Whoever practises istihsan has made a law." These passages give one author's account of the method, not a statement by the Hanafi jurists themselves. {cite:duha-islam p.504}

## Where the school spread
Having originated in Iraq, the school was favoured by the first [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid]] caliphs. It has always been well represented in its home country and in Syria, and it spread early to the east, to [[place/khurasan|Khurasan]], [[place/transoxiana|Transoxania]] and [[place/afghanistan|Afghanistan]], then to Central Asia and [[place/china|China]]. Many famous Hanafi scholars came from Khurasan and Transoxania. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 173}

Some details of the article:

- From the 5th/11th century until well into the Mongol period, the Banu Maza family held political power in [[place/bukhara|Bukhara]] as hereditary chiefs of the Hanafis of the town, with the title of sadr.
- In Khurasan, from the 3rd/9th century, the Hanafis developed a special law of irrigation adapted to the canal system there.
- In the Maghrib, the school had followers alongside the Malikis during the first centuries of Islam, particularly in Ifriqiya under the [[polity/aghlabid-emirate|Aghlabids]], and in Sicily it even predominated, according to al-Maqdisi.

{cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 173}

The school became the favourite of the Turkish [[polity/seljuk-empire|Seljuk]] rulers and of the Ottoman Turks. In the [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] it enjoyed constant dynastic favour and exclusive official recognition. As a legacy of that rule, Hanafi doctrine has kept official status, as far as Islamic law remains valid, even in former Ottoman provinces where most Muslims follow another school, and the article names Egypt, Sudan, Jordan, Israel, Lebanon and Syria. It also reports that the present constitution of Afghanistan gives the school official recognition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 173}

## Early authorities and the classic handbooks
The article lists well-known members of the school in the older period whose works survive.

- [[person/al-khassaf|al-Khassaf]], who died in 261/874, court lawyer of the caliph al-Muhtadi, wrote a classic handbook on waqf, one on the duties of the judge and one on legal devices.
- [[person/al-tahawi|al-Tahawi]], who died in 321/933, was a convert from the Shafi'i school.
- al-Hakim al-Shahid, who died in 334/945, abbreviated al-Shaybani's main works in a book called al-Kafi.
- Abu al-Layth al-Samarkandi, who died in 375/985, wrote widely on fiqh and other religious sciences.
- [[person/al-quduri|al-Quduri]], who died in 428/1036, wrote a Mukhtasar on which later works draw heavily.

{cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 173}

The article adds that throughout this period the school had a strong tradition of books on how Islamic law is applied in practice. The [[work/al-mabsut-sarakhsi|Mabsut]] of [[person/shams-al-aimma-al-sarakhsi|Shams al-A'imma al-Sarakhsi]], who died in 483/1090, is a commentary on al-Kafi and marks a move to a more logical and systematic arrangement within each chapter. The Bada'i al-Sana'i of [[person/ala-al-din-al-kasani|al-Kasani]], who died in 587/1191, follows a strictly systematic arrangement. In the usual course of all the legal schools, later handbooks and their commentaries displaced these older works. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 173}

## Later handbooks, commentaries and fatwa collections
Among the important later works, the article names the following.

- The [[work/al-hidayah-marghinani|Hidaya]] of [[person/burhan-al-din-al-marghinani|al-Marghinani]], which acquired many commentaries and a synopsis, the Wiqayat al-riwaya, by Burhan al-Din Mahmud al-Mahbubi of the 7th/13th century.
- The Jami al-rumuz of al-Qubistani, who died in 950/1543, which carried great authority in Transoxania.
- The Kanz al-daqa'iq of Abu al-Barakat al-Nasafi, who died in 710/1310, with commentaries such as the Tabyin al-haqa'iq of al-Zayla'i and the Bahr al-ra'iq of [[person/ibn-nujaym|Ibn Nujaym]], who died in 970/1563. Ibn Nujaym also wrote the Ashbah wa-al-naza'ir on the systematic structure of positive law.
- In the Ottoman Empire, the Durar al-hukkam of Mulla Khusraw, who died in 885/1480, and above all the Multaqa al-abhur of Ibrahim al-Halabi, who died in 956/1549, which soon became the authoritative handbook there. Its two most popular commentaries are the Majma al-anhur of Shaykh-zade and the Durr al-muntaqa of al-Haskafi.
- Al-Haskafi's Durr al-mukhtar, on which [[person/ibn-abidin|Ibn Abidin]], who died in 1252/1836, wrote the much esteemed Radd al-muhtar, which pays particular attention to problems of the contemporary world.
- The Hukuk-i Islamiyye ve Istilahat-i Fikhiyye Kamusu of Omer Nasuhi Bilmen, mufti of Istanbul, which the article calls the latest great exposition of the doctrine in the traditional style.

{cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 173}

In India, the article names the [[work/fatawa-alamgiriyya|Fatawa al-Alamgiriyya]] as the most authoritative handbook of traditional Hanafi doctrine. It is not a collection of fatwas but a vast compilation of extracts from the school's authoritative works, made by order of the Mughal emperor [[person/aurangzeb-mughal|Awrangzib Alamgir]], who lived from 1067/1658 to 1118/1707. Parts were translated into English. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 173}

Among the more important Hanafi fatwa collections the article lists those of Burhan al-Din Ibn Maza, of [[work/fatawa-qadikhan|Qadi Khan]], of Siraj al-Din al-Sajawandi, who also wrote a popular treatise on inheritance, of al-Bazzazi al-Kardari, of Abu al-Su'ud and of al-Ankarawi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 173}

On legal methodology, usul, the article names the Kanz al-wusul of al-Pazdawi, the Kitab al-usul of al-Sarakhsi, the Manar al-anwar of al-Nasafi, the Tawdih of Sadr al-Shari'a al-Thani, on which the Shafi'i scholar al-Taftazani wrote the commentary Talwih, the Tahrir of Ibn al-Humam with its commentary Taqrir by Ibn Amir al-Hajj, and the Mirqat al-wusul of Mulla Khusraw. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 173} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 174}

## Codification and modern legal systems
The article reports that in British India, from 1772, locally administered Islamic law came under the influence of English legal thought, and a separate body of law, Anglo-Muhammadan law, arose, applying Hanafi doctrine and, for the Shia minority, Shia doctrine. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 174}

In 1877 Ottoman Turkey enacted a codification of the law of contracts and obligations and of civil procedure according to Hanafi doctrine, known as the Mecelle. The author of the article judges that traditional Islamic law cannot be adequately expressed as a code and that the Mecelle, made under European influence, is a secular rather than an Islamic code, though it is often used as a statement of Hanafi doctrine. He adds that it modifies the strict doctrine, mostly by omission, and that through it the Hanafi form of Islamic law deeply influenced the civil law of several Near Eastern countries. In Egypt at about the same time, Muhammad Qadri Pasha put the Hanafi law of family, inheritance, property and waqf into the form of codes, but these were never enacted. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 174}
