## Overview
The Shafi'i school is the school of [[person/al-shafii|Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i]]. Ahmad Amin counts it the second widest of the schools in its use of opinion, after the Hanafi. {cite:duha-islam p.502}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam, in its article on al-Shafi'iyya, defines it as a legal school of Sunni Islam whose members claim to follow the teachings of the imam, who died in 204/820. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 194} The material below is taken from that article, written by E. Chaumont. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 194}

## A school that follows a teacher who warned against following teachers
The article opens with a puzzle that it says applies to every legal school, not only this one. In his Risala, al-Shafi'i mounted a radical criticism of conformism, [[concept/taqlid|taqlid]]. He sought to discredit living local tradition as a source of law and to insist that the opinions of the great imams could not be invoked without further proof of their authority. His biographers also credit him with solemn statements forbidding people to call themselves his followers or to turn his doctrine into a new conformism after his death. Seen from his own thought, the article says, the very existence of a school therefore looks contradictory. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 194}

The article reports that Muslim jurists noticed this tension early and answered it in different ways, from the rare refusal to belong to any school to unquestioning acceptance of the imams' superiority, with compromises between. The author notes that the question has yet to be properly studied. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 194}

## Scholars and lay followers
The article distinguishes two kinds of adherent. A lay person, it explains, is expected to turn to a recognised jurist of his choice when a question of the law arises and to act on the opinion he receives. In theory his membership of a school follows from that choice: he is called a Shafi'i if he relies on a jurist who claims the Shafi'i legacy, and the only effort asked of him is to judge the relative worth of the imams and choose sincerely. Membership was not necessarily fixed: a person could change school, and according to some authors could in a particular matter consult a scholar of another school. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 194}

## When did the school begin?
The article calls the chronology imperfectly resolved. It reports the view of J. Schacht that a school claiming al-Shafi'i and spreading his doctrine arose with his first generation of disciples, above all [[person/al-muzani|al-Muzani]], whose abridgement of the master's teaching would have started its institutionalisation. The article notes that this fits the history of jurisprudence given by the Shafi'i author [[person/abu-ishaq-al-shirazi|Abu Ishaq al-Shirazi]], who describes the study of law passing from regional groups of jurists into the hands of the pupils of al-Shafi'i, [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]], [[person/malik-ibn-anas|Malik]], [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]] and [[person/dawud-al-zahiri|Dawud]]. The author considers it probable that al-Shirazi was reconstructing events after the fact and that the school became a genuine institution later than he suggests. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 194} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 195}

The article places the birth of these "personal" schools, as opposed to the earlier "local" ones, in the first half of the third Islamic century, while admitting that it is hard to say what adherence meant to a jurist then. Citing studies by W.M. Watt and W.B. Hallaq, it states that the birth of personal schools cannot be equated with the "closing of the door of [[concept/ijtihad|ijtihad]]", which never became an institution and was a much later phenomenon. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 195}

## How the school spread
The article sets out two accounts of diffusion and judges them complementary rather than rival. In the first, found in the biographical dictionaries and in most Muslim writing, the school spread through eminent scholars whose teaching and persuasiveness drew pupils from far away; these returned home to teach or to hold judicial office. The author finds this tends to idealise matters, since teaching and the judiciary were not independent of political power. The second account, associated with C. Snouck Hurgronje and Schacht, treats the growth of schools as a matter of state policy. Citing G. Makdisi and A.K.S. Lambton, the article holds that official favour could influence a school's fortunes only to a limited degree. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 195}

## The first Shafi'is
Al-Shafi'i's career included periods in the Hijaz and in [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]], but his teaching won its widest acceptance, and he gained most pupils, towards the end of his life at [[place/al-fustat|Fustat]] in Egypt. The article stresses that two bodies of doctrine are attributed to him: the "ancient", formed before he reached Egypt in 198/814 according to al-Maqrizi, and the "new", formed during his stay there. The school kept only the new doctrine, with many changes over the centuries, so its spread depended on his Egyptian pupils. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 195}

Two direct pupils are known for transmitting the earlier doctrine in Iraq, Abu Ali al-Za'farani and Abu Ali al-Karabisi, the first being reckoned the more reliable. The article says the new doctrine reached Iraq only through Abu l-Qasim al-Anmati, a pupil of two Egyptian Shafi'is. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 195}

Three Egyptian pupils stand out. Al-Shafi'i is said to have called [[person/al-buwayti|al-Buwayti]] his tongue, al-Muzani the one who made his school prevail, and [[person/al-rabi-ibn-sulayman-al-muradi|al-Rabi' al-Muradi]] the transmitter of his books. Al-Buwayti, from Upper Egypt, is described as the favourite pupil and named successor; he suffered in the [[event/mihna-of-the-quran|inquisition over the Qur'an]], was arrested and imprisoned in Baghdad, and died in custody. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 195} The article judges his historical importance rather secondary, unless Abu Talib al-Makki is right, as repeated by al-Ghazali, that al-Buwayti was the real author of the [[work/kitab-al-umm|Kitab al-Umm]] attributed to al-Shafi'i. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 196}

Al-Muzani is described as the most independent of the pupils. His [[work/mukhtasar-al-muzani|Mukhtasar]] drew many commentaries. Al-Nawawi counted it among the school's seminal works, whereas al-Rafi'i held that it too often departed from al-Shafi'i, so that its singular views should not be counted as the school's doctrine. Abu Ishaq al-Shirazi often refuted them in his Muhadhdhab. The article adds, citing al-Asnawi, that al-Muzani also wrote a book setting aside al-Shafi'i's doctrine for his own position. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 196}

Al-Rabi' al-Muradi, called al-Shafi'i's servant, owes his name to faithful transmission rather than to legal brilliance. He was the main transmitter of the new doctrine and of the books that set it out, the Umm and the [[work/al-risala-shafii|Risala]], and wrote a summary that never matched al-Muzani's success. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 196}

## Egypt, Syria and the Hijaz
Thanks to these pupils, Egypt, except Upper Egypt which stayed Maliki, became the cradle of the school. Syria, previously Awza'i, followed. The article reports that until Sultan Baybars introduced the system of four judges in 665/1228, practically all official judicial posts in Egypt and Syria were held by Shafi'is, except under the [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimids]] from 358/969 to 567/1171. Even afterwards the school kept certain prerogatives there, losing them for good with the Ottoman conquest of 1517, which made Hanafism the state's legal doctrine. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 196}

To the west the school did not establish itself: from the fourth Islamic century only a very few Shafi'is appear in the Maghrib and al-Andalus. Its legal theory nonetheless influenced Maliki scholarship there through Maliki scholars who studied in the East, the article giving Abu l-Walid al-Baji, whose usul are barely distinguishable from those of his Shafi'i teacher al-Shirazi, as an example. In the Hijaz, including Mecca and Medina, Shafi'is were broadly in the majority, while Yemen was shared between them and the [[group/zaydiyya|Zaydiyya]], with tensions the article says persist today. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 196}

## Iraq and Khurasan
In Iraq, and especially Baghdad, al-Anmati's role is called decisive. He stands at the head of a line of jurists who theorised and codified Shafi'i usul and fiqh. His chief pupil, the qadi [[person/ahmad-ibn-umar-ibn-surayj|Ibn Surayj]], who died in 306/918, taught many well-known scholars, among them Abu Ishaq al-Marwazi, Ibn Abi Hurayra and al-Qaffal al-Shashi, and the work of later authors such as Abu Hamid al-Isfara'ini, Abu l-Tayyib al-Tabari and al-Shirazi belongs to the same line. The article attributes the rich Shafi'i literature of Baghdad to the presence there of several schools, a rivalry that at best stimulated creativity and at worst bred partisanship. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 196}

In Khurasan, notably [[place/nishapur|Nishapur]] and Marw, the article says, citing al-Subki, the Shafi'is were very numerous. The Khurasani branch, generally more speculative than the Iraqi one, is linked with Abu Ishaq al-Isfara'ini, [[person/imam-al-haramayn-al-juwayni|al-Juwayni]] and [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]]. Political and religious disorders in the sixth Islamic century ended the Shafi'i presence in Khurasan in favour of the Hanafi school and drove many Shafi'i scholars westward, especially to Syria. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 196}

## Renewers of the school
The article records a list by al-Subki of the school's most important figures, each presented as the renewer of religion for his century, as a prophetic saying promises: al-Shafi'i, Ibn Surayj, Abu Hamid al-Isfara'ini, al-Ghazali, [[person/fakhr-al-din-al-razi|Fakhr al-Din al-Razi]] and Ibn Daqiq al-Id. It cautions that the list is not canonical, given the theological views of its author. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 196} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 197}

## Legal theory
Shafi'is took pride in their imam as founder of usul al-fiqh, yet the article states that this science was hardly practised in the school's first two centuries, though not wholly neglected; it flourished from the fourth century. All Shafi'i writers on usul claim allegiance to the Risala, but the article calls this often formal and says it covers several currents. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 197}

One current is strictly legal and its writers were mostly practising jurists. Its main representative is Abu Ishaq al-Shirazi, whose [[work/al-luma-fi-usul-al-fiqh|al-Luma']] long served as a model. Another current was developed by writers who were certainly Shafi'is but often theologians, generally Ash'ari in allegiance. Its texts include al-Juwayni's [[work/al-burhan-fi-usul-al-fiqh|al-Burhan]] and his short Waraqat, al-Ghazali's [[work/al-mustasfa|al-Mustasfa]], al-Amidi's al-Ihkam and al-Razi's al-Mahsul. In the later period these works were endlessly commented upon; the best-known derived work is Tadj al-Din al-Subki's Djam' al-djawami', taught for centuries with the commentary of al-Mahalli. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 197}

## Law and works of reference
In the seventh Islamic century [[person/al-nawawi|al-Nawawi]] listed the fiqh works that had most influenced the school: al-Muzani's Mukhtasar, al-Shirazi's [[work/al-tanbih-fi-al-fiqh-al-shafii|al-Tanbih]] and [[work/al-muhadhdhab-fi-fiqh-al-shafii|al-Muhadhdhab]], al-Ghazali's Wasit and [[work/al-wajiz-fi-fiqh-al-shafii|al-Wajiz]], and al-Rafi'i's commentary on al-Wajiz. The article observes that none of al-Shafi'i's own books appears in the list, and that, judging by the commentaries it produced, al-Muzani's Mukhtasar was more important than the Umm in the development of the doctrine. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 197}

The article ties al-Shirazi's works to the Iraqi element of the school and al-Ghazali's to the Khurasani one, the latter building on the [[work/nihayat-al-matlab-juwayni|Nihaya]] of his teacher al-Juwayni. Al-Rafi'i, in his [[work/fath-al-aziz-rafii|Fath al-aziz]], is reputed to have joined the two elements, after which they are barely distinguished. His Muharrar was summarised by al-Nawawi in the [[work/minhaj-al-talibin-nawawi|Minhaj al-talibin]], itself highly regarded; commentaries on it by [[person/ibn-hajar-al-haytami|Ibn Hajar al-Haytami]] and al-Ramli, with al-Ghazzi's commentary on Abu Shuja's Taqrib, remained reference works for teaching. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 197}

## Theology and Sufism
The article says the school generally kept good relations with Ash'ari theology and with "moderate" Sufism. Scholars who combined being a Shafi'i jurist, an Ash'ari theologian and a Sufi are numerous; [[person/al-qushayri-abu-l-qasim|al-Qushayri]], author of the Risala on Sufism, is one, and al-Ghazali is called the most eminent representative of the trend though not the most original. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 198} The article reasons that Shafi'i law has nothing to dispute with Sufism understood as an inner struggle to realise the Law's injunctions, since it does not replace fiqh and requires no practices that fiqh would find objectionable. It names Sufis claimed by the school, such as al-Muhasibi and Ibn Khafif, and al-Junayd, though al-Qushayri says he followed the school of Abu Thawr. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 198}

On theology, the article calls the relation with Ash'arism more problematical and long debated, and distinguishes two tendencies among Shafi'i jurists. One, represented by scholars such as al-Bayhaqi, Ibn al-Salah, al-Dhahabi and Ibn Kathir, was hostile in varying degrees to speculative theology as such. The other favoured kalam and includes al-Isfara'ini, Ibn Furak, al-Juwayni, al-Razi and al-Amidi, some of the best Muslim theologians. Most Shafi'is who favoured kalam were Ash'ari, with exceptions such as the qadi Abd al-Jabbar, a Shafi'i in law and a Mu'tazili in theology. Most Shafi'i biographers hold that [[person/al-ashari|al-Ash'ari]] was himself a Shafi'i, but the article says this cannot be stated categorically. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 198}

The article concludes that one should not speak of a special bond between the two, since many Shafi'is opposed speculative theology and not all Ash'aris were Shafi'is. Because Ash'arism came over the centuries to be seen as the leading doctrine of Sunni Islam, a majority of Ash'aris among the Shafi'is is unsurprising. It cites al-Subki's observation that Ash'aris form the majority in every school except the Hanbali, and that only the Maliki school is exclusively Ash'ari. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 198}

## Istihsan
Ahmad Amin reports that the Shafi'is rejected the [[concept/istihsan|istihsan]] that the [[group/hanafi-madhhab|Hanafis]] upheld, and transmits a saying of al-Shafi'i in disapproval: "Whoever practises istihsan has made a law." {cite:duha-islam p.504}
