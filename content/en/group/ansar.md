## Overview
The Ansar, meaning "the helpers": the Aws and the Khazraj of Yathrib, who pledged to the Prophet and sheltered him. Their pledge moved Islam from the call of the vulnerable to an established state.

The Encyclopaedia of Islam describes the name as the usual designation of the men of Medina who supported [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]], as distinct from the [[group/muhajirun|Muhajirun]], the emigrants, who were his Meccan followers. Its article follows the Ansar from their first contacts with the Prophet to their gradual loss of influence and their absorption into the wider population of Medina. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 538} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 539}

## The name
The article explains that after the general conversion of the Arabs the older joint name of the two tribes, Banu Kayla, fell out of use and was replaced by Ansar. A single member was called an Ansari. The new name kept the early service of the men of Medina honourably in memory, and the article points to two Qur'anic verses in surah nine that it connects with the term. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 538}

The word is presumably the plural of nasir, though that singular was never used as a technical term. The verb nasara carries the sense of helping a person who has been wronged against his enemy, which the article takes as enough to explain why the Muslims of Medina were called helpers, and sometimes helpers of the Prophet. It adds that the choice may have been influenced by a Qur'anic passage in which Jesus asks his disciples who will be his helpers towards God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 538}

## The Aws and the Khazraj
The article on al-Aws calls it one of the two main Arab tribes of Medina. The other was [[group/al-khazraj|al-Khazraj]]. Before Islam the two were known as Banu Kayla, after their reputed mother, and after the Hijra they became the helpers of Muhammad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 820} The article on al-Khazraj says the same of that tribe. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1248}

The genealogy transmitted by Ibn Sa'd traces al-Aws back through a long line to the Azd and, finally, to Qahtan. The article reads the name al-Aws as probably meaning "the gift", perhaps a shortening of Aws Manat, and notes that it was changed to Aws Allah in Islamic times. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 820}

The traditional story, as the article gives it, is that after a migration from the Yemen the descendants of the emigrants quarrelled, and the Aws and Khazraj separated from the Ghassan and settled in Yathrib, which was then controlled by Jewish clans. For a time Banu Kayla were subordinate to the Jews. Under the leadership of [[person/malik-ibn-al-ajlan|Malik ibn al-'Ajlan]] of the Khazraji clan of Salim they became independent and gained a share of the palm groves and strongholds. His contemporary and rival was Uhayha ibn al-Julah, chief of Banu Jahjaba, a branch of the Aws clan of 'Amr ibn 'Awf. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 820} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1248}

## Clans rather than two tribes
The article on al-Aws doubts that there was, at this stage, any idea of the Aws or the Khazraj as a unity. In its reading the effective units were their subdivisions, called clans, and even these may not have been formed as the genealogies suggest, since those later compilations are wholly patrilineal while many signs show that kinship through the mother mattered in Medina. The feuds before the Hijra are commonly said to be between the two tribes, but the sources speak of fighting between clans and groups of clans. In the [[event/constitution-of-medina|Constitution of Medina]] the units responsible for blood-money are single clans or groups such as al-Nabit, made up of [[group/banu-abd-al-ashhal|'Abd al-Ashhal]], [[group/banu-zafar-al-aws|Zafar]] and Haritha. The article thinks it probable that the idea of the two tribes was fostered to create closer ties between allied clans, and that this was happening shortly before the Hijra. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 820} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 821}

The article on al-Khazraj likewise notes that the Constitution does not name the tribe, only its five clans: al-Najjar, al-Harith, 'Awf, Sa'ida and Jusham. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1248}

## The feuds before Islam
In the generation before the Hijra the leading man of the Aws was Hudayr ibn Simak. The article records that the Khazraji leader 'Amr ibn al-Nu'man of Bayada gained advantages over the neighbouring clan of Zurayk and later fought, with al-Harith, against some Aws clans. The article says the oppressive policy of 'Amr ibn al-Nu'man pushed the Jewish tribes of [[group/banu-qurayza|Qurayza]] and [[group/banu-nadir|al-Nadir]] into alliance with two clans that had been driven out, which let them fight back. The nomadic Muzayna helped them, and the other Aws clans joined, except Haritha, which had been driven from its lands by 'Abd al-Ashhal. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 820} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1248}

The battle that followed, [[event/yawm-buath|Bu'ath]], which the article on al-Khazraj dates to about 617, went in favour of the Aws and their allies. Both leaders, Hudayr and 'Amr ibn al-Nu'man, were killed. No peace was made, but there was no further fighting on a large scale. Two important Khazrajis, 'Abd Allah ibn Ubayy of Banu al-Hubla and 'Amr ibn al-Jamuh of Salima, stayed neutral. The article adds that 'Abd Allah ibn Ubayy had hoped before Muhammad's coming to become "king" of Medina. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 820} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1248}

## History
The Aws and the Khazraj entered two pledges at [[event/pledges-of-aqaba|al-Aqaba]], by which Yathrib accepted to host the Prophet and the Muhajirun and to protect them {cite:ibn-ishaq-guillaume}. After the Hijra, the Ansar became the mainstay of the people: the men of the market, the orchard and the sword, at [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]], [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]], the Trench and after {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

According to the Encyclopaedia, Muhammad's first effective contacts with Medina came at the pilgrimage of 620 with six men of the Khazraj. Because he also aimed at reconciling the two tribes, he seems to have insisted that the Aws be represented in the negotiations. In the traditional accounts of the first and second pledges at al-Aqaba, about a sixth of those who pledged themselves were Aws. The article suggests that Medina had suffered so much from the feuds that its ready acceptance of Muhammad's claims was partly due to a hope that he would restore and keep peace. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 538}

The article admits much obscurity in the details, but concludes that most of the people of Medina, apart from the Jews, entered the agreement with him. The chief exceptions were four Aws clans, [[group/banu-khatma|Khatma]], Wa'il, Wakif and Umayya ibn Zayd, and part of a fifth, 'Amr ibn 'Awf, all with close ties to the Jews. The article distinguishes these non-Muslims from the hypocrites, the [[concept/munafiq|munafiqun]], who had joined the agreement and later disapproved of Muhammad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 538} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 820} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 821}

## Numbers and leaders
The article gives a table of the men of each clan present at the first pledge, the second pledge and Badr. Its figures could not be read reliably in the scanned text and are not reproduced here. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 538}

From the table the author draws that clans such as al-Najjar and Salima, which had many members but had produced no great war leaders, took a leading part in approaching Muhammad. The two chief men of Medina at the time, Sa'd ibn Mu'adh and 'Abd Allah ibn Ubayy, were not at al-Aqaba, and their clans, 'Abd al-Ashhal and Banu al-Hubla, appear to have been little represented. Yet these clans made up three quarters of the Muslim force at Badr. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 538}

The Khazraj, the article on that tribe says, seem to have been more numerous and more enthusiastic Muslims than the Aws: ten men at the first pledge against two, sixty-two at the second against eleven, nine chiefs against three, 175 men at Badr against 63, and 228 women named by Ibn Sa'd against 121. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1248}

Of the leaders, the article judges that [[person/sad-ibn-muadh|Sa'd ibn Mu'adh]], chief of 'Abd al-Ashhal, was the most zealous in the cause of Islam. His conversion was, in the article on al-Aws, a decisive event in the growth of Islam in Medina, and from Badr until his death in 5/627 he was the leading Muslim of Banu Kayla. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 538} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 820} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 821} 'Abd Allah ibn Ubayy, [[person/usayd-ibn-hudayr|Usayd ibn Hudayr]], a rival of Sa'd for the chieftaincy of 'Abd al-Ashhal, and [[person/sad-ibn-ubada|Sa'd ibn 'Ubada]] were absent from Badr. At least until the siege of Medina in 5/627, the article says, 'Abd Allah ibn Ubayy worked to prevent the growth of Muhammad's power, while the others threw in their lot with him after Badr. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 538}

At the meeting about the accusation against [[person/aisha|'A'isha]], called in the article the affair of the lie, it was clear that the first man among the Khazraj was now Sa'd ibn 'Ubada. After the death of Sa'd ibn Mu'adh shortly afterwards, he was recognised as leader of the Ansar as a whole. The article adds that the Ansar remained one of the main foundations of Muhammad's power. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 538}

## Becoming one community
According to the article, the old feuds were slowly forgotten during Muhammad's years in Medina and the Ansar came to feel themselves a unity, especially in contrast to the Muhajirun, with whom they rarely intermarried. The split between Aws and Khazraj was of occasional importance as late as the meeting after Muhammad's death at which Abu Bakr was made caliph, but nothing is heard of it afterwards. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 538} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 539} The article on al-Aws says the same of the enmity between the two tribes. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 821}

At the [[event/saqifa|Saqifa of Banu Sa'ida]] the question of authority was raised, and it was settled for Quraysh under [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] with the agreement of the Ansar. Sunnis see this event as a swift settlement; the Prophet's family and their followers see it as a betrayal of the promised 'Alid succession {cite:watt-1956-medina}.

## Later history
After the wars of conquest, the article says, the Ansar declined in influence despite their honourable place in the new Islamic nobility. They mostly opposed [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|'Uthman]] and supported [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|'Ali]]. Later they formed what the author calls a pious opposition to the Umayyads and took the side of the Abbasids. Before the Abbasids came to power, however, the Ansar had largely merged with members of Quraysh and other tribes who had settled in Medina. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 539}

The article on al-Khazraj adds that under the Umayyads the Khazrajis mostly supported the ruling dynasty, though al-Nu'man ibn Bashir, governor of Homs, sided with 'Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr. It names Khazraji poets: Hassan ibn Thabit of al-Najjar, Ka'b ibn Malik of Salima and 'Abd Allah ibn Rawaha of al-Harith, whose descendants were later found in Spain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1248}

## Legacy
For Sunnis the Ansar are a lasting support, of whom it is said: "none loves them but a believer". In the Shia account they are the party to the dispute that won. Both sides agree that Medina could not have been without them {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.
