## Overview
The first Muslim community, from the oppressed believers of Mecca to the society of Medina, is the nucleus from which "the Muslims" became a nation with borders and sovereignty.

## History
An early consensus formed around precedence. In the Sunni tradition the foremost were the people of Mecca first, then [[group/muhajirun|the Muhajirun]], then [[group/ansar|the Ansar]]. In the Meccan decade the community was an oppressed minority worshipping in secret. Some of it fled [[event/first-migration-to-abyssinia|to Abyssinia]], and the first of them to be martyred was [[person/sumayya-bint-khayyat|Sumayya]], with her family {cite:ibn-ishaq-guillaume}.

In Medina the community took on a political existence. Whoever wished could enter it through faith and emigration. The Charter organised it as a constitution alongside the independent tribes, and it held firm at [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]], [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]] and the [[event/battle-of-the-trench|Trench]] {cite:watt-1956-medina}.

## Legacy
The very concept of "the Companions", meaning whoever saw the Prophet as a believer and died in that state, gave rise to all the sciences of narrators. It made their collective uprightness the cornerstone of Sunni hadith doctrine. Historians, for their part, read it as a term that later transmission built, with a function in the chain of narration {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

## The foremost in al-Raheeq al-Makhtum (pp. 75–76)
The book names the group that answered the Prophet in [[event/al-dawa-al-sirriyya|the secret call]] as the foremost: [[person/khadija|Khadija]], [[person/zayd-ibn-haritha|Zayd ibn Haritha]], [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] and [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] on the first day. Then came those who embraced Islam through Abu Bakr's call: [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]], [[person/zubayr-ibn-al-awwam|al-Zubayr]], [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-awf|Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf]], [[person/saad-ibn-abi-waqqas|Sa'd]] and [[person/talha-ibn-ubaydullah|Talha]]. These eight are the first vanguard and the forerunners of Islam {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.75} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.76} {cite:rahma-lil-alamin 1/50}. Then came Bilal, Abu Ubayda, Abu Salama, al-Arqam, Uthman ibn Maz'un and his two brothers, Ubayda ibn al-Harith, Sa'id ibn Zayd, Fatima bint al-Khattab, Khabbab, Ibn Mas'ud, and others from all the clans of Quraysh. Ibn Hisham counted them at more than forty, though the author questions counting some of them among the foremost {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.76} {cite:ibn-hisham-sira 1/245-262}. Within three years they had formed a body based on brotherhood, mutual cooperation and conveying the message {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.77}.

## For Sprouts
The first Muslims were a small group who believed the Prophet in Mecca while they were weak, and they bore harm with patience. Some, like Sumayya, were hurt for their faith and never gave it up, and some emigrated to Abyssinia and then to Medina. From this small group a great nation began.

## For Explorers
The first Muslims are the community that believed the Prophet in the early years of the call in Mecca: women and men, free and enslaved, from different clans. They were a vulnerable minority worshipping in secret, and they endured mockery, boycott and hard treatment; Sumayya bint Khayyat was their first martyr. Some emigrated to Abyssinia twice, and then they emigrated to Medina, where they became the Muhajirun and, with the Ansar, one community. In Sunni ordering the people of precedence are named first: the Meccans, then the Muhajirun, then the Ansar. From this small nucleus grew the nation that later spread through Arabia and beyond.

## For Scholars
The early Muslims are the first community to answer the call in Mecca, the nucleus from which 'the Muslims' became a nation with borders and sovereignty. It included free and enslaved, women and men, nobles and poor, from Quraysh clans and beyond; the best-known early names include Abu Bakr, Khadija, Ali, Zayd, Bilal, Ammar and Sumayya. In the Meccan decade the community was a vulnerable minority worshipping secretly in the house of al-Arqam, and the sira books record Quraysh's harm against them up to killing — Sumayya and her family were the first martyrs. Some emigrated to Abyssinia twice, and then came the migration to Medina after the two pledges of Aqaba, which turned the group into a society of Muhajirun and Ansar. In the Sunni ordering precedence ranks the Meccans first, then the Muhajirun, then the Ansar, and their generations fill the biographical dictionaries. For scholars, the community reads as an emergent religious movement that built loyalty across clan lines — which explains its rapid growth after Medina.
