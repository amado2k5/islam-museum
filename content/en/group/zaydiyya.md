## Overview
The Zaydiyya, as Ibn al-Nadim defines them, held to the imamate of Zayd ibn Ali. After him they held the imamate to be among the descendants of Fatima, whoever he might be, once the conditions of the imamate were fully met in him. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.253}

## The people of hadith
Ibn al-Nadim says that most of the hadith scholars followed this school, and gives as examples Sufyan ibn Uyayna, Sufyan al-Thawri, Salih ibn Hayy and his son. He reports from him that most of the scholars of hadith were Zaydi, and likewise some of the jurist-traditionists and the leading traditionists. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.253}

## In The Oxford Dictionary of Islam
The Zaydis are described by The Oxford Dictionary of Islam as a moderate wing of Shiism that broke with the other Shii groups over the succession, choosing Zayd ibn Ali, grandson of Husayn, as fifth imam on account of his activism against the Umayyads. A first Zaydi state arose in Iran's north in 864 and stood until 1126; a more durable one in northern Yemen, from 893, lasted to 1962. The entry calls them the Shii faction nearest the Sunnis: keeping a law school of their own, recognizing the rule of Abu Bakr and Umar and partly that of Uthman, and refusing the notion of a supernaturally gifted imam — asking only descent from Ali and Fatimah, bodily soundness and personal piety, plus the capacity to bear the sword in attack or defense, which bars child and hidden imams. In moral teaching, it adds, they incline to strictness, and they look unfavorably on Sufism. {cite:oxford-dictionary-of-islam e2567}
