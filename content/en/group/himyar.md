## Summary
Himyar is one of the two great tribes of [[group/qahtan|Qahtan]], alongside [[group/kahlan|Kahlan]]. Its best-known branches are Zayd al-Jumhur, [[group/qudaa|Qudaa]] and al-Sakasik {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.16} {cite:muhadarat-tarikh-al-umam-al-islamiyya 1/11-13}. It overcame the [[polity/kingdom-of-saba|kingdom of Saba']] around 115 BCE and became the kings of [[place/yemen|Yemen]] until the Abyssinian and Persian occupations {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.24}.

## Himyar's overcoming of Saba'
In the period the author dates from 115 BCE to 300 CE, the tribe of Himyar overcame the kingdom of Saba' and made the town of Raydan its capital instead of [[place/marib|Ma'rib]]. Raydan was later named after Zafar, and its ruins lie on Mount Mudawwar near Yarim {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.24} {cite:tafhim-al-quran 4/195-198}. In this period their decline began, as their trade largely failed: first because the Nabataeans controlled the north of the Hijaz, then because the Romans dominated the sea trade routes after their influence over Egypt, Syria and the north of the Hijaz, and then because the tribes competed among themselves {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.24}.

## Himyar's survival and Kahlan's departure
The author holds that the rivalry between the clans of Kahlan and the clans of Himyar led to Kahlan's departure, and he cites Himyar's survival in Yemen as evidence {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.17}. In the period of unrest after 300 CE, the Abyssinians exploited the rivalry between [[group/banu-hamdan|Hamdan]] and Himyar, and occupied Yemen for the first time in 340 CE {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.24}.

## The last kings of Himyar
Judaism entered Yemen through [[person/tuban-asad-abu-karib|Tubba' As'ad Abu Karib]], who went out to fight at Yathrib, embraced Judaism there, and brought two rabbis of Banu Qurayza back to Yemen {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.40} {cite:tafhim-al-quran 6/297-298}. When his son [[person/dhu-nuwas|Yusuf Dhu Nuwas]] ruled Yemen after him, he attacked the [[group/christians-of-najran|Christians of Najran]], and that led to [[event/massacre-of-najran|the story of the Pit (al-Ukhdud)]] in 523 CE {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.24} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.40}. After the second Abyssinian occupation, the Yemenis sought help from the Persians and gained independence in 575 CE under the leadership of Ma'd Yakrib ibn [[person/sayf-ibn-dhi-yazan|Sayf Dhi Yazan]] al-Himyari. The Abyssinians he had kept with him then killed him, and with his death the kingship ended in the house of Dhi Yazan {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.25}. Details are in [[event/persian-rule-of-yemen|the liberation of Yemen and Persian rule]].

## Not covered here
The author notes that the sources differ considerably over the dating of these events, and that some writers counted these details among the tales of the ancients {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.25}. The academic history of the Himyarite state from inscriptions has not yet been added.

## For Explorers
Himyar, one of Qahtan's two great tribes alongside Kahlan, its best-known clans Zayd al-Jumhur, Quda'a and the Sakasik. It overcame the kingdom of Saba around 115 BCE, becoming Yemen's kings until the Abyssinian and Persian occupations, and took Raydan — later called Zafar — for a capital in place of Ma'rib. Their decline began as their trade failed: Nabataean control of northern Hijaz, then Roman grip on the sea routes, then inter-tribal rivalry. Judaism entered Yemen through Tubban As'ad Abu Karib, and when his son Dhu Nuwas ruled he attacked the Christians of Najran — the Trench story of 523 CE. The Yemenis sought Persian help and won independence in 575 CE under Sayf Dhi Yazan the Himyarite, whom the Abyssinians he kept with him then assassinated, ending Dhu Yazan's house.

In the period the author reckons from 115 BCE to 300 CE it overcame the kingdom of Saba, taking Raydan — later named Zafar, its ruins on Mount Mudawwar near Yarim — for a capital in place of Ma'rib.
