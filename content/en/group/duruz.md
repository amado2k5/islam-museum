## Overview
The Druze are a closed community that has kept to itself, so people know little about its affairs, according to Hasan Ibrahim Hasan. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim p.251-253}

Their preachers are of ranks. The preachers and the wise hold the inner secrets of the doctrine. The Ajawid are the keepers of the outer secrets, because of their relations with others. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim p.251-253}

The author says that they called for the deification of al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah in the year 408 AH. He says that they helped the Muslims against the Crusaders, and took military positions at the siege of the fortress of Shaqif. He says that they helped Hulagu during his raid on Syria, and that they had a role in the wars of Mount Lebanon up to the time of the Mandate. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim p.251-253}

## In The Oxford Dictionary of Islam
The Oxford Dictionary of Islam calls the Druze a millenarian branch of Ismaili Shiism. It says the founder was Hamzah ibn Ali, a missionary from Persia working in Cairo in the 900s, and that the community takes its name from Muhammad al-Darazi, a missionary of the 1000s. In its account the Druze are held to be a religion of their own and not an Islamic sect; they accept the imamate of al-Hakim ibn Amr Allah, the Fatimid caliph, and describe themselves as al-Muwahhidun, the unitarians, because they insist so strongly on monotheism. The Dictionary reports that preaching stopped in 1043 and no later converts were admitted, that the creed is mostly hidden from non-members, that it bans polygyny, concubinage and temporary marriage, and that it teaches the transmigration of souls. It places the biggest communities in Lebanon, Syria and Israel and puts the world total just above one million. {cite:oxford-dictionary-of-islam e560} This is the Dictionary's description; the record above rests on one author writing from outside the community.
