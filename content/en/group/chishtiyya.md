## Overview
The Chishti order is among the greatest Sufi orders of South Asia; traced to Chisht in Khurasan, it spread through India until it became among its most widespread orders {cite:trimingham-sufi-orders-1998}.

## In the sources
Academic study describes the Chishtiyya as the leading Indian Sufi order, extending through the sultanate and Mughal eras {cite:trimingham-sufi-orders-1998}.

## In The Oxford Dictionary of Islam
The Oxford Dictionary of Islam calls the Chishti order among the most popular Sufi brotherhoods in South Asia. It says Muin al-Din Chishti, who died in 1236, founded it in thirteenth-century India, from where it spread across what are now India, Pakistan and Bangladesh. Its members present themselves as embracing poverty and shunning contact with worldly rulers. Its golden age, in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, was marked by a leadership that cared more for a mysticism of practice and feeling, and for the intimate tie between master and follower, than for academic or intellectual refinement. Shah Wali Allah of Delhi revived it around the turn of the eighteenth century, declaring it excellent at adapting early Muslim preaching to present conditions. Under British rule in the nineteenth century it was often linked to unsuccessful efforts at reforming institutions of Islam. Its founder's shrine is the most important Muslim pilgrimage centre in India, it says. The Chishtis developed qawwali, a group song form of Hindustani light classical music that sets mystical verse in Persian, Hindi and Urdu and is sung at shrines in Sufi gatherings to rouse devotional feeling and ecstasy. The brotherhood has a special devotion to Ali ibn Abi Talib, whom it counts as the first successor of Muhammad in its chain of initiation. {cite:oxford-dictionary-of-islam e431}
