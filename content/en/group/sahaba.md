## Overview
The Companions, in Arabic the sahaba, singular sahabi, are the persons who belonged to the circle of the Prophet Muhammad. The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls them in many respects key figures in the early history of Islam. The article gives other plural forms as well, ashab, sahb and suhban. In the critical study of transmitters, the science of men or ilm al-rijal, which forms a section of hadith literature, the Companions are treated as reliable transmitters of the Prophet's statements, deeds and instructions. The article adds that their own deeds and statements are also held worthy of imitation, particularly in the history of Islamic rites. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 892}

## Defining a Companion
The article places the first attempts to define the Companions as a distinct group, and to fix the criteria for the title, probably at the start of the second century of the Hijra, the eighth century CE. At the opening of the chapter on the merits of the Prophet's Companions in his Sahih, [[person/al-bukhari|al-Bukhari]] gives a short definition that, in the article's words, needs further interpretation: the person is a believing Muslim who accompanied the Prophet. The OCR of the rest of the sentence is cut off, so it is not reported here. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 893}

The article says it has always remained a point of discussion whether the simple fact of having seen the Prophet is enough. In general, it reports, taking part in a number of the Prophet's campaigns, having reached adulthood, and being able to transmit directly from him were the basic prerequisites. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 893}

## Classes and the order of merit
According to a passage in Ibn al-Athir, the division of the Companions into classes was already common by the time of [[person/al-waqidi|al-Waqidi]] at the latest. It was a ranking by precedence in Islam, in which the moment of conversion mattered above all. [[person/muhammad-ibn-sad|Ibn Sa'd]] sets that moment in a defined historical context: some individuals accepted Islam before the Prophet entered the house of [[person/al-arqam-ibn-abi-al-arqam|al-Arqam]] near Safa. Those who came early are also called al-sabikun, the foremost, a word the article notes in the Qur'an at sura IX, verse 100, and sura LVI, verse 10. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 893}

Qur'anic commentary, the article says, reads the foremost in the light of the events of Muhammad's mission. It counts among them Muslims who prayed toward both Jerusalem and Mecca, who emigrated with Muhammad to [[place/medina|Medina]], and who took part in the battle of [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]] and the treaty of [[event/treaty-of-hudaybiyya|al-Hudaybiya]]. The participants in the treaty are also called the people of the tree, those who gave the pledge of allegiance under the tree in the oasis of al-Hudaybiya, the [[event/bayat-al-ridwan|bay'at al-ridwan]]. Early Meccan Muslims are further called the first Emigrants, [[group/muhajirun|al-muhajirun]], meaning those who joined the Prophet by March 628, at al-Hudaybiya at the latest, or by another reading by January 624, the date of the [[event/change-of-qibla|change of the qibla]]. Members of Arab tribes who settled in Medina after converting, and gave up returning to their tribes, are also called muhajirun. The article notes that later generations of classifiers did not list them among the Emigrants from [[place/mecca|Mecca]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 893}

The same ranking cannot be made for the Medinan Helpers, [[group/ansar|al-ansar]]. Only the representatives of the two main tribes of Yathrib, the [[group/al-aws|Aws]] and the [[group/al-khazraj|Khazraj]], who joined the secret meetings with the Prophet at [[place/al-aqaba|al-Aqaba]] before the Hijra, are named in this connection. These meetings negotiated guarantees of protection for him and for the Meccan emigrants. The article adds that the Helpers did not take part in Muhammad's campaigns before Badr. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 893}

Another group of early Muslims, converted during the Prophet's stay in Mecca, are the mustad'afun, a term W. M. Watt renders as those considered weak. The article describes them as socially and economically weak and destitute Meccans, whom their clans prevented from joining the Hijra. By Qur'anic commentary, sura IV, verse 75, refers to this group. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 893}

## Rank, endowments and the early lists
The article reports that the date of conversion and the share in the Prophet's campaigns carried weight for the economic and social standing of the Companions and of their descendants. It says both seem to have been considered when the lists for state endowments were drawn up under [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar ibn al-Khattab]]. Early lists in the literature on the campaigns, the maghazi, include a papyrus fragment of the second century of the Hijra with the names of the fighters at Badr, and the article takes them as evidence of historians' keen interest in classification. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 893}

Ibn Sa'd built his Great Book of Classes, the Tabaqat, on this principle and on older lists from the historians. In his first class he places the people of precedence: the Meccan Emigrants and Helpers who fought at Badr, the twelve leaders, nuqaba, at al-Aqaba, and some Muslims whose presence at Badr cannot be proved beyond doubt. His second class holds the early converts among the Emigrants who went to [[place/abyssinia|Abyssinia]], and the fighters at [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]]. A third class, known only through allusions in [[person/ibn-hajar-al-asqalani|Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani]], holds the participants in the battle of the Ditch, [[event/battle-of-the-trench|al-Khandaq]]. A further class gathers those who converted before the [[event/conquest-of-mecca|conquest of Mecca]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 893}

## Virtues and the Ten
The article says the good qualities and virtues of the Companions, measured by their early service to Islam, the manaqib and fada'il, were a favourite theme of the oldest historians in the early eighth century CE. Ninth-century collections of hadith arranged by subject, the musannafat, give the theme a special book. Separate collections were also devoted to the ten Companions to whom, it is said, the Prophet promised paradise, the mubashshirun. The article lists them as the four rightly guided caliphs, [[person/talha-ibn-ubaydullah|Talha]], [[person/zubayr-ibn-al-awwam|al-Zubayr]], [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-awf|Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf]], [[person/saad-ibn-abi-waqqas|Sa'd ibn Malik]], [[person/said-ibn-zayd|Said ibn Zayd]] and [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 893}

## Sunni understanding of their standing
In Sunni Islam, the article reports, the Companions gradually took on charismatic features through the storytelling of the historians. As the article gives the Sunni position, abuse of the Companions, [[concept/sabb-al-sahaba|sabb al-sahaba]], is considered a sin, and on many interpretations is even to be punished by death. It is also a duty to pronounce the formula of pleasure with God whenever a Companion is named. The article states this as Sunni practice and attributes it to Sunni Islam, not to the museum. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 893}

## Hadith criticism and other views
The article contrasts two bodies of writing. Historiography, it says, apart from some controversial representations among Islamic sects, depicts the Companions' contribution to early Islamic history almost without criticism. Hadith literature and its criticism in the late second and early third centuries of the Hijra apply other standards. They do not call into question the credibility of individual Companions as transmitters of the Prophet's statements and as prime witnesses of his deeds, the article says, but they still prefer a chain of transmission, an isnad, with a particular Companion as prime witness over another. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 894}

The article does not describe the controversial sectarian representations in detail. Its bibliography lists a study by E. Kohlberg of Imami Shi'i views on the Companions, and this page does not summarise it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 894}

## Local lists and dictionaries of Companions
Historical writing, local history above all, records in detail what is transmitted about the Companions' stay in provincial towns. About a third of the Conquest of Egypt, [[work/futuh-misr|Futuh Misr]], by the Egyptian author [[person/ibn-abd-al-hakam|Ibn Abd al-Hakam]], who died in 257/871, consists of lists of Companions who stayed in Egypt and whose traditions circulated there. The article names similar works for Homs, Wasit, al-Raqqa and Darayya, and says fragments of these and of lost works survive in Ibn Hajar's al-Isaba and in the Istiab of the Cordoban [[person/ibn-abd-al-barr|Ibn Abd al-Barr]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 894}

Elsewhere in the same volume, a passage lists the dictionaries devoted only to Companions. It names [[work/al-istiab|al-Istiab]] with 4,225 entries, Ibn al-Athir's [[work/usd-al-ghaba|Usd al-ghaba]] with 7,703, al-Dhahabi's Tajrid with 8,859, and Ibn Hajar's [[work/al-isaba-fi-tamyiz-al-sahaba|al-Isaba fi tamyiz al-sahaba]] with 12,290. It adds that in the Isaba not all persons listed are Companions in the technical sense, since Ibn Hajar placed scores of borderline cases in his second, third and fourth divisions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 559}
