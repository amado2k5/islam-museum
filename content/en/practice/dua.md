## Overview
Du'a is supplicatory speech expressing request with humility — also called su'al. Al-Khattabi: its essence is the servant's invoking his Lord's care and drawing on His aid, the display of neediness and disavowal of power and strength — the mark of servanthood — together with praise of God and the ascription of generosity to Him. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.8102}

## Its senses in the Quran
It occurs as seeking rescue, as worship, as calling out, and as petitioning God — the sense here, as in "when My servants ask you of Me — I am near; I answer the call of the caller when he calls" and "your Lord said: call on Me, I will answer you." {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.8102} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.8103}

## Istighfar and dhikr
Every istighfar is du'a, not every du'a istighfar; dhikr in its widest sense includes du'a and more. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.8103}

## Ruling
Al-Nawawi: the chosen doctrine of the jurists, hadith scholars and the mass of the learned is that du'a is recommended; it can be obligatory, like the supplication inside al-Fatiha in prayer and the janaza supplication. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.8103}

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 4)

The Sheikh says that the prayer of a transgressor may be answered before that of an upright person for one of two reasons: either God wants to draw him towards prayer, or He does not want him to keep standing at His door {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:23:30}. He mentions a saying attributed to God to the effect of hastening the need of a servant because He dislikes hearing his voice (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:23:30}. The Sheikh describes mothers whose child falls ill and whose illness worsens, who say, Lord, take what he has and give it to us, and he counts this as emotion in its right place, and then asks God for well-being {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:51:00}.

The Sheikh advises that whoever goes to the doctor, himself or his child, make three supplications: that God guide the doctor to a sound diagnosis, since an unsound diagnosis can lead the patient to harm, and that He guide him to prescribe the suitable medicine {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:51:30}. The third supplication is that God not remove the secret of healing from that medicine, and the Sheikh says that God has created no disease without creating a cure for it, except old age {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:52:00}.

## For Sprouts
Du'a is asking God with our hearts: we raise our hands, ask Him for what we need, thank Him and ask His forgiveness. A Muslim may make du'a at any time and place, loves to pray for parents and Muslims, and children learn short easy supplications from the Sunna.

## For Explorers
Du'a in Islam is turning to God in petition and praise: asking Him for religious and worldly needs, seeking forgiveness and giving thanks. The hadith books record that du'a is 'the essence of worship', and that God loves a servant's insistence in du'a without boredom or haste. It has manners: facing the qibla, raising the hands, beginning with praise and blessing on the Prophet, insistence with good opinion of God, and times when answer is hoped — the last third of the night, between the adhan and iqama, Friday, travel and fasting, and the worshipper's prostration. Muslims have learned transmitted supplications from the Qur'an and Sunna for morning, evening, eating and travel.

## For Scholars
Du'a, in the scholars' view, is the heart of inward worship: the Muslim turning to God in petition, humility and praise, divided in fiqh into du'a of worship, du'a of request and du'a of glorification. The hadith books record that du'a is 'the essence of worship' and 'worship' itself, that God loves a servant's insistence without boredom or haste, and the texts warn against praying for sin or severing kinship. Its transmitted manners: facing the qibla, raising the hands, beginning with praise of God and blessing on the Prophet, insistence with good opinion and certainty, and times and places where answer is hoped — the last third of the night, between adhan and iqama, Friday, fasting and breaking fast, travel, prostration, and days like Arafa. The jurists wove the Prophetic supplications into the prayers and daily remembrances, and the adab literature urges taking the transmitted and comprehensive supplications and leaving affectation. Scholars gathered du'as in separate works, and congregational qunut in mosques is among the matters jurists disagreed over.
