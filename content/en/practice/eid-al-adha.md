## Overview
The day of adha is the tenth of Dhu'l-Hijja, one of the Muslims' two Eids, named for the udhiyya slaughtered on it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.11984}

## Names and place in the year
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, gives the festival of the tenth of Dhu'l-Hijja several names: the sacrificial feast, Eid al-Adha, also called Eid al-Qurban or Eid al-Nahr, and "the major festival". In India it is known as Bakar Eid, and in Turkey as Büyük Bayram or Kurban Bayramı. It falls on the day on which the pilgrims sacrifice in the valley of [[place/mina|Mina]] during the [[practice/hajj|Hajj]], and it is the first of the three days of tashriq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039}

The article on the word Eid adds that Arab lexicographers derive the term from a root meaning "returning", while the author regards it as one of the many Aramaic loanwords in the vocabulary of religion. The Muslim year has two canonical festivals, this one on the tenth of Dhu'l-Hijja and [[practice/eid-al-fitr|Eid al-Fitr]] on the first of Shawwal. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039}

## Its rulings
The Eid prayer is emphatic sunna for the Malikis and Shafi'is, obligatory on the operative Hanafi view, and a communal obligation for the Hanbalis; takbir is prescribed on the way, within the prayer, and after prayers during the tashriq days. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.11984} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.11985}

## The sacrifice
Jurists agree the udhiyya is prescribed on it and differ on its ruling: sunna for the majority, obligatory for the Hanafis. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.11985}

The article describes the old Arab custom of sacrificing at Mina on this day as adopted by Islam, for all Muslims and not only for pilgrims, as a sunna. It becomes a necessary duty only through a vow. It also states that the sunna is obligatory on the community collectively, as a duty that binds every free Muslim able to afford a victim. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039}

As the article gives it, a sheep is offered for one person, while a camel or a head of cattle serves one to ten persons. The animals must be of a fixed age and free of certain defects, such as the loss of an eye or lameness. The period of the sacrifice opens with the festival prayer and closes at sunset on the third of the tashriq days. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039}

## Practices recommended to the sacrificer
The article lists five practices recommended to the person offering the sacrifice: saying the basmala, invoking blessing on the Prophet, turning towards the qibla, pronouncing the takbir three times before and after the basmala, and asking that the sacrifice be accepted. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039}

On the use of the meat, the article distinguishes two cases. If the sacrifice is made because of a vow, the one who vowed may eat none of it and gives it all away for pious purposes. If it is a freewill offering, as is usually the case, the sacrificer keeps a portion, given as a third, and gives the rest away. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039}

## The day's practices
Keeping its night alive in worship and bathing are recommended, as the Prophet bathed on the fitr and adha days. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.11985}

## The festival prayer and customs
The article on Eid describes the festival prayer shared by both festivals as a sunna of the whole community. It consists of two rak'as with more takbirs than the ordinary prayer, and is followed by a sermon in two parts. It has no [[practice/adhan|adhan]] and no [[concept/iqama|iqama]], and the only call to it is a short summons to the prayer. It is to be held in the open air at the musalla, though mosques are now often preferred, and its time runs from sunrise to the sun's zenith. The article says it preserves older forms than the daily or Friday prayer and resembles the prayers for drought and eclipse in style. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039}

Both festivals last in practice three or four days. People put on new or their best clothes, visit one another, congratulate one another and exchange gifts, and they visit cemeteries, where some stay for hours. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039}

The article judges that these popular customs are more common at Eid al-Fitr, which it says is celebrated with much more joy because the hardships of Ramadan are over, so that today the minor festival has in practice become more important than the major one. This is the author's assessment of practice and is not a ruling. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039}

## Other notes in the article
The article records that among the Druze the festival is now celebrated in memory of the offering of Cain and Abel. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039} For the details of the rulings, it refers readers to the chapter on the udhiyya in the books of jurisprudence. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1040}

## The word qurban
The separate article on qurban says the word goes back to the Hebrew korban, perhaps through Aramaic. It occurs three times in the Qur'an, where it means sacrifice in two places and, in a third, is read by the commentators as "mediators", a sense linked to the root for nearness. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 474} The term then came to be used in Muslim ritual for the killing of an animal on the tenth of Dhu'l-Hijja, so that the whole celebration of that day and the tashriq days is called Eid al-Qurban, and Kurban Bayrami in Turkish-speaking countries. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 474} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 475}
