## Overview
The Arabic word wudu means cleansing. In law it names the minor ablution, the purification from a minor impurity, which is required before certain religious acts, worship above all. The major ablution is a separate matter, treated under ghusl. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 228}

The jurists count [[concept/tahara|purification]], the state of being pure, among the five cultic acts that make up the foundations of Islam, beside worship, almsgiving, fasting and pilgrimage. The article reads the huge body of Prophetic traditions on the subject, the long treatment in the law books and the endless debates over small details as signs of how much weight the topic carries for Muslims. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 228}

## Summary
Wudu — from wada'a, "beauty and cleanliness" — is, in juristic usage, the use of pure water on the four limbs (face, hands, head, feet) in the prescribed manner; it lifts the minor ritual impurity as ghusl lifts the major one. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17364} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17365}

## Definitions across the schools
The Hanafis define it as washing and wiping specific limbs; the Malikis as a water purification attached to the four limbs in a specified way; the Shafi'is as specific acts opened by intention; the Hanbalis as using pure water on the four limbs in ordered succession with the other obligations. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17364}

## An ancient rite
The majority of jurists held that wudu belongs to the earlier revealed laws and is not exclusive to this community, citing its attribution to earlier prophets; a minority counted it among this community's distinguishing marks, citing the hadith of ghurra and tahjil — "you will come to me radiant with the traces of wudu". {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17365} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17366}

## Its place beside prayer
Jurists agreed that wudu was ordained in Mecca together with prayer, and that no prayer was ever transmitted without it — "no prayer is accepted from one in a state of hadath until he performs wudu". It was obligatory for every prayer in early Islam until, as they relate, that obligation was lifted at the Battle of the Trench except in the case of hadath, so that one wudu may now serve many prayers while remaining recommended. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17366} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17367}

## Wudu, tayammum and ghusl
Wudu and tayammum both lift ritual impurity, but wudu is a water rite for minor hadath while tayammum serves for both minor and major hadath using clean earth; the Lawgiver even called tayammum "wudu" since it stands in its place — "clean earth is the Muslim's wudu". {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17366} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17367}

## The Qur'anic basis and the schools
The article notes that the duty to purify oneself before worship already existed in Jewish ritual, and that it is set out in some detail in [[passage/quran-5-6|Qur'an 5:6]], a verse regarded as Medinan, like 4:43. From this the scholars reached a consensus that worship performed without purity is invalid. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 228}

The details gave rise to many disputes, first between Sunni and [[group/shia|Shi'i]] jurists, and second between the legal schools inside Sunnism, in particular between the [[group/hanafi-madhhab|Hanafis]] and the [[group/shafii-madhhab|Shafi'is]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 228}

## When wudu is needed
Wudu is required when a person is in a state of minor impurity, called [[concept/hadath-impurity|hadath]], and a liquid fit for purification is at hand. If none is, dry ablution, [[practice/tayammum|tayammum]], is allowed. The article records that whether a minor impurity also invalidates the circumambulation of the Kaaba, [[practice/tawaf|tawaf]], during the pilgrimage is a matter of dispute. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 228}

The causes of minor impurity are likewise disputed. According to the Shafi'is there are five: whatever comes out of the two natural orifices, with the urine of suckling infants excepted; sleep, with discussion about a light sleep; loss of reason; touching a person of the other sex; and touching the genitals or anus under certain conditions. The [[group/hanbali-madhhab|Hanbalis]] add apostasy and eating camel meat, and hold that contact with a person of the other sex counts only when it carries a carnal intention. The Hanafis keep a shorter list, and also hold that a fit of uncontrollable laughter during worship breaks purity, so that the person must renew the ablution and begin the worship again. The article refers to Maliki doctrine only through a citation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 228}

## Water and intention
The article calls rainwater, on the basis of Qur'an 5:11 and 25:48, the purifying element above all others. Even here the jurists argued about what quality of water purifies, how pure water is told from impure, and which other liquids may be treated like water. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 228}

On the way of performing wudu, the intention to purify oneself, [[concept/niyya|niyya]], has long been debated between the Hanafis and the other schools. The others require an express intention, or the ablution is invalid, citing the saying that actions are valid only according to intention. The Hanafis hold that among purifications only those made with a solid substance, as in tayammum, need it. The article adds that in Sunnism the question grew so important that it works as one of the signs of belonging to a given school. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 228}

## The acts of wudu
The acts are known from Qur'an 5:6 and from many Prophetic traditions, some of them contradictory, and are divided into obligatory and recommended ones. The article says the schools differ so much here that it gives no detail. It observes that most differences come from reading the vocabulary of purification in different ways, for example what is meant by rubbing the head and where the head is taken to end, and that the jurists argue these points from the language itself. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 228}

As an example of one school's list, the article gives the Shafi'i [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|Abu Hamid al-Ghazali]], who in his [[work/al-wajiz-fi-fiqh-al-shafii|al-Wajiz]] names six necessary acts: the intention, washing the face, washing the hands, rubbing the head, washing the feet, and keeping the order of these steps. He then lists eighteen recommended ones, such as rinsing the mouth and washing the ears. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 228} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 229}

## The toothstick, footwear and doubt
Cleaning the teeth with an arak twig, the [[practice/miswak|siwak]], is treated apart in the law books, probably because of a saying of the Prophet that he would have ordered it before every act of worship had he not feared burdening his community. The jurists take it as strongly recommended but not obligatory. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 229}

The article names one topic that divides Sunnis from Shi'is: whether rubbing one's footwear may replace washing the feet. Sunnis accept it under conditions, and the Shi'is reject it categorically. For this the article refers readers to [[practice/mash-ala-al-khuffayn|wiping over the leather socks]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 229}

To decide a person's own state, the jurists often invoke the principle that doubt does not undo certainty. Someone unsure whether an impurity touched him may regard himself as pure. If it is his state of purity that is in doubt, he is treated as being in a state of impurity and should perform wudu, as the article reports from al-Shirazi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 229}

## For Sprouts
Wudu is a refreshing purification with clean water before prayer, washing the face and hands, wiping the head, and washing feet.

## For Explorers
Wudu is the ritual ablution commanded by God before prayer, preparing believers to stand before Him in purity and cleanliness. It begins with intention, washing the hands, rinsing the mouth and nose, washing the face and arms to the elbows, wiping the head and ears, and washing the feet to the ankles, bringing physical cleanliness and inner serenity.

## For Scholars
The minor ritual ablution (wudu) with water, standing as an indispensable prerequisite for the validity of the ritual prayer (salat), circumambulation of the Kaaba (tawaf), and handling the Qur'anic text, established by the Qur'an in Surat al-Ma'idah. Combining its obligatory integrals (fara'id)—washing the face, washing the forearms including elbows, wiping the head, and washing the feet including ankles—with recommended practices (sunan) like rinsing the mouth, inhaling water, using the toothstick (siwak), and maintaining sequence. Wudu embodies a recurring synthesis of physical hygiene and spiritual revitalization performed multiple times daily.
