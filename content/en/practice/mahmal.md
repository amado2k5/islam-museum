## Overview
## The mahmal
The mahmal was a ceremonial litter carried on a camel that no one rode: a wooden frame with a pointed top fitted to a camel's back, covered by an embroidered sitr — the kiswah of the mahmal — worked with the sultan's tughra and Qur'anic verses; the earliest surviving Mamluk covers are yellow, the later ones red or green. It was not transport but a symbol: the political authority of the sultan who sent it, his custodianship of the holy places.

## Origin and dates
The first recorded mahmal was sent by Sultan al-Zahir Baybars (1260–1277); after him Cairo, Damascus, Yemen, Hyderabad, Darfur and the Timurids sent their own, and a second Egyptian caravan left Asyut annually with its own mahmal from the late fourteenth century. Its origin is uncertain: Evliya Çelebi wrote that the earliest mahmals carried the Prophet's relics — his shoes, gown and bowl — so that it stood for his tomb; others held that Shajar al-Durr made the hajj in a coloured litter and that rulers kept the tradition, litter empty, after her death.

## What was inside
The reports differ: some say it was entirely empty; an anonymous account of 1575 describes one carrying "a Qur'an written all in great letters of gold"; the Anis al-Hujjaj of 1677 depicts the Damascus mahmal holding a Qur'an on a stand; Burckhardt wrote in 1814 that it carried a book of prayers, not a Qur'an, which people kissed on its return for blessing; and Edward William Lane in the 1830s described an empty mahmal with two Qur'ans — a scroll and a bound copy — mounted outside in silver-gilt cases.

## Arrival and end
Its arrival in Mecca was an event the townspeople and pilgrims came out to watch: the plain desert covers were changed for the ornate festival sitr before entering the city, and rival mahmals vied for the best place before the Kaaba. A returning mahmal was held to carry barakah taken by touch — parents lifted children to it and people slipped handkerchiefs inside. The sendings ended in 1926 after the mahmal music incident in which 25 Najdis who objected to the music and the innovation were killed; the mahmal was paraded in Cairo until 1952. {cite:wikipedia-mahmal-en}

## In more detail
The sources give a precise line: folklore credits Shajar al-Durr (d. 655 AH) with the first litter, but the official state caravan is attributed to al-Zahir Baybars in 664 AH/1266 CE, projecting Mamluk claim over the Hijaz. The armed procession ended after the 1926 Mahmal Incident at Mina; the Egyptian kiswa kept travelling until 1953, when the procession ceased for good and production moved to Saudi Arabia in 1962. The surviving mahmals are kept in the Museum of Islamic Art and the National Museum of Egyptian Civilization in Cairo {cite:wikipedia-mahmal-en}.
