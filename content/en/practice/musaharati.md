## Overview
## The musaharati
The musaharati is the caller who wakes people for suhur in Ramadan: he walks the alleys in the last hours of the night beating his small drum (the baza) and singing suhur chants, calling sleepers by name — "wake, sleeper, and worship the Eternal", "suhur, you who slumber". His drum became one of the sounds of the month across Egypt, the Levant, Iraq and Arabia.

## Origin and history
Historians trace the trade to Fatimid Egypt; some say the first musaharati known to Islam was Utba ibn Ishaq, who went out with the governor of Egypt Anbasa ibn Ishaq in 238 AH (853 CE) calling people to suhur. The craft matured under the Mamluks, when the musaharati took his drum, turban and aba as his own livery, and people repaid him at Eid with alms, food and money for his month's service.

## In folklore and today
The musaharati became a folk hero in Egypt and the Levant — in popular songs and television series, followed through the alleys by children who memorise his chants. Though alarm clocks and television ended his practical role, the symbol lives: Arab municipalities re-appoint musaharatis in Ramadan as living heritage, honour them as a traditional craft, and some cities hold contests for the best voice and drum. {cite:wikipedia-mesaharaty-en}

## In more detail
The written trail starts in Abbasid Baghdad's third century AH: in 228 or 238 AH the governor Utba ibn Ishaq walked on foot from al-Askar to the mosque of Amr ibn al-As in Fustat calling 'O servants of God, take suhoor, for in suhoor there is blessing' — the first documented musaharati in the sources (al-Maqrizi's Khitat, al-Subki's Tabaqat). Under al-Nasir in Baghdad the singer Ibn al-Nuqta (d. 597 AH) invented a poetic genre — al-qawma — for waking the caliph, on a state stipend. The small hand-drum (baz) became standard in Mamluk Egypt and Syria; Ibn al-Hajj al-Abdari (d. 737 AH) already critiqued some musahharin innovations in al-Madkhal {cite:wikipedia-mesaharaty-en}.
