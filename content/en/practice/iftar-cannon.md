## Overview
## The iftar cannon
The iftar cannon — with its companions the suhur cannon and the last-of-night cannon — is a custom of Egyptian origin: a gun fired at the maghrib call to announce breaking the fast, and fired again near dawn — once to wake people for suhur and twice to signal the start of the fast. Its report became part of the month's soundscape in Cairo, Jerusalem, Damascus, Jeddah and the Gulf.

## Its origin
Egyptians date it to the reign of Muhammad Ali Pasha in the nineteenth century: the governor tested a new cannon from the Citadel of Saladin at maghrib, and the people took the shot as the iftar signal and liked it, and so the custom was born; another account makes it a gift from the German king to Muhammad Ali in 1870. It travelled from Cairo to Jerusalem, Damascus and Baghdad in the early twentieth century, and to the Gulf with the first broadcasts; some cities still fire a historic naval cannon before crowds.

## Today
With loudspeakers and television universal, the cannon is a symbol rather than a signal: Cairo's citadel still fires it over the city while people gather, Jerusalem keeps it at the wall by Jaffa Gate, Abu Dhabi, Dubai and Kuwait fire historic military pieces before the crowds, and Ramadan programmes race to film the moment — in Cairo, people below the Citadel hear the shot before the microphones carry its echo. {cite:wikipedia-ramadan-cannon-en}

## In more detail
Cairo keeps three competing origin claims: the Mamluk sultan Khushqadam testing a gifted cannon at the Citadel on the first evening of Ramadan (865 AH/1461 CE) — whence the nickname 'al-Haja Fatma'; an accidental firing while soldiers cleaned an old Krupp cannon under Khedive Isma'il in the 1860s; and a deliberate test under Muhammad Ali in 1805. Damascus and Jerusalem adopted the custom in the late Ottoman period, modelled on Cairo {cite:wikipedia-ramadan-cannon-en}.
