## Overview
'Umra is, in language, a visit; in usage, tawaf of the House and sa'y between Safa and Marwa in ihram. Hajj includes its acts and adds the standing at 'Arafa, the night at Mina, and more. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.11821}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls it the lesser pilgrimage, one of the acts of devotion that form part of the Muslim ritual. It sets the rite beside the [[practice/hajj|hajj]], with which it shares the state of consecration and much of its ceremony, and it treats the rite's history and legal status as questions on which the evidence is thin or the jurists divided. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.920}

## How it is performed
The article describes the rite as carried out by one who intends umra alone and notes where it differs for one who joins it to the hajj. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.920}

Like the hajj, it can be performed only in a state of [[concept/ihram|ihram]]. On assuming it the pilgrim must decide whether he will perform the umra by itself or together with the hajj, and he states his intention in a niyya. One who combines the two may assume ihram for both at once. One who performs the umra alone must assume ihram specially for it, in the unconsecrated area outside the sanctuary of [[place/mecca|Mecca]]; the same applies to natives of Mecca, whereas for the hajj they may assume ihram inside the city. Three places are preferred for this: Ji'rana, al-Hudaybiya and above all Tan'im, which for that reason was also called al-'Umra. The ceremony proper begins with the utterance of the talbiya. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.920}

The pilgrim goes to Mecca first of all to circumambulate the [[building/kaaba|Ka'ba]]. The article describes him entering the mosque by the Bab al-Salam, passing under the portal of the Banu Shayba to the [[object/al-hajar-al-aswad|Black Stone]], and then, turning right, making the sevenfold [[practice/tawaf|tawaf]] with prayers throughout. The first three circuits are made at a rapid pace, called ramal, and the last four at an ordinary pace. Afterwards, to gain a special blessing, he presses himself against the part of the Ka'ba wall between the Black Stone and the door, prays two rak'as behind the [[object/maqam-ibrahim|Maqam Ibrahim]], drinks Zamzam water, and touches the Black Stone once more in farewell. The article adds that these last acts are not regarded as absolutely necessary. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.920}

He then leaves by the great Safa door for the second essential part, the [[practice/sai|sa'y]] between al-Safa and al-Marwa. He prays on Safa and goes to Marwa, which the article places over 400 yards farther north, covering a short low-lying stretch at the east corner of the mosque at a quicker pace, called harwala or khabab. He prays again at Marwa and returns the same way, repeating the course until he has covered it seven times and ends at Marwa. The umra is then complete except for having the hair cut or shaved by one of the barbers waiting there. When the umra is combined with the hajj, the pilgrim only has his hair trimmed at this point, and the proper cutting is done on 10 Dhu al-Hijja at the end of the hajj. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.920}

## Origins and the pre-Islamic umra
The article holds that the ceremonies of the Muslim umra were, for the most part, taken over from the pre-Islamic period. It says they lack any close connection with the religion preached by [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] apart from the Islamic prayers used in them, and that he did not alter them but assimilated them to his teaching. The author judges that he allowed them to persist less from personal reverence than from political sense, and that their original significance was probably only dimly understood by his contemporaries. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.920} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.921}

That the Muslim umra as a single group of ceremonies goes back to a pre-Islamic institution is shown, in the article's view, by the fact that Muhammad refers to it by a name which already seems to have been a special term. The author stresses, however, that the earliest form of the Muslim umra is not known, still less that of the Jahiliyya, so the two cannot be compared exactly and conclusions must be drawn by deduction. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.921}

On that basis the article suggests that the pre-Islamic umra probably consisted of ritual acts performed in ihram within Mecca, including the tawaf of the Ka'ba, but not the course between Safa and Marwa. It reasons from the second sura, which distinguishes the hajj and umra from that course and describes its performance in connection with them as irreproachable, even meritorious, yet voluntary. Muhammad himself performed the sa'y after the tawaf in 10/632, and by this example encouraged its incorporation into the Muslim umra. The article also thinks the Muslim umra lost something: the Jahiliyya umra probably included the sacrifice of animals bought for the purpose, a custom later confined mainly to the hajj. It notes that Muhammad brought sacrificial animals to the umra of al-Hudaybiya, which was not completed, and a year later to a further umra. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.921}

## Relation to the hajj
The article observes that the similarity of the two rites led to their being confused and blended, a process that had begun in the last years of the Prophet. In the only hajj he led as head of the Muslim community, shortly before his death, he performed the tawaf and sa'y on arriving in Mecca, acts which did not originally open the hajj but belonged to the umra. He then put off ihram and said that what had been done formed an umra. When [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|'Umar]] and others with him did not approve of putting off ihram and did not follow him, the article reads this as showing how closely, in their view, the ceremonies of the two rites were bound together. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.921}

## Timing and character
The article connects the Meccan custom of performing the umra as an independent rite, preferably in the month of Rajab, with a surviving pre-Islamic tradition. Its reasoning is that the Jahiliyya umra was presumably observed every year in Rajab and so had nothing to do with the hajj, which falls in Dhu al-Hijja. It cites a report that 'Ukkasha had his hair cut in Rajab of the year 2 to look like a pilgrim. Because Muhammad could prepare the combination of umra and hajj but not complete it, the Rajab custom, it says, lasted for centuries, and only in comparatively modern times does Rajab seem to have lost its importance. Where umras are now performed apart from the hajj, the nights of Ramadan are especially favoured, above all the last ten, which are linked with the night of power. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.922}

The article also records a comparison made by scholars it cites: if the pre-Islamic umra fell yearly in Rajab, and if Rajab was originally a spring month, its resemblance to the Jewish Passover is striking, and the animals sacrificed may once have been firstborn. It adds that by Muhammad's time the original meaning seems to have been practically forgotten. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.922}

On its character, the article says the Islamic umra is mainly an expression of personal piety, especially when undertaken apart from the hajj, which the community observes together each year. It suggests that this individual character results from the umra's loss of independence over time and from its being, where not tied to the hajj, a voluntary work, and that before Islam it was probably more collective. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.922}

## Ruling
The Malikis and most Hanafis hold it an emphatic sunna once in a lifetime; some Hanafis obligatory on their usage of wajib; the evident Shafi'i position and the Hanbali school hold it a lifetime obligation once, on "complete the hajj and 'umra for God" and 'A'isha's report "their jihad without fighting: hajj and 'umra"; the recommenders cite Jabir: "was 'umra obligatory? He said: no — and your performing it is better." {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.11821} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.11822}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam adds that the Qur'anic command about completing the hajj and the umra is fairly ambiguous as to the umra's legal status, whereas the status of the hajj is firmly established. The verb used means to bring an undertaking to its conclusion, so on a strict reading the verse need not command performing the umra at all, only carrying it through, leaving nothing out, once begun. On that reading the obligation would concern the manner of the umra and not the act itself. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.922}

On this footing, the article reports, [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]], [[person/malik-ibn-anas|Malik]] and [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]] in his early doctrine would regard the umra as a voluntary pious act, relying also on a saying of the Prophet transmitted by [[person/jabir-ibn-abdullah|Jabir]]; another possible reading of the verse leads to the same result. Other jurists, among them [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ibn Hanbal]], [[person/sufyan-al-thawri|al-Thawri]] and al-Shafi'i in his later doctrine, held that the verb can extend to the sense of accomplishing, and, arguing from further sayings, stressed that the lesser pilgrimage is strictly obligatory. The museum records both positions as the article gives them and gives no verdict. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p.922}

## Its merit
Among the reports on its merit: "'umra to 'umra is expiation for what lies between them." {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.11822}

## For Sprouts
Umrah is a blessed visit to the Sacred House in Mecca, where Muslims circumambulate the Kaaba and walk between Safa and Marwah with prayers.

## For Explorers
Umrah is the lesser pilgrimage to the Sacred Mosque in Mecca, which can be performed at any time of the year. The pilgrim enters the sacred state of ihram, circumambulates the Kaaba seven times (tawaf), walks seven times between Safa and Marwah (sa'i), and concludes with clipping or shaving the hair, bringing spiritual renewal and unity.

## For Scholars
The lesser pilgrimage in Islamic law, consisting of visiting the Sacred House in Mecca to perform specific consecrated rites: entering the state of ihram at the designated station (miqat), performing seven circuits of tawaf around the Kaaba, seven traverses (sa'i) between the hills of Safa and Marwah, and concluding with shaving or clipping the hair (tahallul). Sanctioned by the Qur'an and Sunna as expiation for sins, Umrah may be performed at any time throughout the calendar year, with distinctive merit attached to its observance during Ramadan, fostering perpetual spiritual renewal and connection to the sanctuary of Mecca.
