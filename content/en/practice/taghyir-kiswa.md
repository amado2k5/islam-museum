## Overview
## The kiswah and its age
The covering of the Kaaba predates Islam: Ibn Hisham relates that the first to clothe it was the Himyarite tubba' Abu Karib As'ad in the early fifth century, after his conversion to Judaism and his conversations with two Jewish rabbis; he covered it in qubati and ma'asfiri cloth and then a striped red woolen garment. Quraysh later organised its funding through an annual payment collected from every tribe that worshipped at the House.

## The Prophet's era and the caliphs
The Muslims took no part in the draping until the conquest of Mecca in 8 AH; the Prophet left the old hangings and added a Yemeni kiswah over them. Umar ibn al-Khattab was the first caliph to send an Egyptian kiswah of qubati — the white embroidered linen woven by Egypt's Copts. Mu'awiya removed the accumulated old hangings after complaints of impurity and sent one of silk, qubati and striped wool; the Umayyad caliphs then sent two kiswahs a year, layered one over the other, until the Abbasid al-Mahdi, after his pilgrimage of 160 AH (777 CE), established complete annual replacement, realising the piled covers threatened the Kaaba's structure.

## The Egyptian workshop
From the Ayyubid era under al-Salih Ayyub the kiswah was made in Egypt of material from Egypt, Sudan, India and Iraq, carried to Mecca each year by the Amir al-Hajj appointed by the Mamluk then Ottoman sultans. From Muhammad Ali Pasha's reign in the nineteenth century its cost fell on the state treasury and its workshop was Dar al-Khoronfosh in Cairo's al-Gamaliyya district, until production moved partially to Mecca in 1927 and fully in 1962; today it is woven at the King Abdulaziz Complex for the Kiswah of the Holy Kaaba in Mecca.

## The procession and the mahmal
The earliest account of the kiswah procession is Ibn Jubayr's, from 580 AH (1184 CE): it arrived on camel-back amid drums and flags and was placed folded on the Kaaba's roof. The mahmal — the empty ceremonial litter — accompanied it, a practice credited to Shajar al-Durr but established only in the fifteenth century; the mahmal carried the new kiswah to Mecca and brought the old one back to Cairo after the hajj.

## Design and replacement
Today's kiswah is black silk embroidered with Qur'anic verses in gold- and silver-plated thread — above all the hizam that belts its upper part and the sitara over the door, the richest embroidery of all. It is replaced annually and the date has shifted across the centuries, from 1 Muharram to 9 Dhu al-Hijja on the day of Arafat and back to Muharram; the old cover is cut into pieces that are gifted and displayed. In 2024 women took part for the first time in carrying sections of the new kiswah during the replacement ceremony. {cite:wikipedia-kiswah-en}

## In more detail
The sources trace the colour's full history: Tubba' As'ad al-Himyari is credited in al-Azraqi's *Akhbar Makka* with the first draping, in striped Yemeni cloth; the Prophet and the first three caliphs used white Egyptian linen. Mu'awiya introduced silk brocade and sent two kiswas a year (Muharram and end of Ramadan); Yazid and Ibn al-Zubayr used red; al-Mahdi returned to white in 160 AH; al-Ma'mun alternated white, sending three a year; al-Mutawakkil briefly added yellow. The black tradition dates to al-Nasir li-Din Allah (d. 622 AH), who first ordered green brocade, then black with the gold-embroidered hizam band — the look that has held ever since {cite:wikipedia-kiswah-en}.
