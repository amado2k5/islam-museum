## Overview
## Kahk
Kahk is Egypt's Eid cookie: a ghee pastry stuffed with dates (agwa), nuts (walnut, almond or pistachio) or the agameya syrup, pressed by hand or stamped in carved wooden moulds, baked and then buried under powdered sugar until it is white as snow. The word 'kahk' is said to be ancient Egyptian, found in the pharaonic language for a pastry or cake.

## From the pharaohs to the Fatimids
Historians trace it to pharaonic pastries whose recipes are painted on temple walls at Thebes and Memphis — dough with honey and fat — and then to the pastries of the Fatimid palaces in Cairo, which distributed a 'golden kahk' to the notables on feast days. The khitat books and travel accounts mention Egyptian kahk being made in the 'dahsha' bakeries and its batches distributed across the quarters until 'kahk-baking' became an annual turn for the common people.

## Making kahk today
The kahk season begins two weeks before Eid: family and neighbours gather for the 'dough night' — the household shapes the cookies, laughs and tells stories — or a slot is booked at the neighbourhood bakery where women bring their ready trays and wait their turn at the ovens, the smell of ghee and sesame filling the lane. On Eid morning it is dusted with sugar and served with tea after the prayer — a house without kahk, Egyptians say, is no Eid house. {cite:wikipedia-kahk-en}
