## Overview
## Mudik
Mudik — or pulang kampung, 'going back to the village' — is Indonesia's great Eid migration: a mass homecoming of millions of workers and migrants from the cities back to their villages and hometowns in the days and week before Lebaran, the local name for Eid al-Fitr. It has grown into the largest periodic internal migration in the Muslim world, and perhaps anywhere.

## Numbers and history
About 30 million travelled for Lebaran in 2013, an estimated 33 million in 2017, 80 million in 2022, over 120 million expected in 2023 — and 193 million travellers in the 2024 season by Indonesian police figures. The custom is older than the word: Majapahit-era manuscripts describe nobles returning from the capital Trowulan to their ancestral homes, and 'mudik' entered common speech in the 1970s under Suharto's urbanisation, when rural Javanese poured into Greater Jakarta for work — the word originally meant 'to sail upstream'. The Madurese do their mudik before Eid al-Adha instead.

## How it happens
Train stations, airports and ferries — Merak to Bakauheni most notoriously — are overwhelmed; the Trans-Java toll road and Java's Northern Coast Road clog for kilometres. The state adds trains, boats and flights, runs mudik gratis (free rides for the poor), and police escort the motorcycle convoys that each year top the accident figures. Its motive is simple: to see parents and family, to gather the scattered kin from other cities, provinces and the diaspora — a reunion that for many comes only at Eid. {cite:wikipedia-mudik-en}

## In more detail
The word comes from Betawi/Malay 'menuju udik' — heading upstream, toward the hinterland. Seasonal returns existed under Majapahit, but mudik as mass urban-to-rural migration formed in the 1920s-30s when Javanese workers moved to Batavia and Surabaya; it became a state-managed operation under Suharto's New Order in the 1970s, with annual Posko Mudik monitoring posts and Mudik Gratis free state transport {cite:wikipedia-mudik-en}.
