## Overview
The adhan is, in language, an announcement; in the law, the announcement of the time of an obligatory prayer with fixed, transmitted words in a prescribed form. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2006}

## Iqama and tathwib
The iqama is the announcement of standing to prayer in fixed words; tathwib is the return to announcing after announcing — the added "prayer is better than sleep" twice in the dawn call by agreement, or the added hay'ala phrases between adhan and iqama per the Hanafis. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2006}

## Ruling
The jurists agree the adhan is one of Islam's distinguishing public rites — if a town's people agreed to abandon it they would be fought — but differ on its ruling: a communal obligation (fard kifaya) for the Hanbalis in residence and the Malikis for town residents, and a Shafi'i view; an emphatic sunna per the Hanafis and the sounder Shafi'i view. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2006} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2007}

## In the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, defines the adhan as a technical term for the call to the divine service of Friday and of the five daily prayers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 206} The entry is short and gives the account of origins, the wording, the points on which the schools and communities differ, and a few related customs, as set out in the following sections.

## Origins in Madina
According to Muslim tradition, the Prophet, a year or two after the migration to [[place/medina|Madina]], consulted his companions on how to announce the hour of prayer to the faithful. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 206} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207} Some proposed lighting a fire each time, or blowing a horn, or using a wooden clapper of the kind the Christians of the East then used for the same purpose. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207} [[person/abd-allah-ibn-zayd-al-ansari|Abd Allah ibn Zayd]] related that he had seen in a dream a man calling the Muslims to prayer from a mosque roof; [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] recommended that manner of announcing the prayer, all agreed, and the adhan was introduced by order of the Prophet. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207} From then on [[person/bilal-ibn-rabah|Bilal]] called the believers, and the call is still made at prayer times. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207}

The article adds that the modern scholar Becker finds the historical model of the adhan in Christian worship, and that another scholar, whom the article judges less convincing, finds it in Jewish liturgy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207}

## The formulas
The article gives the adhan of the orthodox Muslim as seven formulas: God is most great; I testify that there is no god but God; I testify that Muhammad is the messenger of God; come to prayer; come to salvation; God is most great; there is no god but God. The sixth repeats the first. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207} The first is said four times in succession, or twice according to the Malikis; the others are said twice, except the final phrase, which is said once. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207}

The second and third formulas, after being said twice, are said a third time in a louder voice. This repetition, called tardji, is generally held to be recommended by the law; only the Hanafis forbid it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207}

## Dawn and the tathwib
At the dawn prayer the words "prayer is better than sleep" are added, also said twice. This addition is called tathwib. It is placed between the fifth and sixth formulas, except among the Hanafis, who say it at the end. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207}

## Differences among communities
The adhan of the Shia differs from that of the Sunnis in having an eighth formula, "come to the best work", placed between the fifth and the sixth. The Shia also say the final formula twice. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207} The article calls these words a shibboleth of the Shia, and says that when they were heard from the minarets of a country of the other tradition, people knew that its government had become Shii. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207}

On melody, the article says there is no fixed tune: the adhan may be modulated with any known air, provided the pronunciation of the words is not harmed. It quotes Snouck Hurgronje on Makka, where several airs may be heard at once and the singing of the adhan is a highly developed art. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207} Among the Hanbalis some doctors allow no melody at all, a position the Wahhabis follow, and the Ibadis likewise do not sing the adhan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207}

## Responding to the call
Those who hear the adhan repeat its formulas, but in place of the fourth and fifth they say that there is no strength or power but in God. In place of the dawn tathwib they say "thou hast spoken truthfully and rightly". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207} The adhan is followed by formulas of glorification that the law recommends and fixes exactly; they are left out only after the call to the sunset prayer, because the time allowed for that prayer is very short. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207}

## Who calls, and other calls
Every Muslim who prays alone or with others, at home or in the field, should pronounce the adhan aloud, as the law recommends. At mosques a [[concept/muezzin|muadhdhin]] is often appointed to call it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207} The other public prayers, such as those of the two feasts and of the eclipses of sun and moon, have a single formula, "come to the public prayer", which is said to have been in use already in the Prophet's time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207} The prayer in the mosque is immediately preceded by a second call, the [[concept/iqama|iqama]], which contains the same formulas as the adhan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207}

## Other uses
Because the adhan contains the profession of faith, Muslims pronounce it in the right ear of a newborn child shortly after birth, and also in the ear of people believed to be possessed by jinn. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207} The article points readers to [[person/al-maqrizi|al-Maqrizi]]'s Khitat for the changes made to the formulas at various times and places since the beginning of Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 207}


## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 2)

The Sheikh says that at the call to come to prayer we say there is no power and no strength save with God, because there are many obstacles on the way to prayer {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep02 00:10:00}. He lists among them devils, distractions, the road and transport, work, an important phone call and the stock-market prices {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep02 00:10:00}.

He holds that at the call to prayer the servant admits his weakness and draws power and strength from God {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep02 00:10:30}. He says that the call to success is a call to be among the successful, and that if God were to punish all the people of the earth none would be saved except the people of the mosques (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep02 00:10:30}.
