## Overview
Hajj is purpose — or purpose toward a great object — and in usage the purpose of a specific place, the Sacred House and 'Arafa, at a specific time, the months of hajj, to perform specific acts: the standing at 'Arafa, tawaf and sa'y for the majority, under specific conditions. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.6836}

## Its relation to 'umra
'Umra is the purpose of the House for tawaf and sa'y; hajj contains its acts and adds to them. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.6836}

## Ruling
Hajj is an individual obligation on every legally capable person once in a lifetime, a pillar of Islam established by Book, Sunna and consensus — "owed to God by the people is hajj to the House, for whoever can make his way to it; and whoever disbelieves — God needs nothing of the worlds" — the phrasing of obligation itself, and disbelief placed opposite the command, proves the obligation. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.6836}

## The Hajj in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, describes the Hajj as the pilgrimage to [[place/mecca|Mecca]], [[place/arafat|Arafat]] and [[place/mina|Mina]], the fifth of the five pillars of Islam. It is also called the Great Pilgrimage, in contrast to the [[practice/umrah|umra]], the Little Pilgrimage. The article says that its yearly observance has a profound influence on the Muslim world: those who do not take part follow the pilgrims in thought, and the gathering lets the community review its extent and strength. It adds that until the eighteenth century Mecca also held, at this season, one of the great commercial fairs of the world. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 40}

## The pilgrimage before Islam
The article says that research into the original meaning of the root h-j-j goes no further than hypotheses. Before Islam, two yearly markets were held in Dhu al-Qa'da, at Ukaz and Majanna, followed early in Dhu al-Hijja by the market of Dhu al-Majaz, after which the people went directly to Arafat. The author therefore judges the practice of going out from Mecca to Arafat to be probably an innovation. The gathering at Arafat took place on 9 Dhu al-Hijja and drew many tribes, which was possible only in times of peace. Dhu al-Qa'da, Dhu al-Hijja and Muharram formed a sacred period in which feuds were suspended and weapons were laid aside in the holy territory. The article adds that pilgrimage to a sanctuary is an old Semitic custom. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 40}

The article then sets out academic reconstructions by European scholars. These are hypotheses of that scholarship, not statements from within the Muslim tradition. Wellhausen thought the original season was autumn, and the article compares the rite with the Feast of Tabernacles. The author reads the standing at Arafat, without which a Hajj is not valid in Islam, as the survival of an older notion, and he names Quzah, a thunder god, as the god of [[place/muzdalifa|Muzdalifa]]. Houtsma is reported as holding that the departure from Arafat and the stone-throwing were once directed against a sun-demon, and other explanations of the stoning, by van Vloten and Chauvin, are said to have been refuted by him. The thesis of Dozy that the Hajj has a Jewish origin is said to be refuted by Snouck Hurgronje. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 40} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 41} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 42}

Some details are reported from tradition. It is uncertain whether the day of Arafat was a fast day, since the Companions are said to have been unsure of Muhammad's view and offered him a drink, which he took. Tradition quotes him as calling the days of tashriq, 11 to 13 Dhu al-Hijja, days of eating and drinking. The standing lasts from just after midday until sunset, and tradition records that he ordered that Arafat not be left before sunset, whereas it had been usual to start the departure earlier. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 41}

## How the Islamic Hajj came about
In this part, A. J. Wensinck gives an academic account that follows Snouck Hurgronje. He holds that [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] had probably taken part in the ceremonies in his youth, that the oldest suras do not mention the festival, and that his interest began only in Medina. The causes he gives are the success at [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]], which raised the thought of conquering Mecca, and disappointment with the Jewish community of Medina. To this period he assigns the doctrine of the religion of Abraham. The [[building/kaaba|Kaaba]] moves to the centre of worship as a house built by [[person/ibrahim|Ibrahim]] with his son [[person/ismail|Ismail]], it becomes the direction of prayer, and the Hajj is called a duty owed to God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 42}

The article then lists the stages. After the failed siege of Medina in year 5, the expedition to Hudaybiyya led to a treaty with the Quraysh, which opened the prospect of an umra the next year; see [[event/treaty-of-hudaybiyya|the Treaty of Hudaybiyya]]. In year 7 Muhammad instituted the ceremonies at the Kaaba, and after [[event/conquest-of-mecca|the conquest of Mecca]] in year 8 the festival could be celebrated in public. In year 9 he sent [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] to lead the pilgrim caravan, and [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]] overtook it to read out the declaration of immunity at the start of the ninth sura, which barred unbelievers from the pilgrimage except those with whom the Prophet had special treaties. In year 10 Muhammad himself led the Hajj, the [[event/farewell-pilgrimage|Farewell Pilgrimage]], and the article says that the accounts of his rites agree in essence with later practice. It stresses his abolition of the intercalated month, [[concept/nasi|nasi']], and the adoption of a purely lunar year, as in [[passage/quran-9-36|Qur'an 9:36]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 42}

## Who is required to go
The article states that the Hajj is obligatory once in a lifetime for every Muslim man or woman who has reached puberty and is of sound mind, provided they have the means. Exempt for as long as the incapacity lasts are the insane, slaves, and people who cannot obtain a mount or save the lawfully earned money for the journey and for their families' support meanwhile. The obligation is lifted when the road is unsafe because of war, exceptional brigandage or epidemics. For some who cannot go, the law allows another Muslim to be accepted or hired as a substitute, who may represent only one person and must already have performed the pilgrimage for himself. The pilgrimage of a child or of a slave is held to be meritorious but does not fulfil the obligation, which must be met again after puberty or freedom. On women, the article says that some hold that a woman must be accompanied by her husband or a close male relative, while others say she must go even without one. It adds that only a small number of Muslims, especially in distant countries, can in practice go. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 42}

## The calendar
The Hajj falls on the same dates of the lunar calendar, in the first two weeks of Dhu al-Hijja. Since Islam suppressed the intercalary month, it comes ten or eleven days earlier each year and passes through all the seasons in about thirty-two or thirty-three years. The journey is harder in high summer, though winter cold at night can also trouble pilgrims in their ritual garments. Only Muslims may be present at the rites. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 42} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 43}

## Travel to Mecca through the centuries
Until the eighteenth century, pilgrims travelled either by sailing ship as far as [[place/jeddah|Jeddah]] or in caravans organised by the authorities of the great Muslim countries. The dangers were of losing the way, sandstorms, floods, attacks by Bedouin and epidemics, and the pilgrim knew he might die on the road. Forts with small garrisons served as water and food stations, a military escort accompanied each convoy, and tribes whose land was crossed were won over with money and robes of honour. Each caravan had a leader, the amir al-hajj. From the seventh/thirteenth century a palanquin, the mahmal, was carried to symbolise the political authority of certain countries, above all Egypt and later the Ottoman state. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 43}

Among the routes, a large caravan gathered at [[place/damascus|Damascus]] and took about thirty days to reach Medina, and the journey from [[place/cairo|Cairo]] to Mecca took about thirty-five days, with the new hangings of the Kaaba, the [[object/kiswa|kiswa]], carried on other camels. In the time of the Crusades, Egyptian pilgrims went across the desert to the port of [[place/aydhab|Aydhab]] and sailed from there. The last official Egyptian overland caravan left in 1300/1883, and the mahmal was last seen in the Holy Places in 1926. Steamships, the short-lived Hijaz railway of 1908, motor vehicles and air travel changed the journey, and quarantine and vaccination ended the great epidemics. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 43}

The article gives only rough figures. For the years between 1807 and 1873 it reports a minimum of 50,000 and a maximum of 160,000, and 250,000 in 1926. According to the Meccan press, from 1962 there were between 140,000 and 180,000 pilgrims a year, not counting those from the Arabian peninsula. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 43}

Many pilgrims used the journey for devotion, study, trade or work. A year in which the standing at Arafat fell on a Friday was thought especially blessed, and the word hajji, added to many Muslim names, is an honorific for one who has made the pilgrimage. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 44}

## Arrival at Mecca and the three forms of the pilgrimage
The pilgrim puts on the [[concept/ihram|ihram]] at one of the places prescribed by tradition, or before boarding a plane. He is then a muhrim, in a state of holiness, who observes its prohibitions and repeats the talbiya. The rites on arrival are the same for all, because the Hajj includes an umra. They are seven circuits of the Kaaba, the [[practice/tawaf|tawaf]]; two prayer cycles facing the Station of Ibrahim and the Kaaba, which the legal schools hold to be either a sunna or an obligation; and seven passages between Safa and Marwa, four going and three returning, the [[practice/sai|sa'y]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 44}

What follows depends on the intention made at the start. Whoever intended the Hajj alone, called ifrad, or the umra and Hajj together, called qiran, stays in ihram. Whoever wishes to perform the umra first and live normally until the Hajj, called tamattu, leaves the ihram by having a few locks of hair cut, resumes it just before the Hajj and must offer a sacrifice, within a period whose length the jurists differ about. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 44}

## The collective rites
The visits to the Holy Places near Mecca are made together in a set order between 8 and 12 Dhu al-Hijja. The article says that each legal school has its own requirements on detail and gives only the broad outline. On the 7th a sermon is preached in the mosque of Mecca. On the 8th, the day of tarwiya, those who left ihram for tamattu enter it again. Arab writers explain the name by the watering of animals, while Wensinck and Gaudefroy-Demombynes prefer to see traces of an old rain rite. The crowd then moves to Mina or on to Arafat. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 44}

The central event is the standing, wuquf, on 9 Dhu al-Hijja before the Jabal al-Rahma at Arafat, which all the legal schools consider an indispensable pillar. It begins at noon with the noon and afternoon prayers combined and lasts until sunset, and a sermon is preached, on which the schools differ as to whether there should be one or two. The article also reports guidance given on Cairo radio on 27 May 1960 by the Rector of al-Azhar, [[person/mahmud-shaltut|Mahmud Shaltut]]: one hour between noon and sunset suffices, the pilgrim should be alone with God, climbing the hill is not required by the law, and staying on into the night is not necessary. Those delayed may still perform a brief standing during the following night until dawn, and after that they must repeat the whole pilgrimage. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 44} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 45}

After sunset the pilgrims depart from Arafat, an event called ifada, for Muzdalifa, where the sunset and night prayers are said together. The night is passed there by all except women, the frail and the sick, who may go ahead to Mina in the second part of the night. On the morning of the 10th, after the dawn prayer, the crowd goes to Mina and throws seven small stones at the Jamrat al-Aqaba, the only stoning of that day. The sacrifices follow, which give the day its name of the Feast of Sacrifices, linked to the [[practice/eid-al-adha|festival of the sacrifice]] and the [[practice/udhiyya|udhiyya]]. The article says that the offering recalls the sacrifice of Abraham, that it springs from private devotion, and that it is not absolutely obligatory in itself, though many offer at this time the sacrifice due for tamattu. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 45}

The head is then shaved or the hair cut short, and the pilgrim returns to Mecca for the tawaf of the ifada, an indispensable pillar, best made on the 10th but movable to later days under conditions set by the jurists. After the shaving the prohibitions of ihram end, except marital relations, which are allowed after that tawaf, or after the sa'y for those doing tamattu. The 11th to 13th are the days of tashriq, on which the pilgrim throws seven stones at each of the three jamras between noon and sunset. It is permitted to leave Mina on the 12th, which the article says pilgrims usually do. Before leaving Mecca for good the pilgrim makes a farewell tawaf, and many go to [[place/medina|Medina]] to visit the tomb of the Prophet. For omissions in secondary rites the law sets acts of reparation, from a sacrifice to fasting and feeding the poor. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 45}

## The spiritual meaning as preached today
Drawing on sermons broadcast from Cairo in the years before it was written, the article outlines how the meaning of the pilgrimage is presented. Pilgrims are told that it is a monotheistic act of worship, performed as guests of God visiting His house, and that they should go for God alone, avoid quarrelling and ask pardon for their sins. The crowd repeats in the talbiya and at Arafat that God is one without partners. Preachers recall the Prophet's last pilgrimage of year 10/632, stress the unity of the community and the equality of all Muslims shown by the uniform ritual garments, and remind pilgrims of death, since many buy cloth large enough to serve later as a shroud. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 45} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 46}

The article observes that a frequently quoted hadith, given by al-Bukhari and Muslim, promises complete forgiveness of sins to a pilgrimage properly performed, but that this forgiveness is not tied to any particular rite, and the pilgrimage includes no sacrifice for sin. It adds that preachers present an ideal while pilgrims are of every kind, from the fervent to those who come out of self-interest or social conformity, and that what spiritual influence the pilgrimage exerts is hard to know. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 46}

## Social and cultural effects
A closing section of the article calls the effects of the pilgrimage on society, culture and the economy in medieval Islam immense. Muslims of many races and classes travelled great distances to a common act of worship, each by personal decision. A merchant combined the pilgrimage with trade and a scholar attended lectures and met colleagues. The author argues that this mobility gave the Muslim world a centre and a forum that helped to form what he calls an Islamic consensus, and he judges the pilgrimage perhaps the most important single factor of cultural unity. He credits returning pilgrims with starting the Almoravid and Almohad movements in the Maghrib and says that Indian pilgrims brought the revived Naqshbandi movement to the Middle East and others brought back the stimulus of Wahhabism. These are the author's judgements, and he notes that the effects have not been adequately studied. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 46} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 47}

## For Sprouts
A journey to Mecca to perform special rites. A Muslim makes it once in a lifetime if able.

## For Explorers
The Hajj is a journey to Mecca, where Muslims perform known rites, such as circling the Kaaba and standing at Arafat. Whoever is able to make the way makes it once in a lifetime.

## For Scholars
The journey to the Sacred House at Mecca to perform prescribed rites, one of the five pillars of Islam. The Qur'an makes it a duty for those able to make the way there, and tells of the call to proclaim it to the people. The Hajj includes the state of ihram, circling the Kaaba, running between Safa and Marwa, standing at Arafat, spending the night at Muzdalifa and at Mina, and throwing the pebbles. The books of sira tell of the Prophet's pilgrimage and the Farewell Sermon. This entry presents the Hajj as a global journey where Muslims gather from many countries, and refers the details of the rites to the entry on the Hajj in law.
