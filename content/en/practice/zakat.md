## Overview
Zakat is the obligatory payment that Muslims make from set kinds of property to the poor and other named classes, and the word also names the portion paid. The Encyclopaedia of Islam article presents it as one of the pillars of Islam, says that denying its obligation amounts to unbelief and that failing to pay it when due is a grave sin, and describes accepting it, for those entitled, as a collective duty. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 420} The article calls the law of zakat a hybrid of ritual and revenue-raising, and it sets out where the schools differ. This page reports those positions side by side and rules on none of them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 423}

## The word and the start of the duty
Muslim scholars almost all took the word to be Arabic, from a verb meaning to increase and to be pure: zakat blesses the property it is taken from and purifies the giver. Modern scholarship has treated it as a loan, almost certainly from Judaeo-Aramaic zakhutha, righteousness, and has also suggested it was shaped to rhyme with salat. The article notes that some rates have parallels outside Islam, such as a tenth or a fortieth, but sees no reason to doubt that the classical system is unique to Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 420}
[[practice/sadaqa|Sadaqa]] is the usual word for voluntary alms but is often used for zakat too, above all for livestock and crops. In the Middle Ages zakat was also the name of various taxes with no basis in Islamic teaching, probably because of its religious associations. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 420}
Scholars disagree about when the duty began. A common view puts it in Shawwal of the year 2/624, after zakat al-fitr, which came in Sha'ban of that year; others give 1/622, 4/625 or even 9/630. Another view holds that it came in general terms before the hijra and in detail at Medina. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 420}

## In the Qur'an
The word occurs thirty-two times, always in the singular. It is so often joined to prayer that it was later called prayer's companion, and earlier prophets and peoples are also described as told to give it. Exegetes sometimes asked whether the zakat known before Islam was the same as the one they knew. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 420}
Those entitled to receive it are listed in the verse on alms, said to have been revealed after the [[event/battle-of-hunayn|battle of Hunayn]] in answer to criticism of how [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] had shared out zakat, which until then he had decided personally. The mention of collecting agents in the same verse is taken as evidence that the institution was in place in his lifetime. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 420} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 421}
Interpreters have found in the Qur'an references to zakat on crops, gold and silver, merchandise and mines, and to the intention that makes it valid. One verse contrasts the increase that people seek through usury, [[concept/riba|riba]], with the real increase of those who give zakat. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 421}
Jurists differed on how far the Qur'an alone suffices. A minority, including the Zaydi imam al-Hadi, found a basis for charging zakat on all property unless evidence showed otherwise, a view sometimes ascribed to [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]]. Others held that the Qur'anic provisions lack detail and need the Prophet's clarification, a position commonly ascribed to [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]]. They also disagree on whether zakat cancelled other obligatory giving and on whether any duty on property exists beyond zakat. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 421}

## In the hadith
Apart from the Qur'anic list of recipients, the main elements of the law rest on hadith: the kinds of property liable, the minimum quantity in each case, the rate, and the one-year holding period. The best-known traditions on camels and sheep are reports of written instructions kept as family heirlooms. They include the instructions to Amr ibn Hazm, sent to Yemen; instructions the Prophet is said to have prepared but not issued before his death, which [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]], [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] and later [[person/umar-ibn-abd-al-aziz|Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz]] carried out; and Abu Bakr's instructions to [[person/anas-ibn-malik|Anas ibn Malik]], sent to collect in Bahrain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 421}
The sources say collection was already in full force in the Prophet's lifetime, with many collectors, some of them prominent Companions such as Umar and [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]]. The hadith also indicates that he died before the definitive guidelines for camels and sheep were issued. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 421}

## Collection through history
In the Prophet's time collectors were charged also with teaching the elements of Islam, and Maliki sources give collectors of livestock a limited judicial authority. The article says that by about 493/1100 state collection had largely ended across the Muslim world for most Muslims. [[person/ibn-abd-al-barr|Ibn Abd al-Barr]] noted that scholars of his day no longer made the arrival of collectors a condition of the duty, as [[person/malik-ibn-anas|Malik]] had in his own time, when they came regularly. [[person/al-qalqashandi|al-Qalqashandi]] reports that in his day collection was confined to the customs officials at ports and some limited other activity. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 423}
Rulers with a reforming mission favoured reviving collection: the [[polity/almohad-caliphate|Almohads]], [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Salah al-Din]], the [[polity/sokoto-caliphate|Sokoto Caliphate]], the Arabian Wahhabis and the [[polity/mahdist-sudan|Sudanese Mahdists]]. Some local dynasties kept a more or less traditional collection into modern times, often beside non-Islamic revenues: the Alawis of Morocco until 1901 and the Zaydis of Yemen until the revolution of 1962. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 423}
Where officials vanished, intermediaries filled the gap, from village imams in pre-colonial Malaysia to Sufi orders in Africa. Sectarian groups built their own channels: among the Twelver Shi'is leading jurists took the imams' place, and among the Indian Bohras the dai mutlak did. In these groups, as among the Ibadis, zakat was largely kept within the community. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 423}

## Ritual and revenue
Insofar as paying zakat is a ritual of purification, attention falls on the payer; as a system of revenue, it falls on the recipients. The Shafi'i texts that follow al-Muzani put the basic rules in the section on ritual and the rules of distribution in a section on state revenue. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 423}
Zakat is often called a tax on property, not income, though the article calls this only approximately true, since crops owe it once, at harvest, with no holding period. Classical jurists explained why only some property is liable through the idea of growth: the property is growing or meant for growth, in fact or in principle. Grazing animals and trade goods grow in fact, gold and silver as money in principle, and crops and minerals are themselves growth. The one-year holding period, hawl, gives the growth time to occur. The theory was almost universally accepted; [[person/ibn-hazm|Ibn Hazm]] was one of its few opponents. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 423}
Jurists also disagree whether the duty lies on the property itself or on the person. The article's example is the owner of forty sheep who pays nothing for two years: on the second view he owes two sheep, on the first only one, since one sheep of the flock is already encumbered and the nisab of forty is no longer met. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 423}

## Livestock
The majority require that animals graze for breeding or milk; the Malikis and some Zahiris, including Ibn Hazm, do not. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 425} For camels the article gives a scale: one sheep or goat for five to nine, two for ten to fourteen, three for fifteen to nineteen, four for twenty to twenty-four, then female camels of rising age, up to two in their fourth year for 91 to 120. Twelver Shi'is set five sheep at 25 camels. For cattle the minimum is thirty, with an animal in its second year due per thirty and one in its third year per forty; for sheep and goats the minimum is forty, with one animal for forty, two for 121, three for 201, four for 400, and one per hundred after that. The Hanafis accept payment in value in all cases. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 425}
Abu Hanifa charged zakat on horses, without a minimum, against his pupils Abu Yusuf and al-Shaybani; many later Hanafis preferred that horses be exempt. For Twelver Shi'is zakat on mares is recommended. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 425}

## Crops
The schools differ widely on which crops are liable. Twelver Shi'is charge only wheat, barley, dates and raisins; the Hanafis and Zaydis charge virtually all produce of the soil; the Shafi'is and Malikis charge dates, grapes and staple foods that are stored, but not vegetables or spices; the Hanbalis charge crops measured by volume and stored. The universal minimum, except for Abu Hanifa, who set none, is five wasqs, a measure fixed by Medina practice. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 425}

## Gold, silver, mines and trade
The rate on each metal is 2.5 per cent. The Hanafis, Twelver Shi'is and Ibadis apply it only to each further forty dirhams or four dinars. Jewellery worn as ornament is exempt except for the Hanafis, Zaydis, Ibadis and Zahiris. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 426}
The Sunni schools other than the Hanafis charge gold and silver on extraction from a mine, and the Hanbalis extend this to other minerals, including petroleum. The Shafi'is charge buried treasure from pre-Islamic times at twenty per cent. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 426}
Trade goods, potentially the widest category, are accepted by all except Ibn Abbas, the Zahiris and al-Shawkani, and are recommended for Twelver Shi'is. The charge falls on their value, at the same minimum and rate as gold and silver. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 426} The Malikis alone distinguish active traders, who value stock once a year, from investors. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 427}

## Who owes it
Zakat applies almost only to private individuals. State property is exempt, except according to some Zaydis. The majority charge the property of minors and the mentally incapacitated, payable by guardians; the Hanafis exempt it except for crops, fruit and zakat al-fitr. Slaves do not pay, except for the Zahiris, and all Sunni schools but the Malikis charge the owner. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 427} For jointly owned property the Shafi'is assess the minimum on the whole, so one sheep is due on forty shared by two owners. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 427}

## Recipients
The verse on alms names eight classes, understood to be exhaustive: the poor, the indigent, collecting agents, those whose hearts are won over, slaves, debtors, those in the path of God, and travellers. The article calls the fourth class the most controversial; the Shafi'is include converts whose commitment is doubtful, while the Hanafis and Malikis, among others, regard it as lapsed. Slaves are usually read as those contracted to buy their freedom, though the Malikis prefer zakat to buy the freedom of full slaves. Most take the path of God to mean volunteers in jihad, while the Twelvers came to read it more broadly, as including repair of mosques and bridges; the Hanafis, among others, rejected such uses because valid zakat requires a transfer of ownership. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 428} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 429}
The schools differ on distribution. The Shafi'is are the most demanding: a private payer must give to at least three persons from each class, whereas the Hanafis allow a single person from any class. They also differ on who counts as wealthy and so barred from receiving: the Shafi'is use a means test of sufficiency over a lifetime, the Hanafis a property test of owning a nisab above basic needs. The article reads this as showing different views of zakat as a means of transferring wealth. Most jurists hold that zakat cannot validly go to the payer's direct ancestors or descendants, and most bar the Prophet's family, though some later Hanafi, Maliki and Twelver jurists tend to allow them to receive it within their needs. Twelver Shi'is require that zakat go only to Twelvers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 429}

## Collection and payment
All agree that rulers must send out collectors, who represent the recipients, but not on how much individuals may distribute themselves. The Zaydis require all zakat to go to the officials of the legitimate imam. Most Sunnis accept payment even to a corrupt ruler; the Hanbalis prefer personal distribution where possible; the Malikis make the collector's arrival a condition for livestock. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 429} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 430}
The majority require payment in kind except for trade goods, while the Hanafis and Twelver Shi'is allow the value; later Shafi'is and Malikis came to favour the Hanafi rule. Most jurists prefer open payment, but the Malikis prefer an agent, to guard the act from vanity. Favoured months include Muharram, Ramadan, Dhu l-Hijja and Rajab. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 430}

## Zakat al-fitr
[[practice/zakat-al-fitr|Zakat al-fitr]] is due for every Muslim at the end of the Ramadan fast. The article says it purifies the giver from lapses during the fast and spares the poor from begging on the festival day. Most jurists call it obligatory, though denying it is not unbelief, while the Hanafis call it wajib. It is paid in wheat, barley, dates and raisins, to which some add other staples, at one sa, or half a sa of wheat for the Hanafis; cash payment is now widespread. The Shafi'is, Hanbalis, Malikis and Hanafis allow payment at different times before the festival. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 431}

## In Sufism
Classical Sufi sources show the Sufi as outside the system: owning no property, he pays no zakat, and his spiritual wealth bars him from receiving it. Sufi writers read zakat widely, as thanks for all God's gifts. Some Sufis favoured accepting it, and [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]] urged giving first to poor Sufis. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 431}

## In the modern period
Since about the Second World War zakat has been re-examined as never since the early centuries of the law, in two camps, jurists and economists, with conferences, manuals and private organisations. [[person/rashid-rida|Rashid Rida]] argued that slaves might include societies held down by colonialism and that the path of God might mean struggle by argument. The article describes a shift from zakat as an act of piety to zakat as the base of an Islamic economic order. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 431}
Modernists urge that governments again collect zakat, and widely treat the old split between visible and hidden property as no longer tenable. They propose that the gold minimum be the general one, that rules for gold and silver apply to paper money, and that salaries, rents, minerals and fish be charged. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 432}
At the time of writing the article says payment is enforceable by law in six countries: [[place/saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]], [[place/libya|Libya]], [[place/yemen|Yemen]], [[place/malaysia|Malaysia]], [[place/pakistan|Pakistan]] and [[place/sudan|Sudan]]. Collection in Saudi Arabia dates from a royal decree of 1370/1951, with individuals permitted to distribute half of what is due. Libya's law of 1971 applies to all property but compels collection only on visible property. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 432}

## Summary
Zakat — from growth, blessing and increase — names both the due that must be paid on specified wealth in a specified way, with hawla and nisab conditions, and the portion paid out itself; it is called thus because it purifies and fructifies wealth. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.9072}

## Beside sadaqa and 'atiyya
Sadaqa covers what is given seeking God's face — obligatory and voluntary — and is sometimes confined to the obligatory, as in "there is no sadaqa on less than five dhawd of camels"; 'atiyya is broader still, any gift whether or not God's face is intended. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.9072} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.9073}

## Its obligatory status
Zakat is an obligation of Islam and a pillar of the religion, established by Book, Sunna and consensus: in the Book, "establish prayer and give zakat" and the warning to hoarders; in the Sunna, "Islam is built on five", and the Prophet's instruction to Mu'adh for Yemen that God had imposed a sadaqa on their wealth "taken from their rich and returned to their poor". {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.9073}

## Fighting those who withhold it
Muslims in every age agreed on its obligation in general, and the Companions agreed to fight its withholders — al-Bukhari relates that Abu Bakr fought them when they withheld it after the Prophet's death. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.9074}

## For Sprouts
Zakat is a compulsory gift of wealth, given once a year to those entitled to it, and it is one of the pillars of Islam.

## For Explorers
Zakat is a compulsory gift. Each year a Muslim gives a part of his wealth to those who are entitled to it, such as the poor and those in need. It is one of the pillars of Islam, and the Qur'an mentions it together with prayer.

## For Scholars
The obligatory alms, one of the five pillars of Islam. The Qur'an pairs it with prayer and names its recipients in the verse on alms: the poor, the needy, those who collect it, and those whose hearts are to be reconciled. The hadith of Jibril calls it the 'obligatory' alms to set it apart from voluntary charity. The jurists differ on which property is liable, on the amounts and on the times of payment, so this entry gives the basis of the duty and its recipients, and leaves the details to the entry on zakat in law. The museum gives what the text says and does not set a ruling that binds any one person.

## In al-Tabari's commentary: "and spend from what We provided them"
Al-Tabari records that the exegetes differed over the spending in this verse. From Ibn Abbas he reports that they pay the zakat seeking reward, and that it is the zakat on their wealth. From al-Dahhak he reports that spending was at first a matter of offerings by which people drew near to God according to their means, until the obligatory alms were revealed in seven verses of Surat al-Tawba, which he calls the confirming abrogators. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 243}
In the other view, from al-Suddi's report from Ibn Abbas, Ibn Masud and some Companions, it is a man's spending on his family, which was before zakat was revealed. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 243} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 244}
Al-Tabari prefers the general sense: that they discharge everything due from their wealth, whether zakat or maintenance of those they must support, such as family, dependants and others by kinship or ownership. God described them as spending from what He provided without singling out a kind of spending, so they are described by every praiseworthy form of spending from the good part of their wealth, meaning what is lawful and untouched by the unlawful. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 244}
