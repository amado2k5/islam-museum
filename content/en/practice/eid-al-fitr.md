## Overview
'Eid is from 'awd, returning, for it recurs; the Muslims have two: the day of fitr, first of Shawwal, and the day of adha, the tenth of Dhu'l-Hijja — no other. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.11984}

## The festival in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam says the Arab lexicographers derive the word for festival from a root meaning "returning periodically", but that it is in fact an Aramaic loanword, like many religious terms, and it points to the Syriac word for a festival or holiday. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039} It records two canonical festivals in the Muslim year: [[practice/eid-al-adha|Eid al-Adha]] on the tenth of Dhu al-Hijja and Eid al-Fitr on the first of Shawwal. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039}

Eid al-Fitr, the festival of breaking the fast, is also called the minor festival, and in Turkish the sugar festival. It is celebrated on the first of Shawwal and the days that follow. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1040} In practice, the article says, the two festivals last three or four days. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039}

## Zakat al-fitr
According to the article, a Muslim who has not paid [[practice/zakat-al-fitr|zakat al-fitr]] before the end of the period of fasting is legally bound to pay it on the first of Shawwal at the latest, and is advised to pay it before the public prayer of that day. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1040}

## The festival prayer as the article describes it
The prayer shared by both festivals is held by the community as a whole and is considered sunna. The article says it preserves an older form than the daily or the Friday prayer, although in some points it has come to resemble the latter, and that in its general style it is much like the prayers for drought and for an eclipse. It has only two rak'as and more takbirs than an ordinary prayer, and it is followed by a [[practice/khutba|sermon]] in two parts. It has no [[practice/adhan|call to prayer]] and no [[concept/iqama|iqama]]; as in the earliest times, the only summons is a short call to the congregation. It should be held in the open air at the prayer ground, which is still often done, though mosques are now frequently preferred. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039} The schools' differing rulings on the prayer are given above from the Kuwaiti jurisprudence encyclopaedia.

## Customs of the days
On both festivals, the article says, people put on new clothes or at least their best, visit one another, congratulate one another and exchange presents. Cemeteries are visited, and people sometimes stay in them for hours. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039}

## Why the minor festival looms large
The article notes that these popular customs are more usual at Eid al-Fitr than at Eid al-Adha. Because the hardships of the fast of [[practice/sawm-ramadan|Ramadan]] are over, the festival of breaking the fast is celebrated with much more joy and festivity, so that today, in its words, the minor festival has in practice become of much greater importance than the major one. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1040}

## The Eid prayer
Jurists differ on its ruling: an emphatic sunna for the Malikis and Shafi'is; obligatory on the operative Hanafi view because of the Prophet's unbroken performance and its congregational form; a communal obligation for the Hanbalis on "so pray to your Lord and sacrifice." {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.11984}

## Takbir
Takbir on the two Eids is prescribed on the way to the prayer in homes, markets and roads until the prayer begins; the extra takbirs within it are sunna per the majority and obligatory per the Hanafis; takbir after prayers on the tashriq days is prescribed by agreement, recommended per the majority and obligatory per the Hanafis. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.11984} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.11985}

## The day's practices
Keeping the two Eid nights alive in worship is recommended — "whoever keeps alive the night of fitr and of adha in hope, his heart shall not die on the day hearts die" — and the Eid bath, as the Prophet bathed on the two days. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.11985}

## For Sprouts
Eid al-Fitr is a joyful day after Ramadan, when we wear fresh clothes, visit family, and share happy greetings.

## For Explorers
Eid al-Fitr is the blessed holiday celebrated on the first day of Shawwal, marking the completion of fasting during Ramadan. The day commences with giving Zakat al-Fitr to ensure joy for the poor, followed by the communal Eid prayer and praises of God, as families exchange sweets and warm wishes in a spirit of generosity.

## For Scholars
One of the two canonical festivals in Islam, falling on the first day of Shawwal to mark the conclusion of the obligatory fast of Ramadan. Instituted in the second year of the Hijra, its religious observances include the mandatory disbursement of Zakat al-Fitr prior to the festival prayer to support the indigent, congregational Eid prayers held in large outdoor prayer areas (musallas), and communal recitations of takbir. The festival integrates profound devotional gratitude for spiritual completion with vital communal renewal, family visitation, and civic solidarity.
