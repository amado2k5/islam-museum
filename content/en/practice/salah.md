## Overview
"The prayer" (salah) is an obligatory worship and one of [[concept/five-pillars|Islam's five pillars]]: in Ibn Umar's hadith as [[person/ibn-khuzayma|Ibn Khuzayma]] gives it, Islam is built on five things, one of them establishing the prayer {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.192}. Ibn Khuzayma opens his Book of Prayer with a chapter on the first prescribing of the five prayers, relating from Anas ibn Malik, from Malik ibn Sa'sa'a, the Prophet's account of the night of [[event/isra-and-miraj|the Isra]] {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.186} {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.188}. In this hadith fifty prayers a day were prescribed at first; then, after Moses advised asking for a lighter duty, they were reduced to five prayers a day {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.188}. In the hadith of [[person/talha-ibn-ubaydullah|Talha ibn Ubayd Allah]] about the Bedouin the obligation is "five prayers in the day and night", and what exceeds them is voluntary {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.529}.

In the Qur'an the command to establish the prayer is joined to the zakat in [[passage/quran-2-43|al-Baqara 43]], the prayer is tied to set times in [[passage/quran-4-103|al-Nisa' 103]], and times for it are mentioned in [[passage/quran-17-78|al-Isra' 78]] {cite:tanzil-quran}. [[person/al-tabari|Al-Tabari]] explains establishing the prayer as performing it with its limits and obligations, as prescribed {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 241}. He reports from Ibn Abbas that establishing it is the completeness of bowing, prostration, recitation, humility and attention to it {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 241} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 242}. He notes that salat in Arabic usage means supplication, and holds that the obligatory prayer was so named because the one praying seeks through his act the reward he asks of God, as a supplicant seeks his need through his supplication {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 242} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 243}.

## In the Qur'an
The Qur'an commands establishing the prayer coupled with the zakat: [[passage/quran-2-43|al-Baqara 43]]. It describes it as "a timed ordinance" upon the believers: [[passage/quran-4-103|al-Nisa' 103]]. The verse of al-Isra' mentions prayer times: [[passage/quran-17-78|al-Isra' 78]].

## In the hadith
Ibn Khuzayma gives a chapter on the proof that establishing the prayer is part of Islam, and in it reports Umar's account of Gabriel's question about [[concept/islam-din|Islam]] {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.191}.

Ibn Khuzayma narrates the hadith of Talha ibn Ubayd Allah about the Bedouin with the words "five prayers in the day and night"; the man asked whether anything else was required, and the Prophet said: "No, unless you do something voluntarily" {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.529}.

## Prayer in the Qur'an

The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, treats salat as the principal prayer of Islam. It belongs to the ibadat, the acts of worship, and is distinct from the prayer of supplication, the remembrance of God's names and the litanies of the Sufi orders. The article says the word comes from the Syriac word for prayer and had its Arabic form before Islam {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 991}.

The article stresses that the Qur'an sets salat inside a wider atmosphere of prayer and invocation. Prophets before Islam, the Prophet Muhammad and his community, and even the people of the Garden are shown praying. It also warns against reading the technical sense of ritual prayer into every use of the word: some early verses and some uses of the verb refer to benediction, to the devotions of unbelievers, or to Muhammad's whole life of worship {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 991}.

The word appears 65 times in the singular with the definite article, and the article takes these to mean the ritual prayer, an act with prescribed gestures and words. It points to the opening sura, which is recited in every rak'a, and to the Qur'an's picture of all earlier prophets, among them [[person/ibrahim|Abraham]], [[person/musa|Moses]] and Jesus, as people who practised or were charged with prayer, so that prayer is presented as a constant companion of belief in one God {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 991}.

## How the Qur'anic picture develops

The article counts 67 uses of the word in the technical sense and notes that none occurs in the earliest suras. It suggests the word may not appear before the years of about 620 to 624, on either side of the Hijra. Five Medinan suras, which belong to the time of or after the change of the qibla, hold 33 of the uses, half of the total in about a fifth of the text, and the article connects this with the establishment of Islam as an institutional religion {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 991}.

Prayer is joined to [[practice/zakat|zakat]] at least 25 times. The article reads this pairing as a sign of the communal weight of both duties, as zakat moved from free giving to an obligation paid into the community's treasury {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 991} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 992}.

On times, the article reasons that Muslim prayer grew out of the personal prayer of Muhammad. The oldest passages that give times address him alone and call for night prayer and praise at dawn and at dusk. Soon a group forms around him, and the community is addressed. It reads the Qur'an's vocabulary as showing three main times, dawn, the decline of the day and the night, with a median prayer added somewhat later, and it notes that the Friday prayer is mentioned in a single passage {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 992}.

The article also lists what the Qur'an says of the surroundings of prayer: a public call, purification before prayer with sand allowed when water is absent, and facing the sacred mosque at Mecca. The passage that institutes the [[concept/qibla|qibla]] is dated by the article to 2/624, probably in the middle of Sha'ban. It replaced an earlier direction toward [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]], and the article says the practice before the Hijra remains uncertain. The article adds that standing, bowing, prostration and public recitation of the Qur'an are all present in the Qur'anic description, while the technical term rak'a is not {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 992} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 993}.

## In hadith and in law

The two chief Sunni collections, those of [[person/al-bukhari|al-Bukhari]] and [[person/muslim-ibn-al-hajjaj|Muslim]], open with the pillars of Islam and then give prayer the largest share of their sections on worship, about 200 pages in al-Bukhari and more than double that in Muslim. Both arrange the material in three blocks: legal purity as the condition of prayer, the general elements of prayer, and particular prayers such as those of the feasts and of funerals {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 993}.

The article notes that by the third/ninth century the timetable and conduct of prayer were fixed, and that the founders of the four Sunni schools had worked in the second/eighth century. It says it will not set out the schools' differences on prayer, which it calls quite modest, and it records them only where they bear on a particular rule. Where it does name them, they appear below with the school identified {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 993}.

## General principles

Hadiths given by [[person/anas-ibn-malik|Anas ibn Malik]] describe how the five daily prayers were prescribed on the Prophet's ascension to the heavens, after some bargaining. A report from Abu Mas'ud al-Ansari has [[person/jibril|Gabriel]] descend five times to teach the times. The five prayers bind every Muslim man or woman past puberty and of sound mind {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 993}.

Further principles the article lists are these. Facing the [[building/kaaba|Ka'ba]] is strictly required in all the ritual prayers. The Arabic language is firmly required in most schools, though the Hanafis have allowed other languages for the recitation of the opening sura by one who cannot say it in Arabic. Each prayer is announced by the muezzin with two calls, the [[practice/adhan|adhan]] and the iqama, which were established at Medina. The rak'a is a fixed sequence of postures and words, and every ritual prayer except the one over the dead has at least two {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 993}.

Many traditions stress the worth of praying in congregation, among them one saying that it outweighs prayer at home or in the market by twenty-five times. The article says the Malikis assert the duty of attending more strongly than the Hanafis, and that women's attendance is permitted but not recommended. The favoured place is the [[concept/masjid|mosque]]. For congregational prayer two adults suffice, one of them the imam, who should be male, of good reputation and educated. The worshippers stand in rows behind him and follow his movements {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 993}.

The article explains that, on the basis of reports of Muhammad's practice, certain rak'as are recited aloud and others in a low voice. It describes calm and measured performance as a basic requirement, citing a report in which the Prophet told a hasty worshipper to start again three times. A worshipper who omits or misplaces an element and notices before the end repairs it and then adds two prostrations of forgetfulness, with differences between schools and many fine distinctions. The article also mentions the sutra, an object placed in front of the worshipper which others should not pass between {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 993} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 994}.

The article stresses the law's flexibility. Reports show the Prophet shortening a prayer when a child cried, and objecting to over-long recitation. Congregational prayer may be cancelled in very bad weather, the noon prayer may be delayed in great heat, and the noon and afternoon prayers, or the sunset and night prayers, may be combined, a concession given especially to one travelling in haste {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 994}.

## The five daily prayers

The article describes the five as differing in length, in whether the recitation is aloud, and above all in time {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 994}.

- The dawn prayer has two rak'as and aloud recitation. Its time starts with the true dawn and lasts until daybreak, before the sun appears.
- The noon prayer has four rak'as with low recitation. Its time starts when the sun passes the zenith and normally lasts until an object's shadow equals its height.
- The afternoon prayer has four rak'as with low recitation. Its time starts at that point and normally lasts until the sunlight yellows, though it may be performed until sunset.
- The sunset prayer has three rak'as with aloud recitation. Its time starts when the sun has gone below the horizon and normally lasts until the red glow of twilight disappears.
- The night prayer has four rak'as with aloud recitation. It begins when the red glow disappears. The article notes that Abu Hanifa read this glow as the whiteness that follows the redness, an opinion it calls isolated. The normal time lasts to the end of the first third of the night {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 994} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 995}.

## The shape of the prayer

The article lists nine conditions, following Ibn Abd al-Wahhab: being Muslim, sound mind, discernment at the age of reason, which it puts at seven years, two kinds of purity, covering what the law requires, being present when the time comes, facing the qibla, and the [[concept/niyya|intention]] to perform the particular prayer. Purity is of two kinds, the legal kind gained by [[practice/wudu|wudu]] or a full wash, and the real kind, freedom from impurity on body, clothing and place. The article calls these conditions common to all schools, though the schools differ on postures, words and the legal weight of particular elements {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 995}.

The rite opens with the raising of the hands and the words God is most great. The hands are then placed on the chest, right over left, while the Imamis and, classically, the Malikis let the arms hang instead. The article adds that one author permits the first position and that it is spreading in the Maghrib. Most Sunnis add an opening supplication, and, except the Malikis, a phrase seeking refuge in God {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 995}.

Each rak'a then runs as follows. The worshipper stands and recites the opening sura of the Qur'an, saying Amin, and in the first two rak'as adds a further passage, shorter in the second. He bows with hands on the knees and glorifies God three times, rises with the words that God hears those who praise Him, and prostrates with forehead, nose, palms, knees and feet on the ground. He sits briefly, prostrates a second time, and rises. The Hanbalis and Shafi'is take a short sitting before rising, a practice now widespread among the Malikis {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 995}.

After the second rak'a the worshipper sits and says the tashahhud, the declaration of faith that greets the Prophet and the righteous servants of God, raising the index finger at the declaration of God's oneness. At the end of the last rak'a the tashahhud is repeated with the Abrahamic blessing on Muhammad and his family. The prayer ends with a greeting to the right and then to the left, and in a congregation the imam gives only one. The article notes that only the first two words of the greeting are strictly required {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 995} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 996}.

On practice as lived, the article says that countries such as Egypt are more observant than others, but that precise studies by country, sex, congregational or private prayer, and the effects of urbanisation or emigration are lacking {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 996}.

## Other ritual prayers

The article groups the other prayers by how binding they are, since schools vary in classing them as fard, wajib or sunna {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 996}.

The [[practice/salat-al-jumua|Friday prayer]] takes the place of the noon prayer and is held in a mosque in a sizeable settlement. The required number of resident men, the imam included, is four for the Hanafis, twelve for the Malikis and forty for the Shafi'is and Hanbalis. Women may attend but do not count. A double [[practice/khutba|sermon]] precedes two rak'as led by the preacher. The prayers of the two feasts, [[practice/eid-al-fitr|the breaking of the fast]] and [[practice/eid-al-adha|the sacrifice]], have two rak'as with extra takbirs, no call and no iqama, and the sermon follows the prayer. Women are urged to attend even when impure, in which case they do not pray {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 996}.

The [[practice/salat-al-witr|witr]] is a duty short of an obligation for the Hanafis and a strongly recommended sunna for the other schools, to be prayed between the night and dawn prayers, generally in three rak'as. On the supplication called qunut, the Malikis deny it in witr, the Shafi'is use a formula from al-Tirmidhi, the Hanafis treat a particular supplication as a duty, and the Imamis treat it as an optional personal supplication said in witr and in each of the five prayers {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 996} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 997}. Voluntary prayers in twos or fours may be added around the five, and a forenoon prayer is also described {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 997}.

For a journey, the article says the noon, afternoon and night prayers shorten to two rak'as on every lawful journey of sufficient length, which it sets for the Hanafis at three days' walking, and the sunset prayer is unchanged. A halt of fifteen days ends the journey for the Hanafis, and four full days for the other schools. The Hanafis regard shortening as a duty, and the others as a permission {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 997}.

The [[practice/salat-al-istisqa|prayer for rain]] is held in the open air in ordinary clothes, with two rak'as, a double sermon and the turning of cloaks. The [[practice/salat-al-kusuf|eclipse prayer]] is a congregational two rak'as with a second bowing and standing in each, no call and no sermon. The article refers the [[practice/salat-al-khawf|prayer of fear]], said in war, to its own entry {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 997}.

The [[practice/salat-al-janaza|funeral prayer]] is a communal duty and has no bowing or prostration. The imam says four takbirs aloud, with praise, blessing on the Prophet, supplication for the dead, and the closing greeting. The article reports that the opening sura after the first takbir is obligatory for al-Shafi'i and Ibn Hanbal, and may replace the praise for the Hanafis and Malikis. On who may be denied the prayer, it notes that al-Qurtubi held it owed to all Muslims except innovators and declared rebels, and mentions that in 385/995 the judge Abd al-Jabbar declined to pray over the vizier Ibn Abbad because of his unrepented extortions {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 997} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 998}.

## Prayer among the religions of the region

The article sets Islam's prayer beside the Near East's older practices. Rabbinic Judaism had three daily prayers, Syriac Christianity seven monastic offices, Manichaean hearers four daily prayers, and Zoroastrians five daily prayers said individually. It records that Ignaz Goldziher saw Persian influence in the number five, and judges it unwise to give the number much weight, preferring to compare how each religion brings the cosmos into prayer {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 998}.

The article insists that Muslim prayer is fundamentally individual. The obligation is personal, nearly all its formulas are in the first person singular, and the imam does not represent the congregation but models correct practice. It adds that the opening sura and a phrase of the tashahhud are in the plural, so that each rak'a opens and closes with solidarity {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 998}.

## Prayer in Muslim life

The article calls ritual prayer the heart of Islam. In its account, prayer keeps the worshipper in contact with the Qur'an through recitation, repeats the declaration of faith in the call and the tashahhud, and is tied to zakat, fasting and the [[practice/hajj|pilgrimage]]. It also links the individual to the community through the preference for congregational prayer, to the Prophet through his name in the tashahhud and the blessing, and to God. It reports two sayings, one that only abandoning prayer lies between a man and unbelief, and one that whoever prays toward our qibla is the Muslim. The article notes that the phrase ahl al-qibla has often been used to name the community {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 998} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 999}.

It also says nearly all of the ritual's detail rests on the practice of Muhammad, and that salat is in effect an imitation of him. It presents prayer as a route to guidance in decisions, a source of cleansing, likened in a hadith to bathing five times a day in a stream, and as a moment of closeness, citing the saying that a man is nearest his Lord in prostration {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 999}.

On the inner side, the article says mystics applied the idea of ihsan to prayer, and that [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]] devoted a volume of his Ihya to it, stressing humility and presence of the heart. It gives [[person/fakhr-al-din-al-razi|al-Razi]]'s comparison of the worshipper to a man in a fine garment who cannot attend to filth. The Sufis, it says, developed this presence along two lines, confidential converse with God and awe before Him, and some compared prayer to the Prophet's ascension. It closes with the saying that the key of the Garden is prayer and the key of prayer is purification, which it says shows both the legal and the spiritual sides of salat {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 999}.

## For Sprouts
Prayer is performed five times a day. It is one of the pillars of Islam, and in it the Muslim stands before God with the whole heart.

## For Explorers
Prayer is performed five times a day and night, and it is one of the pillars of Islam. In it the Muslim stands before God, recites, bows and prostrates, and prays in the mosque or at home.

## For Scholars
The prescribed prayer, five times in the day and night, one of the five pillars of Islam. The Qur'an commands its establishment and calls it a timed obligation on the believers, and the hadith adds that other prayers are voluntary. It consists of standing, recitation, bowing and prostration, and the jurists fixed its times from the Book and the Sunna. The jurists differ on the details of its pillars, conditions and recommended acts, so this entry gives its basis without weighing the schools against each other. Wudu and the mosque are linked to prayer, and each has its own entry.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 02)

The Sheikh explains that the person praying says, when he gives the closing salam, the plural form 'peace be upon you and God's mercy', that in the tashahhud he says 'peace be upon us and upon the righteous servants of God', and that the singular form of address is used only to the Prophet in 'peace be upon you, O Prophet' {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep02 01:01:30}.

He mentions that Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali used to sit before the Prophet and greet him, and that the person praying has his teacher, as it were, in front of him, so he says 'peace be upon you, O Prophet' so that his mind does not wander; he begins with the greetings, prayers and good things belonging to God, then greets the Prophet, then himself (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep02 01:02:00}.

The Sheikh takes up the case of someone who begins to pray after years of not praying, giving the example of a man who starts at twenty with some seven or eight years of prayers owed, and he reports of the Hanbalis that once a person has begun to pray nothing is owed for what has passed, noting that Imam Ahmad regarded a person who does not pray as a disbeliever outside the community (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep02 01:12:30}.

He says that the majority hold that a person who owes days of prayers should make them up, taking the more cautious course, and mentions that there is an easier view, that he need not make them up but should pray many voluntary prayers, and he thinks that scholarly honesty requires presenting both views {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep02 01:13:00}.

He says he leans to the view of the majority, so that a person prays what is owed until he is fairly sure he has made up everything he missed, and if it turns out on the Day of Resurrection that nothing was owed, he hopes that God will count them in his favour {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep02 01:13:30}.

He speaks of women's prayers lost because of bleeding, such as the forty days after childbirth, mentions that a mother may tell her daughter to wait forty days without praying or fasting even though the bleeding may stop after a week, and suggests, as the more cautious course, that a make-up prayer be prayed before the obligatory prayer (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep02 01:14:00}.

He urges making up missed prayer, fasting and zakat because they are a debt owed to God, then mentions the rights of people, the nearest being the right of one's wife and children not to be fed from what is unlawful or doubtful, and then a person's repentance with himself and his heart {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep02 01:14:30}.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 07)

The Sheikh says that an imam's error in recitation does not invalidate the prayer and that he should not be blamed for it, and he mentions that sins fall away as the leaves of a tree fall (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep07 00:24:30}.

He holds that error in recitation is to be expected, because the Qur'an is precious and anyone may slip over a vowel or a shortening, so one should not be strict about it, and he says that the follower has no prostration of forgetfulness of his own but follows his imam {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep07 00:25:00}.

He says that the imam is a guarantor and the muezzin is trusted, that the imam bears responsibility for the prayer of those behind him, and that the follower follows him in the prostration of forgetfulness even if he himself did not forget (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep07 00:25:30}.

## From Omar Abdelkafy's programme Bustan al-Amal
The Sheikh distinguishes establishing prayer from performing it: performing it is praying the noon and sunset prayers and then backbiting, wronging and being untrustworthy with words, money and children; establishing it is that it forbids one indecency, wrongdoing and oppression; he cites that Ibrahim settled his offspring by the House 'that they may establish prayer'. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy bustan-el-amal/ep08 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy bustan-el-amal/ep08 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy bustan-el-amal/ep08 00:08:30}

## From Omar Abdelkafy's programme al-Munjiyat
In al-Munjiyat Omar Abdelkafy relates the hadith of the man who prayed hastily, whom the Prophet told 'go back and pray, for you have not prayed', then taught to be still in bowing, prostrating and sitting throughout his prayer, a prayer without this being deficient. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-manjeyat/ep09 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-manjeyat/ep09 00:03:30} He mentions that delaying the prayer past its time is among what corrupts the heart, and that the Muslim answers the call to prayer. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-manjeyat/ep06 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-manjeyat/ep06 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-manjeyat/ep06 00:02:30}

## In al-Tabari's commentary: "and establish the prayer"
Al-Tabari explains that establishing the prayer means performing it with its limits, obligations and what is required in it, as prescribed; he compares the way a people are said to "establish" their market when they do not leave it idle of buying and selling, and cites a line of verse whose author the editor could not identify. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 241}
He reports from Ibn Abbas that they establish the prayer with its obligations, and that establishing it is the completeness of bowing, prostration, recitation, humility and turning to it within it; and from al-Dahhak that the obligatory prayer is meant. The edition's editor notes that the al-Dahhak chain is very weak because of Juwaybir. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 241} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 242}
He observes that salat in Arabic usage means supplication, citing two lines of al-A'sha. He holds that the obligatory prayer was named salat because the one praying exposes himself, through his act, to success in his request for God's reward, along with the needs he asks of his Lord, as a supplicant does by his prayer. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 242} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 243}
