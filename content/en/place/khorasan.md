## Overview
Khurasan is a vast historic region of the eastern Islamic world, covering parts of what are now eastern Iran, Afghanistan and Turkmenistan. Yaqut al-Hamawi's Mu'jam al-Buldan describes it as a wide land holding the mother-cities, and gives its limits, the origin of its name and its conquest. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.350}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-Buldan
Yaqut sets the first boundary on the Iraq side at Azadhwar, the chief town of Juwayn, and Bayhaq. On the side facing India he names Tukharistan, Ghazna, Sijistan and Kirman, and adds that these are not truly part of it but the fringes of its border. In his account it contains the mother-cities, among them Nishapur, Herat and Marw, which was its capital, as well as Balkh, Talaqan, Nasa, Abiward, Sarakhs and the cities between them on the near side of the Jayhun (Amu Darya). He notes that some people include the districts of Khwarazm and count Transoxiana as part of it, and says this is not so. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.350} See [[place/nishapur|Nishapur]], [[place/herat|Herat]], [[place/merv|Marw]], [[place/balkh|Balkh]] and [[place/transoxiana|Transoxiana]].

Yaqut says most of these lands were taken, some by force and some by treaty, in year 31 of the Hijra, in the days of Uthman, under the governorship of Abd Allah ibn Amir ibn Kurayz. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.350}

For the name he reports three explanations. The genealogist Daghfal said that Khurasan and Haytal were sons of Alim son of Sam son of Noah, who settled, when tongues were confused at Babil, each in the land called after him: Haytal beyond the Jayhun and Khurasan in the lands Yaqut has listed on this side of the river. A second says that khur is the Dari Persian word for the sun and asan means the origin and place of a thing. A third says the name means "eat easily". {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.350}

Yaqut then relays al-Baladhuri's division of Khurasan into four quarters: first Iran Shahr; second Marw al-Shahijan, Sarakhs, Nasa, Abiward, Marw al-Rudh, Talaqan, Khwarazm and Amul; third, west of the river, al-Faryab, al-Juzjan, Upper Tukharistan, Baghlan, Badakhshan, Tirmidh, al-Saghaniyan and others; fourth, Transoxiana with Bukhara, al-Shash, Sughd, Samarqand and Farghana. Yaqut then comments that the correct limit of Khurasan is the one he gave first, that al-Baladhuri listed these lands because all were under the governor of Khurasan and the name gathered them, and that Transoxiana, the land of the Hayatila, is a province in itself, as is Sijistan. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.351}

He also quotes Shurayk ibn Abd Allah, who said Khurasan is God's quiver, from which He shoots a people when He is angry with them, and another report that no banner has risen from Khurasan in the pagan era or in Islam and been turned back before it reached its end. Yaqut only relays these sayings and does not assess the report. He quotes Ibn Qutayba that the people of Khurasan were the people of the call and the supporters of the state, and that in most of the Persian kings' rule they paid no tribute or land tax to anyone. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.351}

The quoted account goes on to the Hayatila's defeat and capture of a Persian king, Firuz, whom they released on his oath not to cross a boundary they set with a stone; he broke the oath, carried the stone before him and marched against them, and was killed. Islam then came, and the people of those lands accepted it willingly and made peace over their lands, with a light tax; this lasted through the days of the Umayyads until armies of the people of Khurasan rose under Abu Muslim al-Khurasani. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.351} See [[person/abu-muslim-al-khurasani|Abu Muslim al-Khurasani]].
