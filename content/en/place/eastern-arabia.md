## Overview
For Yaqut, al-Bahrayn is the general name of the lands on the Indian Ocean coast between Basra and Oman, with Hajar as its capital; it is the region this record carries as Eastern Arabia. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 1 p.346}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut says the name is pronounced al-Bahrayn in every case, notes that al-Zamakhshari reports a dual form, which had not reached him from any other source, then quotes the author of the astronomical tables for its position, giving length and breadth in degrees, and records the disagreement over whether it lies in the second or third climate. He says it is a general name for lands on the coast of the Indian Ocean between Basra and Oman; some say its capital is Hajar, others that Hajar is the capital of al-Bahrayn. It has springs, waters and wide lands. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 1 p.346} {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 1 p.347}

He holds that al-Yamama is a separate district in the middle of the road between Mecca and al-Bahrayn, though some counted it as part of the region. He relays Ibn ʿAbbas that al-Bahrayn belonged to Iraq and was bounded from Oman on the side of Jarfar, and, from Ibn al-Faqih, that under the Abbasids Oman, al-Bahrayn and al-Yamama were made one province. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 1 p.347}

From Abu ʿUbayda he gives distances: ten days between al-Bahrayn and al-Yamama, fifteen days by camel between Hajar and Basra, and a month between Hajar and Oman. He lists its parts as al-Khatt, al-Qatif, al-Ara, Hajar, Binunah, al-Zara, Juwatha, al-Sabur, Darin and al-Ghaba, with al-Safa and al-Mushaqqar as the capital of Hajar. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 1 p.347}

On the origin of the name he gives two explanations transmitted from Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn al-Qasim, calls them strained, and prefers al-Azhari's: the name comes from a lake in the area of its villages at the gate of al-Ahsa, about three miles by three, still, brackish and never overflowing, with the villages of Hajar ten farsakhs from the green sea. He notes the nisba is Bahrani, not Bahri, and names scholars so called, among them Muhammad ibn Muʿammar al-Bahrani, from whom al-Bukhari narrated, al-ʿAbbas ibn Yazid al-Bahrani, who died in 258, and Zakariya ibn ʿAtiyya. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 1 p.347}

For the conquest, Yaqut says al-Bahrayn was in the Persian kingdom, with many of ʿAbd al-Qays, Bakr ibn Wa'il and Tamim camping in its desert and al-Mundhir ibn Sawa al-Tamimi acting for the Persians. In the year 8 AH the Prophet sent al-ʿAla ibn ʿAbd Allah ibn ʿImad al-Hadrami, an ally of Banu ʿAbd Shams, to call its people to Islam or the poll tax, with letters to al-Mundhir and to Sibakht, the marzban of Hajar. Both accepted Islam, and the Arabs there and some non-Arabs with them. The Zoroastrians, Jews and Christians made a settlement with al-ʿAla to bear the labour and share the fruit, with a dinar from each adult as head tax. He also gives another date for the mission and Qatada's remark that there was no fighting in al-Bahrayn. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 1 p.348}

He then lists the later governors: al-ʿAla, then Aban ibn Saʿid ibn al-ʿAs, then al-ʿAla again at the request of its people to Abu Bakr until his death, in one account in the year 20, then Abu Hurayra, with differing reports on al-ʿAla's death and on the appointments of ʿUthman ibn Abi al-ʿAs and Qudama ibn Mazʿun. He adds an account from Ibn Sirin of ʿUmar calling Abu Hurayra to account for money from al-Bahrayn. Yaqut gives these reports with their chains of narrators and does not decide between them here. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 1 p.348}
