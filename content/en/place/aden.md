## Overview
Aden: a port in the south of [[place/yemen|Yemen]] on the Indian Ocean, and a major station on the sea-trade routes. {cite:geonames-gazetteer}

## In the geographers' books
al-Maqdisi called it "the corridor of China", and said that whoever wished to trade should go to Aden, Oman or Egypt. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim pp.31-32}

al-Hamdani counted it among the oldest markets of the Arabs. He described it as a city surrounded by a mountain that had no road until a passage was cut through the mountain at the iron gate of Bazbar, which became a path to the interior. Its water comes from the sand of a desert district. Its people include the Mirbiyyun, the Hamhamiyyun and the Mallahiyyun, and the Mirbiyyun claim descent from the sons of Harun. {cite:sifat-jazirat-al-arab p.36}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut records Adan with haraka and a final nun, from 'adana bil-makan, "he settled in a place", for which it was named; al-Tabari said Adan and Abin were named for the two sons of Adnan, and Yaqut remarks "this is strange — I have seen no one else record that Adnan had a son called Adan save in this passage". It is a known city on the shore of the sea of India in the Yemen, a wretched place with neither water nor pasture, its people drinking from a spring a day's journey away — and yet it is the anchorage of India's ships, where the merchants gather, a town of trade; it is attached to Abin, which is Adan's mikhlaf. Al-Hamdani the Yemeni makes it a southern Tihami town, the oldest fair of the Arabs, ringed by a mountain that had no road until a gate was cut through it with iron tools; its watering-place is al-Habaq, ahsa-gravel in sand on the edge of the waste of Iram, and it holds brackish wells; its people are the Maribiyyun and the Jamajimiyyun, the Maribiyyun claiming descent from the sons of Harun. Al-Zajjaji and Ibn al-Kalbi derive the name from Adan ibn Sinan ibn Ibrahim, its first settler; Abd al-Mun'im reports from Wahb that the Abyssinians crossed in their ships, came ashore there and said 'adna, "we have come over", so it was named. Between Adan and Sana'a are sixty-eight farsakhs. Amara distinguishes this Adan of Abin from Adan La'ah, a pretty village in Mount Sabr of Sana'a's workings which he entered himself. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.89-90}

## The museum's identification
Yaqut's Adan is the record's sea-of-India anchorage whose mikhlaf is Abin; the foul water he describes stands beside the trading rank he and the record's own al-Muqaddasi and al-Hamdani give it, and Adan La'ah of Mount Sabr is the other place he explicitly separates. Between it and Sana'a the Adan entry prints sixty-eight farsakhs and the Sana'a entry sixty-eight miles, reported as printed (by the two records' points about 300.0 km).
