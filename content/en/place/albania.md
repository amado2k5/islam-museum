## Overview
Albania, called Arnawutluk in Ottoman usage, lies on the Adriatic in south-eastern Europe. The entry treats its language, literature, geography and population in turn. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 699-700}

## Language
Albanian is an Indo-European language. It has no literary records before 1496, but ancient Illyrian and ancient Epirote, judged from personal and place names, are held to be the prototypes of its northern (Geg) and southern (Tosk) forms. Its native vocabulary is small, and its borrowings are large: the wheel, cart and plough, and the terms of kinship, are not native words; city life, road-building, horticulture, law, religion and family relations come from Latin, much disguised by sound change; Orthodox ritual terms are Greek; names of dishes, garments, parts of the house and Islamic terms came through Turkish. Albanian is spoken by about 1,500,000 people in Albania, 700,000 in Kosovo-Metohija in Yugoslavia and some 40,000 in Epirus. Archaic forms survive on the Greek islands of Hydra and Spetsai and in Sicily and Calabria, where Tosk exiles settled after the Turkish conquests. The Geg dialect, of the north including Kosovo-Metohija and the capital Tirana, differs from Tosk, which has the larger literature, in its use of the subjunctive for the infinitive and in other points. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 699-700}

## Literature
From the third century the Roman Church kept a bishopric at Scutari in northern Albania, which became the first cultural centre; Bishop John Buzuk's liturgy of 1555 and the seventeenth-century works of Budi and Bogdani belong to it. Literary activity, tolerated by the Turks in the Catholic north, was suppressed in the Muslim centre and the Orthodox south, and took root among the exiled colonies of Sicily and Calabria. Matranga, a descendant of the exiles, began hymn-writing in folk rhythms in 1592, continued by Brancato (1675-1741) and the Calabrian Variboba (born 1725). The movement became secular with the folk songs and rhapsodies of De Rada (1813-1903), an ardent spokesman of Albanian liberation. The Tosk brothers Abdyl, Sami and Naim Frashëri formed a league at Prizren in 1878, and after the San Stefano settlement they sought Albanian autonomy and literary freedom. Forced into exile after years in Istanbul, where they were joined by the lexicographer and Bible translator Kristoforidhi (1827-1895), they formed a literary society at Bucharest and printed Albanian books from 1885. Books printed in exile were smuggled into Albania by caravan. The lack of a literary centre and of a standard alphabet hampered the movement, until a digraphic script replaced Sami's phonetic spelling. After independence in November 1912 the currents combined. In the Catholic north the poets and writers included Shiroka (1847-1917), Xanoni (1863-1915), Mjeda (1866-1937), the satirist Fishta (1871-1940), Prennushi (1885-1946) and Koliqi (born 1903); the Tosk writers included Drenova (born 1872) and Poradeci (born 1899). Konitza (1875-1943) and others moved to Boston, where the Vatra society and its journal Dielli were founded in 1912. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 699-700}

## Geography
Albania (Shqipni, Shqiperi) lies between about 39 degrees 38 minutes and 40 degrees 41 minutes north, and 20 degrees east of Greenwich on a north-south axis. Its area is 11,097 square miles (28,748 sq km). It is bounded by Yugoslavia, Greece and the Adriatic. Its length is 207 miles; it narrows to 50 miles at Peshkopi and widens to 90 miles at the lake of Little Prespa. The limestone ranges continue the Dinaric Alps, and the land is highest in the east, reaching about 7,000 feet. The largest of the western lowlands, some below sea level, is the fertile Myzeqeja plain. The Drin, the longest river, rises in Lake Ohrid and reaches the Adriatic below Shengjin; the Shkumbi, a winter torrent, divides the country into two roughly equal areas, Gegnija and Toskerija. The highest peak is Tomorr near Berat (2,396 m). Deforestation has left much of the country bare. Durres is the main port; Valona has a fine natural harbour; Saranda is a fishing port and Shengjin ships ore. Tirana, the capital, is given about 100,000 people, Shkoder 35,000, Korce 25,000, Durres 16,000, Vlore 15,000 and Gjirokastra 12,000. A railway of 80 miles links Tirana with Durres, Peqin and Elbasan; most towns are reached by road. The climate ranges from European in the highlands to sub-tropical in the south-west, with Mediterranean vegetation. The densest forests are at Mamuras near Kruja. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 699-700}

## Population
The census of 1955 counted 1,394,310 people in Albania, against 1,003,097 in 1930. Albanians also lived in Yugoslavia (750,000 by the Yugoslav census of 1948), Greece (an estimated 30,000 to 60,000) and Italy (an estimated 150,000 to 250,000); the estimate for Albanians by birth worldwide was about three million. The 1930 census counted 45,000 Vlachs, 35,000 Slavs, 20,000 Turks and 15,000 Greeks in Albania. The entry divides the Albanians into two groups: the Gegs north of the Shkumbi and the Tosks to the south, which the Turks called Gegalik and Toskalik. The Gegs differ from the Tosks in dialect, outlook and social behaviour, and are thought to have kept national traits more purely. The barren mountains gave too little to support a growing population; when epidemics killed livestock, people emigrated or moved to neighbouring plains, usually as mercenaries, shepherds or farm workers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 699-700}
