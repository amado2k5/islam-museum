## Overview
Albania, called Arnawutluk in Ottoman usage, lies on the Adriatic in south-eastern Europe. The entry treats its language, literature, geography and population in turn. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 699-700}

## Language
Albanian is an Indo-European language. It has no literary records before 1496, but ancient Illyrian and ancient Epirote, judged from personal and place names, are held to be the prototypes of its northern (Geg) and southern (Tosk) forms. Its native vocabulary is small, and its borrowings are large: the wheel, cart and plough, and the terms of kinship, are not native words; city life, road-building, horticulture, law, religion and family relations come from Latin, much disguised by sound change; Orthodox ritual terms are Greek; names of dishes, garments, parts of the house and Islamic terms came through Turkish. Albanian is spoken by about 1,500,000 people in Albania, 700,000 in Kosovo-Metohija in Yugoslavia and some 40,000 in Epirus. Archaic forms survive on the Greek islands of Hydra and Spetsai and in Sicily and Calabria, where Tosk exiles settled after the Turkish conquests. The Geg dialect, of the north including Kosovo-Metohija and the capital Tirana, differs from Tosk, which has the larger literature, in its use of the subjunctive for the infinitive and in other points. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 699-700}

## Literature
From the third century the Roman Church kept a bishopric at Scutari in northern Albania, which became the first cultural centre; Bishop John Buzuk's liturgy of 1555 and the seventeenth-century works of Budi and Bogdani belong to it. Literary activity, tolerated by the Turks in the Catholic north, was suppressed in the Muslim centre and the Orthodox south, and took root among the exiled colonies of Sicily and Calabria. Matranga, a descendant of the exiles, began hymn-writing in folk rhythms in 1592, continued by Brancato (1675-1741) and the Calabrian Variboba (born 1725). The movement became secular with the folk songs and rhapsodies of De Rada (1813-1903), an ardent spokesman of Albanian liberation. The Tosk brothers Abdyl, Sami and Naim Frashëri formed a league at Prizren in 1878, and after the San Stefano settlement they sought Albanian autonomy and literary freedom. Forced into exile after years in Istanbul, where they were joined by the lexicographer and Bible translator Kristoforidhi (1827-1895), they formed a literary society at Bucharest and printed Albanian books from 1885. Books printed in exile were smuggled into Albania by caravan. The lack of a literary centre and of a standard alphabet hampered the movement, until a digraphic script replaced Sami's phonetic spelling. After independence in November 1912 the currents combined. In the Catholic north the poets and writers included Shiroka (1847-1917), Xanoni (1863-1915), Mjeda (1866-1937), the satirist Fishta (1871-1940), Prennushi (1885-1946) and Koliqi (born 1903); the Tosk writers included Drenova (born 1872) and Poradeci (born 1899). Konitza (1875-1943) and others moved to Boston, where the Vatra society and its journal Dielli were founded in 1912. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 699-700}

## Geography
Albania (Shqipni, Shqiperi) lies between about 39 degrees 38 minutes and 40 degrees 41 minutes north, and 20 degrees east of Greenwich on a north-south axis. Its area is 11,097 square miles (28,748 sq km). It is bounded by Yugoslavia, Greece and the Adriatic. Its length is 207 miles; it narrows to 50 miles at Peshkopi and widens to 90 miles at the lake of Little Prespa. The limestone ranges continue the Dinaric Alps, and the land is highest in the east, reaching about 7,000 feet. The largest of the western lowlands, some below sea level, is the fertile Myzeqeja plain. The Drin, the longest river, rises in Lake Ohrid and reaches the Adriatic below Shengjin; the Shkumbi, a winter torrent, divides the country into two roughly equal areas, Gegnija and Toskerija. The highest peak is Tomorr near Berat (2,396 m). Deforestation has left much of the country bare. Durres is the main port; Valona has a fine natural harbour; Saranda is a fishing port and Shengjin ships ore. Tirana, the capital, is given about 100,000 people, Shkoder 35,000, Korce 25,000, Durres 16,000, Vlore 15,000 and Gjirokastra 12,000. A railway of 80 miles links Tirana with Durres, Peqin and Elbasan; most towns are reached by road. The climate ranges from European in the highlands to sub-tropical in the south-west, with Mediterranean vegetation. The densest forests are at Mamuras near Kruja. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 699-700}

## Population
The census of 1955 counted 1,394,310 people in Albania, against 1,003,097 in 1930. Albanians also lived in Yugoslavia (750,000 by the Yugoslav census of 1948), Greece (an estimated 30,000 to 60,000) and Italy (an estimated 150,000 to 250,000); the estimate for Albanians by birth worldwide was about three million. The 1930 census counted 45,000 Vlachs, 35,000 Slavs, 20,000 Turks and 15,000 Greeks in Albania. The entry divides the Albanians into two groups: the Gegs north of the Shkumbi and the Tosks to the south, which the Turks called Gegalik and Toskalik. The Gegs differ from the Tosks in dialect, outlook and social behaviour, and are thought to have kept national traits more purely. The barren mountains gave too little to support a growing population; when epidemics killed livestock, people emigrated or moved to neighbouring plains, usually as mercenaries, shepherds or farm workers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 699-700}

## Encyclopaedia of Islam: migration and settlement
From the middle of the fourteenth century Albanians, under Serbian pressure or as mercenaries of feudal lords in Greece, settled in Epirus, Thessaly, the Morea and even the Aegean islands. There most were gradually Hellenised, or later moved to southern Italy under Ottoman pressure. About 1466 Thessaly still had Albanian districts in its towns and 24 Albanian katunes at Livadia (Lebadea) and 34 at Istifa. When Iskender-beg died in 1468, some of his followers withdrew to the mountains or went to the kingdom of Naples; in 1478, 1481 and 1492 more Albanians went to southern Italy and Sicily, where they kept their language and customs to the present day. In the fifteenth century the Ottomans moved some Albanian timar-holders from feudal families, the Mazeraki and Heykal, elsewhere. The only significant Turkish settlements recorded in Albania were a few exiles from Konya, called Konici, and the Yuruks of Kodjadilk on the mountains east of Dibra, stationed to guard the Rumeli-Albania highway. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 700-701}

The second significant expansion of Albanians in Rumeli came in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, into the plains of Yakova, Prizren, Ipek (Pec), Kalkandelen (Tetovo) and Kosovo, especially after the mass migration of Serbs from these areas in 1690. This settlement was mostly the result of the muqata'a land system, under which Albanians leased small tracts from large muqata'a holders and settled there as tenants. The Vlachs had lived a pastoral life in the mountains of northern Albania alongside the Albanians since the Slavic invasion of the seventh century, and from the fifteenth century took part in the Albanian expansion. The Ottoman register of 835 AH (1431) records Vlachs and their katunes in southern Albania, especially east of Kanina. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 700-701}

## Encyclopaedia of Islam: the highland tribes
The Albanian tribes north of the Drin are called Malj-i-sor, "highlanders". Around 1881 there were nineteen such tribes, with 35,000 Roman Catholics, 15,000 Muslims and 220 Orthodox; the most famous were the Hoti, Klementi, Shkreli, Kastrati, Kocaj and Pulati, in the mountains east of Scutari. During the Ottoman conquest of Albania from 1385 to the end of the fifteenth century the rebellious clans retreated to the most rugged highlands, and their raids on the lowlands coincided with the weakening of Ottoman control in the seventeenth century, when they became the terror of Rumeli. The Ottomans respected the tribal organisation and autonomy of these tribes, because they controlled the mountain passes from Rumeli into Albania. In return for guarding the passes they were exempt from taxation. A register of 1496 says that the Klementi nahiye, of five villages, paid kharadj and ispendje, was exempt from the diwani and other taxes, and was made pass-guardian of Petrishban's territory and the route Medun-Kuca-Plava. In the seventeenth century the Klementi caused trouble through raids in Rumeli and co-operation with the rebel tribes of Montenegro. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 700-701}

South of the Drin lived the Mirdita, numbering 32,000 in 1881, all Roman Catholic. They were divided into five clans called bayraks: Oroshi, Fandi, Spashi, Kushneni and Dibri. The Hoti, for services against the Venetians in 1696, came to head the clans; the entry notes that the bayrak system was itself an Ottoman institution, a banner of authority given to military chiefs. Each clan had a hereditary bayrakdar, or standard-bearer, and its affairs were decided by a council of hereditary elders; the five clans met yearly at Orosh, and a bolukbashi appointed by the Ottoman governor handled business between the administration and the clans. The Mirdita captains claimed descent from Leke Dukagjini, who played an outstanding part in Iskender-beg's struggle and is credited with codifying the customary law, the Kanuni i Leke Dukagjinit. The tribes sent an auxiliary force of one man per household to the Ottoman army, a practice also applied to the Yuruks and Kurds. From the end of the sixteenth century the empire needed more troops in its long wars, and Albanian auxiliaries grew in importance, especially in local wars against the Montenegrins. The Mirdita were regarded as the bravest soldiers in Rumeli, though Heguard (1855) called them the greatest plunderers in the world. When the Tanzimat administration tried in 1855 to disarm them and enrol them in the regular army, they rose and disturbed the Zadrima area, and the government gave up the attempt the next year. Later the Mirdita chief Prenk Bib Doda took a leading part in the independence movement of 1908. A "Republic of Mirdita", proclaimed under Yugoslav auspices in 1921, collapsed the next year. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 700-701}

## Encyclopaedia of Islam: religion
Italian statistics of 1942 gave a population of 1,128,143: 779,417 Muslims, 232,320 Orthodox and 116,259 Catholics. The only significant Catholic group lies in the Shkodra district, while large Orthodox communities live in the districts of Gjirokastra, Korce, Berat and Vlore, and Muslims are spread across the country, mostly in the centre. Albania was attached to the patriarchate of Constantinople in 732 and split between Rome and Constantinople in 1054, the north passing to Rome. The Normans and Angevins strengthened Catholicism; Antivari was the seat of the archbishop of Albania, and Durazzo that of the Latin archbishop; the Orthodox of Albania were directly dependent on the archbishopric of Ohrid. The Ottomans, even before they restored the patriarchate of Constantinople in 1454, favoured Orthodoxy over Catholicism, but for political reasons the Porte tolerated the Catholic church in Albania, and Albanian lords shifted between East and West according to the political conditions. The Orthodox Albanian immigrants to southern Italy kept their own Uniate church recognising the pope. The Ottoman yearbook of 1895 counted, in the province of Yanya (Epirus and Albania south of the Devoll), 223,885 Muslims, 118,033 Greeks, 129,517 Orthodox Albanians, 3,517 Jews and only 93 Roman Catholics. Some of those counted as Greeks were Orthodox Albanians who had been Hellenised through Greek religious and educational institutions from the second half of the eighteenth century. After Albanian independence an autocephalous Orthodox church of Albania was finally recognised by the patriarchate in 1937. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 700-701}

The first converts to Islam were the Albanian feudal lords who held timars from the Ottomans. Conversion was not required to keep a timar: allegiance to the Ottoman state was enough, and Christians still received timars through the fifteenth century, though by its end only a few Christian timar-holders were left through voluntary conversion. Elbasan, built by Mehmed II in 870 AH (1466), was a Muslim centre from the outset, as was Yenishehir in Thessaly. Common people converted more slowly: at the start of the sixteenth century the four sanjaks of Elbasan, Ohrid, Avlona and Iskenderiye had only about three thousand Muslim raiya families, and a Catholic source of about 1622 estimated that only one Albanian in thirty was Muslim. In the seventeenth century the Venetians and Austrians tried to stir up insurrection among the Catholic Albanians and the Orthodox Serbs, who resented the rising jizya. In 1614 church dignitaries at Kuci asked for papal aid; about 1622 the first Franciscan missionaries reached Albania and southern Serbia. Albanian Catholics and Serbs co-operated with the Venetians in 1649 and with the Austrians in 1689-1690, and the Porte resolved to retaliate {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 700-701}
