## Overview
Damascus, in Arabic Dimashk or Dimashq al-Sham, or simply al-Sham, is the largest city of Syria. It was the capital of the [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad caliphate]] from 661 to 750 CE and is home to the Umayyad Mosque. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose account this page follows, describes a city with a recorded history of several thousand years that was attacked many times but never destroyed, and whose fortunes alternated between the desert and the east on one side and Mediterranean and western influence on the other. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 291} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 292}

## Location and setting
Its approximate coordinates are 33.5102 N and 36.2913 E {cite:geonames-gazetteer}. The article places the city at an altitude of nearly 700 metres, on the edge of the desert at the foot of Jabal Qasiyun, a massif of the eastern Anti-Lebanon. Steppe runs east and north-east to the Euphrates, and to the south the land merges with Arabia. The mountains behind the Lebanese coast cut the city off from sea winds and cloud, so rainfall is only 250 to 300 mm a year, against 850 to 930 at Beirut, and summers are long and harsh. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 291}

Nature fixed the site in advance, the article says. It lies where the Barada, the only perennial watercourse of the region, leaves the mountains and crosses the plain before losing itself in the desert. An ingenious irrigation system turned a corner of the desert into the oasis of [[place/al-ghuta|the Ghuta]], which Muslim tradition counts among the three earthly paradises, with Samarkand and al-Ubulla. Protected by mountains on the west, well supplied with water and set on the north-south road across Syria, the city served as a market for nomads and a halt for the caravans linking the Euphrates and the Nile. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 292}

## Before Islam
The date of the founding is unknown, but excavations in 1950 at Tell al-Salihiyya, south-east of the city, revealed an urban centre of the fourth millennium. Damascus appears in history in the Tell al-Amarna tablets, as a town taken in the fifteenth century BCE by Thutmose III. In the eleventh century BCE it was the capital of the land of Aram, and the Aramaeans are credited with its grid plan and much of its canal system. In 732 BCE the Assyrian king Tiglath-pileser III ended the kingdom of Damascus; Babylonians, Achaemenids, Greeks and Romans followed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 292}

Alexander's conquest in 333 BCE placed the city under western influence until the Arab conquest. A Greek town grew beside the Aramaean one, with colonnaded streets and an agora, and the Nabataeans held the city twice, in 85 BCE and again from 37 to 54 CE. The article connects the second period with the visit of Saul, the future St Paul, to the city's Jewish colony. Under Roman rule Damascus gained prosperity and a new plan: rectangular walls of about 1,500 by 750 metres with seven gates, an aqueduct, and two great colonnaded streets, one of which is today the Suq Midhat Pasha, known to foreigners as the Street called Straight. The temple of Jupiter of the Damascenes, successor to the storm god Hadad, stood on the site of the later Great Mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 292} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 293}

After Syria passed to the Eastern Empire in 395, the temple was rebuilt as a cathedral dedicated to St John the Baptist. The head of Yahya ibn Zakariyya is kept in a crypt now inside the Great Mosque and is venerated by Christians and Muslims alike. Soldiers of the Persian king Khusraw II occupied the city; the article notes that its population was mostly Jacobite Monophysite and hostile to the Melkite [[polity/byzantine-empire|Byzantines]], and that the Persians did not ravage it as they ravaged Jerusalem. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 293}

## The Arab conquest
Arab expeditions crossed the frontier each year, and in Jumada I 13/July 634 the men of [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] moved north through the Jawlan. In Muharram 14/March 635 Byzantine troops fell back on Damascus, and Khalid set up headquarters to the north-east of the town. The article says a group of notables, among them the bishop and the controller-general Mansur ibn Sarjun, father of St John of Damascus, negotiated to spare the people needless suffering, and that in Rajab 14/September 635 the Eastern Gate was opened to the Muslims. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 293}

Several traditions describe the capture. Ibn Asakir has Khalid forcing the Eastern Gate sword in hand while [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah]] entered by the Jabiya Gate after granting terms, the two meeting in the middle of the city; al-Baladhuri has Khalid receiving the surrender at the Eastern Gate and Abu Ubayda entering by force. The article reports that Caetani's argument that Abu Ubayda was not in Syria in year 14 undermines both accounts, that Lammens proposed substituting the name of [[person/yazid-ibn-abi-sufyan|Yazid ibn Abi Sufyan]], and that Lammens judged the story of a divided city unlikely and late, arising only at the time of the Crusades. The Muslims guaranteed the Christians their land, houses and churches, but required a heavy tribute and poll tax. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 293} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 294}

In spring 15/636 a Byzantine army advanced and Khalid withdrew towards the Yarmuk, where the Byzantines were routed on 12 Rajab 15/20 August 636. The city then capitulated a second time, in Dhu al-Qa'da 15/December 636, to Abu Ubayda. The article calls the fall of Damascus an event of incalculable importance, ending almost a thousand years of western supremacy; it says the largely Aramaic-speaking, Monophysite population received the conquerors without reluctance, and that within half a century Arabic had become the common language. Caliph [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] appointed Yazid ibn Abi Sufyan governor. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 294}

## A holy city
Damascus soon took on the character of a holy city, the article says, since tradition recognised places made famous by prophets. Pilgrims went to Jabal Qasiyun to see the cave of Adam, the Cave of the Blood, where Abel was believed to have been killed, and the cavern of Gabriel; Abraham's birthplace was honoured at Barzeh, and the tomb of Moses was placed in the district of Qadam. Jesus was said to have stayed at Rabwa and to be destined to descend at the end of time on a white minaret, either the one at the Eastern Gate or the eastern minaret of the Great Mosque, to fight the Antichrist. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 294}

## The Umayyad capital
In 18/639 Yazid ibn Abi Sufyan died of plague and his brother [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Muawiya]] succeeded him. Elected caliph in 36/656, Muawiya made Damascus his seat, and for a century it was the urban centre of the caliphate. The article notes that Christians predominated at court until the reign of [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik]], when Arabic replaced Greek as the language of administration, and that early discipline, prosperity and tolerance gave way to civil strife and the end of Umayyad rule. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 294}

The new regime changed the plan of the city little. Muawiya remodelled the Byzantine governors' residence as al-Khadra, the Green Palace, with a hostel for ambassadors beside it; [[person/yazid-ibn-muawiya|Yazid I]] rebuilt a Nabataean canal, still called the Nahr Yazid; and a son of Abd al-Malik built a palace outside the walls, remembered in the district name Qasr al-Hajjaj. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 294}

Caliph [[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|al-Walid I]] built [[building/umayyad-mosque|the Great Mosque]], which the article calls one of the most impressive masterpieces of Muslim architecture. Until then the church of St John and a small Muslim prayer place had stood side by side; Muawiya did not press for the church, and Abd al-Malik's offer to buy it failed. When al-Walid wished to enlarge the mosque, no free space remained, and in 86/705 he took the church from the Christians, giving them other places of worship in exchange. The article rejects as a translation error the legend that the church was divided between the two communities, and adds that scholars who studied the building on the spot regard it as a Muslim achievement. The architects kept the ancient enclosure walls and corner towers, about 120 by 80 metres, placed a court to the north and a great prayer hall with a central cupola along the south wall, and covered the walls with marble inlay and glass mosaic. The article judges it a symbol of the political supremacy and moral prestige of Islam. Two new Muslim cemeteries were also opened, one of which, south of the Little Gate, holds most of the Companions buried in the city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 294} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 295}

## Abbasids, Tulunids and Fatimids
After the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasids]] overthrew the Umayyads, Abd Allah ibn Ali took Damascus in Ramadan 132/April 750. The article says Umayyad buildings were sacked, the defences dismantled and tombs profaned, and that the city shrank to a provincial town while the caliphate's capital moved to Iraq. Unrest continued: revolts under al-Mahdi, a punitive expedition under Ja'far al-Barmaki in 180/796 in the reign of [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]], visits by [[person/abd-allah-al-mamun|al-Ma'mun]] in 215/830 and 218/833, and a violent revolt in 240/854. Al-Mutawakkil tried to move his capital to Damascus but stayed only 38 days. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 295}

[[person/ahmad-ibn-tulun|Ahmad ibn Tulun]], governor of Egypt, occupied Damascus in 264/878, and his [[polity/tulunid-dynasty|Tulunid]] son Khumarawayh was assassinated there in 282/896. The [[polity/ikhshidid-dynasty|Ikhshidids]] later held the city in return for tribute to the rulers of Aleppo, and the [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimids]], who replaced them in Cairo in 357/968, expelled the Qarmatians from Damascus in 359/970. The article describes the following century as one of political anarchy and decadence, with the Maghribi soldiers of Cairo exasperating the population. Most houses were built of unfired brick with poplar framing, so that a brawl in 461/1069 between townspeople and Berber soldiers started a fire that seriously damaged the Great Mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 295} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 296}

## Turkish rule and the Burids
The Turkoman chief [[person/atsiz-ibn-uwaq|Atsiz ibn Uvak]] took Damascus in 468/1076, ending Egyptian rule. The Seljuk sultan gave the city to his brother [[person/tutush|Tutush]], who arrived in 471/1079 and had Atsiz killed. On Tutush's death in 488/1095 his son Duqaq held Damascus, with his atabeg [[person/tughtakin-zahir-al-din|Tughtakin]] as the real ruler; after Duqaq died in 497/1104, Tughtakin ruled alone and founded the [[polity/burid-dynasty|Burid dynasty]], which lasted until Nur al-Din's arrival in 549/1154. The article credits Tughtakin's quarter-century with a remarkable improvement in the city's moral and economic state. His son Buri had his vizier killed in 523/1129, which set off a massacre of several hundred Batiniyya, and was himself wounded in an attempt on his life and died in 526/1132. The Burids defended an advance position on the road to Fatimid Egypt, relied on grain from the Hawran and the Biqa, and sometimes negotiated with the Franks, who attacked Damascus in 543/1148. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 296}

## Quarters, suqs and schools
After three centuries of anarchy, the article says, from the fourth/tenth century the Roman-era plan broke up into watertight districts, each with its own mosque, baths, bakery and small market, a chief and a militia. The communities lived apart: Muslims in the west near the citadel and Great Mosque, Christians in the north-east and Jews in the south-east. Houses no longer opened directly on the streets, and each district was served by a street closed at both ends by heavy gates. The city walls, the Great Mosque and the suqs, supervised by the muhtasib, remained the shared points of unity. Colonnades were filled with shops, and covered qaysariyyas traded in jewels, embroideries, carpets and furs. Under Tughtakin new districts such as al-Ukayba, Shaghur and Qasr al-Hajjaj were built. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 296} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 297}

Under Duqaq the city's oldest hospital was built, and in 491/1098 the first madrasa, the Sadiriyya, for the Hanafis. The Shafi'is had the Aminiyya by 514/1120 and the Hanbalis the Sharafiyya in 536/1142; on the eve of Nur al-Din's arrival there were seven madrasas but none for the Maliki school. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 297}

## Nur al-Din
With the arrival of [[person/nur-al-din-mahmud-ibn-zangi|Nur al-Din]] in 549/1154, already master of Aleppo, Damascus became again the capital of a vast, unified Muslim state for the first time since the Umayyads. The article describes it as a rampart of Sunni orthodoxy against the Fatimids and the Franks, with efforts concentrated on jihad. He strengthened the walls, gates and citadel, and set aside parade grounds, the Maydan al-Akhdar and the Maydan al-Khasa. Two families led religious life, the Shafi'i Banu Asakir and the Hanbali Banu Qudama, associated with the district of [[place/al-salihiyya|al-Salihiyya]]. He founded the first school for the teaching of traditions, the Dar al-Hadith, whose first teacher was the historian [[person/ibn-asakir|Ibn Asakir]], the first Maliki madrasa, al-Salahiyya, begun by him and finished by Saladin, the great al-Adiliyya madrasa, the Dar al-Adl, where representatives of the four schools sat with the Shafi'i qadi, and a hospital that also served as a medical school. His funerary madrasa dates from 567/1171, and by the end of his reign Damascus had 242 places of worship inside the walls and 178 outside. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 297}

## The Ayyubids
Nur al-Din died in 569/1174. Agents of [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Saladin]], presenting him as the champion of Islam, won over the population, and he entered Damascus in 571/1176. The victory of [[event/battle-of-hattin-583|Hattin]] in 583/1187 restored Jerusalem to Islam, and Saladin died at Damascus on 27 Safar 589/4 March 1193; he lies in the Aziziyya madrasa north of the Great Mosque. After disputes among his sons and brother, al-Adil was recognised as head of the [[polity/ayyubid-dynasty|Ayyubid]] family in 597/1200. Cairo then became the empire's capital, but Damascus remained an important political, military and economic centre. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 297} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 298}

Quarrels among al-Adil's heirs continued, and the last Ayyubid ruler of Damascus, the prince of Aleppo al-Malik al-Nasir Yusuf, took it in 648/1250. The article credits the dynasty with a princely court that raised population and trade; silk brocades, inlaid copper, gilded glass and tanned lambskins were in demand, and warehouses multiplied beside the qaysariyyas. Gates and citadel were rebuilt or strengthened, the citadel completely from 604/1207. The article says the number of madrasas, about twenty at the time of Ibn Jubayr's visit in 1184, quadrupled in a century, and that the madrasa was increasingly joined to its founder's tomb. It describes a Damascene tomb type with a sixteen-sided cupola, the earliest dated example being 567/1172, and the Ayyubid taste for ashlar façades with alternating basalt and limestone. The seventh/thirteenth century was one of the city's most brilliant epochs, and most monuments adorning it today date from then. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 298}

## The Mamluk period
In Rabi I 658/March 1260 the troops of Hulagu entered Damascus, ending Ayyubid rule; the Mongol advance was halted at Ayn Jalut by the Mamluks. The Church of St Mary was destroyed at this time, and Cairo, where a shadow caliphate had been kept since 656/1258, replaced Damascus as the centre. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 298} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 299}

The city remained the most important of the Syrian province. [[person/baybars|Baybars]] restored the citadel and built the Qasr al-Ablaq on the Maydan al-Akhdar; he died there in 676/1277 and was buried in the Zahiriyya madrasa, now the National Library. To limit the governor's power, the article explains, there were two officials, the deputy of the city and the deputy of the citadel, whose rivalry protected the sultan's authority. A change of sultan often provoked revolt: Sunqur al-Ashqar proclaimed himself sultan in Damascus in 677/1278 against [[person/qalawun|Qalawun]] and was defeated in 679/1280. In 699/1300 a Mongol army under [[person/ghazan-khan|Ghazan]] entered the city and seized the Great Mosque, but could not take the citadel. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 299}

From 712/1312 the governor [[person/tankiz-al-husami|Tankiz]] ruled Syria in fact for almost a quarter of a century. This was the time of [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] and the historian al-Safadi, and Tankiz built a mosque, repaired the Great Mosque and founded a Dar al-Hadith before falling from favour in 740/1340. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 299}

In 803/1401 [[person/timur-lang|Timur]], who had received [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]] in his camp, left Damascus after pillage and fires that spared neither the Great Mosque nor the Dar al-Sa'ada; he took the surviving craftsmen to Samarkand, a deportation the article counts among the city's greatest catastrophes. Recovery came under Barsbay and, above all, under [[person/qaytbay|Qa'itbay]], whose rule ran from 872/1468 to 901/1495 and who visited in 1477. The last Mamluk sultan, Qansuh al-Ghawri, died in battle in Ramadan 922/October 1516. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 299} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 300}

The article observes that many buildings rose in this troubled age, mostly outside the walls, as mausoleums and funerary mosques multiplied, and that the suqs expanded and Damascus remained the market for Hawran grain and a producer of luxury goods and military equipment. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 300}

## The Ottoman period
On 25 Rajab 922/24 August 1516 the [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottoman]] army defeated the Mamluks at Marj Dabiq, and [[person/selim-i-ottoman|Selim I]] entered [[event/capture-of-damascus-1516|Damascus]] on 1 Ramadan 922/28 September 1516. To the population, the article says, it seemed a mere change of masters. Janbirdi al-Ghazali, the governor, rebelled after Selim's death and was killed at Qabun on 17 Safar 927/27 January 1521; Janissaries then destroyed a third of the city. Under [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Sulayman]] Damascus became a modest pashalik with a Hanafi qadi, and between 923/1517 and 1103/1679 it had 133 governors. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 300} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 301}

The city nevertheless prospered through trade and the pilgrimage to Mecca. Following France's capitulations treaty of 942/1535, European merchants imported manufactures and exported raw materials and spices, and numerous khans served as hotels and exchanges, among them the Khan al-Harir of 980/1572, the Khan Sulayman Pasha of 1144/1732 and the great Khan As'ad Pasha of 1165/1752. Damascus was the last stop of the pilgrim road in settled country and the annual gathering place for tens of thousands of pilgrims; the pasha, holding the title of amir al-hajj, led the official caravan south. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 301}

Ottoman patronage brought faience-decorated mosques and a new type, the Turkish mosque with a hemispherical cupola, first seen in the Sulaymaniyya complex of 962/1555 on the site of the Qasr al-Ablaq, to plans by the architect Sinan. [[building/sinan-pasha-mosque-damascus|The mosque of Sinan Pasha]] was built between 994/1586 and 999/1591. The article notes that these religious monuments stood outside the walls, showing that the Great Mosque was no longer the unique gathering place. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 301} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 302}

## The nineteenth century to 1920
From 1832 to 1840 Damascus was held by [[person/ibrahim-pasha-misri|Ibrahim Pasha]], son of [[person/muhammad-ali-pasha|Muhammad Ali]], who allowed Europeans to return and founded a college. After his withdrawal, on 12 July 1860 Muslims attacked the Christian quarters, and the Algerian exile [[person/abd-al-qadir-al-jazairi|Abd al-Qadir]] saved some hundreds of lives; the Sultan punished the outbreak severely and French troops landed. The article notes that the first Arabic newspaper printed in Damascus appeared only in 1897. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 302} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 307}

As governor from 1878, [[person/midhat-pasha|Midhat Pasha]] governed about 150,000 people and improved hygiene and traffic. The Hijaz railway opened in 1908, cutting the journey to Medina to five days instead of forty. In the First World War Damascus was a headquarters of the combined German and Turkish forces; on 6 May 1916 Jemal Pasha hanged twenty-one supporters of the Arab cause, still commemorated as the Day of the Martyrs. Turkish troops left on 30 September 1918, and the Allied forces entered the next day. A national Syrian government formed in December 1919 and Faysal was proclaimed king on 7 March 1920, but French troops entered Damascus on 25 July 1920 and the mandate began. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 307}

## The contemporary period
The article records the 1925 revolt from Jabal Druze, which reached the suburbs of Damascus, and the French bombardment of 18 October 1925. From 1926 the city grew fast in the western manner, and a plan of 1929 guided its extension. Syria's independence was proclaimed in 1941, with constitutional life from 1943; French bombardment on 29 May 1945 was followed by British intervention and the foreign troops' departure. Damascus was capital of the northern region of the United Arab Republic in 1958 and again of the Syrian Arab Republic after 1961. In 1955 it had 408,800 inhabitants, 90 percent of them Sunni Arabs, with Kurdish, Druze and Armenian minorities. Its institutions included the Arab Academy of 1919, the National Library, the Syrian University founded in 1923 and the National Museum founded in 1921. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 308}

## Notes of Golius on Damascus
Golius states that the Umayyad Mosque was built in the year 87 AH under al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik, and that Hulagu, brother of Mongke Khan, conquered and plundered Damascus in the first half of Jumada, the printed year being 698, which conflicts with the known date and is therefore treated as doubtful. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 374}

## For Sprouts
Damascus is the historic capital of Syria, home to the Great Umayyad Mosque with golden mosaics and historic markets.

## For Explorers
Damascus is considered one of the oldest continuously inhabited capitals in the world and a historic center in the Levant. It served as the capital of the Umayyad Caliphate for a century, graced by the Great Umayyad Mosque built by Caliph al-Walid I with golden mosaics, and celebrated for the orchards of the Ghouta, colleges, and libraries.

## For Scholars
The premier metropolis of the Levant and one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities on earth, nestled beneath Mount Qasiyun along the Barada River oasis. Liberated in 14 AH (635 CE) under Caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab through commanders Abu Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah and Khalid ibn al-Walid, it served as the imperial capital of the Umayyad Caliphate (41–132 AH / 661–750 CE). Anchored by the monumental Umayyad Mosque built under Caliph al-Walid I with Byzantine glass mosaics, Damascus regained strategic leadership during the Ayyubid and Mamluk eras, housing prestigious madrasas and producing seminal scholars such as Ibn Asakir, al-Nawawi, and Ibn Kathir.
