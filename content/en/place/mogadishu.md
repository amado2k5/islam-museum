## Overview
Mogadishu, written Maqdashu in both Yaqut and Ibn Battuta, is a port city on the Somali coast of [[place/east-africa|East Africa]]. Yaqut describes it as a coastal trading city, and Ibn Battuta, who visited it, left a long account of its sultan, its judge and its customs of hospitality {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.173} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 p.160}.

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-Buldan
Yaqut fixes the name with a fatha on the mim, a sukun, a fatha on the dal and a shin. He says it is a city at the start of the land of the Zanj, in the south of Yemen, in the country of the Barbar, in the middle of their land, and he explains that these Barbar are not the Berbers of the Maghrib: they are black and resemble the Zanj, a people between the Abyssinians and the Zanj. It lies on the sea coast, and its inhabitants are all strangers, not black, and have no king; their affairs are managed by the foremost among them according to a custom of their own. A merchant who comes to them must lodge with one of these and seek his protection, and the man then looks after him. Sandalwood, ebony, ambergris and ivory are shipped out of it, and these are most of its goods, though other goods are also brought to it from elsewhere {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.173}.

## In Ibn Battuta's Rihla
Ibn Battuta reached Mogadishu from [[place/zeila|Zayla]] after fifteen nights at sea {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 p.160}. Earlier he had described Zayla as the town of the Barbara, a Black people of the Shafi'i school whose lands are a desert two months long, running from Zayla to Mogadishu {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 p.159} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 p.160}.

He calls Mogadishu an exceedingly large city. Its people own many camels, of which they slaughter hundreds each day, and many sheep; they are strong merchants, and cloth named after the city, without equal, is made there and exported to Egypt and elsewhere {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 p.160}.

He describes the custom of hospitality. When a ship reaches the anchorage, small boats carrying young men of the city go out to it; each carries a covered dish of food and presents it to one of the merchants, saying "this is my guest". The merchant lodges only in the house of his host, who sells and buys for him, and a sale or purchase made without the host present is void among them. One of the young men said Ibn Battuta was no merchant but a jurist, and called out that he was the guest of the judge, Ibn al-Burhan, of Egyptian origin; the judge came down to the shore with a party of students and said that by custom a jurist, sharif or righteous man does not disembark before seeing the sultan, so Ibn Battuta went with them to the sultan {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 p.160}.

## The sultan and the judge
He says that the sultan of Mogadishu is called simply "the Shaykh", and was then Abu Bakr ibn al-Shaykh Umar, a Berber by origin who speaks the language of Mogadishu and also knows Arabic. By custom, when a ship arrives, the sultan's boat goes out to ask where it comes from, who owns it, who captains it, what cargo it carries and which merchants are aboard; the findings are put before the sultan, and whoever deserves it is lodged as his guest {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 p.160}.

At the sultan's house a young man came out and returned with a tray of betel leaves and areca nut and a flask of Damascene rose water, and the visitor was told that the sultan had ordered him lodged in the house kept for students, near the sultan's own; food came from the sultan's house with the vizier in charge of guests {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 p.161}.

The meal was rice cooked with ghee in a large wooden dish, with stews of chicken, meat, fish and vegetables on top, unripe bananas cooked in fresh milk, soured milk, and pickled lemon, pepper clusters and green ginger; with them the mango, which he compares to an apple with a stone and says is very sweet when ripe. He describes the people of Mogadishu as very large in body, one man eating as much as a group of his own people would {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 p.161}.

For three days food was brought three times a day. On the fourth, a Friday, the judge, the students and one of the viziers brought him clothes: a silk waist-wrapper worn in place of trousers, a robe of Egyptian cloth, a lined outer garment and an Egyptian turban. He prayed in the mosque behind the maqsura; the sultan then came out into the courtyard and stood at the tomb of his father, who is buried there {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 p.161}.

In the Friday procession the sultan walked to his house while the people went barefoot, four umbrellas of coloured silk held above him, each topped with a golden bird, with drums and horns sounding before him and the emirs, the judge, the jurists and the sharifs about him {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 p.161} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 p.162}. On Saturdays the Shaykh sits for the people in order of rank: the poor, the sharifs, the shaykhs and pilgrims, the viziers and emirs, the leading soldiers; the judge, the viziers, the private secretary and four senior emirs decide cases, the judge taking matters of the Law and the council the rest, and what needs the sultan's view is written to him and answered on the back of the slip {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 p.162}.

From Mogadishu Ibn Battuta then sailed on toward the lands of the coast {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 p.162}.
