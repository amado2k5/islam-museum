## Overview
A city in Yemen, forty-eight miles from [[place/sanaa|Sanaa]]. It lies on the road of the valley that passes through it going down, and in it is grown the wars, a yellow plant resembling saffron, according to al-Idrisi. {cite:nuzhat-al-mushtaq-2002 p.53}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-Buldan
Yaqut explains the word dhimar as what lies behind a man and what he is bound to protect, so that one says of a man that he is a protector of his dhimar, with either a kasra or a fatha. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.7} Citing al-Bukhari, he says Dhamar is a village in Yemen two stages from Sanaa, to which a number of scholars are attributed, among them Abu Hisham Abd al-Malik ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Dhamari, who heard al-Thawri, and the reciter Marwan al-Dhamari, nicknamed Muzna, whom he describes, on the authority of Abu al-Qasim al-Dimashqi, as an ascetic of Damascus who held its judgeship. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.7} He reports that some say Dhamar is a name of Sanaa itself, Sanaa being an Abyssinian word for a strong fortress, said by the Abyssinians when they came to Yemen with Abraha and Aryat, and that others put it sixteen farsakhs from Sanaa. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.7} He adds that the hadith scholars mostly read it with a kasra, while Ibn Durayd gave it with a fatha, and he relays from Ibn Durayd the story of a stone found in the foundations of the Kaaba when Quraysh rebuilt it before Islam, inscribed in the musnad script, assigning Dhamar in turn to Himyar, the Abyssinians, the Persians and Quraysh the merchants. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.7}
