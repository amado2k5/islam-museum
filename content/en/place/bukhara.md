## Overview
Bukhara lies in a large oasis on the lower course of the Zarafshan river, in present-day Uzbekistan, about 222 metres above sea level. The Encyclopaedia of Islam says that little is known of it before Islam and that its history can be reconstructed only from the Arab conquests onward. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1357} It was a city on the Silk Road and a centre of learning in the time of the Samanids. al-Maqdisi reports that no meat like its mutton is to be seen, and that they have a kind of melon called the saff. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p.247}

In the book Futuh al-Buldan there is an account of the khatun of Bukhara and her peace with [[person/ubayd-allah-ibn-ziyad|Ubayd Allah ibn Ziyad]], and then her breaking of it in the days of [[person/said-ibn-uthman|Sa'id ibn Uthman]], and the restoration of peace by a pledge. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407} What is said of the upbringing of [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]] there needs an additional source.

Golius, as quoted in the Farghani edition, calls Ibn Sina, Avicenna, a man of Bukhara, a physician and philosopher of many writings. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 420} He also places Bukhara about thirty-seven parasangs from [[place/samarqand|Samarqand]] on the river of Sughd and says the Arabs took it in the caliphate of Mu'awiya; the numeral for the year of conquest is unclear in the scan and needs checking. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 421}

## Name and early history
The article notes that Alexander the Great's Sogdiana is unlikely to correspond to the modern city, though towns certainly stood in the oasis from early times. The earliest written mention of the name is in Chinese sources of the seventh century CE, and the local form on coins suggests the name may be several centuries older. It judges a derivation from the Sanskrit vihara, "monastery", not improbable, since a vihara stood near the town it takes to be Bukhara's predecessor. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1357} The native dynasty is called the Bukhar Khudat in Islamic sources; its coins carry a Sogdian legend for "king of Bukhara", which shows that the local speech was at least a Sogdian dialect. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1357}

## The Arab conquest and the Bukhar Khudat
The article calls the accounts of the first Arab raids across the Oxus partly legendary. The first Arab army is said to have come before Bukhara in 54/674 under Ubayd Allah ibn Ziyad, when the city was ruled by the widow of the late ruler; according to Narshakhi she governed for fifteen years as regent for her infant son Tughshada. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1357} The article names [[person/qutayba-ibn-muslim|Qutayba ibn Muslim]] as the man who put Islamic rule on a firm footing, and says that in 91/710 he installed the same Bukhar Khudat as prince. Tughshada was murdered in Ramadan 121/739 in the camp of the governor of Khurasan, [[person/nasr-ibn-sayyar|Nasr ibn Sayyar]]. His reign saw several rebellions against Arab suzerainty and Turkish invasions, and in 110/728-9 the city itself was lost and had to be besieged and retaken the next year. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1357} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1358}

In 133/750 a revolt by the Arab Sharik ibn Shaykh against the Abbasids was put down by Ziyad ibn Salih, lieutenant of [[person/abu-muslim-al-khurasani|Abu Muslim]], with the help of the Bukhar Khudat. The latter was nevertheless soon accused of apostasy and put to death on Abu Muslim's order. His brother and successor Bunyat met the same fate, probably in 166/782, when the caliph [[person/al-mahdi|al-Mahdi]] had him executed as a follower of al-Muqanna, whom the article calls a heretic. After this the Bukhar Khudats counted for little in government but stayed influential through their estates; early in the reign of the Samanid Isma'il one was deprived of his lands but kept the same income, twenty thousand dirhams, from the treasury. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1358}

Beside the native prince there was an Arab governor subordinate to the amir of Khurasan at [[place/marw|Marw]], with which Bukhara was more closely tied than with Samarqand; the Bukhar Khudat even had a palace in Marw. When the Khurasan amirs moved to [[place/nishapur|Nishapur]] in the third/ninth century, Bukhara's administration stayed separate from the rest of [[place/transoxiana|Transoxiana]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1358}

## Under the Samanids
Until 260/874 Bukhara was not Samanid but under its own governor answerable to the [[polity/tahirid-dynasty|Tahirids]]. After the Tahirids fell in 259/873, [[person/yaqub-ibn-layth|Ya'qub ibn Layth]] was recognised there briefly as amir of Khurasan. The clergy and people then appealed to the Samanid [[person/nasr-ibn-ahmad|Nasr ibn Ahmad]] at Samarqand, who appointed his younger brother [[person/ismail-ibn-ahmad|Isma'il]] governor, and the [[polity/samanid-dynasty|Samanids]] ruled the city until their fall. Isma'il stayed in Bukhara after Nasr's death in 279/892, when all of Transoxiana came under him, and after his victory over Amr ibn Layth in 287/900, when the caliph confirmed him as amir of Khurasan. The city became the seat of a great kingdom, and the article records that the New Persian literary renaissance flowered there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1358}

## The shape of the city
The article says Bukhara has always stood on the same site and plan as in the third/ninth century, growing outward rather than moving. Arab geographers distinguish the citadel, in Persian kuhandiz and from the seventh/thirteenth century called the arg; the town proper, shahristan; and the suburbs, rabad, between the old town and the wall of Muslim times. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1358}

The citadel, about a mile round and some twenty-three acres, held the palace of the Bukhar Khudat, which Istakhri shows the early Samanids using; according to al-Maqdisi the later Samanids kept only treasuries and prisons there. It also held the oldest Friday mosque, built by Qutayba, supposedly on a pagan temple, later used as the revenue office. The citadel was destroyed several times in the sixth/twelfth and seventh/thirteenth centuries. Unlike most towns it stood outside the shahristan, which was about twice its size and walled with seven gates; in Narshakhi's account the whole town at the time of the conquest was the shahristan alone. A new Friday mosque was built there in 515/1121 by Arslan Khan Muhammad ibn Sulayman, probably in the southern part, where the Mir-i Arab madrasa and the great minaret now stand. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1358}

According to Narshakhi the shahristan was joined to the suburbs and walled in 235/849-50, and in the fourth/tenth century a wider wall with eleven gates was built. The Registan held a pre-Islamic palace. The Samanid Nasr II, who reigned 301-331/914-943, built one there with rooms for the ten state diwans; it is said to have burned under [[person/mansur-ibn-nuh|Mansur ibn Nuh]], 350-365/961-76, yet al-Maqdisi still describes the Dar al-Mulk standing and praises it. Another royal palace lay on a canal north of the citadel. In 360/971 a new musalla was made half a farsakh from the citadel, because the Registan could not hold the worshippers. In the fourth/tenth century the town was crowded and unhealthy, with bad water, as al-Maqdisi and some poets are cited as showing. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1358}

Narshakhi and the geographers also describe the villages and the canals led from the Zarafshan; some canals date from before Islam and many names survive. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1359} Long walls protecting the oasis from the Turks were begun in 166/782 and finished in 215/830, with the city in their western half; they were not maintained after Isma'il ibn Ahmad, and traces survive on the border between the cultivated land of Bukhara and Karmina under the name Kampir Duwal, "wall of the old woman". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1359}

## From the Karakhanids to the Mongols
After the Samanids fell in 389/999 Bukhara lost much of its political weight and passed to the Karakhanids. In the later fifth/eleventh century Shams al-Mulk Nasr ibn Ibrahim built the palace and hunting ground of Shamsabad south of the city; the hunting ground became a musalla in 513/1119. Bukhara kept its name as a centre of learning. In the sixth/twelfth century the scholarly family of the Al-i Burhan built a kind of hierarchy there and for a time made the area independent; its heads, the sadrs, kept good relations with the pagan overlords and took refuge with them in 1207 when a popular rising drove them out. In that year the city passed to Muhammad ibn Takash, the Khwarazm Shah, who renovated the citadel. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1359}

Ibn al-Athir reports that Bukhara submitted to the army of [[person/genghis-khan|Chingiz Khan]] in Dhu l-Hijja 616/February 1220. The town was sacked and burned except for the Friday mosque and a few palaces, but soon recovered as a populous seat of learning. A peasant revolt under Mahmud Tarabi in 636/1238 was put down by the Mongols, and a Mongol princess paid for a madrasa there, the Khaniyya. On 7 Rajab 671/28 January 1273 the army of Abaqa, Il-Khan of Persia, destroyed and emptied the city; it was rebuilt and ravaged again in Rajab 716/September-October 1316 by the Mongols of Persia and the Chaghatay prince Yasawur. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1359} Under the Chaghatays and [[polity/timurid-empire|Timurids]] it seems to have had little political role, though Baha al-Din Naqshband, who died in 791/1389, and his order flourished there, and [[person/ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]], who died in 853/1449, built a madrasa in the centre of town. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1359}

## Uzbeks, emirs and Russians
Shibani Khan's Uzbeks took Bukhara at the end of 905/1500, and it stayed in Uzbek hands until the Russian Revolution, apart from two brief interruptions. Two Shibanid princes, Ubayd Allah ibn Mahmud, 918-946/1512-1539, and Abd Allah ibn Iskandar, 964-1006/1557-1598, made it their capital, and the later Janid or Ashtarkhanid dynasty also ruled from it. Native historians regarded the reign of Abd al-Aziz, 1055-91/1645-80, as the last great period; afterwards princes became independent and authority passed largely to an atalik. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1359} Nadir Shah took the city in 1153/1740. The atalik Muhammad Rahim of the Mankit then proclaimed himself khan, and his successor Murad claimed the title of amir in 1199/1785. Under Haydar, 1215-1242/1800-26, religious ordinances were enforced more harshly. Nasr Allah, 1242-1277/1827-60, strengthened the throne and created a standing army; native chroniclers and European travellers describe him, in the article's words, as a bloodthirsty tyrant. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1359} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1360}

Under Muzaffar al-Din, 1860-85, the emirate submitted to Russia and ceded Jizzakh, Ura-Tube, Samarqand and Katta Kurghan in 1868, though in 1873 it gained territory in the west from Khiva. Under Abd al-Ahad, 1885-1910, the border with Afghanistan was fixed on the Panj river by agreement of England and Russia, and from 1887 a railway crossed the domains, its Bukhara station ten miles away in the town now called Kagan. Mir Alim, who succeeded in 1910 after schooling in St Petersburg, ruled until the Revolution drove him to Afghanistan; Bukhara is now part of the Uzbek republic and a large cotton-growing area. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1360}

## Monuments and research
Archaeological work has advanced greatly since the 1930s. The article lists as the main surviving monuments the so-called mausoleum of Isma'il the Samanid from the fourth/tenth century; the Kalan minaret, 45.3 metres high, of the sixth/twelfth century; the Magoki Attar mosque, last rebuilt in 1547; the Namazgah mosque of 1119; the mausoleum of Sayf al-Din Bukharzi, who died in 1261; the Chashma mausoleum; the madrasa of Ulugh Beg; the sixteenth-century Kalan mosque beside the older minaret; the Mir-i Arab madrasa; and the Khwaja Zayn al-Din mosque, often restored. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1360}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-Buldan
[[person/yaqut-al-hamawi|Yaqut al-Hamawi]] describes Bukhara as one of the greatest and most splendid cities of Transoxiana, reached by crossing from Amul al-Shatt, two days from the Oxus, and the seat of the Samanid kingdom. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.485} He says he looked for the derivation of its name and found none, that in his day its fruit was carried to Marw, twelve stages away, and that it lay seven days or thirty-seven parasangs from Samarqand. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.486}

### The city and its walls
Yaqut quotes an author on the regions who had seen no town in Islam with finer surroundings than Bukhara: from the top of its citadel you see nothing but unbroken green. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.486} Its old name was Bumijkath; it was built of latticed timber; a wall twelve parasangs by twelve enclosed its palaces, gardens and villages, a second wall about a parasang square ran inside it, then the strong wall of the city itself, with a citadel outside housing the Samanid governors of Khurasan and the congregational mosque at the citadel gate. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.486} The Sughd river, at its last reach, runs through its suburb. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.487}

### Coinage
Under the Samanids the people of Bukhara dealt in dirhams, not dinars, treating gold as a commodity; they had dirhams called *ghitrifi*, alloyed of iron, brass, lead and other metals, current only in Bukhara and its districts, and others called *musayyabi* and *muhammadi*. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.487}

### The conquest
Yaqut relates from al-Baladhuri that when Ziyad died in 53 AH Mu'awiya gave Khurasan to his son Ubayd Allah, who crossed the river with twenty-four thousand men; Bukhara was then ruled by a woman called the *Khatun*, who called on the Turks, who were routed, and she then made peace for a million. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.488} Sa'id ibn Uthman ibn Affan crossed the river in 55 AH; the Khatun broke the treaty and then restored it after the Sughdians and Turks were beaten. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.489} Yaqut says he found nothing more on the city until 87 AH, when Qutayba ibn Muslim crossed to it, was besieged for four months by the forces of Sughd, Farghana, al-Shash and Bukhara, defeated them and took it, after which "Bukhara was clear for the Muslims". {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.489}

### People named after it
Among those named after the city Yaqut lists [[person/al-bukhari|Muhammad ibn Isma'il al-Bukhari]], author of the *Sahih*, born in 194 AH and died on the night of Eid al-Fitr 256 AH at Khartank after he had been forced out of Bukhara, {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.489} and Abu Ali ibn Sina, vizier to the Buyid Shams al-Dawla of Hamadhan, who died on Saturday 6 Sha'ban 428 AH. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.490} He also cites, without grading it, a report with a chain going back to Hudhayfa ibn al-Yaman on the merit of a city beyond the Oxus called Bukhara, {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.487} and verses by poets who disparaged the filth of its lanes. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.487} {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.488}
