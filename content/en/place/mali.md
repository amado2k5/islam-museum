## Overview
Mali is the name of a Sahel state today, standing on the land of the old kingdom of Mali. This page sets out what Ibn Battuta wrote in his Rihla about the kingdom of Mali under Mansa Sulayman: by his own dates he entered the capital in 753 AH and left it in 754 AH, an eyewitness account from the eighth century AH. The editor of the edition used here says in his introduction that the city of Mali, once the seat of the king of the Sudan, has vanished, and that the present Republic of Mali takes its name from it. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.20} The page does not narrate the history of the modern state.

## The road to Mali
Ibn Battuta says he left Sijilmasa on the first of Muharram 753 AH with a caravan led by Abu Muhammad Yandakan al-Massufi, which included a group of Sijilmasa merchants. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.686} After twenty-five days they reached the salt village of Taghaza, which he calls a village with nothing good in it: its houses and mosque are built of blocks of salt and roofed with camel hides, and only the slaves of the Massufa live there, digging the salt and living on dates and meat brought to them. He says a camel-load of it sells at Walata for between eight and ten mithqals and in the city of Mali for twenty to thirty, sometimes forty, and that the people of the Sudan use salt as money as others use gold and silver. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.687} They then crossed a ten-day desert with almost no water, and reached Walata (Ayawalatan) on the first of Rabi I, two full months after leaving Sijilmasa; he calls it the first district of the Sudan, where the sultan's deputy is called the farba, at that time Farba Husayn. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.689}

He stayed at Walata about fifty days. He says he first regretted coming because of the farba's disdain for the white merchants and the meagre hospitality offered, but that the townspeople then treated him generously, among them the qadi Muhammad ibn Abd Allah ibn Yanumar and his brother the teacher Yahya. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.690} He describes the Massufa who live there as Muslims who keep the prayers, study jurisprudence and memorise the Qur'an, yet he is struck that people are named after their maternal uncles and that only a sister's sons inherit, which he says he had seen nowhere else except among the non-Muslim Hindus of Malabar, and by the free companionship between women and men outside their families, a custom he does not approve by the standard of his own country. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.690} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.691}

He then hired a Massufa guide and set out for Mali with three companions; the journey from Walata takes twenty-four days for a fast traveller. He describes a road lined with huge trees, and says a traveller there carries neither food nor coin but pieces of salt, glass beads and a few aromatics, and buys his food from the village women. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.691} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.692} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.693} Ten days from Walata he reached the village of Zaghari, where Sudanese merchants called Wanjarata live together with a group of whites of the Ibadi school, called Sughanaghu, while the Sunni Maliki whites are called Turi. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.693} He then came to the great river, which he calls the Nile; the editor adds in a footnote that the river meant is the Niger, not the Nile. On it he names Karsakhu, Kabara and Zagha, says the rulers of Kabara and Zagha owe obedience to the king of Mali, and says the river runs on to Timbuktu and Gao. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.693} At the river Sansara, about ten miles from Mali, he crossed by ferry unhindered, entered the city, lodged first by its cemetery and then in the quarter of the white residents. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.694}

## In the capital
He took a house that Muhammad ibn al-Faqih al-Jazuli had rented for him next to his own, and was visited by the qadi Abd al-Rahman, whom he describes as a Black pilgrim of fine character, by the interpreter Dugha, and by several of the white residents, among them Shams al-Din ibn al-Naqwish the Egyptian and Ali al-Zawdi of Marrakesh. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.694} Ten days after arriving he ate a porridge of a plant called qafi, and he and his companions, six in all, fell ill; one died, he fainted during the dawn prayer, and he stayed ill for two months. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.695}

He names the sultan of Mali as Mansa Sulayman, explains that mansa means sultan, and calls him a stingy king from whom no great gift is to be hoped. The sultan held a meal for the mourning of the Marinid sultan Abu al-Hasan, to which he summoned the amirs, jurists, qadi and preacher; they read the Qur'an and prayed for Abu al-Hasan and for Mansa Sulayman, and Ibn Battuta greeted the sultan there. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.695} The hospitality sent afterwards turned out to be three loaves of bread, a piece of beef fried in gharti and a gourd of sour milk, and he laughed at how highly they regarded it. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.696} After two further months, at the start of Ramadan, he spoke to the sultan through the interpreter Dugha, complaining that he had been four months in his land without hospitality; the sultan replied that he had not seen him or known of him, then ordered him a house and a stipend. On the night of 27 Ramadan he gave him thirty-three and a third mithqals along with the sum distributed to the qadi, preacher and jurists as zakat, and on his departure a hundred mithqals of gold. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.696}

## The sultan's audiences and etiquette
Ibn Battuta describes the sultan's pavilion, where he sits most days, with wooden tiers covered in plates of silver and gold; people know he is sitting when the curtains are raised and a patterned handkerchief appears on a silk tassel, whereupon drums and trumpets sound and about three hundred slaves come out with bows and spears. Two saddled horses and two rams, said to ward off the evil eye, stand by. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.696} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.697} At other times he sits in the audience court on a platform under a tree called the pempi, in a red robe of Byzantine cloth and a gold cap, with singers carrying gold and silver instruments before him. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.697} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.698} He says the people of the Sudan show their king extreme humility: they swear by his name, a summoned man enters in old clothes, strikes the ground with his elbows, and throws dust on his head and back when speaking to him. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.698}

He attended both Eids in Mali. People went out to the prayer ground near the sultan's palace in white clothes; after the prayer and sermon a man with a spear explained the preacher's words to the people in their own language, and the sermon was exhortation, praise of the sultan and encouragement to obey him. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.699} On Eid the poets, called jalis, recite before the sultan in a costume of feathers with a red beak; Ibn Battuta reports what he was told, that their verse is a kind of exhortation reminding the sultan of the good deeds of earlier kings who sat on the pempi, and that this custom is older than Islam among them. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.700}

He tells stories he witnessed. A jurist from a distant land reports a locust plague to the sultan, who declares himself innocent of injustice and says he will punish any wrongdoer; a merchant of the Massufa cries out in the mosque on a Friday that he has been wronged by the governor of Walata, the sultan sends for the governor, the qadi finds for the merchant and the governor is dismissed. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.701} He also recounts a rift between the sultan and his senior wife Qasa, a word meaning queen, who shares his rule and whose name is mentioned with his from the pulpit, and the events that followed, including her taking refuge in the preacher's house. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.701} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.702}

## What he approved and disliked
Among the good things he lists the rarity of injustice and the general security, so that neither traveller nor resident fears a thief or a robber; that they do not touch the estate of a white man who dies among them but leave it with a trusted man until the rightful heir takes it; their regular attendance at congregational prayer and their white clothes on Friday; and their care for memorising the Qur'an, so much that they put their children in shackles until they have memorised it. He saw the qadi on Eid with his sons shackled, and the qadi said he would not free them until they had memorised the Qur'an. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.703} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.704} Among the things he disliked, by the standards of his time and country, he lists servants, slave girls and young girls appearing unclothed, and other customs he mentions. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.704}

## Mansa Musa and his predecessors
He reports that Mansa Sulayman was disliked by the Sudanese for his stinginess, that before him came Mansa Magha and before Magha Mansa Musa, a generous and virtuous king who loved the white men and treated them well, and who gave Abu Ishaq al-Sahili four thousand mithqals in a single day. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.702} He tells of a man from Tlemcen who did Mansa Musa a small kindness when he was a boy and was repaid, once Musa was sultan, with seven hundred mithqals, clothing and slaves. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.703} On the road away from Mali he mentions a Sudanese governor and pilgrim called Farba Magha who had gone on the pilgrimage with Mansa Musa. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.705} See [[person/mansa-musa|Mansa Musa]], [[person/mansa-sulayman|Mansa Sulayman]], [[person/abu-ishaq-al-sahili|Abu Ishaq al-Sahili]] and [[event/mansa-musa-hajj-724|the pilgrimage of Mansa Musa]].

## Leaving Mali: Timbuktu and Gao
Ibn Battuta says he entered Mali on 14 Jumada I 753 AH and left on 22 Muharram 754 AH, by the road to Mima. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.704} The editor's introduction, by contrast, says he stayed about one year and seven months, an estimate that differs from the dates Ibn Battuta himself gives; the pages read do not settle which is meant. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.20} At a large inlet of the river he saw river horses and describes how they are hunted. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.705} He then reached Timbuktu, four miles from the river, mostly inhabited by Massufa and governed by Farba Musa, where lie the tomb of the poet Abu Ishaq al-Sahili of Granada and that of the Alexandrian merchant Siraj al-Din ibn al-Kuwayk. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.707} He tells that Mansa Musa had stayed during his pilgrimage in a garden of Siraj al-Din outside Cairo and borrowed money from him, that Siraj al-Din sent his agent to collect it and then went himself, and died at Timbuktu as the guest of al-Sahili. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.707} From Timbuktu he went down the river in a small boat hollowed from a single tree to Gao, which he calls a large city on the river, one of the best, largest and most fertile in the Sudan, with rice, milk, chickens, fish and a fine cucumber, where cowries serve as currency as in Mali; he stayed there about a month. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.708} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.709} He then left with a large caravan for Takedda. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.709} See [[place/timbuktu|Timbuktu]], [[place/gao|Gao]], [[place/sijilmasa|Sijilmasa]] and [[event/ibn-battuta-journey-to-mali-1352|Ibn Battuta's journey to Mali]].

## A note on the source
This page rests on one account, Ibn Battuta's, dictated after his return; what he says of Mali is what he saw or heard in the period he names, and he states where he reports others. The edition used gives no Gregorian dates for these stops, so only the Hijri dates he gives are written here. The page does not relay what he reports of distant peoples south of Mali, which he says he heard and did not see.
