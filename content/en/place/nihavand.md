## Overview
Nihavand is a town in western Persia, near which took place the well-known battle in the time of Umar ibn al-Khattab, in the year 20 or 21 of the Hijra. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 465}

Golius notes that Nihavand is about fourteen farsakhs south of Hamadhan and counts it among the oldest cities of the Jibal, whose good fruit was carried to Ashur. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 464}

Golius reports from his sources that Umar ibn al-Khattab fought a battle here in the year 20 or 21 of the Hijra, and that Nu'man ibn Muqarrin, whom the text calls Nu'man ibn Mikran, entered the city after the negotiation ended in peace, so that it was taken by surrender. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 465}

The mountain of Alvand lies between it and Hamadhan. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 464}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut describes Nihavand as a great city south of Hamadhan, three days away, and the oldest city in the Jibal. He gives several accounts of the name: from Hisham, that it was named because they found it as it was; another that it was built by Noah and was originally Nuh-awand, shortened; and from Hamza that it was originally Bunuhawand, meaning doubled good. He quotes Ptolemy that it lies in the fourth climate. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.313}

On the conquest he says it came in the year 19, or 20 according to some, and quotes Abu Bakr al-Hudhali from Muhammad ibn al-Hasan that the battle of Nihavand was in the year 21, in the days of Umar ibn al-Khattab, with al-Nu'man ibn Muqarrin al-Muzani as commander of the Muslims. He relates that Umar said that if al-Nu'man fell the commander should be Hudhayfa ibn al-Yaman, then Jarir ibn Abd Allah, then al-Mughira ibn Shu'ba, then al-Ash'ath ibn Qays; al-Nu'man, a Companion, was killed, Hudhayfa took the banner, and the conquest was completed at his hand by a treaty. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.313}

On its administration he quotes al-Mubarak ibn Sa'id from his father: Nihavand was a conquest of the people of Kufa and Dinawar of the people of Basra. When the population of Kufa grew they needed districts whose tax had been fixed by treaty, so Dinawar was assigned to them and the people of Basra were compensated with Nihavand, which is near Isfahan; the difference in tax between the two went to Kufa. Nihavand therefore became known as Mah al-Basra and Dinawar as Mah al-Kufa, in the days of Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.313}

From Ibn al-Faqih he reports two talismans on the mountain of Nihavand, a fish and an ox made of snow that never melt in winter or summer and are said to be for the water, so that it should not run short. He quotes Mis'ar ibn Muhalhil, Abu Dulaf, who saw a fish and an ox of stone of fine form said to be a talisman against certain afflictions, fine remains of the Persians, a fortress of wonderful construction and great height in the middle of the town, the graves of Arabs who were martyred in the early days of Islam, a pure and wholesome water, and the khilaf tree from which polo sticks are made, unmatched elsewhere for hardness and quality. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.313}

He also notes a reed from which dharira, a funeral perfume, is made, which has no scent while it is in Nihavand or its districts and gives off its scent once carried past the pass called Aqabat al-Rakab, a tale for which he found support in medical books, and a black clay for seals along the banks of its river. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.313} {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.314}

## The museum's identification
This record is Yaqut's Nihavand; the museum links them by the shared name and the battle of Nihavand in Umar's time. The record's distance of about fourteen farsakhs south of Hamadhan comes from Golius' Latin notes, while Yaqut says three days; the two are stated differently and the museum has not reconciled them. On the date, Yaqut gives 19 or 20 for the conquest and 21 for the battle, and Golius 20 or 21.
