## Overview
The Abbasid Caliphate was the caliphal state that rose in Khurasan and Iraq on the ruins of the Umayyad state. Muhammad Suhayl Taqqush describes its coming as a comprehensive revolution in Islamic history, not merely the transfer of a pledge of allegiance from one caliph to another: it changed Muslim society and opened to non-Arab Muslims the door to appearing in political, cultural and economic life. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.7} It lasted through four historical ages until the fall of Baghdad to the Mongols in 656 AH / 1258 CE. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.8} This page treats the state as a political and cultural region, with Baghdad, Samarra and Kufa among its cities, as Taqqush tells it, and adds Hasan Ibrahim Hasan on the building of Baghdad.

## The Abbasid call and the founding of the state
Taqqush says the Abbasid imam made Kufa the seat of the missionaries because it was known for its loyalty to the Prophet's family and its hostility to the Umayyads, and because it could link al-Humayma with Merv, the field of the movement; Muhammad ibn Ali advised his missionaries to work as traders to hide their aim and to spread the call with wisdom. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.21} He divides the call into two phases: the first from the opening of the second Hijri century until Abu Muslim al-Khurasani joined, entirely secret and free of violence; the second from Abu Muslim's joining until the fall of the Umayyads and the rise of the Abbasid state in 132 AH. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush pp.21-22} He names the missionaries of Iraq as Maysara al-Abdi, Bukayr ibn Mahan and Abu Salama al-Khallal, and those of Khurasan, among them Abu Ikrima al-Sarraj and Sulayman ibn Kathir al-Khuza'i, chief of the chiefs. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.22} The imam Muhammad died in 125 AH / 743 CE and left the imamate to his son Ibrahim. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.22} See [[group/abbasid-dawa|the Abbasid call]] and [[event/abbasid-dawa-in-khurasan|the Abbasid call in Khurasan]].

In his introduction Taqqush recounts that Abu Muslim al-Khurasani, whom Ibrahim ibn Ali had sent to Khurasan, entered Merv and took Fars; his armies then moved on Iraq, defeated the Umayyad armies and entered Basra and Kufa in 132 AH / 749 CE; Abu al-Abbas al-Saffah then proclaimed the Abbasid caliphate in Kufa, and his commanders finished off the remaining Umayyads. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.8} Muhammad Bey al-Khudari adds that Abu al-Abbas was given the pledge at Kufa in Rabi al-Awwal 132 AH, that his army under his uncle Abdallah ibn Ali met Marwan ibn Muhammad at the river Zab in Jumada II of the same year and Marwan was beaten, and that Marwan was killed at Busir before the end of Dhu al-Hijja, which ended the Umayyad state. {cite:khudari-umayyad-state p.353} See [[person/abu-muslim-al-khurasani|Abu Muslim al-Khurasani]], [[person/abu-al-abbas-al-saffah|Abu al-Abbas al-Saffah]], [[person/marwan-ibn-muhammad|Marwan ibn Muhammad]] and [[place/al-zab|al-Zab]].

Taqqush explains the change by saying that the Umayyads' unbalanced policy towards non-Arab peoples and some Arab groups caused a plain stumbling that the Abbasids exploited, and that Khurasan was ready ground because it was far from the caliphal centre, its Arabs were in dispute and it held many Persians and clients; he notes that historians differ in interpreting it, some seeing a Persian revolt against Arab rule, some a mere removal of the Umayyads, and one group calling it inevitable. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush pp.7-8}

## Frontiers and the four ages
Taqqush describes the frontiers of the Islamic world when the caliphate passed to the Abbasids as including the provinces of Jurjan and Tukharistan, bordering the lands of the Turks and China, reaching Kashmir in the south-east and Nubia in the south, the edge of the desert south of the Maghrib, the Caucasus mountains and Armenia in the north, the Byzantine frontier, and in al-Andalus the south of the Frankish lands. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush pp.8-9}

He divides Abbasid history into four ages. The first runs from 132 AH to the death of al-Wathiq in 232 AH / 847 CE, an age of strength, expansion and prosperity. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.10} The second begins in 232 AH, when the ruling body weakened, provinces broke away, regional states arose, the Turks came to the fore and the caliph withdrew into the shade. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.10} The third begins in 334 AH / 945 CE, the age of the Buyids, who kept the office of caliph but held the reins of power. The fourth begins in 447 AH / 1055 CE, the age of the Seljuks, who followed Sunni Islam and regarded the Abbasid caliph as their spiritual head; in it the caliphate called on the Khwarazmians and then on the Mongols, and fell beneath their blows in 656 AH / 1258 CE. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.11}

## Baghdad, the City of Peace
Abu Jafar al-Mansur began building his capital in 145 AH / 762 CE, bringing thousands of workers and craftsmen from the provinces under the supervision of engineers; it took about four years and was finished in 149 AH / 766 CE. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.71} Al-Mansur called it Madinat al-Salam, the official name in documents and on coins; Taqqush judges that the name Baghdad is probably Persian, from bagh and dad, "gift of God". {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.71}

On the site, Taqqush says it lay on the western bank of the Tigris, above the point where it meets the river Sarat, a link between Syria and Persia and a trading centre on the water and land routes, receiving provisions from Syria, the Jazira, Egypt, India, Sind, Basra, Wasit, Armenia and Azerbaijan. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.69} Al-Mansur sent scouts to choose a place; they chose one near Barma south of Mosul, but he turned to another place whose air pleased him and consulted the people of the district, a dihqan and the monks. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush pp.69-70} Before Islam the place held a Sasanian palace, a bridge of boats and small Christian villages around monasteries, so the city was not built on empty ground. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.70} See [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]].

On the plan, Taqqush says the city was round, ringed by double walls and an outer moat six metres wide, its four gates fitted with corridors turning off from the outer doors so that an attacker's flank was exposed; at its centre stood the caliph's palace, the mosque, the palaces of his sons and the government offices. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush pp.68-71} Hasan Ibrahim Hasan gives the details: two walls, the inner fifty cubits wide at the base and the outer thirty cubits high, and four gates, the Kufa gate, the Basra gate, the Khurasan gate and the Syria gate. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim 2 p.302} He reports that al-Mansur chose engineers and surveyors of good character, that the labourers came from Syria, Mosul, Basra, Kufa, Wasit and Daylam, and that historians put their number at one hundred thousand, a figure Hasan thinks plainly exaggerated. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim 2 p.300} He also relates what "it is said": that al-Mansur laid the first brick with his own hand after the basmala, and that Abu Hanifa supervised the counting of bricks with a reed; Hasan gives these as reports and does not vouch for them. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim 2 p.300} Taqqush, citing Abd al-Aziz al-Duri, sees Persian influence in the plan: the caliph is set apart from his subjects in a lofty place hard to reach, and the idea of a circle and closed districts, which he takes to point to an absolute authority at odds with the tolerance of Islam and what was known of the Umayyads. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.72} This is Taqqush's interpretation, not the museum's judgement.

In 151 AH / 768 CE al-Mansur built al-Rusafa, or East Baghdad, for his son al-Mahdi on the east bank of the Tigris; it was known as the Camp of al-Mahdi, was given a wall, a parade ground and a garden, was joined to the city by three bridges, and was finished in 159 AH / 776 CE under al-Mahdi. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.72}

## The House of Wisdom and translation
Taqqush says al-Mamun founded the House of Wisdom in 215 AH / 830 CE, a scholarly institute with a library for copying foreign books, a house for translating and studying them and an observatory, and that missions went out to look for Greek books. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.135} He adds that the translators were paid from the treasury and that the movement became official, supported by the state. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.135} Among the translators at the House of Wisdom was Hunayn ibn Ishaq, who rendered many works of Galen, Aristotle, Plato, Hippocrates and Ptolemy and, it is said, was rewarded by al-Mamun with the weight of his books in gold; his son Ishaq completed the work. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.136} He says al-Khwarizmi used the Indian numerals in his tables in 198 AH / 814 CE and wrote a treatise on the numerals of the Indians in 210 AH / 825 CE. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.136} See [[group/bayt-al-hikma|the House of Wisdom]], [[person/hunayn-ibn-ishaq|Hunayn ibn Ishaq]] and [[person/al-khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]].

## Samarra and the Turks
Taqqush holds that al-Mutasim took the Turks into the army on a large scale under commanders of their own, drawing them from Transoxiana by purchase, capture or gifts, and settled them in Samarra, which he built for them; he deprived the Arabs of army command and struck their names from the registers, which angered them, and some plotted to kill him, but the plot failed. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.145} He believes the policy had far-reaching results, weakening the caliph's authority, and that the rise of breakaway states may be one of them, though it was not clear until after the murder of al-Mutawakkil in 247 AH / 861 CE. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush pp.145-146} See [[place/samarra|Samarra]] and [[person/al-mutasim|al-Mutasim]].

In his reign al-Afshin ended the movement of Babak al-Khurrami in 223 AH / 838 CE after a series of campaigns; he entered al-Badhdh on the tenth of Ramadan 222 AH, and Babak was handed over and brought to Samarra. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush pp.140-141} Al-Afshin himself was later tried and died in prison in 226 AH / 841 CE; Taqqush notes that the record of the trial does not mention the plot but charges him with never truly embracing Islam. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.146}

## The fall of Baghdad
Taqqush relates that Hulagu asked the caliph al-Mustasim for an army to help him against the Ismailis; the caliph took counsel and his advisers told him to refuse because Hulagu wished to empty Baghdad of its defenders, and he refused. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.252} Hulagu then sent a letter of reproach demanding that fortifications be razed and moats filled, which the caliph rejected, expecting help from the Ayyubids in Syria and the Mamluks in Egypt, whose own troubles kept them away. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush pp.252-253} Drawing on Rashid al-Din, he reports that the vizier Ibn al-Alqami advised money, gifts and the khutba and coinage in Hulagu's name, as in the days of the Buyids and Seljuks, while the junior dawatdar Mujahid al-Din Aybak refused and insisted on resistance, and the caliph took the course of resistance. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.253}

The Mongol armies reached Baghdad on 13 Muharram 656 AH / January 1258 CE and laid a close siege. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.253} Taqqush says Hulagu asked the caliph to send Sulayman Shah and the dawatdar, then sent them back to Baghdad to fetch their followers on the pretext of exile to Syria and Egypt; Baghdad's soldiers and many residents went out with them and Hulagu ordered them all killed; and that Ibn al-Alqami, according to Ibn Kathir, persuaded the caliph to surrender. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.254} On 4 Safar 656 AH / February 1258 CE the caliph came out and gave himself and his capital up; the Mongols entered and ravaged the city for a week, destroying mosques and many books, and many scholars perished; al-Mustasim and two of his sons were killed. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush pp.254-255} See [[person/hulagu|Hulagu]] and [[person/al-mustasim-billah|al-Mustasim]].

He adds that the office of caliph stood vacant until the Mamluk sultan al-Zahir Baybars installed a member of the Abbasid family as caliph in Egypt in 659 AH / 1261 CE; this lasted until the Ottoman sultan Selim took Egypt in 923 AH / 1517 CE. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.255}

## The author's assessment
Taqqush closes by calling Abu Jafar al-Mansur the true founder of the state, and by saying that the first Abbasid age took on a Persian character while keeping an Arab stamp, so that a new culture arose with the Persian as its general frame and Arab details within it. {cite:tarikh-dawla-abbasiyya-taqqush p.256} This is a modern author's appraisal and does not replace other sources.
