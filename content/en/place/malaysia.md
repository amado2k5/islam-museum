## Overview
Malaysia, as the place card says, is a federal state in Southeast Asia whose capital is Kuala Lumpur; it did not exist when the sources behind this page were written. Hadir al-Alam al-Islami, by Lothrop Stoddard with Shakib Arslan's commentary, written in the 1920s and 1930s, uses the word "Malaysia" and "Malaysians" in a wide sense for the whole Malay world, from the Dutch islands to the Philippines, not for today's state. At the end of its account of Islam in the Far East it says the end-point of that Islam is in Malaysia, and it puts the Muslims of the Dutch colonies, Borneo and the other islands as far as Papua and the Philippines at about forty million. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 scan p.373} This page therefore keeps to what the sources say about lands that lie today within Malaysia: northern Borneo under the names of the time, a mention of Malacca, and what Ibn Battuta reports of "Mul Jawa". This is little beside what they say of the wider archipelago, and the page should be read with [[place/indonesia|Indonesia]].

## "Mul Jawa" in Ibn Battuta's Rihla
[[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]] says he sailed from Sumatra in a junk fitted out by its sultan, travelled twenty-one nights along that sultan's country, and then reached "Mul Jawa", which he calls a land of unbelievers two months' journey long, rich in aromatic spices and fine aloeswood of the Qaqula and Qamari kinds, with Qaqula and Qamara among its towns; he says that the sultan al-Zahir's own country has only benzoin, camphor and a little clove and Indian aloeswood, and that most of these are in Mul Jawa. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.633} He names the land no otherwise. The introduction of the edition used here says that "Mul Jawa" is the Malay peninsula, part of Malaysia today; that is the editor's identification, not Ibn Battuta's. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.18}

## Arabs and Malays in Arslan's account
In his chapter "Islam in Java and its neighbours" Arslan summarises what Dutch scholars wrote. He says the Arab geographers knew the lands of Malaysia from early times, and that in the tenth, eleventh and twelfth centuries many Arab travellers toured the coasts of India, China and the Malay islands. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.362} He adds that the Arab was made a trader by nature, and that the Malays learned from the Arabs the principles of trade, the ways of selling and bargaining, the fixing of the prices of grain and goods, the founding of warehouses, and the bill of exchange. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.366}

In the lecture of Sayyid Ismail al-Attas included in the book, many literate people of those islands write to one another in Malay in Arabic letters, and more than a quarter of the words of that language are taken from Arabic. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.386} This is what the book says of the Malay language in general, a language that extends beyond today's borders.

## Northern Borneo: Sarawak, Brunei and Labuan
The book says Borneo is divided between Britain and Holland, and that the British share is the north: the principality of "Sarawak", the lands of the English company in north Borneo, the island of Labuan and the town of Brunei; among its peaks it names "Kinabalu" in the north of the island, 4,175 metres high in its figures. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.368} See [[place/borneo|Borneo]] and [[place/brunei|Brunei]].

A little further on Arslan tells the origin of the principality of Sarawak, whose capital is Kuching. An English sailor named James Brooke reached Brunei in his own ship and found conditions unbearable, with oppression, no security and people stripped of their money. An amir there called Muda Hasan relied on Captain James and entrusted affairs to him, and he set things right; the sultan of Brunei then recognised him as ruler of Sarawak. The text says he used pagans against Muslims in 1842, that the English government sent him some help in his fights with Arabs and Malays, and that he treated Europeans and natives alike, so that the people of the principality prospered and its borders widened. He died in 1863 and his nephew Charles Brooke succeeded him; the realm then came under British protection. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.370} In a footnote Arslan says he wishes Brooke's government and other European governments would follow this policy of equal treatment. The year of the sultan's recognition is not legible in the scanned text.

The book puts the population of the whole of Borneo, not only the north, at one million seven hundred thousand, among them about sixty thousand Chinese, a few thousand Arabs and about two thousand Europeans. The Dayaks live inland and Muslim Malays on the coast; the Muslims, it says, always hold dominion over the Dayaks, a Dayak who accepts Islam is counted a Malay, and the Malays hold the river mouths that are the roads of communication and so grasp the reins of trade. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.370-371}

## Malacca
In a list of the lineages of the Alawi sayyids of Hadramawt, given in the book's account of Islam in Madagascar, it names a branch settled in East Africa and in "Malaka", that is Malacca, in the Malay land; their cousins are known as Al-Qadri. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 scan p.165} See [[place/malacca|Malacca]], [[polity/malacca-sultanate|the Sultanate of Malacca]] and [[place/malay-peninsula|the Malay Peninsula]].
