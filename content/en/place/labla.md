## Overview
Labla is a fine, ancient town of middling size, which a river reaches from the direction of the mountain and which is crossed by a bridge. It has trade and many benefits, and its people drink from springs in it, according to al-Idrisi. {cite:nuzhat-al-mushtaq-2002 p.541}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut gives "Labla", with a fatha on the first letter, a sukun, then a second lam. He calls it the chief town (qasaba) of a large kura of al-Andalus whose district joins that of Ukshuniyya; it lies east of Ukshuniyya and west of Cordoba, five days from Cordoba on the road by Seville, forty-four farsakhs, and forty-two miles from Seville. He calls it a land of country and coast, abundant in good things, fruit, crops and trees, with a relish (udm) better than any other, and with towns of its own; it is known as al-Hamra', which he says is mentioned under its own heading. Gentian, a druggist's drug, is brought from it. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.10} Several men take their name from it: Abu al-Hasan Thabit ibn Muhammad al-Labli, who settled at Jayyan, counted by Ibn Mufarrij al-Nabati among his teachers and praised for knowledge and piety; Abu al-Abbas Ahmad ibn Tamim ibn Hisham ibn Hayyun al-Labli, who heard in Baghdad and Khurasan, was in Damascus in Yaqut's own time and known as al-Muhibb, and died on Thursday, 27 Rajab 625; and Jabir ibn Ghayth al-Labli, Abu Malik, a scholar of Arabic, poetry and letters, whom Hashim ibn Abd al-Aziz made tutor to his son, which is why he lived in Cordoba; he died in 299, according to Ibn al-Faradi. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.10-11}

## The museum's identification
Yaqut and al-Idrisi agree that Labla is a prosperous place of many advantages between Cordoba and Seville, but al-Idrisi describes the town, its river and its springs and calls it of medium size, while Yaqut speaks of the size of its kura, what is brought from it and its scholars. The museum's reading of it as Niebla in the province of Huelva rests on the name, on the Althurayya identifier recorded for it and on the record's point (37.3614, -6.6778); Yaqut gives no coordinates. The museum measured the distances between the points of its own records: about 62 km to its Seville record and about 178 km to its Cordoba record in a straight line, about 182 km by way of Seville. Forty-two miles to Seville would be about 62 km if the mile were the Roman one of about 1.5 km, but Yaqut does not say which mile he means, so the museum does not count that as proof. The forty-four farsakhs come to about 260 km at six kilometres to the farsakh, far more than the records show, and are not used. As for his remark that it is known as al-Hamra' and is mentioned under that heading, the museum found the entry in vol 2 p.301: "al-Hamra': the name of the city of Labla in al-Andalus, an ancient city with wonderful remains, on the river Tantis, with a spring of alum and a spring of vitriol." {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.301} The cross-reference is therefore resolved. The museum reads Tantis as the Rio Tinto, on which Niebla stands; that reading comes from the name and is not Yaqut's text. In the same entry and elsewhere Yaqut lists other places called al-Hamra' that are not Labla: a fortress near Jerusalem, villages in Egypt and a village of Sanhan in Yemen under that entry, the fortress of Baz al-Hamra' (vol 1 p.321) and a small town in Upper Egypt (vol 5 p.420).
