## Overview
Iraq: the land of Ur from which Abraham emigrated, seat of the Persian-subject kingdom of al-Hira, and home of the Jews' old migrations to the Hijaz.

## The place
The father-stock of the Arabised Arabs, Abraham, is from the land of Iraq — from a town called Ur on the western bank of the Euphrates near Kufa {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}.

Persia ruled Iraq and its neighbours since Cyrus the Great; in it stood the Lakhmid kingdom of al-Hira as wardens over the desert Arabs {cite:muhadarat-tarikh-al-umam-al-islamiyya}. From it a part of Palestine's Jews emigrated to the Hijaz in the era of the Babylonian conquests {cite:qalb-jazirat-al-arab}.

## For young visitors
Iraq is the land of the Tigris and Euphrates; from it Abraham set out, and in it were al-Hira's kings, Persia's allies.

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-Buldan
Yaqut begins by separating the several senses of the word: waters belonging to Banu Sa'd ibn Malik and Banu Mazin, a large quarter in the city of Akhmim in Egypt, and then the famous Iraq, which he says is a land; the two Iraqs are Kufa and Basra. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.93}

On the name he gives many opinions. Ibn al-A'rabi, he says, held it was called Iraq because it lies lower than Najd and close to the sea, from the doubled stitching at the base of a water-skin. Qutrub is reported as saying it is near the sea and has salt flats and trees; al-Khalil, that it is the sea shore, because it runs along the Tigris and Euphrates until it joins the sea; al-Asma'i, that it is an Arabised form of Iranshahr, which Yaqut finds far from the sound of the word; others, that it comes from tree roots; and Abu Amr, that the Hijazis call whatever is near the sea iraq. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.93}

He explains that the verse of Abu Sakhr al-Hudhali describing a cloud refers to the iraq of the Hijaz, the sea coast, not to the land of Babylon, and that Mulayh al-Hudhali states this plainly. He reports Hamza al-Isfahani as saying that the Persian word for the shore is irah, that Iraq is an Arabisation of iraf, meaning the place where waters drain away, because the Tigris, Euphrates and Tamarra flow down to it from Armenia and the Byzantine lands, and that the king's two seats were Babil and Ctesiphon. Another derivation he gives is from the evenness of its ground. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.94}

He gives its latitude and length and places most of it in the third climate and about a quarter in the fourth, and he faults Abu Bakr Ahmad ibn Thabit for putting Iraq and Baghdad in the fourth. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.94}

On its limits he says there is disagreement. Some say it is the Sawad, which he holds to be correct; he reports al-Mada'ini giving its limit as the dug channel of Abu Musa on the Najd side and everything lower; others say al-Tur, the Jazira and al-Ibr; Ibn Ayyash counts al-Bahrayn as part of Iraq; and al-Mada'ini says the governor of Iraq in Umayyad times administered lands from Hit as far as China, Sind, India and Khurasan, which Yaqut explains as a matter of the governor's remit, not of those lands being Iraq. In his view Iraq is Babil alone. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.94}

He then reproduces a long passage, introduced only as what "they say", praising the balance of Iraq's air and water and the character of its people by contrast with other regions, and concludes that the province of Babil is the middle of the necklace; he takes this as proof that Iraq means the land of Babil. He closes with verses of longing for the land and remarks that poetry on it is beyond counting. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.95}
