## Overview
Casablanca, as the place card says, is Morocco's largest city and its main Atlantic port. The sources read here say little of it. Al-Idrisi, in the twelfth century CE, lists the harbours of this coast but does not name Casablanca as such; he names a harbour "Anfa", which the museum's [[place/anfa|Anfa page]] places at the coordinates of today's city; and Hadir al-Alam al-Islami, written in the 1920s and 1930s, mentions the city twice. This page is as short as the sources allow, and should be read with [[place/morocco|Morocco]].

## The harbour of Anfa in al-Idrisi
In Nuzhat al-Mushtaq al-Idrisi lists the harbours of Morocco's Atlantic coast. From Fedala to the harbour of Anfa, he says, is forty miles; it is a harbour much visited, ships come to it and load wheat and barley from it, and inland it joins settlements of Berbers of Banu Yadfar, as the scanned text reads it, of Dukkala and others. He then says that from Anfa to the harbour of Mazighan is sixty-five miles. {cite:nuzhat-al-mushtaq-2002 p.240} On the same page he names a place "al-Bayda", a bay thirty miles from Mazighan; the text does not link that name with Casablanca, and neither does this page. Nor does al-Idrisi say that Anfa is the site of the present city; that identification is on the museum's Anfa card and still needs to be fixed.

## The French landing at Casablanca
In Hadir al-Alam al-Islami, in a chapter on Tripolitania and Italy, Arslan summarises how France and Britain divided spheres of influence in Africa. He says the Algeciras conference decided on the independence of the sultanate of Morocco but France went on working by the secret agreement; it occupied Oujda from the east, "then sent an army that landed at Casablanca, and that was the beginning of its extending the protection over Morocco". He adds that the Moroccans saw weakness in their sultan Abd al-Aziz in resisting the French and pledged allegiance to his brother Abd al-Hafiz on condition that the land be cleared of foreigners, but that the French used military force and politics, and the matter ended with persuading Abd al-Hafiz to accept the protectorate. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 scan p.120} He then says Germany objected, that its emperor came to Tangier, and that it sent a warship to Agadir. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 scan p.121} This is Arslan's account, given in the course of his defence of Muslim causes, not a neutral history; the page does not give the year the army landed.

## The Berber decree and Casablanca's prison
In a chapter on "European fanaticism or Islamic fanaticism?" Arslan treats what he calls the "Berber dahir", a decree issued, he says, by the young sultan under pressure from the French authority, which abolished the shari'a courts among the Berbers. He says Moroccans rose against it, that the French authority presented it as a judicial reform leaving the Berbers to their customs, and that in his view this convinced no one since judicial reform was possible without abolishing the shari'a courts, and since France had pledged in the protectorate treaty to respect the Islamic religion. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 scan p.337} He says about a hundred young students of the Qarawiyyin at Fez were beaten with whips and hundreds were jailed in Fez, Rabat, Casablanca and Meknes, with many notables and students exiled; that a delegation of Fez notables and scholars went to the sultan at Rabat with a petition, which Arslan published in his journal La Nation Arabe in issue eight of 1930. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 scan p.339} He himself published an article on the subject in the journal al-Jami'a al-Arabiyya at Jerusalem on 6 Safar 1351 AH and included it in his book. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 scan p.338} Casablanca therefore appears in this account only in passing, among the cities where protesters were jailed; see [[place/fez|Fez]] and [[place/rabat|Rabat]]. All of this is the view of Arslan and of the Moroccans he reports; the French authorities' view is given only as he reports it.
