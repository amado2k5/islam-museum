## Overview
Al-Qarafa, the Southern Cemetery, is Cairo's great cemetery, then as now a hive of individual and collective pilgrimage and of visits to the graves of relatives. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.112} As Yeomans's map shows, it holds the [[building/mausoleum-of-imam-al-shafii|mausoleum of al-Shafi'i]], the shrine of [[building/sayyida-nafisa-mosque|Sayyida Nafisa]], the [[building/mausoleum-sayyida-ruqayya-cairo|mashhad of Sayyida Ruqayya]], the [[building/shajarat-al-durr-tomb|tomb of Shajar al-Durr]] and the [[building/mausoleum-of-the-abbasid-caliphs-cairo|mausoleum of the Abbasid caliphs]]. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.103}

## Shrines in the Fatimid period
Yeomans notes that the prestige of the Fatimid lineage grew in the first half of the twelfth century through popular devotion at the Qarafa tombs of the Prophet's descendants, and reports Jonathan Bloom's view that women, for whom the cemeteries allowed a less formal devotion than the mosque, still make their presence felt there today. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.64} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.65}

## In the Ayyubid period
Salah al-Din made the tomb of al-Shafi'i a Sunni place of pilgrimage with a madrasa beside it, and [[person/al-malik-al-kamil|al-Kamil]] formalised weekly pilgrimages, often leading the Friday-night procession from the shrine of Sayyida Nafisa to the shrines of the seven shaykhs. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.111} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.112} [[person/ibn-jubayr-muhammad|Ibn Jubayr]], spending the night of Wednesday 8 April 1183 there, called it one of the wonders of the world for its tombs of prophets, the Prophet's kin, Companions, Followers, scholars and ascetics, all built up with mosques and inhabited shrines lodging strangers, scholars and the poor, subsidised by the sultan. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.112}
