## Overview
Baghdad is a city on the [[place/tigris|Tigris]], founded by the Abbasid caliph [[person/al-mansur|al-Mansur]] in 762 CE and named by him Madinat al-Salam {cite:tdv-bagdat-duri Overview} {cite:lestrange-baghdad-1900 p.15–19}. The Turkish encyclopaedia of Islam calls it one of the important centres of history, learning and culture of the Islamic world and the seat of the caliphate from its foundation to the fall of the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid state]] in 1258 CE, adding that the caliphs resided at [[place/samarra|Samarra]] from 836 to 892 CE {cite:tdv-bagdat-duri Overview} {cite:tdv-bagdat-duri Development}. [[person/al-muqaddasi|Al-Maqdisi]] relates that before building, al-Mansur asked about the site's winter and summer, rains and air, and had men sleep there through the seasons {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p. 107}. He reports that al-Mansur summoned men learned in law and geometry, among them [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]], had the city laid out and its foundations dug in 145 AH, and that it was finished in 149 AH {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p. 108}.

Its core was a [[invention/round-city-baghdad|round city]] with a double wall and four gates, of Kufa, Basra, Khurasan and Syria, with the caliph's palace and the great mosque at its centre {cite:lestrange-baghdad-1900 p.15–19}. Al-Mansur moved the treasury and the diwans there from [[place/kufa|Kufa]] in 146 AH/763 CE {cite:lestrange-baghdad-1900 p.15–19}. Its markets were then moved to [[place/al-karkh|al-Karkh]] in 773 CE, and the city spread over both banks of the Tigris {cite:tdv-bagdat-duri Markets} {cite:lestrange-baghdad-1900 p.15–19}. The biography of [[person/al-khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]] reports that an Indian astronomical text reached the court in Baghdad around 770 CE {cite:khwarizmi-mactutor Astronomy paragraph}. In the book he completed in 375 AH, al-Maqdisi praised its "delicate air and subtle learning", and noted that Madinat al-Salam itself lay ruined in his day, its mosque still used on Fridays {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p. 12} {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p. 106} {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p. 107}.

## Name and earlier settlements
Baghdad lies on both banks of the Tigris. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose account this page follows, says it was founded in the eighth century, remained the centre of the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid caliphate]] until that caliphate fell, and was for centuries the cultural metropolis of the Muslim world. After 1258 it became a provincial centre, under the Ottomans the centre of the Baghdad province, and in 1921 the capital of modern Iraq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 953}

The article treats the name as pre-Islamic. Arab authors looked for a Persian origin and most often explained it as "given by God", and many modern writers favour this too, while others propose an Aramaic origin meaning an enclosure of sheep. The article points to cuneiform and Talmudic mentions of a place called Bagdadu or Bagdatha, one of them from the time of Hammurabi, and concludes that the origin of the name is not clear. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 953}

[[person/al-mansur|Al-Mansur]] called his city Madinat al-Salam, city of peace, in reference to paradise, and this was its official name on documents and weights. Other names such as Madinat al-Khulafa, Madinat al-Mansur and al-Zawra were also used. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 953} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 954}

Arab authors say he built where older settlements stood, the best known being the village of Baghdad on the west bank, with Aramaean villages near [[place/al-karkh|Karkh]], a Sasanian palace and several monasteries. None gained political or commercial importance, so the article regards the caliph's city as a new foundation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 954}

## Why al-Mansur chose the site
The Abbasids looked east for a capital that would symbolise their dynasty. The first caliph moved from [[place/kufa|Kufa]] to Anbar, and al-Mansur to [[place/al-hashimiyya|Hashimiyya]], but he judged Kufa a bad influence on his army and Hashimiyya vulnerable, as the Rawandiyya rising showed. After exploring, he chose Baghdad for military, economic and climatic reasons: a fertile plain on both sides of the river, the Khurasan road, a meeting place of caravan routes with monthly fairs, a net of canals that could serve as ramparts, a central position in Mesopotamia and a temperate climate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 954}

## The Round City
The Round City looked like a large fortress. A ditch surrounded it, then a first wall, then an empty defensive space, and then the main wall of sun-dried brick with great towers and a domed room above each gate for the guards. Inside lay a ring where officers and loyal followers built houses, a simpler third wall, and an inner space holding the caliph's palace, the great mosque, the offices of state, the houses of the caliph's sons and the quarters of the heads of the guard and the police. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 954}

Two roads between four equidistant gates divided the city into four parts: the Khurasan gate to the north-east, the Basra gate to the south-west, the Syria gate to the north-west and the Kufa gate to the south-east. The article reads the seclusion of the caliph and the lockable quarters as ancient imperial traditions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 954} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 955}

The glory of the city was the Green Dome over the palace, which fell on a stormy night in 329/941, probably struck by lightning. The mosque was rebuilt in brick by [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]] in 191/807 and enlarged in 260-1/875 and 280/893. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 955}

Each quarter generally had a homogeneous population, by ethnic group or by trade. Soldiers lived outside the walls, and for security al-Mansur ordered the markets moved from the Round City to Karkh, to keep the turbulent populace and possible spies away. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 955}

## Building the city
One report says the plan was drawn in 141/755, and work began in Jumada 145/2 August 762. The article names four architects and says the caliph assembled 100,000 workers and craftsmen; a canal was drawn from the Karkhaya canal for drinking water and building. In 146/763 the palace, mosque and offices at least were finished and al-Mansur moved in, and by 149/766 the Round City was complete. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 955} The author notes that circular plans were known earlier in the Near East. Reports on its size differ, and the article reaches a diameter of about 2,352 metres. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 955} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 956}

Reports of the cost also differ, from eighteen million, understood as dinars, to a hundred million dirhams, against an official figure from the caliphal archives of about four million dirhams. The author explains the low sum by cheap labour and the caliph's strict accounting. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 956}

## Expansion east of the Tigris
In 157/773 al-Mansur built the palace of al-Khuld below the Khurasan gate. Strategic considerations and lack of space soon led him to build a camp for his heir [[person/al-mahdi|al-Mahdi]] on the east bank, in work from 151/768 to 157/773. It became [[place/al-rusafa|Rusafa]], with a palace, a mosque and the markets of Bab al-Tak. People crowded into east Baghdad, attracted by al-Mahdi's gifts and later by the [[group/barmakids|Barmakids]], and [[person/zubayda-bint-jafar|Zubayda]] built a mosque and a palace. Poets called the city paradise on earth. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 956}

## War, Samarra and the height of the city
The conflict between [[person/al-amin|al-Amin]] and al-Ma'mun brought a fourteen-month siege, and Tahir ordered the houses of the defenders destroyed; Khuld, Karkh and eastern quarters suffered heavily. The city revived after al-Ma'mun's return in 204/819. [[person/al-mutasim|Al-Mu'tasim]] then sought a new capital for his Turkish troops, and during the period of [[place/samarra|Samarra]], 836-892, Baghdad lost the caliphs' attention but stayed a great centre of commerce and culture. Al-Musta'in was besieged there in 251/865-6, and the wall was extended. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 956}

Al-Mu'tamid returned in 278/892, and in 280/893 [[person/al-mutadid|al-Mu'tadid]] rebuilt the Hasani palace as the seat of the caliphate. Later he built the palace of al-Thurayya, and the article dates the ruin of the Round City to this reign, after the wall was partly pulled down and houses gradually spread over it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 956} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 957} [[person/al-muktafi-billah|Al-Muktafi]] built a palace with halls and domes and a Friday mosque, and [[person/al-muqtadir-billah|al-Muqtadir]] enlarged the royal palaces, where, on al-Khatib's account, a silver tree stood and twenty-three palaces were counted. The article says Baghdad reached its height in this period. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 957}

## Commerce, crafts and daily life
Geography, an active people, state encouragement of trade and the caliphate's prestige made Baghdad the great commercial centre. Each trade had its market, including booksellers with over a hundred shops, and a market inspector had watched over them since al-Mansur's time. The city exported cotton and silk textiles, and was noted for leather, paper and swords. Money-changers and government bankers developed a banking system. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 957}

The people were of many nations and creeds, and the populace began to play an important part, as in the price revolt of 307/919. The author calls the reported numbers of mosques and baths exaggerated, and from other figures reasons toward a population of about a million and a half in the fourth/tenth century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 957} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 958}

## Learning, hospitals and bridges
The article calls Baghdad the great centre of culture and the home of the Hanafi and Hanbali schools of law, with translation in the [[building/bayt-al-hikma|Bayt al-Hikma]] and outside it, and bookshops that were sometimes literary salons. Public libraries followed, and when the madrasa appeared Baghdad led with the Nizamiyya and Mustansiriyya. Hospitals received much attention, and al-Muqtadir had doctors examined, with 860 licensed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 958}

## Floods, fires and unrest
Flood and fire played a growing role from the late third/ninth century: a flood in 270/883 ruined 7,000 houses in Karkh, and after 320/932 scarcities and plague became recurrent. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 958} The article attributes the rise of the [[concept/ayyar|'ayyarun]], active especially in the last quarter of the fourth/tenth century, to hard living conditions and political chaos; one leader, Burjumi, practically ruled the city for four years from 422/1030. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 959}

## Buyid and Seljuk periods
Under the [[polity/buyid-dynasty|Buyids]] the author finds Baghdad declining. [[person/muizz-al-dawla|Mu'izz al-Dawla]] built a great palace in 350/961, and [[person/adud-al-dawla|'Adud al-Dawla]] cleared canals, rebuilt mosques and bridges and founded the 'Adudi hospital in 372/982. West Baghdad shrank, with Karkh its most flourishing part. Conflict between Sunnis and Shi'is grew: Karkh was pillaged in 338/949, and the Buyids made the tenth of Muharram a day of public mourning. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 958} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 959}

In 447/1055 [[person/tughril-beg|Tughril Beg]] entered Baghdad and the [[polity/seljuk-empire|Seljuks]] reversed Buyid policy and encouraged the Sunnis. The article says the city then took a shape that changed little afterwards. East Baghdad centred on the caliphal palaces; al-Mustarshid built a wall around the Harim quarters in 517/1123, which marked the limit of east Baghdad until the end of the Ottoman period. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 959} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 960} The Nizamiyya madrasa, founded in 459/1066, and the Mustansiriyya, founded in 631/1233, were the most famous colleges. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 961}

## Visitors before the fall
Benjamin of Tudela, around 567/1171, found 40,000 Jews with ten schools. [[person/ibn-jubayr-muhammad|Ibn Jubayr]], in 581/1185, saw general decline and ruined quarters, but also about thirty madrasas with ample endowments. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 960} Fires, floods and fights between quarters worsened in the last years of the caliphate, and the worst flood came in 654/1256. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 961}

## The Mongol conquest and after
On 4 Safar 656/10 February 1258 the caliph [[person/al-mustasim-billah|al-Musta'sim]] surrendered unconditionally, and the people were put to the sword for over a week. Estimates of the dead run from 800,000 to two million, growing with time; the author thinks the figure probably exceeded a hundred thousand. Before leaving, Hulegu ordered some public buildings restored. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 961} The museum's record of the event is [[event/sack-of-baghdad-656|the sack of Baghdad]].

Under the Ilkhanids the city revived slowly, notably under the Persian governor [[person/ata-malik-al-juwayni|'Ata Malik al-Juwayni]], who had the Nizamiyya market and the minaret rebuilt. Non-Muslims prospered and then suffered, as in 690/1291. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 961} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 962} Hasan Buzurg founded the Jalayirid dynasty there in 740/1339. [[person/timur-lang|Timur]] took the city in 795/1392-3 with little damage and in 803/1401 put its people to the sword, which the author calls the devastating blow to culture there. Under the Turkoman dynasties al-Maqrizi, writing of 841/1437, is quoted as calling it ruined. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 962}

## Safavids and Ottomans
In 914/1507-8 Baghdad passed to Shah [[person/ismail-i-safavi|Isma'il]], and a long Persian-Ottoman contest began. Sultan [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Sulayman]] entered in 941/1534 and rebuilt Sunni shrines. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 962} Shah 'Abbas conquered it in 1032/1623, and Sultan [[person/murad-iv-uthmani|Murad IV]] regained it in 1048/1638. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 963} The wall of east Baghdad was roughly circular, and travellers' figures put the population at only 15,000. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 963}

From 1749 Mamluk governors, beginning with Sulayman Pasha, ruled Baghdad, training Mamluks in palace schools; Sulayman Pasha the Great from 1193/1779 restored order, and Dawud Pasha from 1816 encouraged industry. The plague and flood of 1247/1831 ended this era. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 964} Baghdad was then governed directly from Constantinople. [[person/midhat-pasha|Midhat Pasha]] introduced the modern province system, the first newspaper and modern schools and pulled down the city walls. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 965} Felix Jones counted 63 quarters in the east and 25 in Karkh in 1853-4, and the municipality counted 466,733 people in 1947 and 735,000 in 1957. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 966} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 967}

## For Sprouts
Baghdad is Iraq's historic capital of learning, built by the Abbasids on the Tigris River, famous for the House of Wisdom.

## For Explorers
Baghdad is the capital of Iraq and the historic center of the Abbasid Caliphate for centuries, founded by Caliph al-Mansur as the City of Peace. Famous for its round design on the Tigris River, it became the preeminent metropolis of science and arts, home to the House of Wisdom, the Nizamiyya academy, and hospitals.

## For Scholars
The imperial capital of the Abbasid Caliphate and the cosmopolitan epicenter of medieval Islamic civilization, founded in 145 AH (762 CE) by Caliph Abu Jafar al-Mansur on the Tigris River at a vital crossroads of Afro-Eurasian trade. For five centuries, it stood as the undisputed global capital of sciences, philosophy, translation, and literature, nurturing the House of Wisdom, the Nizamiyya, and early teaching hospitals. Home to founding jurists like Abu Hanifa and Ahmad ibn Hanbal and master scientists like al-Khwarizmi, Baghdad remains the enduring symbol of the Islamic Golden Age.

## The round nucleus is not the whole city
[[invention/round-city-baghdad|Baghdad's round-city nucleus plan]] concerns a seat connecting palace,mosque,four entrances and rings for defence,circulation and housing,not every area inhabited over centuries. Settlements,canals,routes and circular-planning precedents predated al-Mansur's commission {cite:tdv-bagdat-duri Foundation and plan}. Le Strange gives145AH/762foundation,146AH/763treasury/bureau transfer and149AH/766completion,not one opening day or new conversions {cite:lestrange-baghdad-1900 p.15–19}. Two ramparts differ from the central enclosure; every drawn ring does not share one function.

## Housing,markets,water and access
Radial routes,rings and neighbourhoods coexist,and street/arcade uses changed. Le Strange describes market removal,guard accommodation,water carriers,Matbak prison and riding restrictions into the centre {cite:lestrange-baghdad-1900 p.25–29}. Duri dates market relocation to773and describes expansion/changing centres {cite:tdv-bagdat-duri Markets,Development}. Geometric order establishes neither equal access nor absence of coercion,and does not place every later scholarly institution inside the circle. Water networks helped make habitation possible; their complete operation cannot be recovered from a wall outline.

## Map,measurements and retained accounts
Le Strange's1900map is reconstruction,not aerial photography or foundation survey; not every marginal feature necessarily belongs to762 {cite:commons-round-city-lestrange}. Duri/Le Strange differ on Kufa/Basra compass labels and diameter estimates. Existing city coordinates have not been replaced by an inferred circle centre {cite:tdv-bagdat-duri Measurements} {cite:lestrange-baghdad-1900 p.17–18}. The retained Muqaddasi-based Arabic account gives4,000,833dirhams,whereas Duri gives4,000,883. Retain each attribution rather than silently correct one tradition {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p.107–109} {cite:tdv-bagdat-duri Costs}. Masudi's gate/prison narrative also remains an attributed account,not modern engineering measurement {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.276–278}.

## Site of covered-conduit arrangement
Baghdad is also linked to [[invention/mansur-covered-water-conduits|the covered water-conduit arrangement]] as place of implementation,not as the first city ever to know water management. Reports connect the urban core with outside supplies such as Dujayl/Karkhaya through lined/covered channels {cite:lestrange-baghdad-1900 p.28–29}. This adds a service-history dimension to place description beyond geometric form alone.

## From fixed nucleus to changing city
The site's historical value includes movement from a defined political nucleus to a broader city with shifting functions. Water is part of that movement: sustaining habitation,enabling market relocation,and supporting reorganization of circulation/work {cite:tdv-bagdat-duri Markets,Development}. So the round nucleus should be read as a beginning of an evolving urban process,not a static final map.

## Limits of spatial certainty
Even with strong textual/cartographic evidence,this page cannot by itself deliver a complete operational map of every historical water line. We preserve the distinction between interpretive witness (the1900reconstruction map) and field-execution evidence,and between historical narrative and modern engineering measurement. That keeps Baghdad's record precise: a site with documented organized water service,but not fully recoverable technical geometry from one source layer alone.

## Site of reported brick reckoning
Baghdad in this record is not only a round-plan and conduit site; it is also the setting of the reported [[invention/abu-hanifa-brick-stack-reckoning|measurement-based brick-stack reckoning]] episode in construction supervision {cite:lestrange-baghdad-1900 p.17}. This adds materials-management history to place memory.

## Material estimation and city organization
Reading brick-estimation reports together with water,market and access reports highlights Baghdad as a web of operational decisions,not architecture alone. Estimation affects scheduling,spending and pace of service completion in a growing capital {cite:tdv-bagdat-duri Development}. The place therefore carries administrative-technique history as well as political symbolism.

## Markets inside and outside the walls
In [[invention/karkh-market-relocation-zoning|the reported Karkh market relocation]], Duri describes four inner markets along arcaded roads and four other market places outside the walls before the relocation order {cite:tdv-bagdat-duri Markets}. Le Strange says shops were removed from the inner roads and the cleared arcades were then used as barracks for city police and horse-guard {cite:lestrange-baghdad-1900 p.25–26}. Duri dates the transfer toward Karkh to773CE, while Le Strange also describes suburbs outside several gates; the accounts do not establish that every market moved to one street {cite:tdv-bagdat-duri Markets} {cite:lestrange-baghdad-1900 p.65–66}. In his account of Baghdad's growth, Duri describes separate bazaars for crafts and trades and quarters for merchants arriving from different cities, without dating every later arrangement to the relocation itself {cite:tdv-bagdat-duri Markets}.

## What this report does not prove alone
From this report alone we do not derive daily production tables,precise efficiency percentages or a full modern engineering model. What it does establish is a transmitted estimation logic inside city-building practice. Keeping that line clear preserves spatial and historical accuracy.

## Baghdad and the coefficient-rule setting
[[person/al-karaji|Karaji]]'s principal mathematical works are associated with Baghdad, and TDV identifies Fakhr al-Mulk's Buyid patronage without establishing the author's personal religious school {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography} {cite:tdv-kereci pp. 277–278}. The [[invention/karaji-binomial-coefficient-recurrence|coefficient recurrence]] belongs to this scholarly context, not a verified workshop address for composition of the lost work {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography} {cite:samawal-dsb algebra}. Al-Samawal preserved its account in al-Bahir, but that does not supply dated routes carrying the table from the city to Europe or China {cite:samawal-mactutor Biography} {cite:jia-xian-mactutor Biography}.

## Baghdad and the Sindhind zij
The al-Khwarizmi biography reports an Indian astronomical text reaching the Baghdad court around 770 and places al-Khwarizmi in the city's scholarly setting {cite:khwarizmi-mactutor Astronomy paragraph}. The record for [[invention/khwarizmi-sindhind-tables|the Sindhind tables adaptation]] connects this setting to an astronomical work attributed to him; it does not claim arrival at a particular writing room or use of a named observatory {cite:khwarizmi-mactutor Astronomy paragraph} {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227}.

## What the place tie establishes
The report of Indian material reaching the court documents an intellectual context in Baghdad, while the biography lists the zij's topics without locating the copying of each table or its observations {cite:khwarizmi-mactutor Astronomy paragraph}. The city record therefore adds a site-of relation at city scale only; it does not turn that link into an institutional or workshop address {cite:khwarizmi-mactutor Astronomy paragraph}.

## Geographic limits
The two Arabic versions are lost, and the biography describes an Andalusian revision and Latin translations without giving a dated itinerary for each stage {cite:khwarizmi-mactutor Astronomy paragraph}. The invention record has no spread map because the sources read here do not establish a dated route that can safely be plotted {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227} {cite:khwarizmi-mactutor Astronomy paragraph}.
