## Overview
Misr is a land whose crops are watered from the Nile without rain. Its borders run south to the land of Nubia and north to the sea. It has orchards, trees, palms and sugar cane, and its people sow on the water of the flood, according to al-Idrisi. {cite:nuzhat-al-mushtaq-2002 p.322}

## Egypt's place among the climes
Al-Maqrizi reports the geographers' view that parts of Egypt lie in the second clime and parts in the third. What lies in Upper Egypt, at Qus, Ikhmim and Aswan, is in the second; what lies from Asyut to Fustat, al-Fayyum, Cairo, Alexandria and Damietta is in the third. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.84}

He gives the longitude of Fustat and Cairo as fifty-five degrees, and their latitude as thirty degrees. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.84}

Egypt cannot be reached except through a desert: the Red Sea lies to its east, the desert of the Maghrib to its west, the desert of Nubia and Abyssinia to its south, and the Syrian sea to its north. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.84}

## The name Misr
The Encyclopaedia of Islam treats Misr as three related things. As a proper noun, Misr is the eponym of Egypt, the ancestor of the Berbers and the Copts, who in the Biblical genealogy is a son of Ham and a grandson of Noah. As a common noun, misr, plural amsar, was in earliest Islam the settlement that grew out of an armed camp established by the Arabs in a conquered province, and later the capital of such a province. The word is of ancient Semitic origin and carries the sense of a frontier or boundary. In the sense that concerns this record, Misr is also the land of Egypt and its capital. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 170}

The article explains how the name travelled from city to country. After the Muslim conquest the name Misr was regularly given to the settlement on the right bank of the Nile, between the mosque of [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn al-As]] and the river. The phrase Fustat Misr, [[place/al-fustat|Fustat]] in Egypt, probably formed the link between the name of the capital and the name of the country. The article adds that the papyri never call either Babylon or Fustat by the name Misr, and it records a disagreement over whether a city of that name already existed before the conquest. Once [[place/cairo|Cairo]] was founded, the old settlement was called Old Misr, and Cairo itself is known in full as Misr al-Qahira. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 170} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 171}

## Byzantine Egypt on the eve of the conquest
The article describes Byzantine Egypt as a land that kept Graeco-Roman patterns of culture and administration, with Greek the language of most official documents and Coptic spoken beside it. Its church was divided by the dispute over the divine and human natures of Christ, between the Monophysites and the Melkites favoured by the emperors. Shortly before the Arab arrival the Byzantines removed the Monophysite archbishop of [[place/alexandria|Alexandria]], Benjamin, and installed the Orthodox Cyrus, a step the article judges to have weakened the Byzantine position. It also notes the growth of large private estates in the sixth century and an active commercial class engaged in shipping. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 176}

Around the turn of the seventh century Egypt suffered under the emperor Phocas, became a battleground between Phocas and [[person/heraclius|Heraclius]], and then fell to the Persians, who held it between 619 and 629 or 630. The author stresses that Egyptians were not a single anti-Byzantine bloc, and that the line between Coptic and Greek elements was not clear. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 177}

## The Arab conquest
The article warns that the sources for the [[event/conquest-of-egypt|conquest of Egypt]] are confused and contradictory; it names Ibn Abd al-Hakam as the most trustworthy Arab author. The expedition was led by Amr ibn al-As, who reached the frontier town of [[place/al-arish|al-Arish]] on 10 Dhu l-Hijja 18 AH with about four thousand men, mostly horsemen and without siege machines. He took Pelusium after a siege of a month and [[place/bilbays|Bilbays]] after another, and halted before the fortress of Babylon, where he asked the caliph for reinforcements. About four thousand men under al-Zubayr joined him in June 640, and in July 640 the Arabs defeated the Byzantines at Heliopolis. The defenders of Babylon, discouraged by the death of Heraclius, left the fortress on 21 Rabi II 20 AH, 9 April 641, and sailed to Alexandria. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 177} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 178}

Fayyum and Middle and Upper Egypt were subdued, probably while the siege of Alexandria went on. Alexandria was taken without real resistance, which the article attributes to Byzantine demoralisation, quarrels among the Byzantine officials and riots by the circus parties. The patriarch and governor Cyrus, known in the Arabic sources as [[person/al-muqawqis|al-Muqawqis]], concluded the final treaty with Amr at Babylon; al-Tabari calls it the treaty of Ayn Shams. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 178}

The treaty was a pledge of security to all the people of Egypt whatever their origin or religion, with a [[concept/jizya|poll-tax]] which Ibn Abd al-Hakam and others put at two dinars for each adult male. Muslim jurists disagreed on whether Egypt was taken by force or by treaty, a difference that mattered because force would have made the land spoils and its people without the protection granted to non-Muslims; Ibn Abd al-Hakam's report, the article says, is that part was taken by agreement and part by force, and that [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] placed all of it under protection. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 178} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 179}

In 645 the Byzantines sent a force under Manuel, which landed at Alexandria and was then defeated by Amr. The need to meet such raids led the Muslims to recruit Egyptian sailors and use Egyptian ships. The article lists the reasons for the fall of Egypt: the exhaustion of the Byzantines after their war with Persia, their heavy taxation and chaotic administration, and the coordination and flexible tactics of the Arab army. It judges that the role of Bedouin allies was smaller than in Syria, and that the Egyptians' resentment of Byzantine religious policy has been exaggerated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 179} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 180}

## Egypt under the Umayyads
The first governor held absolute judicial authority as well as administrative power and answered directly to the caliph. Egypt was divided into an upper and a lower unit. Abd Allah ibn Sad ibn Abi Sarh, who succeeded Amr, organised the office of finance. Most Arab conquerors lived in garrison towns, chiefly Fustat and Alexandria, and received cash stipends; Fustat was chosen as capital because it lay between Upper and Lower Egypt and on a route to the Red Sea. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 180} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 182}

Papyri show a detailed system of taxation within fifty years of the conquest, forced labour for public works and for naval expeditions against Byzantium, and many peasants who fled their villages. Conversion to Islam was limited, since converts were not at first relieved of the land tax; the article says the edict of [[person/umar-ibn-abd-al-aziz|Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz]] in 100 AH encouraged fiscal relief for converts. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 181}

Under [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik]] monks began to pay the poll-tax, coins lost their Christian designs for epigraphic ones with Qur'anic inscriptions, Christians could no longer become high officials, and the language of administration changed from Greek to Arabic. Under Hisham, around 106 AH, the finance director Ubayd Allah ibn al-Habhab ordered a new land survey and raised taxation sharply, which the article links to the first Coptic revolt of 107 AH. He also settled about three thousand Arabs of the Qays tribe near Bilbays in 109 AH, and five thousand more later, which contributed to the Arabisation and Islamisation of Egypt. The article adds that Coptic was still used by some Egyptians as late as the third to fifth centuries AH. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 183} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 184}

## Under the Abbasids, Tulunids and Ikhshidids
When the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasids]] took over in 132 AH, the article says, Islam and Arab culture were still confined to a ruling elite and most Egyptians were Coptic Christians. Governors sent from Baghdad changed often, and only Yazid ibn Hatim stayed for more than a few years. After the civil war that followed the death of [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]] in 193 AH, Khurasani troops seized Fustat, refugees from al-Andalus took Alexandria until they were expelled to [[place/crete|Crete]], and the local Arab elite lost its power and its stipends. Egypt was thereafter ruled by outsiders, Turks from the east or Berbers from the west. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 184} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 185}

[[person/ahmad-ibn-tulun|Ahmad ibn Tulun]], who ruled from 254 to 270 AH, kept the revenues in the country, raised a new army, and built a suburb called al-Qatai with his [[building/masjid-ibn-tulun|congregational mosque]]. His rule began Egyptian involvement in [[place/syria|Syria]], a concern that the article calls one of the major issues of Egyptian history as long as the country was independent. Neither the [[polity/tulunid-dynasty|Tulunids]] nor the later [[polity/ikhshidid-dynasty|Ikhshidids]] broke formally with the Abbasid caliphate. Abbasid forces reoccupied Egypt in 292 AH, and Fatimid attacks in the early tenth century were repelled. Immigrants from Iraq, among them officials and Babylonian Jews, formed a new Muslim civilian elite and helped speed conversion. In 323 AH power passed to [[person/muhammad-ibn-tughj-al-ikhshid|Muhammad ibn Tughj]], called al-Ikhshid, and after his death the black eunuch [[person/kafur-al-ikhshidi|Kafur]] ruled until 357 AH, at a court that patronised the poet [[person/al-mutanabbi|al-Mutanabbi]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 185}

## The Fatimid period
In 358 AH, 969 CE, the general [[person/jawhar-al-siqilli|Jawhar]] entered Fustat on behalf of the [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]] caliph, after granting a guarantee of security which promised tolerance of the Sunni cult. He founded the garrison town that became Cairo, renamed al-Qahira when the caliph [[person/al-muizz-li-din-allah|al-Muizz]] arrived in 362 AH. Cairo was reserved for the caliph, his court and army, with [[building/al-azhar|al-Azhar]] as a centre of Ismailism, while Fustat remained a Sunni stronghold. The article says the Fatimids never tried to impose the Ismaili faith by force, though they preached it in every province, and that Ismailism disappeared quickly after their fall. Christian and Jewish officials held many posts, and the caliph [[person/al-hakim-bi-amr-allah|al-Hakim]] ordered the destruction of some churches, monasteries and synagogues between 398 and 410 AH, a policy which he himself abandoned. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 186}

Under al-Aziz, al-Hakim, al-Zahir and al-Mustansir, Egypt was a major Mediterranean power and controlled the Red Sea and trade between the Indian Ocean and the Mediterranean, which the article says made it the pivot of international commerce. It supplied grain to [[place/mecca|Mecca]] and [[place/medina|Medina]], and the Persian traveller [[person/nasir-khusraw|Nasir-i Khusraw]] described Cairo in 439 AH at its height. From 444 AH repeated failures of the Nile flood brought famine and unrest, and the Armenian general [[person/badr-al-jamali|Badr al-Jamali]] was called in during the winter of 466 AH and governed with his son [[person/al-afdal-ibn-badr-al-jamali|al-Afdal]] with almost unlimited power for about half a century. The loss of Syria to the Seljuks and the Crusaders confined the state to Egypt. In 564 AH the Syrian general Shirkuh entered Cairo, and his nephew Salah al-Din deposed the last Fatimid caliph on 7 Muharram 567 AH, 10 September 1171, proclaimed the sermon for the Abbasid caliph, and restored Sunnism. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 186} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 187}

## The Ayyubid period
[[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Salah al-Din]] made [[place/damascus|Damascus]] the first capital of the [[polity/ayyubid-dynasty|Ayyubid]] empire. His most radical innovation in Egypt, according to the article, was the military fief system called [[concept/iqta|iqta]], applied after a land survey in 572 AH that was put into effect in 577 AH, replacing civilian tax farmers with soldiers who kept the land tax of their holdings. His cavalry consisted largely of Kurdish free mercenaries, while his successor al-Malik al-Salih later bought Turkish slaves and trained them on the island of Rawda. Trade with Venice, Genoa and Pisa grew, until the three republics supported the Crusade against Damietta in 615 to 618 AH, whose failure ended these arrangements. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 188} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 189}

## The Mamluk sultanate
The article opens its account of the [[polity/mamluk-sultanate|Mamluks]] with a remark by the fifteenth-century jurist Abu Hamid al-Qudsi, and draws three developments from it: Egypt became the leading power of the Near East, it became a bulwark of orthodox religious conservatism, and the caliphate was transferred to Cairo. The Mamluks came as slaves from the steppes, and the article says they lacked conventional Islamic legitimacy, which they sought through their battlefield successes and a shadow Abbasid caliph. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 189} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 190}

The Mamluks of al-Malik al-Salih defeated the vanguard of [[person/louis-ix|Louis IX]] at [[place/al-mansura|al-Mansura]] in 648 AH, and on 2 May 1250 leading Mamluks killed his son Turanshah. [[person/shajar-al-durr|Shajar al-Durr]] was acknowledged as interim ruler and then yielded to Aybak, whom most historians regard as the first Mamluk sultan. At [[event/ayn-jalut-658|Ayn Jalut]] on 25 Ramadan 658 AH, 3 September 1260, the army of [[person/qutuz|Qutuz]] and [[person/baybars|Baybars]] defeated the Mongols, an event the article calls a symbol of Mamluk resilience even though more Mongol attacks followed until 713 AH. Baybars, who then took the sultanate, laid the foundations of the centralised state, installed an Abbasid refugee as caliph in Cairo in 659 AH and extended Egyptian influence into Nubia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 190} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 191} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 192}

[[person/qalawun|Qalawun]] and his son [[person/al-ashraf-khalil|al-Ashraf Khalil]] took the last Crusader strongholds, Acre falling in 690 AH. The third reign of [[person/al-nasir-muhammad-ibn-qalawun|al-Nasir Muhammad]], from 709 to 741 AH, is described as the high point of Mamluk history: a treaty with the [[polity/ilkhanate|Ilkhan]] was concluded in 723 AH, a cadastral survey in 715 and 716 AH shifted land to his own Mamluks, and canals and buildings multiplied. The same period saw pressure on Christians, and the article says that a large share of Coptic families adopted Islam and that Coptic ceased to be spoken. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 192} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 193} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 194}

Later the plague came in 747 to 750 AH, Peter I of Cyprus sacked Alexandria in 767 AH, and [[person/timur-lang|Timur]] invaded Syria in 803 AH, an onslaught from which Syria and Egypt never fully recovered. The Circassian sultanate is traced to [[person/barquq|Barquq]], who took the throne in 784 AH and held it firmly from 792 AH. In the fifteenth century the Black Death, Bedouin unrest and the decline of rural revenue weakened the Mamluk elite, whose army, the article says, resisted gunpowder weapons. Its crop yield fell from nine to under two million dinars. Yet [[person/qaytbay|Qaytbay]] and [[person/qansuh-al-ghawri|Qansuh al-Ghawri]] presided over a lively religious and cultural court. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 195} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 196} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 198} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 199}

## Ottoman Egypt
In 922 AH [[person/selim-i-uthmani|Selim I]] invaded Syria and defeated the Mamluks at [[place/marj-dabiq|Marj Dabiq]]. The article reports that on 23 January 1517 the Ottoman army destroyed the last Mamluk force at al-Raydaniyya, and that the sultan Tuman Bay was hanged at the Zuwayla Gate on 14 April 1517. The outcome, it says, was a symbiosis of Mamluk and Ottoman elements rather than the destruction of the Mamluks. Egypt was governed by [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottoman]] viceroys, garrison corps and tax farmers, while neo-Mamluk households of beys gained the real power. The Great Insurrection of 1123 AH, 1711, left them dominant. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 200} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 201} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 202} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 203}

[[person/ali-bey-al-kabir|Ali Bey]] ruled autocratically as shaykh al-balad and was driven into exile in 1186 AH. An Ottoman expedition under Hasan Pasha arrived in 1200 AH, 1786, but withdrew, leaving the duumvirate of Ibrahim Bey and Murad Bey in power until the French arrived. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 203}

## From the French expedition to 1882
Bonaparte landed near Alexandria on the night of 1 to 2 July 1798 and defeated the Mamluk army at the Battle of the Pyramids on 21 July. The French set up the Institut d'Egypte on 23 August 1798, and the Description de l'Egypte came out of its work. The article judges that the expedition did not start all modern Egypt but brought the country into contact with European culture and politics. The French withdrew from Alexandria on 2 September 1801. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 204}

[[person/muhammad-ali-pasha|Muhammad Ali]], an ambitious officer from Macedonia who commanded the Albanian contingent, was invested with the governorship by the ulama on 13 May 1805, an event which some scholars place in the tradition of the ulama and which national-minded Egyptians value as popular sovereignty. He destroyed the Mamluks in the [[building/citadel-of-cairo|Citadel]] on 1 March 1811, fought the Wahhabi movement in Arabia, and expanded into the [[place/sudan|Sudan]]. From 1831 his son Ibrahim conquered Syria and reached Kutahya, but European intervention forced the settlement of 10 June 1841, which left Muhammad Ali Egypt and the Sudan, with hereditary rule in his [[polity/muhammad-ali-dynasty|family]]. Through monopolies, conscription, schools, factories and new crops he built a centralised state, though the article says the 1838 Anglo-Turkish convention undermined his economic policy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 204} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 205} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 206}

Under Abbas, Said and Ismail the country gained railways, harbours and the Suez Canal, opened on 17 November 1869, but also heavy foreign debt of nearly 100 million pounds. Ismail won the title khedive in 1867, and in 1875 sold his Suez Canal shares to the British government. European control grew through the debt commission and dual control of 1876. The Sultan deposed Ismail on 26 June 1879. In 1881 and 1882 the colonel [[person/ahmad-urabi|Ahmad Urabi]] led a movement of Egyptian officers and notables that the article says had not aimed at revolution. After the British bombardment of Alexandria on 11 and 12 July, Urabi's army was beaten at Tall al-Kabir on 13 September 1882. The article's accounts of the British protectorate and republican Egypt are in the Supplement. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 206} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 207} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 208} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 209} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 170}

## For Sprouts
Egypt is a land whose crops are watered by the Nile without rain, as al-Idrisi says, bordering the land of Nubia to the south and the sea to the north. It has gardens, trees, palms and sugar cane, and it is reached only through desert crossings — the Red Sea to the east and the western Sahara.

## For Explorers
Egypt in al-Idrisi's account is a land whose crops are watered by the Nile without rain, bordering Nubia to the south and the sea to the north, holding gardens, trees, palms and sugar cane, its people sowing on the floodwater. Al-Maqrizi reports from the geographers that the lands of Egypt fall partly in the Second Clime — Qus, Ikhmim and Aswan in Upper Egypt — and partly in the Third — from Asyut to Fustat, Fayyum, Cairo, Alexandria and Damietta; Fustat and Cairo sit at fifty-five degrees longitude and thirty degrees latitude. It is reached only through a wilderness: the Red Sea to the east, the Sahara of the Maghrib to the west, the wastes of Nubia and Abyssinia to the south and the sea of Syria to the north.

## For Scholars
Egypt in al-Idrisi's Nuzhat al-Mushtaq is a land whose crops are watered by the Nile without rain, bordering the land of Nubia to the south and the sea to the north; it holds gardens, trees, palms and sugar cane, its people sowing on the floodwater. Al-Maqrizi in the Khitat reports from the geographers that the lands of Egypt fall partly in the Second Clime — what lies in Upper Egypt like Qus, Ikhmim and Aswan — and partly in the Third Clime — from Asyut to Fustat, Fayyum, Cairo, Alexandria and Damietta; Fustat and Cairo lying at fifty-five degrees longitude and thirty degrees latitude. It is reached only through a wilderness: to its east the Red Sea (Bahr al-Qulzum), to its west the Sahara of the Maghrib, to its south the wastes of Nubia and Abyssinia, to its north the sea of Syria.
