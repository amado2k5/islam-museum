## Overview
The Encyclopaedia of Islam article TARAZ treats the medieval town of Taraz, also called Talas, in the Syr Darya province of Central Asia; the site of the present city, formerly Aulie Ata and in the article's day Dzhambul, now lies just within Kazakhstan, while the old name Talas has been revived for a modern settlement a little to the east, just within Kirghizia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 237} The original Talas lay in the river valley between two mountain ranges that run westwards and end in the Ak Kum desert. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 237}

## An old town on a trade route
The valley carried an important trade route eastwards to the Chu valley and Semirechye. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 237} Talas was an ancient town, mentioned in the report of the Byzantine envoy Zemarchos, who travelled to the camp of the Kaghan of the Western Turks in 568, and known as a commercial centre to the Chinese Buddhist pilgrim Xuanzang around 630; its origin was possibly Sogdian, and in the 5th/11th century Mahmud al-Kashghari states that Sogdian and Turkish were both spoken there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 237} Excavations show that Zoroastrianism, Christianity and Buddhism were professed in the region in early times. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 237}

## The battle of Talas and the Samanids
Talas first comes into prominence in Islamic history as the general locale of the battle fought in Dhu l-Hijja 133/July 751 between the Chinese governor of Kucha, Kao Hsien-chih, with his Turkish allies, and the Arab commander Ziyad b. Salih al-Khuza'i, a battle that checked Chinese ambitions to establish direct control over Central Asia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 237} In 280/893 the Samanid amir Isma'il b. Ahmad raided Talas and turned its Christian church into a mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 237} In the 4th/10th century Ibn Hawqal and al-Muqaddasi describe it as strongly fortified and populous, a centre for trade with the Karluk Turks on the steppes beyond, with goatskins among its products, and the Hudud al-alam says that both Muslim and non-Muslim Turks lived there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 237}

## Karakhanids and Mongols
Under the Karakhanids Talas became of special importance: it formed part of the patrimony of Qadir Khan Yusuf, who died in 423 or 424/1032, and then of his son Yighan-tigin Muhammad, d. 449/1057, and the article notes Karakhanid coins minted at Talas. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 pp. 237-238} In 607/1210 a battle was fought at Talas between the Khwarazm Shah Ala al-Din Muhammad, allied with the Karakhanid Uthman b. Ibrahim, and the Kara Khitay, which was indecisive but exhausted the Kara Khitay resources. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 238} Under the Mongols the town was ruled by governors who minted coins there; William of Rubruck passed through in 1253, and the first Mongol kuriltay held in Central Asia took place on the banks of the Talas River in spring 1269, when Kaydu and Barak agreed on their respective territories. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 238} In Mongol times the name Yangi, "the New", appears beside Talas, sometimes as Yangi-Taraz, but thereafter Talas largely disappears from the historical sources. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 238}
