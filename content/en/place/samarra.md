## Overview
Samarra is a town on the east bank of the middle [[place/tigris|Tigris]] in Iraq, 125 km north of [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]]. It covered about 35 hectares in 1924 and about 120 in the 1970s. For a few decades in the ninth century it was the capital of the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid caliphs]], until 279/892, and in that time it grew to an occupied area of 57 square kilometres, one of the largest cities of the ancient world. The remains of its collapsed walls of pressed earth and brick can still largely be seen. This page follows the article in the Encyclopaedia of Islam, which is the work of A. Northedge. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1115}

## Before the caliphs
The district was only lightly settled in antiquity. The Chalcolithic Samarran culture was excavated at the rich site of Tell al-Suwwan, and an Assyrian city refounded by Sennacherib in 690 BC, according to a stele in Baltimore, may doubtfully be identified with a fortified Assyrian site at al-Huwaysh, across from the modern town. The article lists ancient names for the place: Souma in Greek, Sumere in Latin, a fort mentioned during the retreat of Julian's army in 364 AD, and Shumara in Syriac, where it is described as a village. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1115}

The region revived with the digging of the Katul al-Kisrawi, the northern extension of the [[place/al-nahrawan|Nahrawan]] canal, which drew water from the Tigris near Samarra. Yaqut attributes it to the Sasanian [[person/khosrow-i-anushirvan|Khusraw Anushirvan]]. A commemorative tower stood at the southern inlet and a palace with a walled hunting park at the northern one. A further canal, the Katul Abi 'l-Jund, was dug under [[person/harun-al-rashid|al-Rashid]] and commemorated by a city laid out as a regular octagon, at the site now called Husn al-Qadisiyya, which was abandoned unfinished in 180/796. Its plan was based on that of the Round City of Baghdad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1115}

## A new capital
Probably in 220/834-5 the caliph [[person/al-mutasim|al-Mu'tasim]] left Baghdad to look for a new capital. The sources agree on the reason: conflict between his regiment of Central Asian Turks and the people of Baghdad. He wanted a residence for the court and a base for the Abbasid army outside the city, and was drawn to a region known for hunting but otherwise poor. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1115}

The caliph's city was formally named Surra Man Ra'a, meaning that whoever sees it is delighted. Yaqut says the name was later shortened in popular speech to Samarra. The article thinks it more probable that Samarra is the Arabic form of the pre-Islamic place name, and that Surra Man Ra'a is a word-play invented at court, in a verbal form unusual in Arabic that recalls older Akkadian and Sumerian practice. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1115}

## The first city
Surra Man Ra'a was laid out in 221/836 on the east bank around the older settlement, with the principal palace to the north on the site of a monastery. The sources call this palace complex by several names, among them Dar al-Khilafa, and it had two main parts: the Dar al-'Amma, the public palace where the caliph held audience on Mondays and Thursdays, and al-Jawsak, the residence of the caliphs and their families, where four of them are buried. The palace site covers 125 hectares and was excavated by Viollet in 1910, by Herzfeld in 1911-3 and recently by the Iraqi Directorate-General of Antiquities. It has a square building identified as the Dar al-'Amma opening onto a garden on the Tigris, a court behind it, two basins cut into the conglomerate for summer use, and a polo ground. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1115}

From the palace an avenue, later called Shari' Abi Ahmad by al-Ya'qubi, ran 3.5 km south to the markets and the mosque of al-Mu'tasim, both now under the modern town. East of it lay the cantonments of the Turk Wasif, and to the west, on the river bank, those of the Maghariba, an army unit apparently of Egyptian origin. Two other cantonments lay outside the city: that of the Ushrusaniyya, under [[person/al-afshin|al-Afshin]], at al-Matira, 4 km south of the modern town, and that of the Turks under Ashnas at Karkh Fayruz, 10 km north. The area east of the city was walled as a hunting park. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1115}

## Al-Wathiq and al-Mutawakkil
When al-Mu'tasim died in 227/842 the question was whether Samarra would be abandoned, as many princely sites were when their founder died. His successor [[person/al-wathiq|al-Wathiq]] chose to stay, and the population turned the military camp into a real city. According to al-Ya'qubi, al-Wathiq changed some of the military arrangements but gave most attention to economic development, and built a new palace, al-Haruni, at al-Kuwayr on the Tigris, a site that has not been excavated and has been partly flooded since the 1950s by the barrage. Al-Haruni remained the residence of [[person/al-mutawakkil|al-Mutawakkil]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1115}

The article stresses al-Mutawakkil, who reigned 232-47/847-61 and seems to have loved architecture. His building projects, listed in several versions, include a new Congregational Mosque and up to twenty palaces, costing between 258 and 294 million dirhams. The mosque, with its spiral minaret, was built between 235/849 and 237/851 as part of an eastward extension of the city into the old hunting park; see the record of the [[building/al-jami-al-kabir-samarra|Great Mosque of Samarra]]. Two palaces with hunting parks went up in the south, at al-Istablat and al-Musharrahat. Another, Balkuwara, excavated by Herzfeld in 1911, stood south of al-Matira inside a cantonment for a new army corps under the caliph's second son, [[person/al-mutazz|al-Mu'tazz]]. Racecourses were also built east of the city, two of them out-and-back courses 80 m wide and 10.42 km long with a pavilion at the start, and one with four circles around a central pavilion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1115} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1116}

## The city at its height
Under al-Mutawakkil the city seems to have reached its greatest extent, as described by al-Ya'qubi after the caliph's death. There were seven parallel avenues. The one beside the Tigris, Shari' al-Khalij, held the quays for the river transport that was the main means of supplying the city. The principal avenue, al-Shari' al-A'zam, seems to follow the ancient road from Baghdad to [[place/mosul|Mosul]]; along it stood the tax registry, the caliph's stables, the slave market, the police court, the great prison and the main markets around al-Mu'tasim's old mosque. The second avenue, Shari' Abi Ahmad, was the original avenue of al-Mu'tasim's time, narrowed from 60 to 10 metres, and ended at the south gate of the palace; outside it stood the residences of the leading Turks. The remaining avenues were the quarters of military units, among them the Turks, the Khazars and the Khurasanis. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1116}

In 245/859 al-Mutawakkil began to replace Surra Man Ra'a with a new caliphal city to the north, called al-Mutawakkiliyya on its coins, though written sources call it al-Ja'fariyya or al-Mahuza. A canal dug from 62 km to the north was meant to supply it, but the levelling was badly calculated and little water flowed. Its main palace, al-Ja'fari, was modelled on the Dar al-Khilafa, and its plan centred on an avenue passing the Abu Dulaf mosque; see the record of the [[building/abu-dulaf-mosque-samarra|Abu Dulaf Mosque]]. After al-Mutawakkil was assassinated in 247/861, the new city was abandoned. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1116}

## Troubled decades and the return to Baghdad
The article judges that al-Mutawakkil's reign was fundamental to the history of Abbasid Samarra. The spending on architecture, a high though not exactly calculable share of the state budget, stimulated the city's economy, but the drain on the treasury also played a part in the decade of troubles after his death, when four caliphs were made and unmade and there was military action in Samarra in three phases, in 248/862-3, 251-2/865-6 and 256/870. Perhaps more significant, in the article's view, was the isolation of the caliph with his army in Samarra, which exposed him to attempts by the soldiers to control him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1116}

After [[person/al-mutamid|al-Mu'tamid]] came to the throne in 256/870, the army was moved out of Samarra by his brother Abu Ahmad al-Muwaffaq, although the city remained the caliph's official residence until 279/892, when a caliph re-established Baghdad as the capital. Al-Mu'tamid is not known to have revisited Samarra after 269/884, but he was buried there in 279/892. Between 274/887-8 and 281/894-5 there are several reports of looting, after which Samarra is rarely mentioned in the chronicles; the article infers a major depopulation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1116}

The area around the markets stayed occupied, together with the outlying towns of al-Matira and al-Karkh. [[person/al-muktafi-billah|Al-Muktafi]] tried to resettle Samarra in 290/903 but found al-Jawsak in ruins. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1116}

## The shrine and the later town
Two Shi'i Imams, [[person/ali-al-hadi|'Ali al-Hadi]], who died in 254/868, and [[person/al-hasan-al-askari|al-Hasan al-'Askari]], who died in 260/874, had a house on Shari' Abi Ahmad, probably beside al-Mu'tasim's mosque, and were buried there. The article places the disappearance of the Twelfth Imam nearby, in a cleft commemorated by the Sardab al-Mahdi in 260/874. The tomb was first developed in 333/944-5 by the [[polity/hamdanid-dynasty|Hamdanid]] Nasir al-Dawla and then by the [[polity/buyid-dynasty|Buyids]]. A nineteenth-century verse history of the shrine, by al-Shaykh Muhammad al-Samawi, records frequent rebuilding of the double shrine, notably in 445/1053-4 by [[person/al-basasiri|Arslan al-Basasiri]] and in 606/1209-10 by the caliph al-Nasir li-Din Allah, whose work is commemorated by an inscription in the Sardab. The present appearance is attributed to work by the Qajar ruler [[person/naser-al-din-shah-qajar|Nasir al-Din Shah]] in 1285/1868-9 and to other more recent work. From the fourth/tenth century Samarra became a pilgrimage town. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1116}

In the sixth/twelfth and seventh/thirteenth centuries the Tigris shifted eastward south of Samarra, so the Baghdad to Mosul road moved to the west bank and the town lost trade. It was apparently not walled until 1834, when a wall was built from Abbasid bricks with the help of a charitable donation. In the 1950s a barrage was built on the Tigris to divert spring floodwater down Wadi Tharthar and end the periodic flooding of Baghdad. The lake behind it drove farming communities of the flood plain onto the steppe among the Abbasid ruins and enlarged the town, which remains the market centre of its district. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1116}

## In the Pleiades gazetteer
Pleiades, the open gazetteer of ancient places, lists this place under the title «[Samarra]/‘Sumere’» (Pleiades no. 894082), classed as settlement, archaeological site and findspot, with activity attested from 750 BCE to 640 CE. Among its recorded names are «Samarra», «Sumere» and «Dar al-Khilafa». Pleiades describes the site of a major Islamic capital that for a century controlled the Abbasid provinces from Tunisia to Central Asia and was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2007. {cite:pleiades 894082}
