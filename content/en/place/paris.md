## Overview
Paris is the capital and largest city of [[place/france|France]], according to the place card. It appears in three books read for the museum, al-Qazwini's *Athar al-Bilad* (he died in 682 AH), Ali Muhammad al-Salabi's *al-Dawla al-Uthmaniyya* (2001) and the Arabic *Hadir al-Alam al-Islami* with the notes of Shakib Arslan, in four roles: the capital of the land of the Franks for al-Qazwini; a city against which the capitals of medieval Islam are measured; a stage for Ottoman ambassadors, students and opponents in the nineteenth century; and the seat of Arab and Islamic congresses and delegations in the twentieth. Each judgement is attributed to its author. {cite:athar-al-bilad-qazwini p.575} {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.312} {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.86}

## Paris in al-Qazwini
In the entry "Afranja" of *Athar al-Bilad wa Akhbar al-Ibad* al-Qazwini says that the capital of this wide land, which he reports from al-Mas'udi holds about a hundred and fifty cities, is Paris; he gives Paris no entry of its own. {cite:athar-al-bilad-qazwini p.575} See [[place/france|France]].

## Paris in the book's comparisons
*Hadir al-Alam al-Islami* quotes a translated lecture saying that Madinat al-Zahra was to Cordoba what Versailles is to Paris. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.131} Of the Andalusi surgeon Abu al-Qasim al-Zahrawi it reports that Lanfranc, who had gone to Italy and seen a translation of his works, said on returning to Paris in the late thirteenth century that the surgeons of Paris were ignorant and that scarcely one among them knew his craft. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.133} In a chapter on European and Islamic fanaticism the book gives the Romanian minister Djuvara's remark that the civilisation of Baghdad and Basra rose in the late twelfth century above that of Aachen and Paris, and that it was the Franks under Charles Martel who stopped the Muslims at Poitiers. This is Djuvara's statement as the book relays it. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 p.218}

Arslan's notes give a list of works on Muslim religious orders printed in Paris, among them a book on the Sanusiyya of 1884 and one on the orders of the Hijaz of 1887. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.300} Al-Salabi reports that the French ambassador in Istanbul, in a dispatch to his foreign ministry in Paris, described the "Dar al-Ulum al-Hamidiyya" in Beijing as "the Hamidiyya University in Beijing". {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.430}

## Ambassadors, students and reformers
Al-Salabi writes that Sultan Ahmad sent Ottoman ambassadors to Vienna and Paris "for the first time", to bring back information on European diplomacy and military strength. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.312} He notes that Mustafa Rashid Pasha was the state's ambassador in London and Paris and then foreign minister late in Mahmud II's reign, and that his first reform was the Gulhane decree of 1839. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.374} He says [[person/rifaa-al-tahtawi|Rifa'a al-Tahtawi]] went as imam with the mission [[person/muhammad-ali-pasha|Muhammad Ali]] sent to France and wrote a book on the news of Paris, which al-Salabi criticises for its call to free women and to allow mixed company; the criticism is his alone. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.347}

## Ottoman opposition societies
Al-Salabi relates that Namik Kemal, Ziya and Ali Suavi joined the Egyptian prince Mustafa Fazil in Paris and formed the "New Ottomans", and that the name "Young Turkey Party" spread in Europe from there. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.451} He reports that [[person/ahmed-riza|Ahmed Riza]] went to Paris in 1889 on the pretext of attending its international exhibition and declared he would not return, staying about six years until he issued his newspaper in 1895. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.454} The military and civilian wings of the society agreed in Paris to act against [[person/abd-al-hamid-ii-uthmani|Sultan Abd al-Hamid]], and the society forced him on 24 July 1908 to proclaim the constitution. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.455}

## Arab congresses and delegations
Al-Salabi writes that "al-Arabiyya al-Fatat" was formed in Paris in 1329 AH on the model of Young Turkey by students studying there, and then moved its seat to Beirut and Damascus. After the Unionists' crackdown on Arab societies it held an Arab congress in Paris in 1332 AH (1913) under Abd al-Hamid al-Zahrawi, and the French arranged the venue. The delegates resolved on rapid reform, Arab participation in central government, official status for Arabic in the Arab provinces, local military service and sympathy with Armenian demands, and stressed that their movement was not religious. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.465} {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.466} See [[event/arab-congress-paris-1332|the Arab Congress in Paris]].

*Hadir al-Alam al-Islami* says the "Arab National Society" published a leaflet in Paris in 1906 addressed to the great powers, setting out Arab demands for an Arab empire from the Tigris and Euphrates valley to the Suez Canal and from the Mediterranean to the Sea of Oman, under an Arab sultan with a constitutional government, and that a French-language book, *The Awakening of the Arab Nation*, by Najib Azoury, was printed in Paris in 1905. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.86}

## Paris after the Great War
The book says the peace conference refused to receive the Iranian delegation and kept it waiting in Paris while Britain pressed the government in Tehran into an agreement that, in the book's view, made Iran a protectorate; and that Egyptians sent a delegation to Paris which the conference refused to hear, telling it that the British protectorate over Egypt was settled. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.125} The Egyptian delegation lodged a formal protest calling the verdict passed on it in Paris null and without weight. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.128} While the delegation was reaching Paris, the nationalists in Egypt put their demands to the British authority, which then arrested most of the nationalist leaders and deported them to Malta in the spring of 1919. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.129} See [[person/saad-zaghloul|Saad Zaghloul]].

In Arslan's notes the fifth section of the League for the Rights of Man in Paris took up the "Berber decree" in Morocco in 1930 and resolved to ask its central committee to protest the policy of assimilation and to seek the release of exiles. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 p.337} The main text also mentions a "Little Paris" in Algiers, with its Moorish-Eastern decoration, among its examples of distinct European quarters in Eastern cities. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.27}

## Note on the sources
A search of Yaqut's *Mu'jam al-Buldan* found no entry for Paris. Apart from al-Qazwini, the material on this page comes from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and from comparisons made in those books. The Arabic text of *Hadir al-Alam al-Islami* combines Stoddard's translated text with Arslan's notes, and each statement is attributed where the page shows whose it is.
