## Overview
Shiraz is the capital of the province of [[place/fars|Fars]] in southern Iran, and carries the title dar al-ilm, "abode of learning". The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose article by Ann K. S. Lambton this page follows, describes it as an Islamic foundation on a continually inhabited site, which may go back to Sasanian or even earlier times. It stands at about 5,000 feet above sea level, at 29 degrees 36 minutes north and 52 degrees 32 minutes east. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 485}

## Foundation
The article says the city was probably founded, or restored, in 74/693, either by Muhammad, the brother of [[person/al-hajjaj-ibn-yusuf|al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf]], or by his cousin [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-qasim|Muhammad ibn al-Qasim]]. The founder is therefore not settled by the source, which gives both names. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 485}

## Saffarids and Buyids
In the middle of the third/ninth century [[person/yaqub-ibn-layth|Ya'qub ibn al-Layth]] of the [[polity/saffarid-dynasty|Saffarid dynasty]] seized Fars and made Shiraz his capital. His brother and successor [[person/amr-ibn-al-layth|'Amr ibn al-Layth]] built a cathedral mosque on the site where the present congregational mosque stands. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 486}

The [[polity/buyid-dynasty|Buyids]] took Fars in 321/933 under 'Ali ibn Buya, called 'Imad al-Dawla. His nephew [[person/adud-al-dawla|'Adud al-Dawla]] ruled Fars from 338/949 to 366/977, and Iraq and Fars together from 366/977 to 372/983. Under him Shiraz became an important economic and cultural centre. The anonymous geography Hudud al-'alam, written in 372/982-3, calls it a large and flourishing town with two fire-temples. 'Adud al-Dawla built a large library, a hospital, mosques, gardens, palaces, bazaars and caravanserais, and a cantonment for his troops called Kard Fana Khusraw, which grew into a small commercial town. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 486}

The article cites the Fars-nama of Ibn al-Balkhi for the figures. Kard Fana Khusraw alone yielded 16,000 dinars a year, and together with Shiraz it accounted for 316,000 dinars out of a provincial revenue of more than 2,150,000. After 'Adud al-Dawla's death the cantonment decayed and by the first decade of the sixth/twelfth century was a hamlet, with an estimated revenue of 250 dinars, of which no more than 120 was collected. By then the hospital was also in decay, but the library, looked after by the family of the chief judge of Fars, was still in good condition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 486}

Towards the end of the Buyid period there was disorder around the city, and a late Buyid ruler, fearing attacks, built a strong wall round it. Al-Muqaddasi gave the city eight gates, though the article notes that some authorities mention eleven. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 486}

## The Seljuk period
The article calls the accounts of Fars in the early years of [[polity/seljuk-empire|Seljuk]] rule somewhat confused. In 439/1047-8 the Buyid Abu Kalijar made peace with [[person/tughril-beg|Tughril Beg]] and governed Shiraz on his behalf. His son Fulad Sultan was overthrown in 454/1062 by Fadluya, leader of the Shabankara, who was himself defeated the next year by an army from Kirman under Kawurd. Shiraz was plundered repeatedly in these years. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 486}

After the death of [[person/malikshah|Malik Shah]] in 485/1092 control over Fars weakened and rival amirs struggled for the province, yet several Seljuk governors, among them Chawli Saqao, Karaja, Mengu-Bars and Boz-Aba, seem to have established a measure of security and good government in the city. Karaja, atabeg of a son of Muhammad ibn Malik Shah, built and endowed a madrasa that was still one of the city's great madrasas in the eighth/fourteenth century. Mengu-Bars also built a madrasa, and during his government Abu Nasr Lala founded another near the Istakhr Gate. Boz-Aba took Fars in 532/1137-8, was expelled, retook it in 534/1139-40 and died in 542/1147. His wife Zahida Khatun is reputed to have governed Shiraz for twenty-one years, and the article suggests this covered both his lifetime, when he was often away on campaign, and the time after. She built a magnificent madrasa and set up many endowments for it; sixty jurists received daily allowances there and many pious and learned men lived in it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 486}

## The Mongol period and the Muzaffarids
The text of the pages that deal with the thirteenth century is badly damaged in the scan, so that part is not reported here. What remains legible concerns the last decades of the [[polity/ilkhanate|Ilkhanate]]. The historian Wassaf praised Kurdujin for her care of the people and for maintaining buildings put up by her forebears, among them the 'Adudi madrasa. According to the article, this madrasa was built by Terken Khatun, wife of Sa'd ibn Abi Bakr, and was possibly named after her son Muhammad, whose title was 'Adud al-Din. Wassaf states that its endowments yielded more than 200,000 dinars when he wrote in 727/1326, and that Kurdujin spent them on their proper purposes and increased them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 488}

In the reign of [[person/abu-said-ilkhan|Abu Sa'id]] Mahmud Shah, son of Muhammad Shah Inju, was sent to Fars by Oljeytu to run the royal estates and made himself practically independent in Shiraz and Fars. His son Mas'ud gave up Shiraz in 740/1339 to Pir Husayn, grandson of Coban. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 488}

The traveller [[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]] singled out the shrine of Ahmad ibn Musa, which the people of Shiraz held in great reverence. Tash Khatun, mother of Abu Ishaq, built a large college and hospice at the tomb, where food was given to all comers and Qur'an readers recited without pause. Ibn Battuta says she went to the sanctuary every Monday eve, when judges, jurists and sharifs gathered there, and that more than 1,400 sharifs, children and adults, received stipends. The tomb-mosque was known locally as Shah Chiragh; it was rebuilt in 1506 and later, but by then the college and hospice were gone. He also mentions the mausoleum of [[person/ruzbihan-al-baqli|Ruzbihan Baqli]] and the tomb of Zarkub. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 488}

In 754/1353 Mubariz al-Din Muhammad of the [[polity/muzaffarid-dynasty|Muzaffarids]] captured Shiraz. Abu Ishaq fled but was caught and executed in 758/1357. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 488}

## Timur, the Timurids and the Turkmen
[[person/timur-lang|Timur]] invaded southern Persia in 789/1387 and put the Muzaffarid Shah Yahya in charge of Shiraz; after Timur withdrew, Shah Mansur took the city from him, and was killed in 795/1393 in a clash with Timurid forces outside the walls. The article finds a cultural revival under the [[polity/timurid-empire|Timurids]]. Iskandar ibn 'Umar Shaykh, who ruled 812-17/1409-14, made a number of new buildings, and during his government and that of [[person/ibrahim-sultan-timurid|Ibrahim ibn Shahrukh]], governor of Fars from 817/1414, a new style of painting flourished in Shiraz. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 488}

The later Timurids disputed Fars with the [[polity/qara-qoyunlu|Kara Koyunlu]] and the [[group/aq-qoyunlu|Ak Koyunlu]]. In the reign of [[person/uzun-hasan|Uzun Hasan]], who beat the last Kara Koyunlu ruler Jahanshah in 872/1467 and the Timurid Abu Sa'id the following year, Shiraz again became a thriving city. The article cites the Venetian traveller Josafa Barbaro, who was in Persia between 1436 and 1451, as saying that it was a great city with a prosperous transit trade, with jewels, silks and spices on sale in quantity, high mud walls, deep ditches, excellent mosques and good houses, and that security prevailed. Ludovico di Varthema, who set out for the East in 1502, also reports large quantities of jewels. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 488}

## The Safavid period
Shiraz fell to the [[polity/safavid-iran|Safavids]] in 909/1503. Under the early Safavids it was ruled by Dhu 'l-Qadr governors. [[person/abbas-i-safavi|'Abbas I]] then appointed the qullar aqasi Allahwirdi Khan, who died in 1022/1613, governor in 1004/1595-6, and he was followed by his son Imam Quli Khan. Under them Fars enjoyed considerable independence and Shiraz prospered. Allahwirdi Khan built the Khan Madrasa for [[person/mulla-sadra|Mulla Sadra]], who returned to Shiraz and taught there for roughly the last thirty years of his life. Imam Quli Khan built a palace in the maydan, raised walls round the city, and planted cypresses on both sides of the Isfahan road in imitation of the Chahar Bagh of [[place/isfahan|Isfahan]]. He entertained the English envoy Sir Dodmore Cotton in 1628. Shah Safi suspected him of rebellious intentions and had him murdered in 1042/1632, after which the city was administered from the central diwan under a wazir. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 488}

The city lay on the direct route from the Persian Gulf to Isfahan, the Safavid capital, and many European travellers passed through it and recorded impressions; the article names Della Valle, Herbert, Tavernier, Thevenot, Chardin, Fryer, Kaempfer and de Bruin. Herbert still saw part of the old walls, which had vanished by the time of Tavernier and Chardin. The English East India Company set up a factory in 1617, but trade had shrunk by the middle of the century through Dutch rivalry. A Carmelite house was founded in 1623, closed temporarily in 1631 and reopened in 1634. Floods partly destroyed the city in 1630 and 1668, the second followed by pestilence, but when Fryer came in 1676 it had largely recovered. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 488}

## Karim Khan Zand
The scan is damaged for the years between the fall of the Safavids and the rise of the Zands, so they are omitted here. [[person/karim-khan-zand|Karim Khan]] of the [[polity/zand-dynasty|Zand dynasty]] made Shiraz his capital and carried out a large building programme, in which craftsmen and workmen from all over Persia took part. He built a new wall with a dry ditch; the traveller William Francklin, there in 1786-7, gives the wall as 25 feet high and 10 feet thick, with round towers 80 paces apart and six gates, and the chronicler Rustam al-Hukama' says 12,000 labourers dug the ditch. Karim Khan also built or repaired the fortress, raised a citadel where his successor Ja'far Khan lived, and added a diwan-khana, an artillery park and a fine brick covered market, the Wakil Bazaar, whose shops the Khan let to merchants for a monthly rent. The foundations of a splendid mosque and its buildings were laid but not finished before he died, and he built several thousand houses for the Lurs and Laks of his army. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 489}

In this period the city had eleven quarters, five Haydari and five Ni'mati, the eleventh being inhabited by the Jews, who had grown in number. The Armenians, mainly in the wine trade, had also increased. After Karim Khan's death [[person/agha-muhammad-khan-qajar|Agha Muhammad Khan Qajar]], who had been held hostage in Shiraz, escaped. In 1204/1789-90 he marched south and besieged Lutf 'Ali Khan Zand, who had succeeded Ja'far Khan in 1203/1789 and fled to Shiraz; after three months he lifted the siege to deal with Turkmen disorders. While Lutf 'Ali was away trying to retake Isfahan in 1205/1791, Hajji Ibrahim, the kalantar left in charge, seized Shiraz and negotiated its surrender to Agha Muhammad Khan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 489}

## The Qajar period
Under the [[polity/qajar-iran|Qajars]] Shiraz stayed the provincial capital, but Qajar princes held the governorship for much of the time and were often absent on campaign or at court, so that the wazirs of Fars did most of the administering. Distance from [[place/tehran|Tehran]] made central control precarious and intermittent. In 1794-5 Fath 'Ali Mirza, later [[person/fath-ali-shah-qajar|Fath 'Ali Shah]], was made governor of Fars, Kirman and Yazd. On becoming shah in 1797 he named his brother Husayn Quli Mirza governor, who rebelled the next year and at once submitted. Later governors included Fath 'Ali Shah's son Husayn 'Ali Mirza Farman-Farma, who brought 800 riflemen from Nur in Mazandaran; they were unpopular and caused many disorders, and were ordered back to Tehran in 1244/1828-9. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 489} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 490}

Visitors found the city worn down. Scott Waring in 1802 reported at least a quarter in ruins, Sir William Gore Ouseley noted its decay in 1811, and James Morier cautiously estimated the population in 1810 at no more than 19,000. The nineteenth century was marked by natural disasters, family and tribal rivalries and financial mismanagement. The chronicler Fasa'i says that 6,000 people died of pestilence in five or six days in 1237/1822. Cholera recurred, locusts and plague brought famine in 1247/1830-1, and a severe famine ran from 1860 to 1871-2, during which the governor Muhammad Qasim Khan set up workhouses. In 1876 the governor Farhad Mirza tried to regulate the building trade, fixing the number of bricks, their price and the master bricklayer's fee, and in 1299/1881-2 the streets were paved with stone. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 490}

Hajji Ibrahim became Agha Muhammad Khan's first minister. Fath 'Ali Shah put him to death in 1215/1801, but his son Mirza 'Ali Akbar was made kalantar of Fars in 1226/1811-12 and in 1245/1829-30 received the title Qavam al-Mulk. He and his descendants led the Khamsa tribal federation and had a major part in provincial and city politics; their main rivals were the chiefs of the Kashkai. The ulama also counted in city politics. Morier records great discontent in about 1811 over the price of bread, when the people turned to the Shaykh al-Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 490}

Several travellers describe the economy. Scott Waring says the commander of the citadel levied a toll on goods entering the city, and Binning, in the middle of the century, says each craft and trade paid the government a lump sum, apportioned among its members. Consul Abbott, writing at the same time, counted about 1,200 shops in the bazaars and notes hardware, cutlery, guns, swords, daggers and inlay work among local products. Irregular payment of provincial taxes led to disputes with the court. Fath 'Ali Shah came to Shiraz in 1244/1828-9 and accepted 200,000 tumans from Husayn 'Ali Mirza, and when he set out again to collect arrears he fell ill at Isfahan and died there on 23 October 1834. Husayn 'Ali Mirza then had the khutba read in his own name and marched on Isfahan, but was defeated, rioting broke out in Shiraz, and he surrendered. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 490}

Disorder was frequent. An insurrection in 1254-5/1839, partly provoked by the Azerbaijani Turkish soldiers and stirred up by Shaykh Abu Turab, led to the dismissal of the governor Firaydun Mirza Farman-Farma. In 1261/1845 Sayyid 'Ali Muhammad declared himself the Bab in Shiraz and was arrested and expelled from the city. The movement is known as that of the [[group/babis|Babis]]. Consul Abbott called the population unruly and reported riot and bloodshed under Bahram Mirza in 1848-9, as well as frequent factional strife between Haydaris and Ni'matis. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 490}

In March 1865 the Indo-European telegraph line from Tehran to Bushire, which passed through Shiraz, began to work. Zill al-Sultan, governor of Isfahan from 1874, was given Fars in 1881 and until 1887 most of southern Persia was virtually independent of the central government, Fars being governed from Isfahan through subordinates. The census of 1301/1883-4 counted 6,327 houses in the eleven quarters, with 25,284 men and boys and 28,323 women and girls. In 1891, in the Tobacco affair, there was violent opposition to the Regie in Shiraz. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 491}

## The twentieth century
The constitutional movement of the early twentieth century reached Shiraz, though it never became one of its main centres, with disturbances in 1906 and riots in March 1907. Much of Fars was in turmoil, and the disorder continued through the First World War. In autumn 1915 the Kashkai and a mutinous gendarmerie seized the British consulate, the Imperial Bank of Persia and the telegraph company's offices and took members of the British community prisoner. The Southern Persian Rifles were formed and recognised in March 1917, the Kashkai besieged the city in the summer of 1918 and were defeated in October, and in the influenza epidemic of that year 10,000 people in Shiraz died. In the 1920s Rida Shah brought the tribes under control. Shiraz took little part in the industrial growth of his reign, but developed considerably after the Second World War. Its population was 129,023 in 1961-2 and about 325,000 by 1972. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 491}

## In the early Islamic sources
Al-Baladhuri reports that Abu Musa and Uthman ibn Abi al-As conquered Shiraz in the last years of Umar's caliphate. It lies in the land of Ardashir Khurra, and its people were kept as protected subjects who paid tribute, except those who chose to leave, and they were not to be killed or enslaved. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.546}

Idrisi describes Shiraz as a town of the district of Ardashir Khurra, and as the capital of Fars and the seat of its kingdom, where governors and officials lived and where the diwan and the tax office stood. He says it was an Islamic city founded by Muhammad ibn al-Qasim ibn Abi Aqil, a cousin of al-Hajjaj. Its name is explained as "the belly of the lion", because provisions were brought to it from every land and none left it. The Muslim army camped on its site until Istakhr and its district were taken, and then they built Shiraz there. {cite:nuzhat-al-mushtaq-2002 p.405}
