## Overview
In this record Turkistan is a broad region, the lands of the Turks in and beyond Transoxiana. In Yaqut al-Hamawi's Mu'jam al-Buldan the word, written Turkistan, is a name that embraces all the lands of the Turks {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.23}. The museum's data place it roughly at 43.3 degrees north and 68.3 degrees east.

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-Buldan
Yaqut cites traditions and sayings attributed to the Prophet and to some Companions about the Turks, among them the saying to leave the Turks alone as long as they leave you alone, and he passes on reports of how many young a sheep bears in their lands {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.23}. He says the widest of the Turkish lands is that of the Toghuzghuz, bounded by China, Tibet, the Khallukh, the Kimak, the Ghuzz and others; that its first limit on the Muslim side is the city of Farab; and that its famous cities are sixteen {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.23}.

He relates that Hisham ibn Abd al-Malik sent an envoy to the Turkish king to invite him to Islam. The king received him while making a saddle with his own hands, then showed him massed horsemen to make the point that none of them was a cupper, cobbler or tailor, and asked how they would eat if they took on the duties of Islam {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.24}. On the same page he describes the Kimak as nomads who sell pasture {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.24}. He also reports the journey of Tamim ibn Bahr al-Mutawwi'i to the khaqan of the Toghuzghuz: a bitter cold in which the road can be travelled only six months of the year, twenty days through open country with springs and grass, then twenty days through continuous villages whose people were mostly fire-worshippers of the Magian way, with some Manichaean zindiqs among them, and then the fortified royal city with twelve great iron gates, which he estimated at about three hundred farsakhs from China {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.24}.

On a belief common in the east, as Yaqut passes it on, the Turks bring rain with a stone. The son of a Ghuzz king is quoted telling a story that one of his forebears found such stones in a far land and carried some home; the teller denies that it is a trick of theirs and refers it to the power of God {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.25}. Yaqut also quotes the Samanid Isma'il ibn Ahmad, governor of Khurasan, that he campaigned against the Turks with about twenty thousand Muslims and was met by sixty thousand, and that when a vast cloud appeared he dismounted, prayed two rak'as and asked God to turn its harm away {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.25}.

## A note on scope
The only source used is Yaqut, who gathers reports from travellers and informants, so statements here are attributed to those who made them. Nothing was read on Turkistan in the modern period.
