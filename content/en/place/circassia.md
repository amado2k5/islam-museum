## Overview
Circassia, which the Arabic sources call "the land of the Jarkas", is a region of the North Caucasus, as the place card says. The sources read here say little about it as such, so this page gathers what they say about the Circassians in scattered places: Ibn Battuta in the presence of Sultan Uzbek of the Golden Horde, and Hadir al-Alam al-Islami in the 1920s and 1930s, where Shakib Arslan copies from his "notebooks" a division of the Caucasus and speaks of a Caucasian delegation he knew in person. Everything the book says of the Circassians describes their state in that age, in its author's voice. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 scan p.373}

## The Caucasus in three parts, in Arslan's notes
Shakib Arslan copies from his "notebooks", which he says take their contents from sources he trusts, that Caucasia has three parts: Georgia, Dagestan and the land of the Circassians. Georgia holds the Georgian nation, which has a language of its own, some Muslim and some Christian, the Christians being more numerous, and its capital is Tbilisi. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 scan p.373} The Circassians he puts at one to two million, and counts among them the Kabardians, the Abzakh, the Shapsug and a tribe the scanned text reads as "Ubukh"; he counts the Kumyks, the Chechens and other peoples of the eastern mountains with Dagestan, not with the land of the Circassians. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 scan p.374} The grouping and the limits of the Circassians in this passage are Arslan's classification of that time. See [[place/caucasus|the Caucasus]] and [[place/georgia|Georgia]].

## The war with Russia and the Caucasian republics
In a separate chapter, "The land of Dagestan and Shaykh Shamil", the book treats the mountain war with the Russians. It says Shamil led the jihad for about thirty-five years and it ended with his surrender to the Russians on 6 September 1859, that the Russians removed the local princes from their seats in 1862, and that the Dagestanis rose again in the war of 1877 between Russia and the Ottoman state. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 scan p.195-196} See [[person/imam-shamil|Imam Shamil]]. The chapter's subject is Dagestan, not the land of the Circassians, but in its course Arslan mentions a delegation of both peoples.

He tells that when the Bolshevik government declared the independence of nations in 1917, republics arose in Georgia, Dagestan, Azerbaijan and Erivan, and each sent a delegation to Istanbul to negotiate with the Turks and the Germans for recognition. The "Dagestani-Circassian delegation" consisted of Abd al-Majid Bey, Ali Bey and Haydar Bey Bammat, who had been Dagestan's foreign minister. At the request of Enver Pasha and of the delegation, Arslan worked in Berlin for equality among all the Caucasian republics. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 scan p.196-197} He then says that the British occupied the Caucasus when the war ended, that General Denikin invaded Dagestan, that the Bolsheviks afterwards sent armies against the four republics and joined them to Moscow, and that some heads of the independent governments, among them Abd al-Majid Bey and Haydar Bey Bammat, were driven to Europe. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 scan p.197}

He says he met the Dagestani-Circassian delegation at Bern in Switzerland in 1919 and that Haydar Bey Bammat asked him to write letters for them to the heads of their country in classical Arabic, because the language of learning in the Dagestan mountains is Arabic. Among the Circassians he names his friend Aziz Bey Mekir, secretary of the Turkish embassy in Moscow, "one of the worthy men of the Circassian nation" in his words, who wrote a treatise in French full of news of that nation. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 scan p.198}

## Circassians who rose elsewhere
In the chapter on the West's mastery over the East, the book treats the Muslim reformers who took up the means of reform, and gives as its example [[person/khayr-al-din-al-tunisi|Khayr al-Din Pasha]] in Tunisia, whom it calls "the bold commander, Circassian in origin", who won the trust of his master the Bey, gained great standing with him, was made his minister and was handed the reins of affairs. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 scan p.19} See [[place/tunisia|Tunisia]].

In speaking of Egypt the book says that the small upper class which held power until the British occupation is mostly descended from the last Asian conquerors, Arabs, Kurds, Circassians, Albanians and Turks. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 scan p.95}

## Circassians at Sarai in Ibn Battuta
On his journey to the Golden Horde, [[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]] reached the city of "al-Sara", the seat of Sultan Uzbek, which he calls one of the finest cities, extremely large, on a plain, crowded with people. He lists its peoples: the Mongols, who are the country's people and its sultans, some of them Muslims; the Alans, who are Muslims; and the Qipchaq, Circassians, Russians and Greeks, who are Christians. Each group lives in its own quarter with its own markets, while merchants and strangers from Iraq, Egypt, Syria and elsewhere live in a walled quarter to protect trade goods. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.363} In Ibn Battuta the Circassians are therefore a Christian community among the inhabitants of the capital; see [[place/golden-horde|the Golden Horde]].
