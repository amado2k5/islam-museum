## Overview
Bandar Seri Begawan is the capital of Brunei today, on the island of Borneo. Hadir al-Alam al-Islami, with Shakib Arslan's notes, mentions "the city of Brunei" and the town of Brunei, and never gives the name Bandar Seri Begawan. This page assumes that the volume's town of Brunei is the city the museum's card describes; that identification is the page author's, not the text's. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.351} What the volume says of the city itself is little; most of it concerns the island, its sultanates and events around it, in the chapter on Islam in Java and its neighbours.

## Brunei in the description of the island
The book calls Borneo the largest island of the Malay archipelago and, after New Guinea, the largest on earth, 746 thousand square kilometres in area, on the equator, its mountains covered with trees, with large rivers, minerals, precious stones and petroleum extracted in quantity. It is divided between England and Holland: the Dutch part lies to the east, south and west and the English part to the north, made up of the principality of Sarawak, the lands of the English company in northern Borneo, the island of Labuan and the town of Brunei. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.351} See [[place/borneo|Borneo]] and [[place/brunei|Brunei]].

It says Borneo had relations with China to the north and with India, that many of its kings were of Indian origin and that it holds many temples of Indian worship. It says Islam did not enter Borneo until the middle of the sixteenth century, and that the first Muslim sultan there was Giri Kusuma, who came to the throne of Sukadana in 1590. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.352} That is the book's statement about the island as a whole, with an example from the west coast; this page does not take it as a statement about Islam in Brunei, for which the book gives no date. Elsewhere the book says, citing research by the orientalist Hurgronje, that Islam spread from Java to Sumatra and to part of Borneo, Celebes and the eastern islands through Muslim merchants from India, peacefully in his account. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.339}

## The amir of Brunei and Sambas
In its account of the sultanate of Sambas the book says it was founded by Malays of Johor and made a treaty with the Dutch East India Company in 1609, and that in the first half of the seventeenth century Radin Sulayman, son of Raja Tengah, amir of Brunei, overcame the rule of Sambas and drove its ruler out; his mother, of the royal house of Sukadana, lived in Sambas. Radin Sulayman then ruled under the name Sultan Muhammad Safi al-Din, the first king of the royal line that holds it to the time of the book. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.353}

## James Brooke and Brunei
The book tells the origin of the principality of Sarawak, whose capital is Kuching: an English sailor, James Brooke, reached the town of Brunei in his own ship and found conditions intolerable, with oppression, tyranny, a lack of security and people being stripped of their money. An amir there called Muda Hasan relied on the English captain and entrusted affairs to him; he improved conditions and secured order, and the sultan of Brunei recognised James as ruler of Sarawak, and so he became its prince. The year in which the sultan recognised him is incomplete in the page read; the book afterwards gives 1842 beside its statement that he used the pagans against the Muslims, and that the English government sent him some reinforcements in his battles with the Arabs and Malays. It says he treated Europeans and natives alike, so the people of the principality prospered and its borders widened, and that he died in 1863 and was succeeded by his nephew Charles Brooke, whose realm came under the protection of Great Britain. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.353} In a footnote Arslan wishes that European governments would follow his policy of equal treatment. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.353} See [[place/kuching|Kuching]], [[event/anglo-bruneian-war|the Anglo-Bruneian war]] and [[event/battle-of-brunei-town-1845|the battle of Brunei town, 1845]]; these are museum cards, and nothing was read in the book that bears on them.

## The island's people
The book puts Borneo's population at one million seven hundred thousand, including about sixty thousand Chinese, a few thousand Arabs and about two thousand Europeans, and says it is sparsely peopled for its size. It says they are of a people called the Dayaks in the interior and of Muslim Malays on the coast. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.353}

## A note on the source
The figures and estimates in the book are from the early twentieth century; it does not describe the city of Brunei, its mosque or its court, and it does not mention the name Bandar Seri Begawan. Some dates came through the scan incomplete or garbled and were not written. Arslan reports what he reports from European sources and comments in his own voice, so what the book says of the origins of royal houses and of Europeans is what it relates.
