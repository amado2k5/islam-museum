## Overview
Indonesia is the name of the modern state that covers the Southeast Asian archipelago, and it is a name the sources behind this page do not use. The old geographers and travellers speak of "Jawa" and similar names, and Hadir al-Alam al-Islami, written in the 1920s and 1930s, speaks of the "Dutch East Indies", Java and Sumatra. This page gathers what these sources say about the Islam of the archipelago, each in the voice of its own time. The widest account is a lecture given by Sayyid Ismail al-Attas, one of the Hadrami sayyids settled in Java, at the Young Men's Muslim Association in Cairo on 6 January 1929, which Shakib Arslan included in Hadir al-Alam al-Islami. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.381}

## Jawa in al-Qazwini
Zakariyya al-Qazwini lists "Jawa" in Athar al-Bilad wa Akhbar al-Ibad as a land on the coast of the China Sea next to the lands of India. In his time, he says, merchants from China reach no further than this land, since reaching any other is hard because of the distance and the difference in religions; merchants bring from it Javanese aloeswood, camphor, spikenard, cloves and mace. {cite:athar-al-bilad-qazwini p.28} He does not say which island he means by the name: Ibn Battuta applies it to the island that holds Sumatra, and Hadir al-Alam al-Islami applies it to the island of Java.

## Ibn Battuta at Sumatra
[[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]] says that after a sea voyage he reached "the island of al-Jawa", written with a jim, the island from which Javanese benzoin takes its name. It appeared to him from half a day's distance, green and fresh, its trees mostly coconut, areca, clove, aloeswood and camphor-reed; the people buy and sell with pieces of tin and unrefined Chinese gold, and much of the aromatic spice sold in the lands of the unbelievers comes from it. The islanders came out to the ship in small boats with coconuts, bananas and fish, as is their custom in giving gifts to merchants. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.629}

The seat of its ruler was the city of "Samatra" (Sumatra), which he calls a fine large city with a wooden wall and wooden towers. Its sultan was al-Malik al-Zahir, whom he describes as one of the finest and most generous of kings, a Shafi'i who loved jurists, who attended his council to read and discuss; much given to jihad and raiding, and so humble that he walked to Friday prayer on foot. His people, Shafi'is eager for jihad, go out with him as volunteers and prevail over the unbelievers near them, who pay them tribute by treaty. Among those who came out to receive him were the qadi and sharif Amir Sayyid al-Shirazi and Taj al-Din al-Isfahani, with other jurists. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.630}

He describes the way a guest is received there: a newcomer does not greet the sultan until three days have passed, so that the fatigue of travel leaves him, and then meets him after the Friday prayer in the mosque's enclosure. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.631} He tells how the sultan sat with him discussing jurisprudence on the school of Imam al-Shafi'i until the afternoon prayer, and how he then saw the sultan ride in procession among elephants and horses with his viziers and amirs in ranks before him. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.632}

He relates the revolt of the sultan's nephew and its cause, and that the sultan afterwards built the wall round Sumatra because it had none when the rebel entered. The sultan then fitted out a junk for him with provisions, and he sailed twenty-one nights along the length of the sultan's country before reaching "Mul Jawa", which he says is a land of unbelievers two months' journey long where spices and fine aloeswood abound. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.633} See [[place/sumatra|Sumatra]].

## How Islam came to Java: Arslan's account
Hadir al-Alam al-Islami has a chapter on "Islam in Java and its neighbours" in which Arslan summarises what Dutch scholars wrote. According to them the Arabs brought Islam to those islands through trade and navigation, landing first at the seaports and famous anchorages and spreading inland from there; it did not appear that they at first intended to found a kingdom or to conquer lands. He adds that when the Malay people came to rival them and block their way, these merchant sailors resorted to armed force, and the kingdom of "Demak" was the first Arab conquest in Java. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.362} See [[place/demak|Demak]].

He says Islam spread slowly in Java at first and advanced faster in the west of the island, among the Sundanese, than among the Javanese; the Sundanese are still firmer in their religion and know it better. He observes that the Arabs did not put up grand religious buildings in Java as the Brahmans and Buddhists had before them, though mosques are numerous and no town lacks one, that the number of pilgrims each year is very large, and that the title "haji" is held in the highest regard. The historians he follows reckon the entry of Islam into Java at five centuries, from the twelfth to the Dutch occupation of Batavia in the seventeenth. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.366}

He says the greatest Hindu kingdom there was "Majapahit", and that the princes of the states under it turned toward Islam until it fell; he dates the fall to 1418 of the Javanese calendar, equal to 1488 CE as he reports it (the pair is the book's; counted on the Saka reckoning, year 1418 would fall in 1496 CE, so this page does not vouch for the equivalence). The greatest sultanate Islam founded in Java was in the land of "Mataram", which stayed strong until the eighteenth century and then began to fall under Dutch attacks. He concludes that the Arabs did not found one comprehensive Islamic sultanate in Java, since many obstacles stood in the way of its union; they founded an Islamic social body able to endure above the crumbling kingdoms. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.367}

In the same chapter he quotes Professor Hurgronje on Dutch policy toward the Hadramis: the Dutch government harassed those who entered and watched their movements closely, and some prominent Arabs brought their case to the caliphate in Turkey and filled the Muslim press with complaints, which in the orientalist's view were greatly exaggerated. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.361}

The chapter describes Java with Madura as the centre of the Dutch colonies, its capital Batavia where the governor-general sits for Holland, and says all the native Javanese are Muslims, with Europeans, Chinese and Arabs among the inhabitants; the population figures on the scanned page are inconsistent and are not repeated here. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.368}

## The lecture of Sayyid Ismail al-Attas
The lecture begins before history, mentioning the discovery of iron tools and of the remains of a skeleton that archaeologists call "ape-man", and judges it likely that its owners were the ancient population of the island. It then turns to the Hindu age: the first Indian arrival is tied to the year 75 or 78 CE; the state of "Mataram" in central Java reached its height in the ninth century CE and produced the builders who raised the Borobudur temple; and the Malay language holds many words of Sanskrit origin. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.381-382}

It tells that "Maulana Ibrahim" spread Islam in the village of "Leran" near the town of Gresik in the time of Majapahit, that other preachers followed and some princes were converted at their hands, and that in the second half of the fourteenth century CE there were eight Muslim princes with the title "Susuhunan". A war of four years followed between them and Majapahit, led by a Majapahit prince who had himself accepted Islam; the Muslims were first broken, then regrouped, and Majapahit's armies were defeated in a battle lasting five days in 1475 CE, after which Islam spread as the people and princes entered it in groups and singly. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.382-383} The lecture therefore dates the fall of Majapahit to 1475 and Arslan's own passage above to 1488; the book does not decide between them.

The lecturer says the date of Islam's entry into the archipelago is not fully known; it probably came with Arab traders in the early centuries of the Hijra, and Chinese calendar books mention an Arab community on the west coast of Sumatra in 674 CE. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.383} Some scholars infer from the people's following of the Shafi'i school, which also prevails on the Malabar coast, that Islam reached the islands from south India; traces of Shi'ism survive; and Ibn Battuta shows that the Muslim ruler of Sumatra had good relations with the king of Delhi and kept two scholars from Persia near him, one from Shiraz and one from Isfahan. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.384} Marco Polo, who spent five months on Sumatra's north coast in 1292, said all the inhabitants were idolaters except those of a small kingdom in the north-east, who had accepted Islam through Arab traders; and in 1345, the lecturer says, Ibn Battuta entered Sumatra and found a Muslim king named al-Malik al-Zahir. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.384-385}

He describes the standing of Arabs among the islanders, especially sayyids and sharifs whose marriage ties the princes prized, and says that from the seventeenth century most Arab migrants were Hadramis who lived by trade and then by shipping; their sailing ships were at their peak between 1845 and 1855 and then declined before European steamships. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.385} He gives the Muslims of the Dutch East Indies alone as fifty million, says many literate islanders correspond in Malay in Arabic letters, and says more than a quarter of the words of the Malay language come from Arabic. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.386}

## The Portuguese and the Dutch
The lecturer says the Portuguese arrived in 1511 when ports of Java were still Hindu-Buddhist; a Buddhist ruler at war with the Muslim prince of Cirebon sought their help, but when they returned the sultan of Cirebon had won and taken the harbour. The place names are garbled in the scanned text. He says merchants of Amsterdam founded a company for East Indian trade and their four ships sailed in 1595 and reached Bantam in Java in 1596, that the fort was named Batavia on 12 March 1619, and that the town became the capital of the Dutch East Indies. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 scan p.386-387} See [[place/jakarta|Jakarta]] and [[place/dutch-east-indies|the Dutch East Indies]].

Later in the book Arslan corrects his earlier estimate of the Muslims of Java and Sumatra, saying the latest census puts them at fifty million, not thirty-five. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 scan p.370} See also [[place/java|Java]] and [[place/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]].
