## Overview
Kufa was one of the two garrison cities, with [[place/basra|Basra]], that early Islam founded in Iraq. It was a permanent military base of the Arabs in Mesopotamia and kept the whole of the Iraqi Sawad under its control. It took an active part in the Muslim advance into Iranian lands and, through the seventh century, was a place of intense political ferment. The Encyclopaedia of Islam adds that it was there, as at Basra, that Arabo-Islamic civilisation and culture took shape over some three centuries. The city then declined and fell into ruin, and the article says that only a few traces remain today, mostly late or restored. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 383}

## Foundation
The article dates the founding to 17 AH, 638 CE, by [[person/saad-ibn-abi-waqqas|Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas]], the victor of [[event/battle-of-al-qadisiyya|al-Qadisiyya]]. By then the whole of Iraq had been taken from the Sasanians, and the Arabs had captured [[place/al-madain|al-Mada'in]] and Ctesiphon in 16/637, whose climate they could not endure. The article adds other reasons. [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] preferred to keep the Arabs together, apart from the conquered peoples and in spatial continuity with Arabia. It sees in this a set of principles for the relation between conquerors and conquered: no dispersal in the Sawad and no farming settlement, preservation of the military striking force, and a new fiscal system that would draw the revenues of Iraq without the Arabs exploiting the land directly. All of it rested on the coexistence of Arab clans of very different origin. The article judges that Umar wished to make the city the experimental crucible of his system, which took concrete form in the establishment of the diwan between 20 and 23/640 and 643. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 383}

## Site and name
Kufa was a creation from nothing. It stood at the edge of the Arabian steppe, on the right bank of the main branch of the middle Euphrates, on a tongue of dry grey sand mixed with gravel, a few miles north-east of [[place/al-hira|al-Hira]]. The spot guarded the passage towards Babylon and, beyond it, Ctesiphon, and lay in the region of al-Qadisiyya that the Arab army already knew. The article says it was slightly above the water level, escaped floods, was well supplied with water and enjoyed a healthy climate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 383}

The origin of the name is not known exactly. Arab historians and geographers made it a common noun for any round surface of sand, which the article regards as a later reconstruction. It mentions that Massignon derived the name from a Syriac word; the OCR of the rest of this passage is unreadable, so the proposed etymology is not given here. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 383}

## Layout of the Umayyad city
Umayyad Kufa had no ramparts, and the article supposes its diameter did not exceed two kilometres. The monumental centre held the congregational mosque, the fortified [[building/qasr-al-imara-kufa|qasr]], an open ground for ceremonies, and markets built under the governor [[person/khalid-ibn-abdallah-al-qasri|Khalid al-Qasri]], who governed in 105 to 120/723 to 737, though the markets had been put to specialised use much earlier. The article notes that Massignon saw in them the prototype of the markets of Baghdad, and itself suggests that their vaulted form and organisation, with moneychangers who acted as bankers to governors, may have served as a model for medieval Islamic markets generally. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 385}

Tribal lots radiated from the centre and made up most of the dwellings. From the earliest period individual plots were granted as favours to Companions and great figures, whose houses stood near the centre. The article cites a list by al-Ya'qubi of twenty-five such houses, among them those of [[person/abdullah-ibn-masud|Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud]], [[person/abu-musa-al-ashari|Abu Musa al-Ash'ari]], [[person/khalid-ibn-urfuta|Khalid ibn Urfuta]], [[person/adi-ibn-hatim|Adi ibn Hatim]], [[person/jarir-ibn-abd-allah-al-bajali|Jarir ibn Abd Allah al-Bajali]] and [[person/al-ashath-ibn-qays|al-Ash'ath ibn Qays]]. Other sources mention the houses of [[person/al-walid-ibn-uqba|al-Walid ibn Uqba]] and [[person/al-mukhtar-ibn-abi-ubayd|al-Mukhtar]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 385}

Avenues, streets, baths and small clan mosques completed the plan, together with about a dozen open spaces called jabbanat. The article takes these to have existed almost certainly from the time of [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]]. They served as tribal burial grounds and, more importantly, as places of assembly, mobilisation and arming, and they figure in major episodes such as the revolt of al-Mukhtar. Another feature was the Kunasa, first a dumping ground west of the camp, later a place where caravans from Arabia unloaded, an animal market, a site of executions and a fair for poets. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 385}

## From camp to city
The article describes a change from a Bedouin-style camp to a built, functional city under the Umayyads, with urban efforts by [[person/ziyad-ibn-abi-sufyan|Ziyad]] and Khalid al-Qasri. It emphasises that the Arabs of Kufa were truly settled while keeping close ties with Arabia, and that this was the first time Arabs had formed so large an urban concentration. After the early [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasids]] came to power they considered making Kufa their capital, but Alid presence and sympathies there were strong, and they hesitated between Kufa, [[place/al-anbar|al-Anbar]] and Hashimiyya. [[person/al-mansur|Al-Mansur]] then founded [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] in 145 to 146/762 to 763 and moved the treasury and the government offices from Kufa. The article reads this as showing that Kufa had been an administrative capital. After the move, al-Mansur had the city given a rampart and moat at the inhabitants' expense. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 385}

## Decline
By the fourth/tenth century, the article says, Kufa had become provincial. It places this inside a wider crisis of early Islamic state and society, as the caliphate came under [[polity/buyid-dynasty|Buyid]] tutelage. It names the Ismaili outbreak of the late third/ninth century, for which Kufa was a centre, and attacks and pillage by the Qarmatians in 293/905, 312/924 and 315/927, from which it never recovered. Near it, in 334/945, [[place/najaf|Najaf]], the shrine of Ali, arose under Buyid protection as a centre of Shi'i devotion. The article presents this as the point at which the older Arab Kufa, which it says was far from identical with the Shi'i phenomenon, vanished. In 386/996 the Buyid Baha' al-Dawla granted Kufa as a fief to the chief of the Ukaylids, and nomad tribes ruined it further. With the rise of [[place/hilla|al-Hilla]] in 495/1101 it lost its importance and most of its inhabitants. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 386}

## Later visitors
Ibn Jubayr, who passed through between 1182 and 1185, called it deserted and ruined, with a few inhabitants still living there and subject to raids; the area between the mosque and the Euphrates had become orchards. He described the mosque as still standing, with high ceilings, columns leaded on the inside and a prayer hall of five bays, and listed sacred sites in it: the place of prayer of Ibrahim, the mihrab of Ali, the oven of Noah and the supposed tomb of Muslim ibn Aqil. In the Mongol period Mustawfi Qazwini gave the ramparts a circumference of 18,000 paces and stressed the city's agricultural role. [[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]], in the fourteenth century, found it largely ruined but not dead, with only foundations left of the qasr and still beautiful markets. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 386}

Under the Ottomans Kufa was a subdistrict dependent on Najaf. Massignon, who visited in 1908 and 1934, wrote of a deserted site and listed the few marked remains: the mosque, the tombs of Hani ibn Urwa and Muslim ibn Aqil, the Bayt Ali, two guard posts and two small oratories. Since 1938 it has been an archaeological site, with a first season of excavation. The [[building/great-mosque-of-kufa|great mosque]] has been fully and sometimes awkwardly restored, and the much larger qasr has been mapped and is in ruins. Glass, ceramics and Umayyad coins have been found. The article calls the archaeological exploration only at an early stage. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 386}

## Politics and ideology
The article divides the city's history into three: a political Kufa to about 150/767, a cultural Kufa from 150 to 250/767 to 864 and an ideological Kufa from 250 to 350/864 to 961, by then a focal point of doctrinal Shi'ism. The episodes it lists for the first century include taking part in the revolt against [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]], supporting Ali at [[event/battle-of-the-camel|the Camel]] and [[event/battle-of-siffin|Siffin]], the rise of the Kharijite movement, and the beginnings of political Shi'ism with the suppressed action of [[person/hujr-ibn-adi|Hujr ibn Adi]]. It continues with the episode of [[person/muslim-ibn-aqil|Muslim ibn Aqil]] and [[event/battle-of-karbala|Karbala]], the march of the Penitents, the [[event/uprising-of-mukhtar|uprising of al-Mukhtar]], the revolt of [[person/zayd-ibn-ali|Zayd ibn Ali]] and that of Abd Allah ibn Muawiya. It says Kufa directed the Abbasid propaganda and that the first Abbasid caliph was invested in its great mosque in 132/749. It also lists Kharijite attacks and the [[event/revolt-of-ibn-al-ashath|revolt of Ibn al-Ash'ath]], which it describes as a revolt of the garrison cities without ideological content. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 387}

The article explains this turbulence by the city's structure. Kufa sheltered the first conquerors of Iraq and drew most of the Sawad's revenues. Under Umar balance held, but under Uthman conflicts appeared between the earlier elite and the traditional tribal chiefs, among them [[person/malik-al-ashtar|al-Ashtar]] and al-Ash'ath. Ali's coming made Kufa for four years a centre of major decisions and the seat of the caliphate, and the article derives from this the city's persistent attachment to Ali and his family. It stresses that the attachment was not unanimous: the traditional chiefs were lukewarm, and a minority of the Qur'an readers became hostile and joined the Kharijites after the arbitration. The article also says that those chiefs sided with the Umayyad governors against Shi'i risings, including in the killing of [[person/husayn-ibn-ali|al-Husayn]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 387}

Two attitudes towards the city are recorded. The article says its many risings gave it a reputation as turbulent and ambitious, and that for later Shi'i consciousness it became a martyr city. It also quotes a saying attributed to Salman, reported through Massignon, that Kufa is the tent of Islam and that a time will come when every believer will be there or long to be. The OCR for the part describing the Sunni side is unreadable and it is omitted here. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 387}

## Scholarship
Kufa is best remembered as the home of a school of grammar and as a cradle of Shi'ism, the article says. It describes the Kufan grammatical school as headed by [[person/al-kisai|al-Kisa'i]], who died in 179/795, as more rooted in Arab speech, with a taste for anomalies and for poetry, and as often set up as a rival to the school of Basra. On examination it reads the school as a particular development of the contribution of Basra, led by [[person/al-khalil-ibn-ahmad-al-farahidi|al-Khalil]], while noting that the role of [[person/al-ruasi|al-Ru'asi]] needs clarification. Al-Kisa'i, like [[person/sibawayh|Sibawayh]] at Basra, started a line of grammarians that includes [[person/al-farra|al-Farra']] and Tha'lab, who around 250/864 brought together earlier knowledge of both schools. Baghdad, which had gathered the greatest names of Kufa and Basra for two generations, then began producing an eclectic tradition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 389} Part of the article's text on page 388, which appears to deal with the Shi'i character of Kufa and its culture, was not captured by the OCR and is omitted. The article closes by calling Kufa, along with Basra, a specifically Arab city that set the base and true style of Islam's cultural scheme. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 389}
