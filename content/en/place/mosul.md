## Overview
Mosul, in Arabic al-Mawsil, lies on the west bank of the [[place/tigris|Tigris]] in northern [[place/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], opposite the ancient [[place/nineveh|Nineveh]]. In early Islamic times it was the capital of [[place/diyar-rabi-a|Diyar Rabi'a]], the eastern part of the province of [[place/jazira|al-Jazira]]. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose account this page follows, describes it as the third largest city of the Republic of [[place/iraq|Iraq]] at the time of writing. The article has two parts: a history to about 1900 by E. Honigmann, with additions by C. E. Bosworth, and a shorter account of the twentieth century by another hand. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 926} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 928}

## Name and site
The article derives the name from the Arabic verb wasala, "to join", because several arms of the river unite there into a single stream. The town stands close to the Tigris on a spur of the western steppe plateau that juts into the river plain. Quarries beside its walls supplied the plaster used in buildings and mortar, and the walled area was almost three square kilometres, sloping gently south from the old fortress. A bridge of boats crossed the river a little above the south-eastern point where the wall meets the Tigris. According to the excavator E. Herzfeld, all the old buildings, even the court of the Great Mosque, lie below the level of today's streets, which have risen on a thousand years of continuous occupation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 926}

Whether a town stood here in antiquity is unknown. Herzfeld proposed that a town named by Xenophon preserved the name, but the article notes the simple objection that the town in question lay on the east bank. Muslim writers placed the foundation in legendary antiquity and credited it to Rewand, a figure of Persian legend. Another tradition gave an earlier name, Khawlan. The Persian governor bore a title that made the official name Budh-Ardashir, and the Syriac author Bar Bahlul gives yet another Persian name. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 926}

## Christian and Sasanian background
As the metropolis of the diocese of Athur, the town took the place of Nineveh, where Christianity had arrived by the early second century. About 570 Rabban Ishoyahbh, called Bar Kusra, founded a monastery on the west bank opposite Nineveh, still known as Mar Isha'ya, and the Sasanian king Khusraw II had many buildings raised around it. The article thinks this settlement is probably the fortress of the Syriac chronicle called Hesna Ebhraya, which the Arabs later developed into a town. Nineveh was a separate Nestorian bishopric from 554 until the early third/ninth century, when it merged with the see of al-Mawsil, and Monophysite bishops are recorded for roughly the same period. The district just north was then called Beth Nuhadra and the one to the south-west Adiabene. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 926}

## Conquest and the early Islamic centuries
After [[person/utba-ibn-farqad|Utba ibn Farqad]] took Nineveh in 20/641, in the reign of [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar ibn al-Khattab]], the Arabs crossed the Tigris, and the garrison of the fortress on the west bank surrendered, promising the poll tax and gaining leave to go where they pleased. Utba was dismissed as commander and succeeded by [[person/harthama-ibn-arfaja|Harthama ibn Arfaja al-Bariqi]], who settled Arabs in houses of their own, allotted them lands and made al-Mawsil a garrison city, building a Friday Mosque there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 926} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 927}

Under the Umayyads the sources differ on who governed it. One says [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik]] appointed his son Sa'id, another that his brother Muhammad held it with Adharbayjan, and that Muhammad's police chief paved the town and built a wall. Muhammad's son [[person/marwan-ibn-muhammad|Marwan II]] is also described as a builder: he is said to have organised the administration and built roads, walls and the bridge of boats, and a Friday Mosque is ascribed to him. Under him al-Mawsil became capital of the province of al-Jazira. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 927}

After the death of the Abbasid caliph al-Mutawakkil the Kharijite [[person/musawir-al-shari|Musawir]] seized part of the territory, and the Kharijites later took the town itself. The next decades brought rapid changes of governor, among them Taghlibi leaders, a Turkish general's deputy driven out by the citizens, and risings by local groups. The article lists the appointments year by year. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 927}

## Hamdanids and Uqaylids
In 282/895 [[person/hamdan-ibn-hamdun|Hamdan ibn Hamdun]], grandfather of Sayf al-Dawla, rebelled in al-Mawsil. A caliphal army stormed and destroyed the citadel and Hamdan was imprisoned. The Hamdanid Husayn, sent against the Kharijite Harun, took him in 283/896, and the family regained the caliph's favour. Husayn's brother Abu l-Haydja' came to al-Mawsil in Muharram 293/October 906 and the next year subdued the raiding Kurds. From then on the [[polity/hamdanid-dynasty|Hamdanids]] ruled there, first as the caliph's governors and from 317/929, with Nasir al-Dawla Hasan, as sovereign rulers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 927}

The [[polity/uqaylid-dynasty|Uqaylids]] followed, from 386 to 498/996 to 1096. They belonged to the Banu Ka'b, and their kingdom was founded by Husam al-Dawla al-Muqallad, whose independence the Buyids recognised. It stretched as far as Takrit and al-Mada'in. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 927}

## Zengids and their successors
The town developed considerably under the atabeg [[person/imad-al-din-zangi|Imad al-Din Zangi]], who ended [[polity/seljuk-empire|Seljuk]] rule in 521/1127-8. The city was largely in ruins; he gave it splendid buildings, restored its fortifications and surrounded it with flourishing gardens. Under his successor Izz al-Din Mas'ud I the [[polity/ayyubid-dynasty|Ayyubid]] [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Salah al-Din]] besieged it twice without success, in 1182 and 1185, and after peace was made Izz al-Din had to recognise him. The article describes the city of that time as defended by a strong citadel and a double wall, with towers washed by the Tigris on the east and a large southern suburb laid out by the vizier [[person/mujahid-al-din-qaymaz|Mujahid al-Din Qa'imaz]], who died in 595/1199. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 927}

From 607/1210-11 [[person/badr-al-din-lulu|Badr al-Din Lu'lu']] ruled, first as vizier of the last Zengids and from 631/1234 as an independent ruler. In 642/1244-5 he submitted to [[person/hulagu|Hulagu]] and went on his campaigns, so the city escaped the usual sack. When Lu'lu's son al-Malik al-Salih Isma'il joined [[person/baybars|Baybars]] against the Mongols, the town was plundered in 660/1261-2 and the ruler fell in battle. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 927}

## The medieval city
Arab geographers compared the plan to a headcloth, that is an elongated rectangle. A visitor in 358/968-9 found a beautiful town in fertile surroundings, its people mostly Kurds. [[person/al-muqaddasi|Al-Muqaddasi]], about 375/985-6, thought it beautifully built and gave its plan as a semicircle. The citadel then called al-Murabba'a stood where the Zubayda canal joined the Tigris, a Wednesday market lay within the walls, and the Friday Mosque built by Marwan stood on a rise near the river, reached by steps. Most market streets were roofed. The caliph's castle on the east bank, half a mile from the town, commanded Nineveh but was already ruined. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 927}

[[person/ibn-jubayr-muhammad|Ibn Jubayr]] visited on 22 to 26 Safar 580/4 to 8 June 1184 and noted that Nur al-Din had shortly before built [[building/great-mosque-of-al-nuri|a new Friday Mosque]] on the market place. At the highest point stood the citadel, called al-Hadba', "the hunchback". A later writer describes it as ringed by a deep ditch and high walls. The city walls had strong towers and ran down to the river; a highway joined the upper and lower towns; and suburbs with many small mosques, inns and baths stretched in front of the walls. Its hospital and great covered market were famous. Most houses were of tufa or marble from a hill east of the town and had domed roofs, and a third Friday Mosque was later added, overlooking the Tigris. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 927} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 928}

## Holy places and textiles
The ancient Nineveh was known in al-Muqaddasi's day as Tall al-Tawba, the place where the prophet Yunus was said to have stayed. A mosque stood there, around which Nasir al-Dawla built hostels for pilgrims, and half a mile away was the healing spring of Ayn Yunus with its own mosque. The tomb of Nabi Jirjis, who by Muslim legend was martyred in al-Mawsil, lay in the east town, as did that of Nabi Shith, Seth; see [[building/nabi-jirjis-mosque-mosul|the Nabi Jirjis mosque]]. The textiles of the city were especially famed, and the English word muslin comes from its name, although the article notes from Marco Polo that the medieval gold and silver cloths differed from today's thin cottons. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 928}

## Mongols to the Ottomans
In 766/1364-5 Sultan Shaykh Uways of the Jalayirids, successors of the [[polity/ilkhanate|Ilkhans]] in [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]], took al-Mawsil into his kingdom. [[person/timur-lang|Timur]] spared the city, gave rich endowments to the tombs of Nabi Yunus and Nabi Jirjis, made a pilgrimage to them and restored the bridge of boats leading there. The [[group/aq-qoyunlu|Ak Koyunlu]] followed, and then the [[polity/safavid-iran|Safavids]], who took the city after Baghdad in 914/late 1508 but lost it in 941/1535 to [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Sulayman the Magnificent]], who appointed Sayyid Ahmad of Jazirat Ibn Umar as governor. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 928}

Lists of Ottoman pashas of the sanjak exist from 1000/1592; it was long attached to the province of Diyarbakr, and tenure was usually brief, with 48 pashas between 1048/1638 and 1111/1699-1700. [[person/nadir-shah-afshar|Nadir Shah]] besieged it in 1156/1743, but the governor Husayn Djalili refortified it and defended it heroically. Afterwards the pashalik passed fairly continuously to the local Jalili family, originally Christian; Husayn ibn Isma'il held it on eight occasions. Their hold ended in 1834 when Sultan [[person/mahmud-ii-uthmani|Mahmud II]] extended his centralising power and removed Yahya ibn Nu'man al-Jalili. Travellers of the period often remarked on unclean streets and on sectarian strife among Muslims and between rival Christian churches. In 1879 the sanjak became the centre of a province of its own, and the article records a long tradition of French mission and educational work in the city. In the later nineteenth century visitors described mud-brick walls with seven gates, largely ruinous, stone houses with cellars, and about 40,000 people, among them 7,000 Christians. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 928}

## The twentieth century
The second author sees prosperity and importance waning by 1900, largely because the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 cut the overland trade with Aleppo and Damascus, while the port of Basra and steam navigation on the Tigris made Mosul's economy subordinate to Baghdad's. The Tanzimat reforms had little effect, and the central state's control over most of what is now Iraqi Kurdistan was nominal; between 1895 and 1911 one man, Mustafa Sabunji, was virtual dictator of the town. For 1911-12 the province is estimated at about 828,000 people. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 928} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 929}

The town was little touched by the First World War and was occupied by British troops some days after the Armistice of Mudros of 30 October 1918. The Sykes-Picot Agreement of 1916 had assigned the area to France, but in December 1918 Clemenceau agreed to attach it to Iraq, on condition that France share equally in exploiting Mesopotamian oil. Turkey went on contesting the claim, and the province was awarded to Iraq in 1925 after an enquiry by the League of Nations. Under the mandate and the monarchy its standing continued to decline, since Baghdad as capital took over its role and the province was divided into four. Prominent families, including the Shammar shaykhs, gradually acquired much of the surrounding land. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 929}

Such landowners felt threatened by the revolutionary government of [[person/abdul-karim-qasim|Abd al-Karim Qasim]], in power from 14 July 1958, and its agrarian reform. In March 1959 some of them joined local Arab nationalists and former supporters of Qasim in the armed forces in a failed attempt to overthrow him, with outside help promised but not delivered. Four days of fighting killed about 200 people, and the episode was later used as a rallying cry for revenge. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 929}

## The modern city
Mosul was joined to the Iraqi railway in 1939 and served by Iraqi Airways after 1946, and its colleges were merged into a university. In 1969 its province was split into Nineveh and Duhok. The 1977 census made it the third largest city in Iraq. Its industries are mainly agricultural, with food processing and leatherworking, plus textiles and cement, and an oil refinery opened in 1976. The author says the city keeps much of its traditional ethnic and religious mix, and that its medieval core remains distinct from the modern town planning. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 929}

## For Sprouts
Mosul is an old city on the Tigris river in northern Iraq that has seen many rulers over the ages. It holds the al-Nuri Mosque with its famous leaning minaret called al-Hadba. In the middle ages it was a city of trade and learning between Baghdad and Syria.

## For Explorers
Mosul is a city on the west bank of the Tigris in northern Iraq, opposite the ruins of the Assyrian city Nineveh. It gained its place in Islamic history as a centre of the Jazira, and flourished in the twelfth century under the Zengids when Imad al-Din Zengi made it his capital; the al-Nuri Mosque built by Nur al-Din Zengi stands there, with its leaning Hadba minaret that became the city's symbol until it was destroyed in 2017. It was a station on the caravan routes between Baghdad, Syria and Anatolia, and was known for weaving, scholars and crafts.

## For Scholars
Mosul is an Iraqi city on the west bank of the Tigris opposite the ruins of Nineveh, historically tied to the Jazira. The Muslims entered it in the early conquests, and in the middle ages it became a Zengid seat: Imad al-Din Zengi made it his capital in the twelfth century, and building, libraries and craft flourished there. Its most famous monument is the al-Nuri Mosque and its Hadba minaret, which leaned over centuries and was rebuilt after its destruction in 2017. Mosul remained a dry port linking Baghdad to Syria and Anatolia on the trade routes, and a passage between countries. In Ottoman and modern times it became a provincial capital; in the twenty-first century it suffered wide destruction under ISIS (2014–2017) followed by gradual rebuilding. Historians read it as a Jaziran city layering Assyrian, Abbasid, Zengid, Ottoman and modern strata.
