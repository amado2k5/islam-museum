## Overview
Khuzistan is a province, and a great district of the mountain districts. The Muslims conquered it in the caliphate of Umar; it was the seat of the kingdom of Hormuzan, the last of the marzbans of Fars. The horsemen received the terms of their conversion to Islam there, according to al-Baladhuri. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407} See also [[place/al-ahwaz|al-Ahwaz]].

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut vocalizes al-Ahwaz ending in zay and makes it the plural of hawz, whose root is h-w-z: the Persians, using the word constantly, altered it since their speech has no unpointed ha, and the Arabs then took it back corrupted by frequency — on which account al-Ahwaz is an Arabic name bestowed in Islam, its name in Persian days being Khuzistan; in Khuzistan are spots each called Khuz such-and-such, so al-Ahwaz names the whole kura, while the town the common people mean by it today is Suq al-Ahwaz. The root hawz for him is the masdar of haza "to possess"; from Abu Mansur al-Azhari, the hawz of lands is taking a tract, marking its bounds and claiming it. He later read in al-Tuzi that it is called Hurmishir in Persian and was al-Akhwaz with kha, arabized by the people; from Abu Zayd that its name is Hurmuzshahr, the great kura to which all the kuras are ascribed; and in the old books that Sapur built two cities in Khuzistan, naming one for God and one for himself, then joined them under one name Hurmuzdad-Sapur, "God's gift to Sapur", which the Arabs called Suq al-Ahwaz — the market of this kura or of the Khuz, all the people of the land being called al-Khuz; it is also said Ardashir first built it as Hurmuz-Ardashir. From the author of the Book of al-Ayn: al-Ahwaz is seven kuras between Basra and Fars, each with its own name, gathered under al-Ahwaz and never singly called Hawz. Ptolemy gives longitude eighty-four degrees, latitude thirty-five degrees four minutes; the author of the Zij puts it in the third clime, longitude seventy-five, latitude thirty-two. Its kuras: Suq al-Ahwaz, Ramhurmuz, Idhaj, Askar Mukram, Tustar, Jundisabur, Sus, Sarraq, Nahr Tiri and Manadhir; its kharaj thirty million dirhams, the Persians having levied fifty million. From Mus'ar ibn al-Muhalhil, the waterways of Suq al-Ahwaz: the Greater Wadi, water of Tustar, carrying a great bridge with a wide mosque, wondrous mills and water-wheels, its flood-time water red, pouring to the Basiyan and the sea; the Masraqan wadi, its water white; its sugar the finest of al-Ahwaz; on the Greater Wadi a well-made shadharwan holding back the water over several streams, opposite it the mosque of Ali ibn Musa al-Rida built on his passage from Medina toward Khurasan; another river from beyond a wadi called Shurab; and Sasanian remains. On its people he transmits the censure under its reporters' names: from al-Hamdani that they are the vilest and stingiest of people yet the most enduring of exile, there being no land without a sort of Khuz in it; from al-Mughira ibn Sulayman that the land of al-Ahwaz is brass bearing gold and Basra's gold bearing brass; and reports on its fever, its scorpions and snakes, its salt marshes, and the fifty thousand ovens of rice-bread fired daily on the teller's reckoning, the people themselves saying their mountain is petrified flotsam of the Flood, a stone that grows. On its conquest: some say by Hurqus ibn Zuhayr commissioned by Utba ibn Ghazwan while founding Basra; from al-Baladhuri that al-Mughira ibn Shu'ba raided Suq al-Ahwaz at the end of 15 or start of 16, its dihqan al-Bayruwan making terms then breaking them, so Abu Musa al-Ash'ari, made governor of Basra by Umar, took Suq al-Ahwaz and Nahr Tiri by force in 17 with many captives, whom Umar ordered released so kharaj could be laid on them. Assigned to it is the hafiz Abdallah ibn Ahmad ibn Musa al-Jawaliqi al-Ahwazi the qadi known as Abdan, of whom Abu Ali al-Naysaburi said he never saw a better memory among the shaykhs; he died in 306. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 1 p.284-287}

## The museum's identification
The record's al-Ahwaz is Yaqut's kura of Khuzistan, not the town alone: the entry itself distinguishes al-Ahwaz as the name of the whole kura from Suq al-Ahwaz, the town the name sticks to among common people, and the record follows the provincial sense. All the censure of its people is carried under its transmitters' names — al-Hamdani, al-Mughira ibn Sulayman, and the inhabitants themselves on the growing mountain — no verdict of the museum's; the ten kuras and the two kharaj figures, thirty and fifty million, stand as printed.
