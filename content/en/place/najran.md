## Summary
A large region seven stages from Mecca toward Yemen. The sources of the biography describe it as having seventy-three villages, and as the home of a Christian community whose delegation came to Medina in 9 AH {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.450}.

## About the place
Al-Mubarakpuri describes Najran as a large country of seventy-three villages, a day's journey for a swift rider. It could muster a hundred thousand fighters, of the Christian faith {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.450}. Its delegation came in 9 AH: sixty men, twenty-four of them nobles, led by al-Aqib, al-Sayyid and the bishop {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.450}. The Prophet made peace with them on terms of the jizya, under the protection of God and His Messenger, with full freedom in their religion. Islam began to spread among them after that {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.451}.

## For young visitors
An ancient city in the south of the Arabian Peninsula, whose people were Christian. A delegation from them came to the Prophet and made peace with him.

## In the introduction to The Sealed Nectar
The people of Najran became Christian at the hands of the ascetic [[person/faymiyun|Faymiyun]], around the time of the first Abyssinian occupation of Yemen {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.40} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.41} {cite:ibn-hisham-sira 1/31-34}. Then [[person/dhu-nuwas|Dhu Nuwas]] attacked them and dug the trench for them, and burned them in October 523 CE. They are the People of the Ditch mentioned in Surat al-Buruj {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.24} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.40}. See [[group/christians-of-najran|the Christians of Najran]] and [[event/massacre-of-najran|the Ditch]].

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut records Najran with fatha then sukun and a final nun; in their speech the najran is the wooden bar on which the door's bolt turns, from Ibn al-A'rabi and Ibn Durayd. Najran names several places for him, among them Najran of the Yemeni mikhlafs toward Mecca; they said it was named for Najran ibn Zaydan ibn Saba ibn Yashjub ibn Ya'rub ibn Qahtan, the first to settle and dwell there, as written in Ibn al-Kalbi's book. On how its people entered the Christian religion he transmits from Ibn Ishaq, from al-Mughira ibn Labid, from Wahb ibn Munabbih, the story of Faymiyun, a pious wanderer who ate only of his own hands' work, and the withering of the tree they used to venerate at his prayer, whereupon Najran's people followed him on the shari'a of the religion of Isa ibn Maryam — as the report runs; he also transmits from Muhammad ibn Ka'b al-Qurazi and from Najran's own people the tale of the sorcerer who taught their boys and al-Thamir's son Abdallah. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.266-267}

## The museum's identification
The record's Najran is the Yemeni mikhlaf with which Yaqut opens the name's several places. The Faymiyun and sorcerer stories are sira reports he cites with their chains — Ibn Ishaq from Wahb ibn Munabbih, al-Qurazi, and Najran's own people — carried as transmitted accounts, not the museum's assertion; they match the record's material from the sira and al-Rahiq.
