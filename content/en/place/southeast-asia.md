## In The Oxford Dictionary of Islam
According to The Oxford Dictionary of Islam, some 220 million Muslims inhabit the archipelago between southern Thailand and the southern Philippines, taking in Malaysia, Singapore and Indonesia, and small isolated Muslim communities also live in Cambodia, Burma (Myanmar) and the north and south of Thailand. Most of the archipelago's Muslims speak Malay or a variant of it. In its account, in the twelfth century Islam spread into the region along Arab-controlled trade routes that tied the Indian subcontinent, Central Asia and the Mediterranean to one another, and local rulers drew both a justification and a model for their rule from Islamic philosophy and from accounts of the principal Muslim kingdoms found in West and Central Asia. Islam was passed on in Malay, the language of every class, so that it came to be tied to the state and to the Malay language, and a broad and intricate literature, inspired by Islam, developed. In the colonial period, the Dictionary says, religion was separated from the state and Islam turned into a means of protest and of agitation against colonial rule; reform movements from West Asia reached the region in the late nineteenth century and sparked an Islamic revival. Since independence, it adds, Islam has been institutionalized in government ministries and religious-affairs offices in Indonesia, Borneo, Singapore and Malaysia, and Islamic parties take part in the political process everywhere except Thailand and the Philippines. {cite:oxford-dictionary-of-islam e2247}
