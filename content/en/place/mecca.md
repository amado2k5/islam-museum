## Overview
Mecca, in Arabic Makka, is described by the Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, as the most sacred city of Islam, the place where [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] was born and lived for about fifty years and where the [[building/kaaba|Kaaba]] stands. The article follows it from the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods through Abbasid, sharifian, Ottoman and Saudi times, and this page summarises it in the same order. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 154}

## Setting and climate
Mecca lies in the [[place/hijaz|Hijaz]], about 72 km inland from the Red Sea port of [[place/jeddah|Jeddah]]. It is now the capital of the province of Makka in Saudi Arabia, with a normal population of 200,000 to 300,000 that can grow by one and a half to two million at the time of the Hajj. The city sits in a corridor between two ranges of bare, steep hills. The whole corridor is the wadi, and its lower part, al-Batha', was the original settlement and holds the Kaaba. Houses once stood close to the Kaaba, yet a free space always remained round it, and over the centuries this space grew into the present mosque. Side valleys, each called a shi'b and often held by a single clan, ran into the Batha', and the higher outer area of settlement was called the zawahir. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 154}

The article stresses the city's position for trade, with routes north to Syria, north-east to Iraq, south to the [[place/yemen|Yemen]] and west to the Red Sea. Rain is scant and irregular, and when it comes a torrent can flood the Haram. Water came from wells, of which Zamzam beside the Kaaba was the most famous, and one leading Meccan was always charged with the sikaya, the duty of supplying the pilgrims. There was no agriculture nearby. The geographer [[person/al-muqaddasi|al-Muqaddasi]] described the climate in harsh terms, and the wealthier families sent their children to be raised for a time in the desert. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 154}

## Pre-Islamic Mecca
The article says Mecca had been a sacred site from very ancient times and was apparently known to Ptolemy as Macoraba. The Qur'an uses the names Makka and Bakka and speaks of the Kaaba being built by [[person/ibrahim|Abraham]] and [[person/ismail|Ishmael]]; the article adds that Western scholars generally do not accept this, because it cannot be connected with what is otherwise known of Abraham. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 154}

The article reports that the city was long controlled by [[group/jurhum|Jurhum]], then passed to [[group/khuzaa|Khuza'a]], and was taken over by [[group/quraysh|Quraysh]], presumably in the fifth century, through the work of [[person/qusayy-ibn-kilab|Qusayy]]. He gathered the scattered Quraysh and won allies from Kinana and Quda'a, and probably founded the first permanent settlement as distinct from camps. Later Quraysh were divided into those of the Batha' and those of the outer area; the article finds no ground for equating this with patricians and plebeians. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 155}

In the sixth century the article sees rivalry inside the Batha' group. The clan of Abd al-Dar, heir to some of Qusayy's privileges, was challenged by the clan of [[group/banu-abd-shams|Abd Shams]], descendants of another son, Abd Manaf, who had the support of several clans and was known as the Mutayyabun. The party of Abd al-Dar, including [[group/banu-makhzum|Makhzum]], was called the Ahlaf. A compromise was reached without fighting. About 605 the [[event/hilf-al-fudul|Hilf al-Fudul]] is mentioned, a league that seems to continue the Mutayyabun, though only the clans of [[group/banu-hashim|Hashim]] and al-Muttalib among Abd Manaf's descendants joined it. On the evidence of the reported occasion, a debt owed to a Yemeni merchant, the article reads it as an association of commercially weaker clans seeking to curb monopolistic practice by stronger ones, and not as a general league against injustice, which was the view of Caetani. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 155}

## Trade, government and belief
The article judges that Mecca's trade grew enormously in the second half of the sixth century, and it conjectures that wars between the Byzantines and Persians made the western Arabian route more attractive. Leading merchants controlled a large share of the traffic between Syria and the Mediterranean on one side and South Arabia and the Indian Ocean on the other, using caravans that went, according to tradition, to the Yemen in winter and to Syria in summer. They had to make agreements with political authorities and nomadic chiefs along the way. The article suggests that the expedition of [[person/abraha|Abraha]] against the sanctuary may have been prompted by this prosperity, and that the war of the [[event/harb-al-fijar|Fijar]] seems to have removed [[place/taif|al-Ta'if]] as a rival and drawn it into a Meccan system. By about 600 the leading men were skilled in credit and investment. Some women traded on their own account, and the article names [[person/khadija|Khadija]], Asma' bint Mukharriba, mother of [[person/abu-jahl|Abu Jahl]], and Hind, wife of [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-harb|Abu Sufyan]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 155}

Henri Lammens called Mecca a merchant republic; the article says the label fits only up to a point, since the underlying political ideas were those of Arabia. The one organ of government besides clan assemblies was the mala', a meeting of clan chiefs without executive power, and punishment could be taken only by the offender's own clan chief, otherwise the law of retaliation would apply. The Quraysh were known for hilm, a steadiness that in practice put commercial interest first, and most leaders favoured neutrality between the Byzantine and Sasanian empires. Certain functions were tied to families: the sikaya, the rifada or provisioning of pilgrims, the liwa' or carrying of the war standard, and the nasi', the decision on inserting an intercalary month. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 155} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 156}

In religion the article says most Meccans were pagans with many gods, yet the Qur'an describes some who also acknowledged Allah as a supreme god and creator. A few Meccans, it adds, were drawn to monotheism, the hanifs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 156}

## Muhammad and the city
The article places Muhammad's birth in Mecca, in the clan of Hashim, about 570. A posthumous child whose grandfather died when he was about eight, he was by Arabian custom excluded from inheritance. He travelled to Syria with his uncle [[person/abu-talib|Abu Talib]], then worked as steward for Khadija and married her about 595. The article reads the Qur'anic message as answering a moral and spiritual malaise that came with Mecca's sudden wealth, and says it attacked the great merchants for reliance on riches and neglect of the poor. By 614 some younger merchants felt threatened and opposition grew; some followers went to [[place/abyssinia|Abyssinia]]. Muhammad could preach while his clan protected him, but about 619 Abu Talib died and was succeeded by [[person/abu-lahab|Abu Lahab]], who denied that protection. In 622 Muhammad accepted an invitation to [[place/medina|Medina]], the [[event/hijra|Hijra]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 156}

Most of the following decade was dominated by the struggle with the Meccan merchants. The article outlines [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]] in March 624, where Abu Jahl and other leaders were lost, and the leadership of Abu Sufyan for the next three years, with [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]] in 625 and the failure at the [[event/battle-of-the-trench|trench]] in 627. In 628 the Meccans stopped Muhammad and 1,600 Muslims from making the pilgrimage, yet made the [[event/treaty-of-hudaybiyya|treaty of al-Hudaybiyya]] with him as with an equal. A breach of the treaty by Meccan allies led to a Muslim expedition of some 10,000 men, and the town surrendered almost without a blow; apart from a handful guilty of specific offences, the Meccans were assured of safety. The article says Muhammad had been aiming at reconciliation, and that some 2,000 Meccans fought with him at [[event/battle-of-hunayn|Hunayn]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 156} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 157}

After the [[event/conquest-of-mecca|conquest]] a young Muslim of a Meccan family was left as governor and Medina stayed the capital. The Kaaba, already the qibla, was cleared of idols and became a centre of Islamic worship, with the Black Stone kept as an object of reverence, and the annual [[practice/hajj|Hajj]] was retained. The article thinks Meccan commerce dwindled, perhaps because many leading men moved to Medina and found their talents used in running an empire. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 157}

## From the first caliphs to the Abbasids
The article notes that little is heard of Mecca under the first four caliphs, though [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] and [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]] brought Christian engineers to build barrages against flooding, and [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Muawiya]], native of the town, built, developed farming nearby and improved the water supply. For a time after Muawiya's death it was the seat of the rival caliph [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr]], until [[person/al-hajjaj-ibn-yusuf|al-Hajjaj]] defeated and killed him in 73/692. In 63/682, during the presence of an Umayyad army, the Kaaba was partly burned, probably by carelessness on the Zubayrid side; Ibn al-Zubayr rebuilt it including the Hijr, and al-Hajjaj reversed that change. [[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|Al-Walid I]] is credited with the galleries round the courtyard that give the Sacred Mosque its distinctive form. Under the [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyads]] Mecca drew wealthy people who avoided politics and became a place of ease with poets and musicians, and with fewer scholars than Medina. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 157}

Under the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasids]] the Holy Cities were normally governed by Abbasid princes. Alid groups troubled the region, and the Hasanid Husayn ibn Ali was cut down with many followers at [[event/battle-of-fakhkh|Fakhkh]] near Mecca under al-Mahdi. [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]] spent vast sums on nine pilgrimages, which the article judges to have harmed the Meccans' character. Anarchy followed al-Ma'mun, and the Qarmatians, from 304/916, barred the pilgrim caravans. In 318/930 about 1,500 Qarmatian fighters raided Mecca, killed inhabitants in thousands and carried the Black Stone to Bahrayn; they brought it back in 339/950. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 157} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 158}

## The sharifate
Around the middle of the tenth century the Alids of Mecca came to be called sharifs. The article reports that sources disagree on the year in which Ja'far took Mecca, and names him as the first of the Hasanid line. Mecca refused homage to the [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]] caliph in 365/976 and gave in when imports from Egypt were cut off, for the Hijaz depended on Egypt for food. The Musawi line ended with Shukr in 453/1061, and the ruler of the Yemen, al-Sulayhi of the [[polity/sulayhid-dynasty|Sulayhids]], intervened and in the end installed Abu Hashim Muhammad, founder of the Hawashim line, which lasted to 598/1200. The article describes these rulers as offering allegiance to the highest bidder, Fatimid or Seljuk, and adds that Ibn Jubayr, who visited in 578/1183 and 580/1185, gave grim examples of plunder. Under the [[polity/ayyubid-dynasty|Ayyubids]] Saladin's brother ended the Zaydi form of the call to prayer in 582/1186, and the Ayyubids took the place of honour at the Hajj. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 158} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 159}

Qatada, a descendant of the same Musa, built up estates from Yanbu' to Mecca and took the town around 1200, and all later sharifs descend from him. The article says he aimed at an independent principality and pursued what Snouck Hurgronje called a policy of "splendid isolation" for the Hijaz. In the Mamluk age the sharif Abu Numayy ruled firmly from 652/1254 to 700/1301, and later sharifs, such as Hasan, ruled under Egyptian control that included a share of customs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 159} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 160}

## Ottoman, Wahhabi and Egyptian rule
After the Ottoman conquest of Egypt, Istanbul replaced Baghdad as the distant centre for Mecca, with the sharifs' territory extended north to Khaybar and east into Najd, while still depending on Egyptian grain. The article records that the post of chief judge, a Shafi'i office, was sold each year to the highest bidder and filled from Istanbul, and that the sharifs let Persians and Zaydis keep taking part in the pilgrimage despite an Ottoman order to bar them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 160}

When Sharif Ghalib faced the Wahhabi advance, he made a treaty in 1799 with the amir of Dar'iyya. In 1803 the army of Su'ud ibn Abd al-Aziz entered Mecca; the article lists the destruction of domes and burning of pipes and instruments and the removal of praise of the Prophet from the call to prayer. Muhammad Ali took Mecca in 1813 and exiled Ghalib to Salonika, and the article records that Mecca remembered him honourably for restoring pious foundations, renewing grain shipments and giving stipends to scholars. A treaty in 1840 placed the Hijaz directly under the Porte. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 and the telegraph tied Mecca more closely to Istanbul. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 160} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 161}

The last sharif, [[person/husayn-ibn-ali-sharif-makkah|Husayn]], appointed in 1908, declared himself king of the Hijaz on 22 June 1916 and later claimed the caliphate. In September 1924 the troops of Abd al-Aziz Al Su'ud took al-Ta'if and in October Mecca, and Husayn fled; Medina and Jeddah surrendered in December 1925. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 161}

The article draws on Richard Burton, who visited in 1853, and Snouck Hurgronje, who stayed in 1884-5. It says the pilgrimage dominated life, with citizens in guilds such as the zamzamiyyun who distributed Zamzam water, the camel brokers and above all the mutawwifun, the guides, who had links with particular regions; in Snouck Hurgronje's time 180 guides served Indonesian pilgrims alone. The slave trade was still important. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 161} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 162}

## The Saudi takeover and the early kingdom
The later part of the article says that Ali was declared king of the Hijaz on 4 October 1924 after his father abdicated. Mecca had no walls and Ali's army withdrew; the city was looted by local Bedouin, then entered on 16 October by four Ikhwan in pilgrim dress who read a proclamation guaranteeing safety. The next day Ikhwan forces performed the umra. The article reports sporadic destruction, among it the reputed birthplace of the Prophet and two houses venerated as those of Khadija and Abu Bakr, yet judges that good order was on the whole kept. Abd al-Aziz entered on 5 December 1924 in pilgrim dress, setting his camp outside the city, and set up a municipality and a consultative council, headed by Abd al-Qadir al-Shaybi, keeper of the Kaaba key. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 162} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 163}

In the Hajj of 1925 the English visitor Eldon Rutter estimated about 70,000 pilgrims. On 8 January 1926 Meccan notables acknowledged Abd al-Aziz as king of the Hijaz in the Haram, and that year's Hajj drew 191,000. The same Hajj saw the mahmal affair, in which the Egyptian escort and some Ikhwan clashed between Mina and Arafat; the article reports some 25 pilgrims killed and 100 wounded, until the king intervened. The article quotes an observer saying that after it the mahmal never again paraded through Mecca. An organic statute of 1926 named Mecca the capital and provided for a viceroy. In 1927 Abd al-Aziz was proclaimed king of Najd and its dependencies as well, and the unified Kingdom of Saudi Arabia followed in 1932. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 164} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 165} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 166}

The article lists the early administration: a re-established municipality with wide duties, three levels of courts, a committee for enjoining good, a police force and a postal service. Slavery was not outlawed in Saudi Arabia until 1962, and Mecca had been Arabia's largest slave market. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 166} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 167} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 170} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 171}

## People and daily life
Mecca's population is highly mixed, since pilgrims from Java, the Indian subcontinent and Africa settled there; one estimate gives foreign nationals about a fifth of the population in the early 1960s. The article gives customs of the early Saudi period from Rutter. A newborn is named a week after birth with the call to prayer spoken in its ear, and on the fortieth day every child is placed for a moment on the threshold of the Kaaba. The dead are carried to the Haram, prayed over, and buried in the Ma'la cemetery north of it. Twice a year, in Rajab and Dhu'l-Qa'da, the inside of the Kaaba is washed, and the Haram courtyard served as a gathering place. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 169} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 170}

The article lists fifteen pre-oil quarters, among them Ajyad, the old Ottoman government quarter below an Ottoman fort, and Jarwal. Markets once surrounded the Haram. After oil wealth the built-up area grew from about 3 km by 2.5 km to about 8 km by 5.5 km, with ring roads and tunnels to Mina, Muzdalifa and Arafat. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 171} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 172}

## Pilgrimage and economy
The article states that the economy rests on two things, commerce for the local market and the pilgrimage, since agriculture is non-existent. Food came from outside, fruit from al-Ta'if and vegetables from Wadi Fatima. It quotes Rutter that Meccans had no means of earning a living except by serving the pilgrims. Pilgrim fees were charged from 1927 and the head tax was abolished in 1952, once oil income made it unnecessary. The number of non-Saudi pilgrims rose from 216,455 in 1961 to 479,339 in 1971 and 1,003,911 in 1982, and the shift to air travel shortened stays from months to weeks. The first bank opened in 1954 and the first hotel project in 1936-7. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 172} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 173} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 174}

## The Sacred Mosque and the Kaaba
From the moment Abd al-Aziz entered Mecca he and his successors spent effort on the [[building/masjid-al-haram|Sacred Mosque]]. The article lists repairs in 1346/1927, electrification that had begun under Husayn and was improved, the covering of the running place between [[place/al-safa|al-Safa]] and al-Marwa by Husayn in 1920-1, and its paving in 1926-7. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 174} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 175} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 176}

The article tells how the covering of the Kaaba, the [[object/kiswa|kiswa]], came from Egypt until a dispute in 1922; in 1345/1927 a kiswa was made in Mecca on short notice, with the king's name as donor for the first time, and a factory was later opened there. In 1957 King Su'ud ordered repairs of the Kaaba's roofs, walls and marble lining. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 176}

A vast expansion began after a decree of September 1955. The old mosque was kept and surrounded by new construction that took in the running place; the new building covers 160,168 square metres against 131,041 for the old, can hold an estimated 300,000 worshippers, and has seven minarets of 90 m. The work was completed in 1398/1978, and the building above the well of Zamzam was removed in 1963-4, so that the well is reached by a staircase below ground. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 177}

## What this page covers
This page sets out the account in the Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, followed by what the museum links to the place: events, people and buildings. It leaves out the article's section on the 1979 seizure of the Haram, and its sections on education, health and other modern services.

## For Sprouts
Mecca is a blessed and holy city, home to the sacred Kaaba and the Grand Mosque, toward which Muslims pray.

## For Explorers
Mecca is the holiest city in Islam, the birthplace of the Prophet Muhammad, and the cradle of revelation. It embraces the Grand Mosque, the sacred Kaaba built by Abraham and Ishmael, the well of Zamzam, and the hills of Safa and Marwah, welcoming millions of pilgrims annually for the Hajj in brotherhood.

## For Scholars
Umm al-Qura (the Mother of Cities) and the holiest sanctuary in Islam, situated in a desert valley within the Hejaz mountains of western Arabia. Mecca encloses the Sacred Mosque and the Kaaba, the inaugural house of worship consecrated for humanity and the universal Qibla for Islamic prayer. Associated with Abraham, Hagar, and Ishmael, it flourished as a pre-Islamic commercial hub under Quraysh along the seasonal trade routes. The birthplace of the Prophet Muhammad and the cradle of Qur'anic revelation, the sanctuary is governed by inviolable sacred boundaries, hosting millions of pilgrims annually during the Hajj.
