## Overview
Al-Andalus: a region at the far west. al-Baladhuri reports on the authority of al-Waqidi that [[person/tariq-ibn-ziyad|Tariq ibn Ziyad]], the agent of [[person/musa-ibn-nusayr|Musa ibn Nusayr]], first invaded it in the year 92 AH. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

## The invasion
Ilyan, its governor at the passage, met him and gave him safe-conduct to carry his army over in ships. Then its people fought him, and he took control of it. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

al-Baladhuri says that its king, "so they claim", was of the Ashban, whose origin was Isfahan. This is a reported saying, and it is not settled here. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

## For Sprouts
Al-Andalus was a beautiful land in the Iberian Peninsula where Muslims lived for centuries, building cities filled with gardens, mosques, and libraries.

## For Explorers
Al-Andalus was the name given by Muslims to the Iberian Peninsula after they entered it in 92 AH. Its cities, including Cordoba, Toledo, Seville, and Granada, became renowned for universities, libraries, and magnificent architecture, serving as a civilizational bridge that transmitted Arabic sciences, medicine, and philosophy to Europe.

## For Scholars
The name applied to Muslim-ruled regions of the Iberian Peninsula between 92 AH and 897 AH (711–1492 CE). Its political history encompassed the dependent governorate, the Umayyad Emirate and Caliphate of Cordoba, the Taifa kingdoms, the Almoravids and Almohads, and the Nasrid Kingdom of Granada. Al-Andalus served as a foremost hub of cross-cultural learning, famed for jurisprudence, philosophy, medicine, and enduring monuments like the Great Mosque of Cordoba and the Alhambra.

## The name
Al-Andalus, or Diyarat al-Andalus, was the term used in the Islamic world until the end of the Middle Ages for the Iberian peninsula, that is modern Spain and Portugal. The name may be connected with the Vandals (al-Andalish), who gave Baetica the name Vandalicia before they crossed to Africa. It appears on a bilingual dinar of 98/716, where the Latin equivalent is Spania. Arab writers always confined the term to Muslim Spain, whatever its extent, which the Christian Reconquista steadily reduced; in the end it denoted only the small Nasrid principality of Granada. Muslim chroniclers also used Ishbaniya (Hispania) and the Arabic forms of the Christian kingdoms: Liyun (Leon), Kashtala (Castile), Burtukal (Portugal), Aragon and Navarre. The ethnic form andalusi and the collective ahl al-Andalus derive from the name. Today the term is kept for the sub-Mediterranean region of Andalusia, from Almeria to Huelva. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 509}

## Physical situation and relief
The peninsula forms a massive, almost pentagonal promontory at the south-west of Europe, joined to the continent by the Pyrenees and washed by the Atlantic and the Mediterranean. It lies between about 43 degrees 24 minutes and 35 degrees 59 minutes north, and 9 degrees 30 minutes west and 3 degrees 19 minutes east, with an area of about 229,000 square miles, of which modern Portugal is less than a fifth. Cut off from the rest of Europe by the Pyrenees, it is separated from Africa only by the narrow Straits of Gibraltar, which gives it an insular character that has long isolated it from influences beyond the Pyrenees while leaving it open to eastern influences by the Mediterranean. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 509-510}

Most of the peninsula is a large central plateau, the Meseta, with a mean height of about 1,965 feet, which comprises Old and New Castile and is bounded by high escarpments: the Cantabrian range to the north, the Iberian mountains to the north-east and east, the Sierra Morena to the south, and the tablelands of Galicia and Portugal to the west. Three deep depressions cut it: the Ebro, the Guadalquivir and the lower Tagus. In the south the upheaval of the Penibaetic system forms the mountain mass of Upper Andalusia, with the Sierra Nevada, whose Mulhacen is the highest point. The mean height of the whole peninsula is not less than 2,160 feet, and only about 40 per cent of it lies below 1,645 feet, which made the soil hard to exploit. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 510}

## Climate
The peninsula has a dry, generally temperate climate, but the high interior has extreme temperatures, with severe winters and torrid summers; the sub-littoral zones, especially the depression of the Guadalquivir, are the exception. The rainfall divides the country into wet Spain, the western Pyrenees, the Basque country, the Cantabrian coast and nearly all of modern Portugal, and dry Spain, which covers nearly two thirds of the peninsula, where the annual rain varies from about 23 inches to under 15 inches and is largely lost to evaporation unless the land is irrigated, as in the Levant around Valencia and Murcia. The north-west and the Atlantic seaboard have mild, humid weather, and the Mediterranean zone from Catalonia to the Andalusian coast has mild winters, high sunshine and clear air. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 510}

## Rivers
The rivers of the peninsula share the character of North African wadis: most run dry for much of the year and are sudden torrents when they flood, with heavy erosion. The rivers flowing west and north are short coastal streams, chief among them the Mino, which forms the northern frontier of Portugal. The Duero, the Tagus and the Guadiana, all drawing the waters of the Meseta, reach the Atlantic, and the Guadiana forms the southern frontier between Spain and Portugal. The most important river is the Guadalquivir, fed by the Genil from the Sierra Nevada, and the only one navigable in its lower course, for the last 75 miles. The torrential wadis of the Levant, the Segura and the Jucar, provide uncertain supplies for irrigation. The Ebro rises in the Basque country, is fed by the southern Pyrenean slopes, and reaches the Mediterranean through a large alluvial delta. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 510}
