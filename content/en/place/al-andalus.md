## Overview
Al-Andalus: a region at the far west. al-Baladhuri reports on the authority of al-Waqidi that [[person/tariq-ibn-ziyad|Tariq ibn Ziyad]], the agent of [[person/musa-ibn-nusayr|Musa ibn Nusayr]], first invaded it in the year 92 AH. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

## The invasion
Ilyan, its governor at the passage, met him and gave him safe-conduct to carry his army over in ships. Then its people fought him, and he took control of it. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

al-Baladhuri says that its king, "so they claim", was of the Ashban, whose origin was Isfahan. This is a reported saying, and it is not settled here. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

## For Sprouts
Al-Andalus was a beautiful land in the Iberian Peninsula where Muslims lived for centuries, building cities filled with gardens, mosques, and libraries.

## For Explorers
Al-Andalus was the name given by Muslims to the Iberian Peninsula after they entered it in 92 AH. Its cities, including Cordoba, Toledo, Seville, and Granada, became renowned for universities, libraries, and magnificent architecture, serving as a civilizational bridge that transmitted Arabic sciences, medicine, and philosophy to Europe.

## For Scholars
The name applied to Muslim-ruled regions of the Iberian Peninsula between 92 AH and 897 AH (711–1492 CE). Its political history encompassed the dependent governorate, the Umayyad Emirate and Caliphate of Cordoba, the Taifa kingdoms, the Almoravids and Almohads, and the Nasrid Kingdom of Granada. Al-Andalus served as a foremost hub of cross-cultural learning, famed for jurisprudence, philosophy, medicine, and enduring monuments like the Great Mosque of Cordoba and the Alhambra.

## The name
Al-Andalus, or Diyarat al-Andalus, was the term used in the Islamic world until the end of the Middle Ages for the Iberian peninsula, that is modern Spain and Portugal. The name may be connected with the Vandals (al-Andalish), who gave Baetica the name Vandalicia before they crossed to Africa. It appears on a bilingual dinar of 98/716, where the Latin equivalent is Spania. Arab writers always confined the term to Muslim Spain, whatever its extent, which the Christian Reconquista steadily reduced; in the end it denoted only the small Nasrid principality of Granada. Muslim chroniclers also used Ishbaniya (Hispania) and the Arabic forms of the Christian kingdoms: Liyun (Leon), Kashtala (Castile), Burtukal (Portugal), Aragon and Navarre. The ethnic form andalusi and the collective ahl al-Andalus derive from the name. Today the term is kept for the sub-Mediterranean region of Andalusia, from Almeria to Huelva. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 509}

## Physical situation and relief
The peninsula forms a massive, almost pentagonal promontory at the south-west of Europe, joined to the continent by the Pyrenees and washed by the Atlantic and the Mediterranean. It lies between about 43 degrees 24 minutes and 35 degrees 59 minutes north, and 9 degrees 30 minutes west and 3 degrees 19 minutes east, with an area of about 229,000 square miles, of which modern Portugal is less than a fifth. Cut off from the rest of Europe by the Pyrenees, it is separated from Africa only by the narrow Straits of Gibraltar, which gives it an insular character that has long isolated it from influences beyond the Pyrenees while leaving it open to eastern influences by the Mediterranean. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 509-510}

Most of the peninsula is a large central plateau, the Meseta, with a mean height of about 1,965 feet, which comprises Old and New Castile and is bounded by high escarpments: the Cantabrian range to the north, the Iberian mountains to the north-east and east, the Sierra Morena to the south, and the tablelands of Galicia and Portugal to the west. Three deep depressions cut it: the Ebro, the Guadalquivir and the lower Tagus. In the south the upheaval of the Penibaetic system forms the mountain mass of Upper Andalusia, with the Sierra Nevada, whose Mulhacen is the highest point. The mean height of the whole peninsula is not less than 2,160 feet, and only about 40 per cent of it lies below 1,645 feet, which made the soil hard to exploit. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 510}

## Climate
The peninsula has a dry, generally temperate climate, but the high interior has extreme temperatures, with severe winters and torrid summers; the sub-littoral zones, especially the depression of the Guadalquivir, are the exception. The rainfall divides the country into wet Spain, the western Pyrenees, the Basque country, the Cantabrian coast and nearly all of modern Portugal, and dry Spain, which covers nearly two thirds of the peninsula, where the annual rain varies from about 23 inches to under 15 inches and is largely lost to evaporation unless the land is irrigated, as in the Levant around Valencia and Murcia. The north-west and the Atlantic seaboard have mild, humid weather, and the Mediterranean zone from Catalonia to the Andalusian coast has mild winters, high sunshine and clear air. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 510}

## Rivers
The rivers of the peninsula share the character of North African wadis: most run dry for much of the year and are sudden torrents when they flood, with heavy erosion. The rivers flowing west and north are short coastal streams, chief among them the Mino, which forms the northern frontier of Portugal. The Duero, the Tagus and the Guadiana, all drawing the waters of the Meseta, reach the Atlantic, and the Guadiana forms the southern frontier between Spain and Portugal. The most important river is the Guadalquivir, fed by the Genil from the Sierra Nevada, and the only one navigable in its lower course, for the last 75 miles. The torrential wadis of the Levant, the Segura and the Jucar, provide uncertain supplies for irrigation. The Ebro rises in the Basque country, is fed by the southern Pyrenean slopes, and reaches the Mediterranean through a large alluvial delta. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 510}

## Vegetation and landscape
Dry Spain has three kinds of vegetation, the Mediterranean kind: forests of evergreen oaks and holm oaks or cork-trees, the foothills (monte bajo) and the steppe of scrub and esparto grass. Wet Spain, with its forests and natural meadows, stays green all year. The variety makes Spain a country of great contrasts, where one may pass almost at once from a river valley (vega), lush with vegetation, to a steppe burnt by sun and wind. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 510-511}

## The Arab geographers
Most of what is known of al-Andalus in the Middle Ages comes from the Arab geographers, eastern and western. The Road Books (masalik), in de Goeje's Bibliotheca Geographorum Arabicorum, give little space to Spain; those of Ibn Khurradadhbih, al-Ya'qubi, Ibn al-Faqih and Ibn Rusta are so brief that al-Andalus seems to have been little known in the East before the tenth century. After the Marwanid caliphate was restored at Cordova the geographical record became more systematic. Al-Istakhri (d. 322/934) describes fourteen itineraries in the peninsula, with attention to agriculture and commerce. Ibn Hawqal visited Spain himself and brought his information up to date from informants on the road. His pro-Fatimid account is often partial, but it is the first rational, full and coherent description of the Cordovan kingdom. The Palestinian al-Muqaddasi, who did not visit Spain, gives important information on learning, language, weights and measures, and trade. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 511}

The later descriptions, written mostly in the west, depend on the description that the Cordovan historian Ahmad al-Razi (d. 344/955), of eastern origin, placed at the head of his lost history of al-Andalus. Yaqut used it without acknowledgement. Only a Castilian version survives, published by Gayangos in 1852 from a Portuguese version made for King Denis of Portugal (1279-1325). The lost description of Abu 'Ubayd al-Bakri (d. 487/1094) can be largely reconstructed from the notices on al-Andalus in the Rawd al-Mitar of Ibn 'Abd al-Mun'im al-Himyari, who also used al-Idrisi. The al-Maqqari (17th century) in the first volume of his Nafh al-Tib also gives long notices. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 511}

## Al-Andalus in Muslim geography
According to al-Razi, al-Andalus lies at the western end of the fourth clime, a land watered by many rivers and sweet springs. Geographers loved panegyric and the Laudes Hispaniae, in the manner of Isidore of Seville. Al-Andalus was imagined as a triangle, each corner marked by a place of Hispanic legend: at the south-west apex the temple of Cadiz (Sanam Qadis); a second corner on the latitude of the Balearics; a third, in the north-west, at the Tower of Hercules near Corunna. Al-Razi saw that western and eastern Spain differ in winds, rainfall and the direction of rivers: the rivers of the west flow to the Atlantic, watered by the westerly winds, and in the east the winds blow from the east and the rivers flow eastwards. Landmarks include Cape St Vincent, 'the church of the crow' (Kanisat al-Ghurab), and the Temple of Venus, Haykal al-Zahra, at Port-Vendres. Coming from Gaul, the traveller crossed the Pyrenees by its passes (abwab, burtat) into the lands of the Gascons (al-Bashkunish) and the Franks (al-Ifranj). The Atlantic was the Sea of Darkness (Bahr al-Zulumat), the green sea or the surrounding sea, where bold sailors traded along the coast with the land of the blacks and the Canaries (al-Khalidat), and the Mediterranean was the great sea, the middle sea, or the Tyrrhenian sea. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 511-512}

## Mountains and rivers in Muslim geography
Al-Razi counted only three ranges crossing the peninsula from one sea to the other, none crossed by a river. The first is the Sierra Morena, 'the mountains of Cordova' (Jibal Qurtuba), from the Levantine coast to the Algarve. The second is the Pyrenean range, which crosses Spain obliquely from Tortosa to Lisbon, corresponding to the transverse range al-Sharrat in al-Idrisi. Other ranges include the Serrania of Malaga (Jibal Rayyo) and the mountain called Shulayr. The chief river is the Guadalquivir, the 'great river' (al-Nahr al-A'zam), also called Nahr Baiti after the Baetis, which waters Cordova and Seville in Baetica, the richest part of the peninsula. Its tributaries are the Genil (Wadi Sindjil), the Guadajoz, the Guadalimar (al-Wadi l-Ahmar, from the reddish colour of its water) and the Guadalbullon. The Guadiana, 320 miles long, runs underground for part of its course and reaches the Atlantic at Ocsonoba; the Tagus, 580 miles long, reaches the Atlantic at Lisbon; the Duero, 780 miles long, reaches it at Oporto; and the Mino, 300 miles long, runs east to west through Galicia. Of the rivers flowing to the Mediterranean, al-Razi mentions the Segura. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 512}

## The Ebro and the other rivers
The Segura, the one Mediterranean river al-Razi mentions, rises near the sources of the Guadalquivir. The Ebro, Wadi Ibro, rises at Fontibre in Upper Castile and reaches the sea near Tortosa after some 204 miles; its many tributaries include the Gallego (Nahr Djillik), which comes down from the mountains of Cerdagne. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 512}

## Urban names and divisions
Al-Andalus was notable in every period for its many towns, in contrast with North Africa. Almost all the towns of Roman Spain survived the Arab conquest and went on prospering. The new towns founded by the conquerors were numerous, and were nearly always strategic sites or coastal bases meant to check Fatimid ambitions in the western Mediterranean: Murcia, which replaced the old town of Ello, and Almeria, which began as a coastal observation post and became an arsenal and naval station in the tenth century. Most Latin names survived almost unchanged, such as Corduba (Qurtuba), Hispalis (Ishbiliya), Caesaraugusta (Saraqusta) and Valentia (Balansiya), while others were shortened or took new forms: Toletum became Tulaytula, and Ocilis became Madinat Salim (Medinaceli), a name that gave rise to a legendary founder called Salim. Some names came from Arab or Berber tribes settled after the conquest, and in the Valencia region many names were formed with Beni and an eponymous ancestor. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 512-513}

## The marches and the provinces
When al-Razi wrote, Muslim Spain was already separated from the Christian north by a no man's land, bordered by three marches (thughur): the upper, the middle and the lower. Whole regions evacuated under the first pressure of the Reconquista, such as the Hispanic march in the east and the Basque country, had been lost. The expedition of al-Mansur against Santiago de Compostela was only a spectacular raid. The provincial organisation of al-Andalus dated from the eighth century, before the Marwanid restoration. It was based on districts (kura), each with a chief town, governor and garrison; the lists vary, al-Muqaddasi giving 18 names, Yaqut 41, and al-Razi 37. Al-Idrisi's 'climes' (iqlim) have no administrative sense. Reconstructed from al-Razi and al-Bakri, the largest kura was Cordova, bounded to the north by the Fahs al-Ballut (the Llano de los Pedroches), with Ghafiq as its chief place. South of the Guadalquivir lay the kuras of Cabra and Ecija, and to the west the rich districts of Carmona, Seville and Niebla; Ocsonoba, with Silves, was Gharb al-Andalus, the Algarve, and north of it Beja. The south had Meron, Sidona with Calsena, Algeciras, and Takurunna with Ronda; Malaga (Rayyo) had Archidona; Elvira (Ilbira) lay west of Granada; Jaen and Pechina (whose seat moved to Almeria under al-Hakam II). The Levante had three kuras: Tudmir, the old kingdom of Theodemir, with Murcia, Jativa and Valencia, extending to the Ebro delta. Inland, Toledo was a kura, with Santaver and Uclés to its east; the Balearics were probably a separate district; and Merida, Badajoz, Santarem, Lisbon and Murcia completed the list. Nine kuras, the mudun, held privileged fiefs granted in 125/742 to the Syrian djunds brought by Balj ibn Bishr, among them Elvira, Rayyo, Sidona, Niebla, Seville, Beja and Ocsonoba. In the Upper March al-Razi names Tarragona, Lerida, Barbastro, Huesca, Tudela, Tarazona, Arnedo, Calahorra and Najera. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 513}

## Population
The absence of reliable statistics means that the population of al-Andalus at its greatest extent, at the end of the tenth century, cannot be computed. If the Visigothic population on the eve of the conquest was about ten million, it probably stayed roughly the same, with a higher share of town and village dwellers. The distribution, however, was dictated by the physical environment, altitude, soil, climate and the possibility of irrigation, so the regions that are sparsely settled today were probably sparse then too. Among the Muslims the great body were neo-Muslims, Spaniards who converted after the conquest, mostly spontaneously. Among the immigrants of other races the Berbers were the most important, coming from the nearest parts of the Maghrib, the Moroccan Jibal and the Rif; they were pushed back to the uplands by the Arab immigrants who formed the aristocracy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 513}

## The groups of the population
The Berbers were the most important of the immigrant groups. They came from the nearest parts of the Maghrib, the Moroccan Jibal and the Rif, in small numbers, and were pushed back into the uplands by the Arab immigrants who held the best land. Ibn Hazm, in his Jamharat ansab al-'Arab, suggests that the Berber colonies held only certain coastal zones and had to settle in the Meseta. They became Arabised quickly. Only at the end of the tenth century did large numbers of Berber mercenaries from the central and eastern Maghrib come in, and they helped to bring down the structure of the caliphate. The Arabs were never more than a minority. They came with the Conquest and after it, reinforced by Syrian djunds and by emigrants at the time of the Marwanid restoration. Intermarriage and the system of wala' made many people claim Arab descent, but the Arabs were a turbulent element, who despised farm work and left the land to share-cropping tenants. Negroes (Sudan) brought by slave traders formed a steadily growing guard of mercenaries and mixed with the urban population, and Slavs (Saqaliba), captured in Europe or in raids on the frontier, became a numerous and influential group in the economy of Cordova in the second caliphate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 513-514}

The largest group was the Spanish neo-Muslims, the muwalladun, Spaniards who had adopted Islam during or after the Conquest for a better status than that of the dhimmi. Their rapid Arabicisation, which they embraced with real attachment, is remarkable. They soon filled the ranks of Andalusian society, and the rulers used them well to make up for the shortage of old Muslim emigrants. Many lost the memory of their Iberian or Gothic origin, though they often bore Romance names, and bilingualism, with Arabic and the Romance tongue (al-'ajamiyya) on the same footing in daily life, eased the fusion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 514}

The Muslim population came to form social classes like the rest of the Islamic world: the khassa, the great noble families, often hereditary grantees, and the 'amma, the commoners. The middle class of merchants and small landowners became an urban bourgeoisie without charters. The plebs in the towns and above all in the countryside were a mass under severe pressure from authority; there was a rural proletariat of day labourers tied to the soil. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 514}

The tributaries (mu'ahidun) were Christians and Jews. The Christians, the Mozarabs, were the Spaniards who refused to give up their faith at the Conquest. In Cordova, Seville and Toledo their communities were organised under the Muslim authority, with a leader called comes (kumis), or defensor, who exercised police powers and collected taxes, assisted by a judge for the Mozarabs (qadi al-'ajam). Up to the end of the tenth century the peninsula kept the ecclesiastical divisions of the Visigoths: three metropolitan provinces (Toledo, Lusitania and Baetica), each with an archbishopric and several dioceses; al-Bakri preserves them in what he calls 'Constantine's partition'. The Mozarab community of Cordova is the best documented. The Jews had a quarter in every town (harat al-Yahud). In the eleventh century, especially in the Zirid kingdom of Granada, Jews served as excise officials and treasurers; the Banu l-Naghrila family was important, and the pogrom in Granada followed the murder of the crown prince Buluggin. The Jewish community of Lucena formed most of its population. The Jews may have played an active part as counsellors and envoys and controlled the main trade between al-Andalus, Europe and the Muslim East, a question on which the Cairo Geniza may throw light. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 514-515}

## The development of al-Andalus
The geographers are the main source for the way the soil was cultivated and its vegetable and mineral resources exploited. The article then turns to agriculture, crafts and trade in the Umayyad and later periods. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 515}
