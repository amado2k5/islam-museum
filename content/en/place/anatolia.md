## The name
Anatolia, called Anadolu in Turkish and Asia Minor in antiquity, is the mountainous peninsula at the western end of Asia, between about 36 and 42 degrees north and 26 and 45 degrees east. Together with the Balkans it has formed a bridge between Central Europe and Western Asia throughout its history. Arab geographers of the Middle Ages, and the Turks until well into Ottoman times, called the country Bilad al-Rum, 'the land of the Rum'. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 483}

The Byzantines used the name Anatole, 'the rising of the sun', in the administrative sense of the lands east of Constantinople; the prefecture of the East created by Diocletian and Constantine comprised the dioceses of Egypt, the East, Pontus, Asia and Thrace. That administrative term disappeared with the division into themes in the early seventh century. The theme around Amorium and Iconium, much smaller, was called al-Natulus or similar by Ibn Khurradadhbih, and al-Natulik by Kudama; the name of the theme Anatolikon vanished again with the Turkish conquest. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 483}

With the Turks the name became Anadolu. At first it meant only western Anatolia, the Ottoman province of that name embracing the former western Anatolian Turkish principalities. It ceased to be a province name in the reorganisation of the provinces during the Tanzimat in the mid-nineteenth century, and from then on 'Anatolia' meant the whole peninsula, roughly as far as the line Trebizond, Erzincan, Birecik and Alexandretta. In Turkish today Anadolu is the whole Asian part of modern Turkey, including areas that geographically belong to upper Mesopotamia and to Armenia. In 1950 Anatolia covered 743,634 square km of the 767,000 square km of Turkey, and the country had 20,934,670 inhabitants, 19,308,441 of them in Asia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 483}

## Physical geography
Anatolia is a high plateau ringed by longitudinal mountain ranges, higher still, to the north and south. The northern border mountains, known in Europe as the Pontic mountains, are straight parallel ranges 1,200 to 1,500 metres high, often over 2,000, with the Zigana mountains above 3,000 metres and alpine formations. The southern side is the Taurus system. In the west the northern chains link through the mountains south of the Sea of Marmara with the inner Dinaric ranges of the Balkans; in the east the Armenian and Caucasian ranges lead on to the Iranian mountains, with the high plateau of Mount Ararat as an outpost. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 483-484}

The climate follows the geography. Winters on the coast are mild, with January averages above 5 degrees on the Black Sea coast and above 8 degrees on the south coast. Summers on the coasts are oppressively hot, and the Mediterranean dryness of the west and south coasts gives way on the northern coast to summer rain. The central plateau is cold in winter, below freezing in January, and very hot in summer, with a July-August average near 24 degrees; it is a steppe, with less rain than the coasts, but barley and wheat grow there without irrigation. Despite some maps, central Anatolia has no desert. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 484}

Vegetation followed the rainfall. On the south and west coasts the evergreen cover of Mediterranean lands survives in part, and much of it has been turned to arable land, the rest degraded into shrubs and sparse pasture. The border mountains carry dry forest of oak and conifers on their western, southern and eastern sides, much of it cleared for fields and grazing; on the northern mountains nearer the coast the damp forest of beech and pine prevails and resists human pressure better. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 484}

The main settlement lies in the long valleys between the northern ridges, where summers are hot and water makes farming possible: the basins of Bolu, Gerede, Cerkes and Ilgaz-Tosya in the eastern part of ancient Bithynia; the Safranbolu, Kastamonu and Boyabat basin in the centre of ancient Paphlagonia; the basins of the upper Yesil Irmak (Iris) around Amasya, Zile and Tokat in ancient Pontus; and, to the east, the Kelkit-Coruh furrow, over 500 km long. Grain, sheep, goats and, in the east, cattle form the basis of the economy there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 484}

The north coast rises steeply out of the Black Sea, with few bays and a narrow, valley-cut strip, densely settled in the east, where maize, beans and above all hazelnuts are grown around Giresun, Trabzon and Rize. The only larger flats are the deltas of the Yesil Irmak and the Kizil Irmak (Halys), which are partly swamp, and the Kocaeli peninsula, the Thracian plain, and the Adapazari plains on the lower Sakarya (Sangarius). {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 484}

## The rest of the coasts and the Marmara region
Apart from the Bosporus, the only harbour on the Black Sea shore protected from the north-westerly gales is Sinop, which has little importance because its hinterland is poor. Samsun has the best rail and road access to central Anatolia. The coal and industrial areas of Zonguldak and Eregli (Heraclea Pontica) were being greatly developed, and the silver, lead and copper mines of the Zigana mountains, at Gumushhane and Murgul, were once of some importance. The subsidence of the land between the Aegean and the Balkans also affected the northern mountain ranges of the Marmara region, leaving hilly districts and plains around the Sea of Marmara, with a favourable Mediterranean climate. Silk is grown near Bursa, and wine around Tekirdag (Rodosto). {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 484}

Because of its position Constantinople, Istanbul, grew up on the bridge between Anatolia and the Balkans and kept its importance for thousands of years; the most important periods were those in which it was the natural capital of an empire over both areas. Even today it is Turkey's gate to the world and its principal import harbour. The Straits are not a boundary of continents or cultures; such a boundary is rather found in the sparsely settled steppes and heathlands of the interior. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 484}

## The Taurus system
The Taurus system of southern Anatolia is considerably higher than the northern border ranges: long stretches rise above 2,000 metres and at times above 3,000, and the ice-covered Djilo Dagh south-east of Lake Van reaches 4,176 metres. The limestone ranges are often strongly bow-shaped. West of the Gulf of Antalya the Western Taurus, sometimes called the Lycian Taurus, points out towards the sea and the islands; between the Gulf of Antalya and the Adana plain the Central Taurus forms a great arc, whose eastern wing is known as the Cilician Taurus. East of the Gulf of Alexandretta the system continues in two parallel chains. The outer chain runs from the Amanus through the ranges south of Malatya and of the Murad river to the chains south of Lake Van; the inner chain, whose western section is called the Anti-Taurus, runs from the upper Seyhan north of Adana to the Urmia area, past the upper Euphrates (Kara Su) and the upper Aras. Between them lie basins such as Elbistan, Malatya-Elazig, Capakcur and Van. Together they form the Eastern Taurus, which curves northward and merges at its southern end into the Iranian border ranges. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 484}

Between the ranges of the Western and central Taurus lie longitudinal basins, several with lakes, the famous lakes of the old districts of Pisidia and Isauria, and these basins are the main centres of settlement; Isparta and Burdur have valuable special crops. The limestone uplands are thinly peopled for want of water, and their poor grazing, used by goats and sheep in summer, has largely replaced the former dry forest; settlement in the Central Taurus is confined to a few narrow valleys. The Eastern Taurus has wider basins that could support more people, and its thinly settled southern foothills can be farmed on rainfall alone, near the ancient centres of Diyarbakir, Urfa (Edessa), Gaziantep and Aleppo. The most favoured part of these foothills is Hatay, around Antioch, where the nearby Mediterranean allows citrus and other Mediterranean crops. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 484-485}

The coastal strip of the Taurus is narrow and offers few hills for settlement, with malaria a danger; the only really large arable area is the Cilician plain of Adana and Tarsus, formed by the deposits of the Seyhan (Saros) and Ceyhan (Pyramus), where cotton has greatly increased in recent years. The tufaceous plain of Antalya, with cliffs of 30 metres down to the sea, is less favourable. The southern coast has no sheltered anchorage for large ships; Iskenderun (Alexandretta) and Mersin serve the Adana plain and Hatay and ship the chromium ore of the Eastern Taurus, and the small harbour of Fethiye serves the western mountains. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 485}

## Aegean Anatolia
Between the Marmara mountains in the north and the Western Taurus in the south lies Aegean Anatolia, the Turkish Ege region, which roughly corresponds to the area of Ionian Greek colonisation. The broad valleys of the Bakir Cay (Caicus), Gediz (Hermus) and the greater and lesser Menderes (Cayster, Maeander) penetrate some 200 km into the peninsula through crystalline rock between ridges 1,000 to 2,000 metres high. The region is densely settled, growing tobacco, olives, figs and grapes, largely dried as raisins, and more recently cotton. The coast runs at right angles to the mountains and has many harbours, but the large rivers carry so much sediment that the bays fill: Ephesus and Miletus, once ports, now lie several kilometres inland, and Izmir (Smyrna) survived only because the Gediz was diverted. Izmir is the economic centre of the region, linked by rail; Bergama (Pergamum), Afyon and Denizli are local centres. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 485}

To the east the valleys end at a high ridge between the Taurus and the southern border chains of the Marmara region, around Afyon, Kutahya and Usak. This western Anatolian ridge consists of plateaux at 1,200 to 1,500 metres, with massive ranges above them often exceeding 2,000 metres, falling gradually to about 1,100 metres towards the north-east and the upper Sakarya. Its plateaux are steppes of Tertiary clay and sand, and most of its woods have been cut down. Grain and sheep and goats are the livelihood of a scanty population. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 485}

## The central plateau
Central Anatolia comprises large flat stretches at 800 to 1,200 metres, formed by recent sedimentation in the landlocked basins of Konya (Iconium), including the Tuz Golu (salt lake), a huge flat salt pan at 900 metres, often wrongly marked as a salt desert on maps. Similar flats occur on the upper Sakarya and in places on the Kizil Irmak, and there are other broad plateaux of horizontal Neogene deposits. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 485}

## Mountains of the plateau and the eastern highlands
Isolated volcanic mountains rise 500 to 1,500 metres above the central plateaux. Among them are the recent volcanoes, not active now, Erciyes Dagi (3,916 m., the Argaeus of antiquity) above Kayseri and Hasan Dagi (3,258 m.) near Nigde. Settlement favours two positions: the highest plateaux, such as the bend of the Kizil Irmak, and the foot of the mountains, where fast streams come down. Most of the important towns, Ankara (Ancyra), Eskisehir, Konya (Iconium), Nigde, Kayseri (Caesarea) and Sivas (Sebastia), lie at the foot of the mountains, where land can easily be irrigated. The steppes support wheat, barley, sheep and Angora goats, and are least settled in the very dry basins of Tuz Golu and Konya, the Lycaonia of antiquity. Traffic moves more easily across the plateau than across the mountain borders, so the plateau, always the heart of Anatolia, gained importance when the capital moved to Ankara and the roads and railways were extended. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 485-486}

The eastern limit of Anatolia lies on the upper Euphrates, where the northern border ranges and the eastern Taurus join through new mountains. Peaks there generally exceed 2,500 metres, often 3,000; the scanty population lives in the valleys, along which the roads run to Azerbaijan and Iran, and the towns of Erzincan and Erzurum guard those roads. East of the meridian of Erzurum the two systems part again and enclose a highland at 1,500 to 1,700 metres, higher than the central plateau, with large recent volcanoes, Ararat (Agri Dagi, 5,172 m.), Alagoz (4,094 m.) and Suphan (4,434 m.), and dammed lakes such as Van. Its winters are cold and it is used mainly for grazing. It is generally called Armenia, although no Armenians had lived there for a generation, and its population speaks Turkish or Kurdish; the author suggests the neutral and geographically apt name Ararat Highlands for this eastern border region, which lies outside geographical Anatolia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 486}

## The frontier before the Turks
Most of Anatolia escaped the Arab conquests. The Byzantine frontier ran from the Christian states of Armenia and Georgia in the north-east, past the outposts of Kalikala (Theodosiopolis, later Arzan al-Rum, Erzurum) and at times Kamakh, to the Taurus, the 'land of the passes' (bilad al-durub), and so to the Mediterranean. The Arabs raided deep into Byzantine territory but never held the land. The border lands, the military zone of the protecting fortresses (al-awasim), centred on Manbij or Antioch, with the Syrian marches (thughur al-Sham) around Tarsus and the Mesopotamian marches (thughur al-Jazira) around Malatya (Melitene), suffered heavily in the wars, but mostly remained in Arab hands until Nicephorus II Phocas (963-969), John Tzimiskes (969-976) and Basil II (976-1025) recovered them. By the death of Basil II almost all of present-day Turkey was Byzantine, except Amida (Diyarbakir) and its surroundings. The rivalry between the military and civil aristocracies then weakened Byzantium. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 486-487}

## The Seljuk conquest, first phase
The Turkish conquerors of the Saljuq house found the frontier weakened, and sent their warriors against it to wage holy war (jihad). They broke into Byzantine Anatolia: Ani fell in 456/1064 in the Byzantine-Armenian border country, Cilicia was laid waste, and Caesarea (Kayseriyya) was stormed. Romanus IV Diogenes, of the military aristocracy, was raised to the throne on the battlefield in January 1068, after the death of Constantine X, a champion of the civilian nobility. He at first fought the Turks successfully, so that the Saljuq sultan Alp Arslan had to come against him in person. At Manzikert (Malazgirt), near Lake Van, on 463/19 August 1071, the numerically larger Byzantine army was routed, through the indiscipline of its mercenaries and the treachery of the emperor's opponents. Romanus was captured but released under a lenient treaty. The defeat brought a revolution in Constantinople: Romanus lost his throne, was blinded, and died in the summer of 1072. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 487-488}

With Romanus gone, the treaties with Alp Arslan became void and the Turks renewed the holy war against Byzantium. It was waged less by regular Saljuq troops than by individual leaders, the most successful of whom was Malik Danishmend Ahmad Ghazi, who operated in north-eastern Anatolia. Bands of Turkish warriors roamed the countryside, cut the communications between towns and paralysed the Byzantine administration. Sultan Malikshah, Alp Arslan's successor from 465/1072, sent a member of the Saljuq house, Sulayman ibn Qutlumush, to lead the Turkish cavalry in Anatolia. The succession dispute in Byzantium helped him. The emperor Michael VII Ducas, and after his abdication in 1078 Nicephorus III Botaniates, won his help; in return they acknowledged his rights to the lands the Turks held and handed over the newly taken cities of Cyzicus and Nicaea in 1081. Sulayman made Nicaea (Turkish Iznik) his headquarters. Alexius I Comnenus, who began to reign in 1081, confirmed Sulayman's right to settle Turkish troops in the occupied territory while nominally keeping Byzantine suzerainty. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 488}

## The Saljuq sultanate of Rum and the crusades
After Sulayman's death Anatolia was left to its own devices for some time. Other Turkish leaders settled with their followers and founded dominions: Malik Danishmand Ahmad Ghazi in the north-east, with Sebastia (Sivas) as his headquarters; Mengujek Ghazi with Tephrike (Divrigi) and Erzincan; and in the west, at Smyrna, an amir the Byzantines called Tzachas. The Saljuq amirs fought over Antioch, which was still Byzantine, and Tutush, brother of Malikshah, was beaten and killed in battle there in 1086. Turkish bands in Azerbaijan had conquered the Bagratid kingdom of Armenia in 473/1080; the Bagratid Ruben then founded a new state in Cilicia, the kingdom of Lesser Armenia, which lasted under the Rubenids until 1375. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 488}

After Malikshah's death in 1092 his successor Barkiyaruk let Sulayman's son Kilij Arslan return to Anatolia, where he found it hard to establish himself among the Turkish princes. At the start of the First Crusade the crusaders and the Byzantines together defeated the Turks under Kilij Arslan and Malik Danishmand, or his son Ghazi Gumushtegin, and took Nicaea on 20 June 1097. The crusaders' victory at Dorylaeum (near present-day Eskisehir) on 1 July 1097 decided the fate of western Anatolia and opened the road through the rest of the Turkish lands. They took Antioch after a long siege on 3 June 1098, founding there the principality of Antioch, the first crusader state under Byzantine suzerainty; the county of Edessa (Urfa) was founded the same year. The Emperor Alexius then drove the Turks out of western Anatolia and restored it to Byzantium, and reinforced the border across the middle of Anatolia, checking further Turkish conquest. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 488-489}

For over a century the Turkish conquests were confined to central Anatolia. The whole west, roughly from Dorylaeum, and the Black Sea and Mediterranean coasts stayed Byzantine; Cilicia became the Lesser Armenian kingdom; Antioch and Edessa were crusader states; Amid (Diyarbakir) was the seat of the Artukid atabegs. Edessa fell to the atabeg Zengi of Mosul in 1144, and Antioch to the Mamluk sultan Baybars in 1268. Kilij Arslan and Malik Danishmand's successors shared the Turkish centre: Kilij Arslan kept the steppe of central Anatolia with Konya (Iconium) as capital, and the other kept the mountainous north-east with Sivas and Erzincan. The dispute over Melitene (Malatya) was settled in Kilij Arslan's favour around 1104-1106, but his attempt to advance on Mosul in Mesopotamia failed, and he died in the retreat from the Khabur in 1107. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 489}

The Rum Saljuq state, the sultanate of Iconium as the crusaders called it, remained a limited territory in the poorest part of Anatolia. Its sultan Mas'ud I defeated the crusaders of the Second Crusade in a second battle near Dorylaeum on 26 October 1147 and forced them to take the Byzantine rather than the Turkish road. The state grew when Kilij Arslan II absorbed the Danishmandid state in 1174, and beat the Byzantine emperor Manuel I Comnenus in the Phrygian passes near Myriocephalon in 1176. Frederick Barbarossa crossed Turkish Anatolia and took Konya on 18 May 1190, but this had no lasting result, for he drowned in the Saleph (Calycadnus, today Goksu) on 10 June 1190. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 489}

## The Latin conquest and the rival states
The crusaders of the Fourth Crusade took Constantinople in 1204 and set up a Latin empire there. The Byzantine Theodore Lascaris founded a Greek counter-empire in western Anatolia with Nicaea as its capital, while the Comneni brothers David and Alexius, with the help of Queen Tamar of Georgia, formed the empire of the Great Comneni at Trebizond. The Rum Saljuq sultan Ghiyath al-Din Kaykhusraw I took Attalia (Antalya) in 1207, giving his kingdom access to the Mediterranean, but was beaten and killed by Laskaris near Honas in 1210. Laskaris and his successors protected the eastern border of Nicaea with fortifications that stopped the Turks for the time being. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 489}

In 1214 Kaykhusraw's son 'Izz al-Din Kaykaus I forced Trebizond to cede Sinope, giving the sultanate access to the Black Sea and bringing trade and great prosperity. His brother and successor 'Ala al-Din Kaykubad, the greatest of the Rum Saljuq sultans, extended the frontier on the Mediterranean: he took the fort of Alanya, which he enlarged into a harbour town and made his winter residence. In the east he took territory from the Artukids of Amid and Hisn Kayfa, annexed the Mengujek principality of Erzincan in 625/1228, and conquered Erzurum (1230), Akhlat (1231) and Kharput (1234). Under him the Rum Saljuq culture and power reached their peak. His son Ghiyath al-Din Kaykhusraw II, acceding in 634/1237, took Amid, and by then the eastern border of the sultanate was roughly that of present-day Turkey. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 489}

## The second phase: Mongols and Turkmens
Two developments in the mid-thirteenth century changed matters. The Mongol invasion reached Anatolia: the Rum Saljuq army was defeated by the Mongols under Baiju Noyan at Kose Dagh in eastern Anatolia on 26 June 1243, and the Mongols plundered Kayseriyya. The sultanate did not disappear but became a vassal of the Mongols, first of Batu, then of the Ilkhans of Persia. The Mongols brought a new stream of Turkmens into Anatolia, some as their followers, some driven from their homes, and these reinforced the partly nomadic Turkmen element already there. The most important were the hordes of Karaman ibn Nura Sufi, probably of a dervish family, who founded a state on the border of Lycaonia and Cilicia around Ermenek in the Taurus foothills. In 1277 Karaman's son Muhammad Beg tried to take the sultanate with a pretender, Jimri, and took Konya for him, but a Mongol expedition retook it and Muhammad had to withdraw to the mountains; Jimri was beaten on the Sakarya by Saljuq troops in June 1277 and executed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 489-490}

The other development was the Byzantine restoration under Michael VIII Palaeologus, which came too late. The Palaeologi were increasingly engaged in the Balkans against the Latins and could not give Anatolia the attention it needed. Their defences decayed, which let the Turkmen hordes pour into Anatolia on the pretext of holy war and take hold of the western lands, which were richer than the interior and had long tempted them. The Palaeologi were thus forced to give up their Anatolian territories step by step, and the Turks met little resistance in the open country. By about 1300 most of western Anatolia was in Turkish hands. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 490}
