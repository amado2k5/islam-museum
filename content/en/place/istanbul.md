## Overview
Istanbul was the capital of the Ottoman Empire from 20 Jumada I 857/29 May 1453 until 3 Rabi' II 1342/13 October 1923. In strict Ottoman usage the name meant only the area between the Golden Horn, the Marmara coast and the Theodosian land wall, while Galata, Uskudar and Eyup were separate townships, each with its own kadi, though the name was sometimes applied to the whole. The Encyclopaedia of Islam article this page follows deals only with the Ottoman city, and treats its growth through the sultans' endowed complexes, its quarters, its people and the disasters that struck it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 237}

## Names
Under the Seljuks of Anatolia and the early Ottomans the city was written Istinbol, Istanbol or Istanbul, and the article holds that these Turkish forms come from the Greek phrase meaning "to the city". Al-Mas'udi already reports, in the tenth century, that the Greeks called it Stanbulin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 237} The punning name Islambol, "where Islam abounds", is ascribed by an Armenian source to the conqueror [[person/mehmed-ii|Mehmed II]]. It appears in fifteenth-century documents, educated people of the seventeenth century took it for the Ottoman name, and a decree of 1174/1760 ordered it onto coins in place of Kostantiniyye, but the popular form stayed Istanbul, now the official spelling. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 237}

The Byzantine name Constantinopolis passed to Arabic and Persian as Kustantiniyya and remained the name of the chancery, of decrees, endowment deeds and coins. Muslim writers also used Rumiyya al-kubra and Takht-i Rum, echoing New Rome. Official epithets such as Dar al-Saltana, Dar al-Khilafa and Der-i Sa'adet reflect older Iranian and Islamic ideas of central authority, and the common blessing called the city al-mahmiyya, the well-protected. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 237}

## The conquest and its aftermath
Before the assault Mehmed II claimed the stones and land of the city for himself and left other goods and captives to his troops as booty. The troops entered through a breach at dawn on 20 Jumada I 857/29 May 1453, and fighting, with pockets of resistance in the towers, ended towards mid-afternoon. Wishing to keep the city intact as his capital, the sultan halted the fighting that afternoon, and the article concludes that there was no serious plundering from 30 May. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 237} On that day he made his ceremonial entry, declared [[building/hagia-sophia|Hagia Sophia]] the Great Mosque and Istanbul his seat. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 237} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 238} See also the museum's record of the [[event/conquest-constantinople-857|conquest]].

His first concern was repopulation. He proclaimed an amnesty for returning fugitives, invited the Greeks to choose a patriarch, and installed George Scholarius, [[person/gennadios-scolarios|Gennadios II]], on 6 January 1454. He appointed a military prefect, a garrison of 1,500 Janissaries and a kadi, ordered the walls repaired, a citadel built at the Golden Gate and a palace raised at the Forum Tauri, the later Old Palace. Prisoners were settled along the Golden Horn, and ransomed captives were allowed to stay. The Venetian bailo and his son were executed, and Venetians gained trading rights only under the capitulations of 858/1454. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 238} The most effective measure was the compulsory transfer, sürgün, of Muslim, Christian and Jewish families from Rumeli and Anatolia. A fragment of the census of Muharram 860/December 1455 lists 918 houses in 22 quarters, 291 of them empty or ruined, and shows most monasteries and churches deserted or in Muslim hands. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 238}

## An Islamic city in the making
The article sets out the guiding principle: the city was to take the character of a Muslim city, built around a place of worship. Hagia Sophia was the Great Mosque where the ruler met the community on accession and at Friday prayer, and the economic and social institutions grew as its endowments. The first district was that of Hagia Sophia, later districts formed around mosques built by sultans and viziers, and the smaller quarters, mahalles, each around a local mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 239} The endowments of Hagia Sophia also took in converted Byzantine buildings, such as the [[building/kalenderhane-mosque|Kalenderhane]] convent, made a college and then a mosque under Bayezid II. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 238} The holy district of Eyup arose just after the conquest with the tomb of the Companion [[person/abu-ayyub-al-ansari|Abu Ayyub al-Ansari]], see the museum's [[building/eyup-sultan-mosque|Eyup Sultan Mosque]], and many quarters were named for saints around their lodges or tombs. The article notes occasional pressure to close churches and synagogues on the ground that the city was taken by force, and the Shaykh al-Islam himself seeking grounds to protect the dhimmi status of non-Muslims. In daily life Muslims and non-Muslims worked side by side in the markets. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 239}

The pattern of a covered market, the bedestan, with caravanserais and craft markets around it, was reproduced here. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 239} The Great Bedestan, with fifteen domes, is called a supreme example of the type, and it stood where Byzantine commerce had been centred. A later silk bedestan, the [[building/sandal-bedesten|Sandal Bedesten]], grew into the largest of the empire. In 898/1493 ten of the Great Bedestan's merchants were Armenian, five Jewish, three Greek and the rest Muslim. Rows of shops spread around it until the [[building/grand-bazaar-istanbul|Covered Bazaar]] held about a thousand. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 240} Mehmed II also promoted khans, and a harbour district on the Golden Horn, once controlled by Latin colonists, filled with warehouses, customs posts and markets. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 241}

## The great complexes
From about 863/1459 the sultan told his notables to build mosques, khans, baths and markets in areas named after them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 241} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 242} The [[building/fatih-mosque-istanbul|Fatih complex]], begun in the winter of 867/1462-3 and finished in Rajab 875/January 1471, covered about 100,000 square metres, with eight large and eight small colleges, a hospital, a hospice and a school. Its income, drawn largely from outside the city, supported 383 persons on its payroll, and the hospice gave out 3,300 loaves daily. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 242} Within seventy years thirteen districts had formed, each with its own endowments, among them those of [[building/mahmud-pasa-mosque-istanbul|Mahmud Pasha]] and Murad Pasha. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 242} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 243} The foundations of Sultan Bayezid II and of viziers such as Davud Pasha and Mustafa Pasha settled the areas towards the land walls, and several Byzantine churches became mosques. The article names Ali Pasha, who also turned the Chora monastery church into a mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 243} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 244}

The sixteenth century saw rapid growth and the best-known works of Ottoman architecture, under the palace architects led by [[person/mimar-sinan|Sinan]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 244} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 245} The [[building/rustem-pasha-mosque|Rustem Pasha Mosque]] was raised over shops, and [[person/piri-mehmed-pasha|Piri Mehmed Pasha]] founded a large endowment. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 245} [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Suleiman]] built a complex for his wife, one for his son Mehmed, and then, in 957-964/1550-1557, the [[building/suleymaniye-mosque|Suleymaniye]], made of eighteen elements, with four colleges and a hadith school, the highest institutes of learning of the empire. Records show that 51 percent of its 3,523 paid craftsmen were Christians, and that Christians mostly built walls and Muslims did the carving. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 245} The complex founded in 973/1565 by [[person/mihrimah-sultan|Mihrimah Sultan]] near Edirne Gate, the [[building/mihrimah-edirnekapi-mosque|Mihrimah Edirnekapi Mosque]], drew settlement to the walls. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 244} The article also lists the [[building/sokollu-mehmed-pasha-mosque|Sokollu Mehmed Pasha]] foundation at Kadirga among the new works. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 246} The article calls 947-996/1540-1588 the period of Sinan, who with his men built 43 great mosques, 52 small mosques, 49 colleges and 40 baths in the city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 247}

The last great classical complexes were the mosque of [[person/ahmad-i-uthmani|Ahmed I]], the [[building/blue-mosque|Blue Mosque]], built 1018-1026/1609-1617 beside the Hippodrome, and the [[building/new-mosque-istanbul|New Mosque]], begun in 1006/1597 by Safiyya Sultan and finished by [[person/turhan-sultan|Turhan Sultan]] in 1071-1074/1660-1663. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 247} In the eighteenth century came the [[building/nuruosmaniye-mosque|Nuruosmaniye]] and the [[building/laleli-mosque-istanbul|Laleli]] complexes, in a hybrid style touched by European baroque, and the article notes that hospices and hospitals were by then giving way to libraries and fountains. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 247}

## Quarters, streets and houses
Istanbul had 182 mahalles at the end of Mehmed II's reign, 219 by 953/1546 and 292 in 1044/1634, not counting non-Muslim ones. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 248} A mahalle was a community around its place of worship, answerable jointly for order and taxes, led by its imam, who passed on government decrees. Residence of four or five years was needed to count as belonging, and neighbours stood surety for one another. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 248} Streets were crooked and full of dead ends, and the main road, Divan Yolu, was only 3.5 to 5.5 metres wide in the nineteenth century. Gas lighting began on Beyoglu Street in 1273/1856. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 249}

Building was under state control, with repeated rules limiting houses to two storeys, which were widely ignored. Most houses were wooden, and non-Muslims faced stricter limits on height and could not build baths. The article suggests these limits and fear of plague pushed non-Muslims to settle outside the walls. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 249} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 250} It describes five house types, from single rooms for bachelors, counted at 12,000 in 1083/1672, to walled houses with gardens, palaces of statesmen and the villas along the Bosphorus. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 250} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 251}

## Fires and earthquakes
Fires were frequent in a city of wooden houses. One calculation gives 229 fires between 1853 and 1906, destroying 36,000 houses, and the article lists great fires from 977/1569 to 1336/1918. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 251} Fires worried the palace as signs of unrest, and the Janissaries, who fought them, were sometimes blamed for starting them. The homeless took refuge in mosque courtyards, and prices of food and timber rose. Pumps came after 1130/1718 and a regular fire brigade in 1290/1873. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 251} On 6 Jumada I 915/22 August 1509 an earthquake, called the Little Doomsday, felled every minaret, and the article gives estimates of 5,000 to 13,000 dead. A levy and conscripted workmen then paid for rapid rebuilding. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 251} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 252}

## The inhabitants
Mehmed II repopulated the city by deportation, by moving craftsmen and merchants from conquered towns, and by welcoming immigrants of any religion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 252} Selim I brought households from Tabriz and Cairo, and Jews expelled from Spain and elsewhere from 1492 were welcomed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 253} Moriscos from Spain settled in Galata after 978/1570. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 253} In the sixteenth century the population grew sharply as young men from poorer regions came to work as porters, boatmen and labourers, drawn by easier livelihoods and by free food from the hospices. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 253} The authorities, alarmed by shortages and disorder, repeatedly expelled recent unmarried arrivals, to little effect. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 254}

Only the Orthodox and Armenian churches and the Jewish rabbinate were officially recognised. Each community had its own head, whose authority came from a sultan's deed. The Greek patriarchate gained civil duties, and the Greeks prospered in trade and tax contracts, with an aristocracy in Fener. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 255} The Armenians elected a patriarch from 1461 and later controlled silk and banking. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 255} Jews numbered 1,647 households at the end of Mehmed II's reign. Marrano financiers such as the Mendes family rose after 959/1552, and the Romaniot, Sephardic and Karaite communities merged after the fires of 1043/1633 and 1071/1660. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 255} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 256} The article says conversion to Islam was frequent but forced conversion was avoided, and that the court and kapi-kulu troops, outside the tax rolls, shaped city life. The kapi-kulu establishment grew about fivefold in the century after 920/1514, chiefly through the Janissaries, and many became tradesmen, so that mutinies after 1600 were in part popular risings. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 256} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 257} The [[group/janissaries|Janissaries]] also served as police, and the article says the lives and property of non-Muslims were in effect at their mercy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 256}

## Plague and population
Plague returned often, from 871/1466 onward, killing as many as 1,000 a day in 1592 and 3,000 a day in 1792, and 150,000 in 1812 by one account. Quarantine was adopted in 1838. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 257} The article treats claims of 700,000 or more inhabitants before the nineteenth century as exaggerated. It prefers about 300,000 for 1573 and 360,000 counted in 1829, and gives a density in 1927 of 145 persons per hectare over 17.2 square kilometres. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 257} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 258}

A city that was known as Constantinople. Mehmed the Conqueror took it in 1453 CE, and it was the capital of the Ottoman state.

## For Sprouts
Istanbul is a beautiful city bridging two continents across the Bosphorus, famous for its grand domed mosques and soaring minarets.

## For Explorers
Istanbul, historically known as Constantinople, is a city spanning Asia and Europe across the Bosphorus. Opened by Sultan Mehmed II in 857 AH, it served as the capital of the Ottoman Empire. Graced by timeless monuments like the Blue Mosque, the Suleymaniye, and Topkapi Palace, it flourished as a center of commerce, culture, and calligraphy.

## For Scholars
The storied imperial capital straddling Europe and Asia across the Bosphorus Strait between the Black Sea and the Sea of Marmara. Serving as the Byzantine capital Constantinople for over a millennium, it was conquered in 857 AH (1453 CE) by Ottoman Sultan Mehmed II, becoming the administrative seat and caliphal capital of the Ottoman Empire until 1922 CE. It achieved exceptional architectural brilliance during the reign of Suleiman the Magnificent through master architect Mimar Sinan, featuring iconic landmarks like the Suleymaniye Mosque, Topkapi Palace, the Blue Mosque, and rich waqf manuscript libraries bridging East and West.
