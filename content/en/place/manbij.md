## Overview
Manbij is one of the districts of the military province (jund) of [[place/qinnasrin|Qinnasrin]], in the area of Dulük and Ra'ban. Iyad ibn Ghanm came to it and made peace with its people on the same terms as the peace of Antioch, and [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda]] ratified this. Iyad then sent to Dulük and Ra'ban, and their people made peace with him on the same terms as Manbij, on condition that they seek out news of the Romans and write it to the Muslims. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.204-205}

Near the city is the village of the Bridge of Manbij. The bridge did not exist at that time. It is said that it was built in the caliphate of Uthman ibn Affan for the summer campaigns, and it is also said that the site had an older vestige. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.204-205}

Abu Ubayda appointed a governor over every district he conquered, attached to him a group of Muslims, and garrisoned the threatened regions. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.204-205}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-Buldan
Yaqut records the name Manbij with a kasra under the ba, calls the town an ancient one, and thinks it was probably Greek in origin. He lists several possible Arabic derivations, from the verb for sitting on a hillock, from the barking of a dog, from a famine food of the Arabs and from a rude noise, rejects the first because the site is flat, and leaves the reader to choose. He also reports that some say Khosrow was the first to build it after he overcame Syria, and that the name is an Arabised form of a Persian phrase. He adds that Harun al-Rashid set the frontier districts apart as the Awasim, made Manbij their capital and settled Abd al-Malik ibn Salih of the Abbasid house there. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.205}

Yaqut describes it as a large, spacious and prosperous city on open ground that once had a strong stone wall, three farsakhs from the Euphrates and ten from Aleppo. Its people drank from channels running over the ground and from wells in their houses, which they preferred as sweeter, and in his day it belonged to the ruler of Aleppo. He names al-Buhturi as the poet of Manbij, who owned property there, says he knows no other outstanding poet from it, and quotes the line in which al-Mutanabbi mentions the town. On the authority of Ibn Qutayba and al-Batalyawsi he explains that the Manbijani cloak takes its name from the town. Citing Ibn al-Attar, he says Abd al-Malik ibn Salih was born there and retells an exchange in which he praised the town's air to Harun al-Rashid. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.206}

From the Kitab al-Futuh he reports that after the conquest of Aleppo and Antioch Abu Ubayda sent Iyad ahead to Manbij and that its people then made peace on the terms of Antioch, and he gives verses by Ibn al-Mudabbir longing for the town. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.206} Among its scholars he names the hadith transmitter Umar ibn Said al-Ta'i al-Manbiji, who heard in Damascus, and he gives the distances as two days to Aleppo, four to Malatya and one to the Euphrates. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.207}
