## Overview
Antioch: the city of the Syrian frontier. Its people made peace with [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda]] on poll-tax and evacuation; then they broke it, and Iyad ibn Ghanm and Habib ibn Maslama conquered it again, according to al-Baladhuri. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407} See [[event/conquest-of-antioch|the conquest of Antioch]]. It belongs to the district of [[place/qinnasrin|Qinnasrin]] and the frontier cities.

## After the conquest
al-Baladhuri reports that Umar ordered a garrison of Muslims to be arranged there, that Uthman granted estates in it, and that Mu'awiya moved to it in the year 42 AH people from Ba'labakk, Homs and other places. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

## Landmarks mentioned by the sources
al-Mas'udi mentions in it a shrine known as al-Dimas, on the right of the congregational mosque, which the moon enters as it rises in some summer months. It is said that it was built by the Persians and that it was a fire-temple. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.556}

## Encyclopaedia of Islam account
The Encyclopaedia of Islam (2nd ed.), in the entry ANTAKIYA by M. Streck and H. A. R. Gibb, describes Antioch on the Orontes, fourteen miles from the Mediterranean, founded about 300 BC by Seleucus I and taken by Pompey in 64 BC. It became the largest and most important Roman city in Asia. Its decay began with the rise of the Sasanians, who attacked it repeatedly: Shapur I occupied and plundered it in 258 and 260, and Khusraw I destroyed it in 540 and deported its inhabitants. The Arabs occupied it in 16/637-8. It was the headquarters of the frontier organisation of al-'Awasim. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 540-541}

Antioch was annexed by Ahmad ibn Tulun in 265/878 and held by his successors until 285/898, was taken by Sayf al-Dawla in 333/944, and was recaptured by the Byzantine general Michael Bourtzes in 358/969 and governed by Byzantine dukes until 477/1084, when Sulayman ibn Qutlumush took it by treachery. Sulayman was killed by Tutush in 1086. Malikshah gave it in fief to Yaghi-Siyan, from whom the Crusaders took it on 2 June 1098. It was held by the Norman princes descended from Bohemond until the Mamluk sultan Baybars took it on 19 May 1268. After the First World War French troops occupied it in February 1919; it was the capital of the Sanjak of Alexandretta, and it was ceded to Turkey on 23 June 1939. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 541}

Its Byzantine and medieval remains are small, and the only Muslim monument of importance is the sanctuary below Mount Silpius, identified in Muslim tradition with the believer of the Quran (xxxvi. 12 ff.) and called after Habib al-Najjar, the Carpenter. In 1931 the kada had about 99,347 people. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 541}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut vocalizes Antakiya with fatha then sukun and the ya ungeminated, denying that the doubled ya in the verses of Zuhayr and Imru' al-Qays proves the city's form since the doubling is for the nisba, and notes that when something pleased the Arabs they assigned it to Antioch. From al-Haytham ibn Adi: Antiochus was its first builder, the third king after Alexander. From Yahya ibn Jarir al-Takriti the physician: Antigonus founded it in the sixth year after Alexander's death and Seleucus completed it — the same who built Laodicea, Aleppo, Edessa and Apamea; elsewhere in his book the king built it on the Orontes as Antiochia and Seleucus finished and adorned it, naming it for his son Antiochus. It is also said the first to build and settle it was Antakiya daughter of al-Rum ibn al-Yaqan ibn Sam ibn Nuh, sister of Antaliya. Ptolemy gives longitude sixty-nine degrees and latitude thirty-five degrees thirty minutes, in the fourth clime. He calls it the seat of the marches among the Syrian frontier towns, among the country's mother cities, famed for purity, beauty, sweet air and water, plentiful fruit and abundance. He then transmits Ibn Butlan's letter to Abu'l-Hasan Hilal ibn al-Muhassin al-Sabi, written in the year forty-and-some and four hundred: he left Aleppo for Antioch, a day and a night away, through settled land sown under olives, unruined, its villages linked and waters gushing; Antioch a great town with wall and curtain wall of three hundred and sixty towers walked by four thousand guards sent from Constantinople and relieved yearly; the town a half-circle whose diameter meets the mountain, the wall climbing with it to the summit where a castle stands; the mountain shades it from the sun till the second hour; five gates in the wall below; in its midst the church of Cassian, house of the king Cassianus, and on one door a water-clock working night and day among the wonders of the world. The letter goes on to report the earthquake of Monday the fifth of Ab, what was seen in the sky, and what survived unharmed in the church — the throne, the cross, the silver and the lamps — as something to be wondered at. Of its conquest: Abu Ubayda made terms with its people; they broke them, so he sent Iyad ibn Ghanm and Habib ibn Maslama who retook it on the first terms — or, it is said, they broke them after Abu Ubayda returned to Palestine and Amr ibn al-As took it coming from Iliya. Garrison settlers moved in from Homs and Baalbek, among them Muslim ibn Abdallah, killed on a gate by a Frankish stone, whence the gate is called Bab Muslim to this day; al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik granted its troops the land of Seleucia by the shore, which they cultivated, and he built its fortress — the fulthur being a measure of land like the feddan and jarib. It stayed in Muslim hands as a frontier town until the Byzantines took it in 353 after Mopsuestia, Tarsus and Adhana, remaining theirs until Sulayman ibn Qutlumush the Saljuq rescued it in 477 and killed Sharaf al-Dawla Muslim ibn Quraysh in 478, writing to Sultan Jalal al-Dawla Malikshah ibn Alp Arslan with the news. He assigns scholars to it, among them Ibrahim ibn Abd al-Razzaq al-Antaki the qari', author of the book of the eight readings. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 1 p.266-270}

## The museum's identification
The record's Antakiya is Yaqut's city on the Orontes, the seat of the marches taken by the Muslims, lost to the Byzantines in 353 and recovered by Sulayman ibn Qutlumush in 477. Antaliya in the same entry is a different town — with lam for kaf — whose first settler the legend makes Antakiya's sister, and it stays separate. Ibn Butlan's letter to Hilal al-Sabi is an eyewitness text quoted inside the entry, and the foundation tales are carried under their reporters' names, al-Haytham ibn Adi and Yahya ibn Jarir. His "a day and a night" between Aleppo and Antioch stands as printed; the record's point lies 89.8 km from the museum's point for Aleppo (36.2012, 37.1612), the museum's own calculation, not the text's.
