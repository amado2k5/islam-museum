## Overview
Nishapur is a city in [[place/khurasan|Khurasan]] in north-eastern Iran. Its chief city is Abrashahr, one of the districts of Khurasan. It was an important centre for the science of hadith, and Imam Muslim, author of the Sahih, is attributed to it. {cite:geonames-gazetteer} The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose account this page follows for the rest of the history, ranks it as the most important of the four great cities of Khurasan, the others being [[place/marw|Marw]], [[place/herat|Herat]] and [[place/balkh|Balkh]], and as one of the great towns of Persia in the Middle Ages. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 72}

## Name and founding
The name goes back to the Persian New-Shahpur, "Fair Shapur". In Armenian it appears as Niu-Shapuh, in Arabic as Naysabur or Nisabur, and in New Persian as Neshapur, which in the time of [[person/yaqut-al-hamawi|Yaqut]] was pronounced Nishawur. The town sometimes carried the honorific title Iranshahr. The article reports that Hamza al-Isfahani makes the Sasanian king Shapur I, son of Ardashir I, its founder, while some authors say it was founded only under Shapur II. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 72}

Golius, as reported in a source on astronomy and geography, likewise says that Shapur son of Ardashir built it and that its name means "Shapur's reed bed", because of the many reeds at the site. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 431}

## The region
In the wide sense, the region of Nishapur took in a number of districts, among them [[place/al-tabasayn|al-Tabasayn]], Kuhistan, Baward, Abarshahr, Jam, Bakharz, [[place/tus|Tus]], Zuzan and Isparayin. In the narrow sense Nishapur was the capital of the province of [[place/abarshahr|Abarshahr]], which was divided into thirteen rustaqs and four tassujs. The four were Rewand in the west, al-Shamat in the south, Pushtfurushan in the east and Mazul in the north. In the Rewand hills north-west of the town stood one of the three most sacred fire temples of the Sasanians, that of the fire Burzin-Mihr. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 72}

## The conquest and the early Islamic period
The governor of [[place/basra|Basra]], [[person/abd-allah-ibn-amir|Abd Allah ibn Amir]], took Nishapur in 30 or 31 AH, 651 or 652 CE, and its local lord, the Kanarang, capitulated. The article describes the town as insignificant then, with no garrison. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 72} The book Futuh al-Buldan adds that Abd Allah ibn Amir besieged it for months. The lord of one of its quarters asked for safe conduct and admitted the Muslims by night. Its marzban took refuge in the citadel, the qahandiz, and then made peace for all of it for one million dirhams, or, it is said, seven hundred thousand. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407} Another source says it came under Muslim rule in 31 AH in the days of Uthman. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 432}

During the fighting between [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] and [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Muawiya]] in 36 and 37 AH the Arabs were driven out again by a rising in Khurasan and Tukharistan. The article says Peroz III, son of Yazdegerd III and of the daughter of the Kanarang of Nishapur, is said to have lived there for a time. Khulayd ibn Kas was sent against the rebellious town in 37 AH. After Muawiya reappointed Abd Allah ibn Amir to Basra in 41 AH and charged him with conquering Khurasan and Sistan, Abd Allah installed [[person/qays-ibn-al-haytham|Qays ibn al-Haytham al-Sulami]] in Nishapur as governor of Khurasan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 72} In 45 AH [[person/ziyad-ibn-abi-sufyan|Ziyad ibn Abi Sufyan]] made [[person/khulayd-ibn-abdallah-al-hanafi|Khulayd ibn Abd Allah al-Hanafi]] governor of Abarshahr, meaning Nishapur. Abd Allah ibn Khazim rebelled against the Umayyads in 63 AH and fell in 73 AH at Marw fighting Abd al-Malik, after which Umayyad rule returned to Khurasan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 72}

## Tahirids, Saffarids and Samanids
Until the Tahirid governor [[person/abd-allah-ibn-tahir|Abd Allah ibn Tahir]] made it his capital in the years 213 to 230 AH, the article says Nishapur mattered less than Marw, the first Arab capital of the province. Helped by a healthier climate, it then overtook Marw in political weight and became a centre of economic life, above all for its textiles, including the luxury attabi and saqlatuni cloths, and of cultural life. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 72} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 74}

It ceased to be a provincial capital in 259 AH, 863 CE, when the Saffarid amir Yaqub ibn al-Layth took Khurasan from the Tahirids and entered the city. For some thirty years control of it passed between the [[polity/saffarid-dynasty|Saffarids]] and warlords and adventurers such as [[person/rafi-ibn-harthama|Rafi ibn Harthama]], until [[person/amr-ibn-al-layth|Amr ibn al-Layth]] was defeated and captured by the Samanid [[person/ismail-ibn-ahmad|Ismail ibn Ahmad]] in 287 AH, 900 CE. Under the [[polity/samanid-dynasty|Samanids]], in the tenth century CE, the city enjoyed special prosperity, as the provincial capital of Khurasan again and the base and residence of its commander-in-chief. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 74}

## A city of scholars and notables
The article describes arts and crafts such as ceramic production as notable in this period, and sees the general prosperity reflected in an influential bourgeoisie of merchants, craftsmen, officials, scholars and religious figures. These belonged to the two main schools of Khurasan, the [[group/hanafi-madhhab|Hanafis]] and the [[group/shafii-madhhab|Shafiis]], and to their rivals for popular support, the ascetic and pietistic [[group/karramiyya|Karramiyya]]. R. W. Bulliet has called this social group a patriciate. From it came scholars such as Abu Muhammad al-Juwayni and his son [[person/imam-al-haramayn-al-juwayni|Imam al-Haramayn]], the traditionist [[person/al-hakim-al-nisaburi|al-Hakim al-Naysaburi]], and also ambitious statesmen such as Hasanak, minister of [[person/mahmud-ghaznawi|Mahmud of Ghazna]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 74}

The article judges that the large number of traditionists and lawyers the city produced was undoubtedly a stimulus to several biographical dictionaries of Nishapur scholars. The first was that of Ibn al-Bayyi, who died in 405 AH, in eight or twelve volumes, and it became the starting point for continuations and epitomes. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 74}

## The city in the tenth century
The Arab geographers describe a thickly populated town of forty-two wards, one farsakh in length and breadth, made up of the citadel, the city proper and an outer suburb. The chief mosque, built by the Saffarid Amr, stood in the suburb, beside the public market called al-Mu'askar, the governor's palace and a second open place, the Maydan al-Husayniyyin. The citadel had two gates and the city four, among them the Gate of the Bridge and the Gate of the Fortress. The best-known markets were al-Murabbaa al-Kabira, near the Friday mosque, and al-Murabbaa al-Saghira. About fifty main business streets ran in straight lines and crossed at right angles. Canals from the Wadi Saghawar drove seventy mills and gave the houses ample water, and gardens below the city were watered the same way. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 74}

## In the geographers' books
Al-Mas'udi mentions that Zoroaster son of Aspiman made a fire temple in it. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.548} Al-Maqdisi mentions its Ribas, the harisa dish of its people, the turquoise mine in its district, the council of grievances, and the seat of judgement in the Mosque of Raja'. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim pp.247-248}

## Seljuks, Khwarazmshahs and the Ghuzz
A great famine struck in 401 AH, 1011 CE. At the start of the fifth century AH the town was the centre of the pietist Karramis, led by the anchorite Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Ishaq. The Seljuk [[person/tughril-beg|Tughril Beg]] first occupied it in 428 AH, 1037 CE, and later made it his capital. [[person/alp-arslan|Alp Arslan]] also seems to have lived there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 74} In Shawwal 536 AH, May 1142 CE, the Khwarazmshah [[person/atsiz-khwarazmshah|Atsiz]] took the town for a time from the Seljuk sultan [[person/sanjar|Sanjar]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 74}

When the [[group/ghuzz|Ghuzz]] sacked it in 548 AH, 1153 CE, the inhabitants fled, mainly to the suburb of Shadyakh, which the governor al-Mu'ayyid enlarged and fortified. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 74} The same event is noted in another source, which says Nishapur was struck when the Ghuzz seized Sultan Sanjar and plundered Khurasan. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 432} In 583 AH, 1187 CE, the Khwarazmshah [[person/ala-al-din-tekish|Tekish]] took Nishapur and gave it to his eldest son Malik Shah, and in 598 AH, 1202 CE, Ala al-Din Muhammad took Marw and Nishapur from the [[polity/ghurid-dynasty|Ghurids]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 74}

## Earthquakes and the Mongol sack
Besides wars and rebellions, the town suffered repeated earthquakes, in 540, 605 and 679 AH. Yaqut visited in 613 AH, 1216 CE, staying in Shadyakh. He could still see the damage from the first earthquake and from the Ghuzz, yet thought it the finest town in Khurasan. After the second earthquake the people fled for several days to the plain below the city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 74}

In 618 AH, 1221 CE, the Mongols under [[person/genghis-khan|Genghis Khan]] sacked the city completely. The article says its finest days were over but that it soon recovered. The city's centre had moved to Shadyakh after the earthquakes of the early seventh century AH, and the same cause lay behind its rebuilding on a third site towards the end of that century. In the fourteenth century CE Hamd Allah Mustawfi described it as highly flourishing, with extensive walls, and [[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]] called it "Little Damascus" for its fertility and praised its madrasas and crowds of students. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 74}

## Decline and the modern town
Nishapur then slowly declined until a modest revival in the later nineteenth century. In 1890 G. N. Curzon found the region still fertile and the turquoise mines of Bar-i Madin, about fifty kilometres north-west, still worked, though the town walls were ruinous. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 74} The modern town lies at about 36 degrees 13 minutes north and 58 degrees 49 minutes east, at 1,193 metres, on the east side of a plain ringed by hills, below the ridge of Binalud-Kuh, whose streams irrigate its lands. The tombs of two of its famous sons, [[person/umar-khayyam|Umar Khayyam]] and [[person/farid-al-din-attar|Farid al-Din Attar]], are still shown there. The census of 1365 sh, 1986 CE, gave a population of 109,258. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 75}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-Buldan
Yaqut gives the name Nishapur with a fatha on the first letter and notes that the common people say Nashawur. He calls it a great city of weighty merits, a mine of the virtuous and a spring of scholars, and says that in all his travels he saw no city like it. On the name he reports differing accounts: that Shapur passed by when it was full of reeds and judged it fit for a city, or that his companions searched for him after he was lost and, reaching the place, said nist Shapur, meaning that Shapur was not there. He lists Abarshahr and Iranshahr among its names, though he judges that Iranshahr properly means the land between the Oxus and al-Qadisiyya. He gives the distance from Rayy as 160 farsakhs and on to Sarakhs as 40, says that most of its people drank from underground channels reached by stairways, and describes it as rich in fruit and goods, with a rhubarb he knew nowhere else. He reports that the Muslims took it in the days of Uthman ibn Affan under Abdallah ibn Amir in the year 31 by treaty and that he built a congregational mosque there, while another account says al-Ahnaf ibn Qays took it in Umar's time and it later rebelled. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.331}

He recounts what the Ghuzz did to it in 548, when they captured the sultan Sanjar, killed those they found and burned the city, after which al-Mu'ayyad, one of Sanjar's mamluks, moved the people to its quarter of Shadhyakh, rebuilt and walled it until it again became one of the most prosperous of lands. He then describes its fate in 618, when the Tatars came from beyond the river. It held out until an archer on the wall killed one of their leaders, whereupon Genghis Khan came in person, took the city, killed its people and razed it, which Yaqut calls a calamity without equal in the history of Islam. He quotes verses both against and in praise of the city and says that countless leading scholars came from it, naming among them the hadith master Abu Ali al-Husayn ibn Ali al-Naysaburi. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.332}
