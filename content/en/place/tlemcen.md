## Overview
Tlemcen is an Algerian city, which was the capital of the Banu Zayyan kingdom (1235–1554 CE) and a centre of learning in the Maghrib. {cite:geonames-gazetteer} This description needs an additional source.

## In the Pleiades gazetteer
Pleiades, the open gazetteer of ancient places, lists this place under the title «Pomaria» (Pleiades no. 285501), classed as a settlement, with activity attested from 30 BCE to 1999 CE. Recorded names include Pomaria, Tilimsan and Tlemcen. Its description only points to the Barrington Atlas map 29 D2 for the ancient place. {cite:pleiades 285501}

## In the iDAI.gazetteer
The German Archaeological Institute's iDAI.gazetteer holds this place as record 2764518, typed as an administrative unit and a populated place, with the preferred name «Tlemcen» and the parent «Tlemcen (Wilayat)» in its hierarchy. Recorded names include Pomaria (Latin), Tilimsan (Polish) and Tlemcen (Norwegian). {cite:idai-gazetteer 2764518}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut records Tilimsan with two kasras, a still mim and an unpointed sin, some saying Tinimsan with nun for lam; it is in the Maghrib, two adjoining walled cities a stone's throw apart — the old one named Agadir, where the common people live, and the new one laid out by the Veiled Ones, kings of the Maghrib, named Tafarzat, where the troops, the sultan's men and sundry people live — the pair to him like Fustat and Cairo in Egypt. He notes Tlemcen's Rashidiyya horses, prized above all others, the women's wool-made knabish weavings found nowhere else, and one stage to Wahran. Some claim it is the town where al-Khidr raised the wall mentioned in the Qur'an; Yaqut says he heard this from one who had seen the city. Men are named after it, among them Abu l-Husayn Khattab ibn Ahmad al-Tilimsani the poet who reached Baghdad around the year 520. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.44}

## The museum's identification
The record's Tlemcen is Yaqut's adjoining pair — old Agadir and the Veiled Ones' new Tafarzat — which the text itself joins under one name. The al-Khidr and wall report is attributed to "some" heard from an eyewitness, so it is carried as reported claim, not asserted fact, and the museum quotes no Qur'anic wording.
