## Overview
Ifriqiya is a region of the Maghrib between Tripoli and Tangier. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

Al-Baladhuri reports from Ibn al-Kalbi that Ifriqis ibn Qays ibn Sayfi al-Himyari overcame it in the days of ignorance, and it was named after him. He killed its king Jurjir, and said to the Berbers: "How many of you are there in barbarism!" So they were named Berbers. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

Abd Allah ibn Sa'd ibn Abi Sarh raided it in the caliphate of Uthman, and killed its patrician at Subaytila, and its notables made peace with him for three hundred qintars of gold. Then Uqba ibn Nafi conquered it and laid out its city, al-Qayrawan. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

Al-Baladhuri quotes Ibn al-Kalbi that Ifriqis ibn Qays ibn Sayfi al-Himyari ruled it in the Jahiliyya and it was named for him; he killed its king Jarjir, and told the Berbers 'how much you babble' — whence they were called Barabira. ## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut vocalizes Ifriqiya with kasra on the hamza and makes it the name of a broad land and great kingdom facing Sicily, its end reaching opposite al-Andalus, both islands to its north. It was named, he says, for Ifriqis ibn Abraha ibn al-Ra'ish; from Abu'l-Mundhir Hisham ibn Muhammad, for Ifriqis ibn Sayfi ibn Saba' ibn Yashjub ibn Ya'rub ibn Qahtan, who marked it out: campaigning in the Maghreb he reached a wide, well-watered place, ordered a city built, called it Ifriqiya, moved people to it, and the whole province was named for the city; he quotes a companion's verse on the campaign. From Abu Abdallah al-Quda'i: named for Fariq ibn Baysar ibn Ham ibn Nuh, whose brother Misr took Egypt as Fariq took Ifriqiya. He notes that when the Muslims founded Kairouan, Ifriqiya the city fell to ruin and the name remained on the whole region; from Abu'l-Rayhan al-Bayruti that the Egyptians call what lies to their right facing south the Maghreb, so Ifriqiya was named for what divided Egypt from the Maghreb, not for its builder. Its bound runs from Tripoli of the West on the Barqa and Alexandria side to Bijaya — or, it is said, to Milyana, about two and a half months in length; from Abu Ubayd al-Bakri al-Andalusi: in length from Barqa east to Tangier the Green west, in width from the sea to the sands at the head of the land of the Sudan, where the good fanak is hunted. He reports from the sira narrators that Umar wrote to Amr ibn al-As: do not enter Ifriqiya, for it divides its people and gathers them not, its water hardening their hearts. On its conquest, from Ahmad ibn Yahya ibn Jabir: Uthman made Abdallah ibn Sa'd ibn Abi Sarh governor of Egypt and ordered him to open it, reinforcing him with an army carrying al-Mu'abbad ibn al-Abbas, Marwan ibn al-Hakam, al-Harith ibn al-Hakam, Ubaydallah ibn Umar, Abd al-Rahman ibn Abi Bakr, Abdallah ibn Amr ibn al-As, Abdallah ibn al-Zubayr, al-Musawwir ibn Makhrama, Abd al-Rahman ibn Zayd ibn al-Khattab, Abdallah and Asim sons of Umar, Bisr ibn Abi Arta'a and Abu Dhu'ayb al-Hudhali, in year 29 — or 28, or 27. He took it by force, killed its patrician who ruled from Tripoli to Tangier, and its notables ransomed peace for three hundred qintars of gold — or, it is said, for 2,520,000 dinars, from which Yaqut himself works out the qintar at 8,400 dinars — and he returned to Egypt appointing no governor. Then in year 50 Mu'awiya ibn Hudayj al-Sakuni sent Uqba ibn Nafi' al-Fihri, who raided it; the Muslims took it, settled it and founded Kairouan. He then lists its governors: Zuhayr ibn Qays al-Balawi in 69, killed by the Byzantines; Hassan ibn al-Nu'man al-Ghassani; Musa ibn Nusayr; Muhammad ibn Yazid the client of Quraysh in 99; Isma'il ibn Abdallah ibn Abi'l-Muhajir under Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz; Yazid ibn Abi Muslim; Bishr ibn Safwan in 103; Ubayda ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami in 110; Ubaydallah ibn al-Habhab; Kulthum ibn Iyad al-Qushayri in 123, killed by the Berbers; Hanzala ibn Safwan al-Kalbi in 124; then Abd al-Rahman ibn Habib ibn Abi Ubayda ibn Uqba ibn Nafi' expelled him and ruled, acknowledged by Marwan, then pledged to al-Saffah before al-Mansur repudiated him, and his brother Ilyas ibn Habib killed him treacherously; the Kharijites prevailed until al-Mansur appointed Muhammad ibn al-Ash'ath al-Khuza'i, who came in 144; then al-Aghlab ibn Salim in 148, killed in 150; Amr ibn Hafs Hazarmard in 151, killed in 154; Yazid ibn Hatim al-Muhallabi in 154, who settled the land and died at Kairouan in 170; Dawud ibn Yazid; Ruh ibn Hatim, dead in 174; Nasr ibn Habib; al-Fadl ibn Ruh, killed by the Kharijites in 178; Harthama ibn A'yan in 179; Muhammad ibn Muqatil al-Akki, expelled; then Ibrahim ibn al-Aghlab al-Tamimi until he died in 196. He counts the Aghlabid line: Abdallah ibn Ibrahim, Ziyadat Allah 201-223, Abu Iqal to 226, Muhammad to 242, Ibrahim to 249, Ziyadat Allah II to 250, Muhammad ibn Ahmad to 261, Ibrahim ibn Ahmad twenty-eight years to 289, Abdallah ibn Ibrahim killed by his Slav slaves, Ziyadat Allah ibn Abdallah who fled before Abu Abdallah al-Shi'i in 296 — the Aghlabids thus ruling a hundred and twelve years, eleven kings. Then the Ubaydid Alids: al-Mahdi, al-Qa'im, al-Mansur and al-Mu'izz until he took Egypt in 362, the khotba staying theirs to 407; then Yusuf Bulkin ibn Ziri ibn Manad al-Sanhaji by al-Mu'izz's appointment to 373, al-Mansur to 386, Badis to 406, al-Mu'izz ibn Badis who removed the Egyptians' khotba, prayed for al-Qa'im bi-Amr Allah in 435 and was answered by al-Yazuri, al-Mustansir's vizier, loosing the Arabs upon Ifriqiya until they wrecked it — al-Mu'izz dying in 453 after forty-seven years — then Tamim ibn al-Mu'izz to 501, Yahya ibn Tamim to 509, Ali ibn Yahya to 515, al-Hasan ibn Ali, expelled from al-Mahdiyya by Roger lord of Sicily in 543; the Franks held Ifriqiya twelve years until Abd al-Mu'min rescued it on Ashura day in 555 and appointed Muhammad ibn Farraj over it, and it is now in the hands of governors on behalf of his son. He assigns scholars to it, among them Abu Khalid Abd al-Rahman ibn Ziyad ibn An'am al-Ifriqi its qadi, the first child born in Islam in Ifriqiya, and Sahnun ibn Sa'id of Malik's jurists. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 1 p.228-231}

## The museum's identification
The record's Ifriqiya is Yaqut's whole kura between Tripoli and Tangier on al-Bakri's bound; the city Ifriqis founded fell to ruin and the name stayed on the region, so the record follows the provincial sense while the first city is a report of Abu'l-Mundhir's. His qintar of 8,400 dinars is his own arithmetic from the two transmitted figures, not a printed rate. The "do not enter Ifriqiya" letter is carried as a sira report, and the governor list from Uqba to Abd al-Mu'min follows the printed dates.
