## Overview
Merv is an ancient city in present-day Turkmenistan, which was the capital of Khurasan. The Abbasid call (da'wa) set out from it, and al-Ma'mun stayed there before moving to Baghdad. {cite:geonames-gazetteer}

Al-Maqdisi lists among its features the great melon of Merv, which has no equal in the regions of Persia. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p.247}

What is related about the killing of Yazdegerd near it needs an additional source.

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut records Marw al-Shahijan: its nisba is Marwazi against the regular pattern while the cloth is Marwi on the pattern; between it and Nishapur are seventy farsakhs, thirty to Sarakhs and a hundred and twenty-two farsakhs to Balkh, twenty-two stages. Of the word Marw he says that in Arabic it names the white stones from which fire is struck, yet he states plainly that Marw remained Persian and that he never saw any of those stones there at all; Shahijan is Persian for "the king's soul", the town so named for its majesty. He reports from Burayda ibn al-Husayb a Prophetic report on the building and merit of Marw, quotes Ptolemy's Book of the Prophecy for its longitude and latitude in the fifth clime, and names its two Majan canals which thread its streets and water its estates. He recounts how Abdallah ibn al-Mubarak walked the wall with Ibrahim ibn Shammas al-Taliqani and asked who had built so great a city, and counts among its notables Ahmad ibn Hanbal, Sufyan al-Thawri, Ishaq ibn Rahawayh and Ibn al-Mubarak himself. He describes Sultan Sanjar's tomb with its blue dome visible a day's journey off, and two congregational mosques, Hanafi and Shafi'i, inside one wall; he left it in the year 616 at its best after three years' residence in which he found no fault but the townspeople's maddening bile, and had the Tatars not come upon those lands and ruined them he would never have parted from it; the entry closes with Malik ibn al-Rayb's verses as his death drew near at Marw. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.112-115}

## The museum's identification
This record's Merv is Yaqut's Marw al-Shahijan, the city he lived in three years and saw himself; its twin Marw al-Rudh is his neighbouring headword and the museum's other card. The printed seventy farsakhs to Nishapur stands beside the museum's own haversine of about 310.3 km between the two records' points (the museum's calculation, not Yaqut's text). The register also holds a thin duplicate card under place/marw for the same city; the duplication is recorded here and not resolved in this packet.
