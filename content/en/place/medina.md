## Overview
Medina, in Arabic al-Madina, is a city in the Hijaz to which the Prophet migrated in 622 CE, and where the Prophet's Mosque and the first Muslim community were established. The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls it the residence of the Prophet Muhammad after the hijra and one of the sacred cities of Islam {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}. Before Islam it was an oasis of scattered settlements shared by Arab clans and Jewish groups. Under the Prophet it became the home of the first Muslim community, and under the first caliphs the seat of an expanding realm. It then settled into a quieter life as a city of learning, piety and retreat, and in December 1925 it passed under Saudi rule {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 64} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 65} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 66} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 67}. This page follows the article's own order: setting and name, the pre-Islamic oasis, the years of the Prophet, the caliphate, the later centuries, and the modern city.

## Location and setting
Its approximate coordinates are 24.4686 N and 39.6142 E {cite:geonames-gazetteer}. The article places the city in the Hijaz province of Saudi Arabia, about 160 km from the Red Sea and about 350 km north of Mecca {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}. It grew from an oasis on fairly level ground between the hill of [[place/uhud|Uhud]] on the north and the hill of Ayr on the south, with lava flows, called harra, to the east and west {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}. Several wadis cross the oasis from south to north. They carry water only after rain, but they keep the water table high, so wells and springs are many {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}.

The article adds that the central area of al-Manakha used to form a lake after rain, and that floods are recorded which threatened some buildings. On one occasion [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]] built a dam against floodwater. The water is salty in places, and governors in the past built aqueducts to bring better water from wells further south {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}. The soil is everywhere fertile, especially in the south. Palms were numerous before Muhammad's time and cereals were also grown. Oranges, lemons, pomegranates, bananas, peaches, apricots, figs and grapes came later {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}. Winters are cool with slight rain, and summers hot but rarely sultry. In earlier times the air was reckoned pleasant but conducive to fevers {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}.

## The names of the city
In pre-Islamic times the common name was Yathrib, which the article says applied originally to only part of the oasis. It reports that the name occurs once in the Qur'an, that a form of it appears in Ptolemy and Stephanus of Byzantium, and that Minaean inscriptions give it as Ythrb {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}. Al-Madina properly means the town or the place of jurisdiction, and corresponds to the Aramaic word medinta {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}.

The article notes that the word madina is used as a common noun several times in the Qur'an, and that in four relatively late passages al-madina refers to the oasis now inhabited mainly by Muslims, though it may not yet have been a proper name. It says the same of the last clause of the Constitution of Medina, whereas the preamble and two other clauses of that document use Yathrib alone {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}. The article calls it unlikely that the name is a shortened form of madinat al-nabi, the city of the Prophet, citing its use in the Qur'an, including by the Hypocrites. Among poets of the oasis, the pre-Islamic [[person/qays-ibn-al-khatim|Qays ibn al-Khatim]] speaks only of Yathrib, while [[person/hassan-ibn-thabit|Hassan ibn Thabit]] and Ka'b ibn Malik use both names {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}. The article also records that the Umayyads called the town al-khabitha, the dirty, in contrast to the honorific Tayba, the sweet-smelling, which it says Muhammad gave to it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}.

## The pre-Islamic oasis
The article describes the early oasis as not a compact town but a collection of scattered settlements among date groves and fields. For defence, perhaps about two hundred forts or strongholds had been built, where local people took refuge in danger. It suggests the idea of the fort may have come from Yemen {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}. It judges that the later Muslim historians had no reliable information about the earliest history of the oasis and that their views appear conjectural, and it considers it probable that some Arabs lived there before any Jews arrived {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}.

On the Jews of Medina the article is cautious. It thinks it probable that some were refugees from Palestine, perhaps after the defeat of Bar Kokhba, while others may have been Arabs who adopted Judaism. They intermarried with Arabs and shared many customs with them, yet they claimed Hebrew descent, even though the names of their clans and most individuals are Arabic {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62}. At the time of the hijra there were three main Jewish groups: [[group/banu-qurayza|Qurayza]], [[group/banu-nadir|al-Nadir]] and [[group/banu-qaynuqa|Qaynuqa']]. The first two held some of the most fertile land, while Qaynuqa' had none and were armourers and goldsmiths who also ran a market {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 62} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 63}.

Jewish dominance ended some time after the settlement of two large Arab groups, [[group/al-aws|al-Aws]] and [[group/al-khazraj|al-Khazraj]], who are said to have left South Arabia after the breaking of the Marib dam. At first they lived under Jewish protection. A revolt led by Malik ibn al-'Ajlan made them independent, according to accounts the article calls somewhat legendary {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 63}. The article denies that the Jews then became subject to the Arabs: the main Jewish groups kept a measure of independence, and were allied with different Arab clans, sometimes on opposite sides in clan fighting {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 63}.

The effective political units, the article says, were smaller clans rather than the two tribes. For at least fifty years before the hijra there were blood-feuds between Arab groups, perhaps with a desire for better land behind them. The bloodiest "war", that of Hatib, culminated in the battle of [[event/yawm-buath|Bu'ath]] in about 617 CE. Neither side won decisively, no formal peace was made, and the article suggests this unresolved conflict was one reason the Arabs of Medina invited Muhammad {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 63}. It also notes devotion to the deity Manat among Aws and Khazraj, and signs of a movement towards monotheism among some individuals, including [[person/asad-ibn-zurara|As'ad ibn Zurara]]. Abu 'Amir al-Rahib, previously a monotheist and ascetic, became an opponent of Muhammad {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 63}.

## From the hijra to the death of the Prophet
The article says Medina probably knew of Muhammad's mission from an early date. The first definite converts were six men of al-Khazraj in about 620 CE. At the pilgrimage of 621 they returned with twelve men, and the party accepted Islam and made promises, the first [[event/pledges-of-aqaba|pledge of al-Aqaba]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 63}. In 622, seventy-three men and two women from Medina promised to protect Muhammad and his followers as their own kin, the second pledge, called the pledge of war. About seventy Meccan followers then emigrated in small groups. Muhammad and [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] came last and reached Quba in the south of the oasis on 12 Rabi' al-Awwal, 24 September 622 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 63}.

Muhammad accepted none of the offers of hospitality, but let his camel choose. It halted on land belonging to two orphans, which he bought for his mosque and his house. The article suggests the same wish to avoid ties to any one rival clan explains why none of his marriages was with a woman of Aws or Khazraj {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 63}. At al-Aqaba he had asked for twelve representatives, naqibs, and the article reads the later disuse of the office as a sign of a political structure for Medina that faded {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 63}.

The effective structure of the community is, in the article's view, the one set out in the document known as the [[event/constitution-of-medina|Constitution of Medina]]. It describes the text as composite and, in its present form, probably later than the Qurayza affair of 627, although some articles may go back to the pledge of war {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 63} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 64}. By this document the people of Medina form one umma on traditional Arab ideas of confederacy. There are nine primary members: eight local clans, three of Aws and five of Khazraj, and the Emigrants from Mecca. About ten Jewish groups are named in separate articles and confirmed in their own religion. Even unbelievers in Arab clans seem to have been accepted, with restricted rights. Muhammad is given no special powers, but is recognised as prophet and as the one to whom disputes are referred {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 64}. The article judges that until 627 he could not issue commands but had to consult clan leaders, and that after the conquest of Mecca in 630 his authority was unchallenged {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 64}. Aws and Khazraj, as his earliest allies, came to be called the [[group/ansar|Ansar]], the helpers {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 64}.

From the hijra to his death there were over seventy expeditions, the maghazi. In the first small ones only the Emigrants took part, whereas at [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]] in 624 the Medinan Muslims formed about three quarters of his force {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 64}. The article reports that a few who opposed the Muslims, the Hypocrites, were dangerous when the Meccans invaded in 625 and again when they besieged Medina in 627. It adds that some Jews opposed him, which it connects with the expulsion of Qaynuqa' and al-Nadir in 624 and 625 and the execution of the men of Qurayza, with the sale of its women and children, in 627 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 64}. It states that after 627 the remaining Jews gave no further trouble, and that Arab nomads who accepted Islam increasingly settled in Medina {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 64}.

About 630 another group of Hypocrites appeared. During the expedition to [[event/expedition-to-tabuk|Tabuk]] a plan on Muhammad's life was foiled, and a mosque completed in the south of the oasis, the [[building/masjid-dirar|Mosque of Dissension]], was demolished on his order after he judged it a focus of intrigue {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 64} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 65}. The article says that after Tabuk he left Medina only for the [[event/farewell-pilgrimage|Pilgrimage of Farewell]] in March 632. He fell ill about two months later, stayed in A'isha's apartment, and died on 13 Rabi' al-Awwal 11, 8 June 632, and was buried there. He had appointed Abu Bakr to lead the public prayers but, the article says, had made no arrangement for succession to political authority {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 65}.

## The seat of the early caliphs
The article describes how the Ansar met in the hall of the clan of Sa'ida and appointed [[person/sad-ibn-ubada|Sa'd ibn 'Ubada]] as their ruler. [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] and Abu Bakr hurried there and persuaded them that only a man of Quraysh could be accepted by everyone as head of the community, and Abu Bakr was appointed. This is the [[event/saqifa|Saqifa]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 65}. Abu Bakr continued to live in Medina, sent expeditions northward and spent most of his short reign, 11-13 AH, subduing revolts of Arab tribes, the [[event/ridda-wars|wars of the Ridda]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 65}. Umar and Uthman also lived there apart from brief visits to conquered provinces {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 65}.

In the article's judgement Medina was briefly the capital of an empire but had little of the dignity of such a role: the caliph lived in his private house and had no guards, and when insurgents from the provinces attacked Uthman at home he had only the sons of leading Medinans as a token force, and was killed {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 65}. The Muslims of Medina then accepted [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]], but they were by now a small part of the whole community and their choice was not accepted everywhere. The Syrians favoured Mu'awiya, and Talha and al-Zubayr opposed Ali. Ali left for Iraq in October 656 and never returned. In effect Kufa replaced Medina as capital, and after Mu'awiya was acknowledged in 661 the seat moved to [[place/damascus|Damascus]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 65}.

## A quieter city, 661 to 1926
The article says that once it ceased to be the centre of the caliphate, Medina became something of a political backwater. It came into the light briefly in 683 CE, when many leading men disliked Yazid's succession. A large body of Medinans, led by [[person/abdullah-ibn-hanzala|'Abd Allah ibn Hanzala]], renounced allegiance to [[person/yazid-ibn-muawiya|Yazid]] and forced the Umayyads there to take refuge in the quarter of [[person/marwan-ibn-al-hakam|Marwan ibn al-Hakam]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 65} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 66}. A Syrian army under Muslim ibn 'Uqba defeated the rebels at the [[event/waqat-al-harra|battle of the Harra]], and Medina is alleged to have been pillaged for three days. The article sees some anti-Umayyad exaggeration in the accounts, and holds that these events did not greatly change the city's position {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 66}.

Later the article records an Ibadi army from Yemen that took Medina in 130/747 and held it for three months until an army from Syria defeated it. Under the Abbasids Medina was the centre of two short-lived Hasanid revolts, that of [[person/muhammad-al-nafs-al-zakiyya|Muhammad ibn 'Abd Allah, the pure soul]], in 145/762, and that of [[person/husayn-ibn-ali-sahib-fakhkh|Husayn ibn Ali, the master of Fakhkh]], in 169/786. In 230/845 the Turkish general [[person/bugha-al-kabir|Bugha al-Kabir]] defeated the nomadic Sulaym and Hilal who had been raiding near the city {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 66}.

Already under Mu'awiya, the article says, Medina drew those who wanted to stay aloof from political turmoil, among them [[person/abdullah-ibn-umar|'Abd Allah ibn 'Umar]] as a prominent neutral. [[person/hasan-ibn-ali|al-Hasan ibn Ali]] came after renouncing his claim to the caliphate in 41/661, and Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya lived quietly there. Many Qurashis also settled, enjoying the wealth of the conquests, so that life in the city became notorious for luxury {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 66}.

In 1908 the Ottoman government built the Hijaz railway from Damascus to Medina. It was meant mainly for pilgrims but had military value, and after the revolt of the Grand Sharif [[person/husayn-ibn-ali-sharif-makkah|Husayn ibn Ali]] in November 1916 it was attacked. An Ottoman force under Fakhri Pasha held Medina until 10 January 1919, surrendering with 9,800 men {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 66}. In 1924, after the abolition of the caliphate by the Turkish republic, Husayn took the title of caliph and met much opposition. [[person/ibn-saud|Ibn Sa'ud]] invaded the Hijaz in August 1924, and in January 1926 became King of the Hijaz as well as of Najd, so that Medina joined the Saudi kingdom {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 66}.

## The modern city
The article's second part recounts that by mid-1924 Medina was largely cut off from supplies, that refugees from Mecca swelled its population, and that by September 1925 it was completely isolated and its garrison short of necessities. In November the citizens asked for negotiations on surrender, on terms of a general amnesty and safety for defenders and people. The surrender took place on 5 December 1925, and Prince Muhammad ibn 'Abd al-'Aziz entered the next day, prayed in the Prophet's Mosque and ordered rice and flour distributed to the hungry {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 66}. The article says Ibn Sa'ud preferred to wait until Muslim opinion had digested the new rule in Mecca, and that false reports of damage to the Prophet's green dome had already brought protests from abroad {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 66}.

The article then describes the destruction of the tombs in [[place/baqi-al-gharqad|al-Baqi']] cemetery. The chief Wahhabi judge, 'Abd Allah ibn Bulayhid, asked the local scholars for an opinion on the legality of the tombs, and after about two weeks a fatwa sanctioning their destruction was issued by Shaykh Muhammad al-Tayyib al-Ansari, with reluctant approval from his colleagues, which the article says was given partly out of fear. Because the local people were reluctant, the article says Ibn Bulayhid had to hire the Shi'i Nakhawila to do the work. A visitor soon afterwards found al-Baqi' looking like a razed town {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 66}. The article states that all graves were destroyed, those of the Prophet's family, of Uthman and of [[person/malik-ibn-anas|Malik ibn Anas]] among them, and outlying buildings such as the mosque of the tomb of Hamza as well {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 66} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 67}. It adds that full public security, unknown for long years, then provided a basis for a more hopeful future {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 67}. The visit of King 'Abd al-'Aziz in 1926 is also noted {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 67}.

On population the article lists estimates for the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, from the low tens of thousands to a census figure near 200,000 in 1974, and says current estimates for the province are about 500,000, all of them only estimates {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 67}. It notes an unusual age pattern: the group over 65 is larger than the groups just below it, which it explains by religious retirees who wish to spend their last days in the Prophet's city. Newer migrants came from Egypt, Lebanon and Syria as professionals, and mostly from Yemen as labourers {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 67}.

The Medinan dialect, the article says, differs from other Hijazi speech, and Turkish words were still heard in the late twentieth century. The population lived in compartmented quarters, hawsh, and included North Africans attracted by the tomb of Malik ibn Anas, Indians, Africans, Mauritanians and Central Asians. Religiously the large majority are Hanafi with a few Shafi'i, and there are several Shi'i groups, among them the Twelver Nakhawila, whom the article describes as a group formerly kept apart: they were not allowed to pray in the Prophet's Mosque or bury in al-Baqi'. Their origin is obscure and they claim descent from the Ansar {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 67}.

The article reports that the city changed dramatically after the Saudi takeover. Old houses built very close together near the mosque, of granite or basalt and three or four storeys tall, each with a well, gave way to cement and brick. Walls and gates disappeared before bulldozers, the quarters vanished, and from the 1960s a city plan brought wide streets, lighting and parks {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 68} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 69}. Suburbs such as al-Awali, Quba' and Sayyid al-Shuhada' grew, a new airport was built northeast of the city, and traffic and parking became serious problems. The mosque was restored under Sultan [[person/abd-al-majid-i-uthmani|'Abd al-Majid]], 1848-60 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 69} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 70}.

Agriculture, once the glory of the city, has nearly ended because of urban sprawl, new opportunities in the oil economy and a fall in the water table. Small dams built in 1956, 1958 and 1966 stopped destructive floods but also kept water from the acacia forest of al-Ghaba. Dates were an important export, with the best varieties named by one visitor as al-Anbari, al-Shalabi and al-Halwa, and the article adds that dates had a religious aura as a blessing for the eater {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 70}. The article treats the local economy under three headings: agriculture, commerce and industry, and the pilgrimage {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.3 p. 70}.

## For Sprouts
Medina is the blessed city of migration, home to the Prophet's Mosque, where the Prophet and his companions lived in harmony.

## For Explorers
Medina is the illuminated city to which the Prophet and early Muslims migrated, received by the Helpers (Ansar) with profound generosity. There, the first Islamic commonwealth was established based on justice and the Constitution of Medina. It houses the Prophet's Mosque, the Rawdah, and the resting places of the Prophet and his companions Abu Bakr and Umar.

## For Scholars
Taybah, the City of the Prophet, and the second holiest sanctuary in Islam, situated in a fertile volcanic oasis in the Hejaz roughly 400 kilometers north of Mecca. Following the Hijra in 622 CE, it served as the political and spiritual capital of the early Islamic state, where the Prophet forged brotherhood between Emigrants and Helpers and promulgated the Constitution of Medina. Encompassing the Prophet's Mosque, the Rawdah, Jannat al-Baqi, and the Mosque of Quba, Medina was designated an inviolable sanctuary (haram), serving as the crucible for the revelation of communal laws and civic ethics.
