## Overview
Zamakhshar: a small town of mediaeval Khwarazm lying between Nuzwar and Gurgandj (Jurjaniyya), the later capital of the province. Al-Muqaddasi records that in the later fourth/tenth century it had a citadel, a prison, a congregational mosque, walls with iron gates and a protective ditch with drawbridges raised at night. Its fame came from being the birthplace in 467/1075 of the Qur'an commentator and grammarian Abu al-Qasim Mahmud al-Zamakhshari {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 449}.

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut gives "Zamakhshar", with a fatha on the first and second letters, a silent kha, then shin and ra, and calls it a large village (qarya jami'a) in the districts of Khwarazm, from which Abu al-Qasim Mahmud ibn Umar al-Zamakhshari, the grammarian and man of letters, takes his name. He quotes verses by the amir Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Isa ibn Hamza ibn Wahhas al-Hasani al-Alawi in praise of him and of his village, whose sense is that all the villages of the world, except the one where he made his home, may be ransom for Zamakhshar, and that his fame is as great in the Hijaz as in Iraq. He also reports al-Zamakhshari's own words that he was born in an obscure village of Khwarazm called Zamakhshar, and that he heard his father say that a Bedouin passed by, asked its name and the name of its chief, was told "Zamakhshar and al-Rida'", and said, as the words are given, "no good in shar and rad", and did not go in. Yaqut adds that he has set out al-Zamakhshari and his reports in his book of literary men (Kitab al-udaba'). {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.147}

## The museum's identification
Yaqut calls Zamakhshar a large village in the districts of Khwarazm. The record, which comes from the Encyclopaedia of Islam, calls it a small town and the later capital of Khwarazm and gives al-Muqaddasi's account of its citadel, prison, mosque, walls and ditch; none of that is in Yaqut. The museum leaves both descriptions as they stand; they come from different periods and need not conflict. The year 467 that the record gives for al-Zamakhshari's birth is not in Yaqut's entry. Yaqut gives no distances or boundaries against which to test the record's point (42.5, 59.5), which is marked approximate. The story of the Bedouin and the name is told on al-Zamakhshari's own authority from his father, so it is an anecdote, not a date.
