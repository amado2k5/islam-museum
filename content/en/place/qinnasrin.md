## Overview
Qinnasrin is the chief town of the military province (jund) of Qinnasrin and of its Awasim. Its people made peace with Abu Ubayda on the same terms as the peace of Homs. Then they broke the covenant, and al-Simt ibn al-Aswad al-Kindi conquered it, according to al-Baladhuri. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.197} {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.198}

## Tanukh and the people of the city
The Tanukh were present there from their first settling in al-Sham, in tents of hair, and then they built houses. Some of them embraced Islam. The Banu Salih ibn Hulwan of Quda'a remained upon Christianity, and some of them embraced Islam in the days of al-Mahdi. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.198}

## The jund and its districts
Qinnasrin and Homs became a single jund. Among its districts are Aleppo, Manbij, Harran and what surrounds them. It is one of the military provinces of al-Sham ([[place/damascus|Damascus]]), and the site of [[event/conquest-of-qinnasrin|the conquest of Qinnasrin]]. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.197}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-Buldan
Yaqut vocalises the name Qinnasrin with a kasra on the first letter and a doubled nun, and quotes Ptolemy and the author of the astronomical tables for its coordinates. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.403} He reports that its mountain holds a shrine said to be the grave of the prophet Salih, with the footprints of the she-camel, then adds that the sound view places Salih's grave in Yemen at Shabwa, while others say Mecca. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.403} Qinnasrin was conquered by Abu Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah in the year 17, and Hims and Qinnasrin had been one unit. Citing Ahmad ibn Yahya, he says Abu Ubayda marched after the Yarmuk to Hims and then to Qinnasrin with Khalid ibn al-Walid in the vanguard; the townspeople fought, then withdrew into their fort and asked for terms, and he made peace with them. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.403} On the origin of the name he sets out several explanations: Ibn al-Anbari's derivation from the Arabic {qinnasri}, an aged man; the account of Abu al-Mundhir and Abu al-Qasim about Maysara ibn Masruq al-Absi, who said the place looked like the {qinn} (eyrie) of an eagle; and al-Zamakhshari's view that it was carried over from {al-qinnasr}. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.403} He also notes a well-known report from the Prophet that the abode of emigration would be one of three places, Medina, Bahrain or Qinnasrin; he describes Qinnasrin as a district (kura) of al-Sham that includes Aleppo, and the town as a stage from Aleppo near the {awasim}. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.404} It stayed populated, he says, until 351, when the Byzantines overcame Aleppo and the people of Qinnasrin scattered, some crossing the Euphrates and some moved to Aleppo by Sayf al-Dawla; in his own day only a caravan inn, a customs collector and a small settlement remained. He gives a second account that it was ruined in 355, when the Byzantine emperor burned its mosques while Sayf al-Dawla could not meet him. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.404} He closes with its nisba-bearers, the best attested in hadith being Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Baraka, known as Burda'is, whom al-Daraqutni judged weak and who died in 328 according to Ibn Zayd. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.404}
