## Overview
Gallipoli is a peninsula in north-western Turkey on the Dardanelles, and in the sources read its name is tied to a port and fortress on the European shore of the strait that became the first Ottoman base in Europe. See also [[place/gelibolu|Gelibolu]] and [[place/dardanelles|the Dardanelles]]. This page sets out what Ali Muhammad al-Salabi says in al-Dawla al-Uthmaniyya about the Ottoman crossing to it and its use as a base, and what the Arabic Hadir al-Alam al-Islami, with Shakib Arslan's notes, says of it in old European campaign plans and in First World War correspondence. Both works are of the twentieth century, and what they say of the eighth century AH is taken from other historians.

## The first Ottoman base in Europe
Al-Salabi says that Sultan Orhan was eager to conquer Constantinople and drew up a plan to besiege the Byzantine capital from west and east at once, sending his son and heir Sulayman to cross the Dardanelles and take positions on its western side. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.52} He relates that in 758 AH Sulayman crossed the strait by night with forty horsemen, seized Byzantine boats moored on the western bank and brought them back to the eastern bank, since the Ottomans then had no fleet; his men then boarded the boats to the European shore and took a port fortress the text spells Tarnab, Gallipoli with a fortress the text calls Jana Qal'a, Abisala and Rodosto, all on the Dardanelles from south to north. Al-Salabi judges that this step benefited those who came after Orhan in the conquest of Constantinople, and cites in his footnote a book by Jamal Abd al-Hadi. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.52} See [[person/sulayman-pasha-ibn-orhan|Sulayman Pasha ibn Orhan]].

Elsewhere in the book al-Salabi gives an account that differs in how the town was entered: a struggle for power inside the Byzantine state led the emperor Cantacuzenus to ask Orhan's help against his rival, and Orhan sent Ottoman forces; in 1358 an earthquake struck the cities of Thrace, the walls of Gallipoli collapsed and its people left, which made it easy for the Ottomans to enter. He says the Byzantine emperor protested in vain and that Orhan replied that divine providence had opened the city gates to his forces; Gallipoli soon became the first Ottoman base in Europe, and from it went the first campaigns that ended in the taking of the whole Balkan peninsula. When John V Palaiologos came to rule Byzantium alone he recognised Orhan's conquests in Europe in return for the sultan's undertaking to ease the flow of food and supplies to Constantinople. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.56} {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.57} Among the reasons for Orhan's success al-Salabi lists that the Christian world did not notice the danger of the Ottoman state until it had crossed the sea and taken Gallipoli. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.57}

The two accounts in the book differ on how the town was entered: the first gives Sulayman's night crossing in 758 AH, the second the collapse of the walls in an earthquake in 1358 CE. The pages read do not reconcile them, and 758 AH converts to 1357 CE, not 1358 (a conversion made by the page's author, not by al-Salabi). The museum's card for the [[event/fall-of-gallipoli-1354|Fall of Gallipoli]] dates it to 1354 CE, a date al-Salabi does not give in the pages read; the question is left for review.

## Under Murad II
Al-Salabi says Murad II took power after the death of his father Mehmed Chelebi in 824 AH, put down the internal revolts led by his uncle Mustafa with the backing of the state's enemies, and that the Byzantine emperor Manuel II supported Mustafa until he laid siege to Gallipoli to take it from the sultan and make it his base, but Murad seized his uncle and sent him to the gallows. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.79} The book gives no year for this siege, and the museum has a card for the [[event/battle-of-gallipoli-1422|Battle of Gallipoli 1422]] whose sources have not yet been checked on the page. See [[person/murad-ii-uthmani|Murad II]] and [[person/manuel-ii-palaiologos|Manuel II Palaiologos]].

## In old European campaign plans
In his notes Shakib Arslan sets out old European plans for war on the Ottomans. One he ascribes to Bertrandon de la Broquière (the Arabic text spells his name Bertrandon Dolaber Wakiyar), a close associate of Philip the Good, duke of Burgundy, whom the duke sent east as a scout for a crusade. He says the man went to Jerusalem in 1432 and returned to France by Damascus, Antioch, Bursa, Gallipoli, Constantinople, Edirne, Philippopolis, Sofia, Belgrade and Vienna, reaching his lord's court in 1433. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 p.226} Arslan also summarises a plan of 1587 by a captain he calls Lanoue, which proposes that the fleets of Spain and Portugal sail for Gallipoli and that the pope add a hundred warships to them. This is a report of plans drawn up by their authors and does not show they were carried out. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 p.242}

## The First World War
Gallipoli appears in the work in a letter dated 3 December 1915, which the page reads as 25 Muharram 1334 AH, among the published correspondence on Libya; its writer says he has returned to Egypt from visiting the allied armies at Gallipoli and found relations with al-Sanusi changed. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.132} The page read does not name the writer of the letter and does not describe fighting at Gallipoli, so this page says nothing about the Gallipoli campaign itself.

## A note on the sources
Al-Salabi is a modern author who relies in this section on secondary books cited in his footnotes; no Ottoman or Byzantine text of the eighth century AH was read for this page. Arslan reports in Hadir al-Alam al-Islami what he summarises from European sources, in his own voice and not that of the period he describes.
