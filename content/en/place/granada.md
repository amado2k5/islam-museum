## Overview
Granada, in the high valley of the Genil in the south of [[place/al-andalus|al-Andalus]], came to prominence only in the early eleventh century, when a branch of the Sanhaja Zirids made it their capital in place of nearby Elvira. From 635/1238 until 897/1492 it was the capital of the [[polity/emirate-of-granada|Nasrid kingdom]], the last Muslim state in Spain, and the seat of the [[building/alhambra|Alhambra]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1054} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1057}

## Elvira and early Granada
A township of Elvira, the ancient Illiberis, stood on the slopes of the range of that name by the time of Augustus, and a church council met there between 309 and 312. At the Muslim conquest [[person/musa-ibn-nusayr|Musa ibn Nusayr]] left the subduing of the south-east to his son [[person/abd-al-aziz-ibn-musa|'Abd al-'Aziz]], who occupied [[place/malaga|Malaga]] and Elvira-Granada. In 125/743 the governor allotted the district to the men of the Syrian army division of Damascus, whose chiefs later supported the landing of the Umayyad 'Abd al-Rahman I. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1054}

The first Muslim governors lived in Illiberis, which they called Ilbira, and the district kept the name of the province of Elvira. Granada itself in the ninth century was no more than a large walled village on the right bank of the Darro near its meeting with the Genil. Few Muslims lived there; there were more Christians, and Jews were so many that it was sometimes called Granada of the Jews. Facing it, on a rocky height above the left bank, stood an old citadel named the Red, al-Hamra', from its colour. In the reign of the amir 'Abd Allah the Arabs of the district, besieged in that citadel, made a sortie and routed their opponents in what came to be called the Battle of the City. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1054}

## The Zirids
The Zirids came to Spain with a large following under Zawi ibn Ziri to serve the Amirid chief ministers, and became one of the most turbulent parts of the Berber army. When the caliph Sulayman al-Musta'in rewarded his supporters with lands, he gave them the district of Elvira. Elvira was soon supplanted by Granada, and the article quotes the Memoirs of the last Zirid amir, 'Abd Allah, on why: his forebears admired the plain furrowed by streams and judged that an enemy besieging the mountain could not stop them from provisioning it, so everyone, Andalusian or Berber, built a house there, and Elvira fell into ruins. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1054}

The [[polity/taifa-of-granada|Zirid state]] was consolidated under the amirs Habus, 409-29/1019-38, and Badis, 429-65/1038-73, with the support of their Jewish viziers Samuel and his son Yusuf. The article records that Granada was then the scene of an attack on its Jews in which the vizier Yusuf and many of his co-religionists were killed. Afterwards Badis, old and fearing for his rule, spent large sums making the old citadel of the city impregnable. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1054} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1057}

## Almoravids and Almohads
Under the [[polity/almoravid-dynasty|Almoravids]] Granada was ruled by governors sent from Marrakesh, among them Tashfin, a son of [[person/ali-ibn-yusuf-al-murabiti|'Ali ibn Yusuf]], who fought the Castilians for ten years. When 'Ali ordered the walls of the Andalusian cities repaired, the governor of Granada pressed the work on although the Genil rose and swept away his materials; he was dismissed in 522/1128 and tried at Marrakesh on charges brought by the Christians of Granada, which were upheld. The article takes the episode as proof that those Christians kept their rights and were given justice. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1055}

When Almoravid power collapsed, the garrison held out in the old citadel until 551/1156 and then surrendered the city to the [[polity/almohad-caliphate|Almohads]]. An attack by Ibn Hamushk, made with the connivance of the Jewish and Christian townspeople and reinforced by [[person/ibn-mardanish|Ibn Mardanish]] and his Christian mercenaries, shut the Almohad garrison in the old citadel while the attackers held the Alhambra hill. A relieving force was beaten four miles from the city; a second, sent by [[person/abd-al-mumin-ibn-ali|'Abd al-Mu'min]], climbed the cliffs above the Genil by night, surprised the camp at dawn and freed the garrison. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1055} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1056}

## The Nasrid capital
In the general rising of the Spanish Muslims against the Almohads, [[person/ibn-hud-al-judhami|Ibn Hud]] made himself master of al-Andalus and was invested by an envoy of the Abbasid caliph in 630/1232. After he was assassinated at [[place/almeria|Almeria]], his rival [[person/muhammad-i-ibn-al-ahmar|Ibn al-Ahmar]] rose at Arjona, [[event/nasrid-entry-granada-635|took possession of Granada]] and founded the Nasrid dynasty. One of his first acts was to inspect the Alhambra, trace the foundations of its palace and bring water to it from the river; within the year many defensive works stood. He paid for the building by exacting sums from tax collectors, and later spent on it, and on enlarging the mosques of the city, money sent by the Hafsid ruler of Tunis. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1056}

As the Nasrid capital Granada was transformed, above all by the arrival of refugees. The wall was carried north to take in the Albaicin quarter. The article describes a city that traded profitably with Castile and Aragon but closed itself to the spiritual and artistic influence of the Christian world, keeping its Muslim traditions with what it calls pious faithfulness. Building was most active at the end of the thirteenth century and in the fourteenth; in the fifteenth, in a kingdom ever more threatened and shaken by internal strife, important buildings were rare. The Catholic Monarchs [[event/fall-of-granada-897|took the city in 897/1492]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1057}

## What survives in the city
From the tenth century there remain a minaret, now the bell-tower of the church of San Jose, and parts of a concrete wall. From the Zirid century there is the high rampart with its two gates, which have the oldest known bent entrances in Spain, and the bath called the Banuelo, whose three parallel vaulted rooms the article calls the perfect example of an Andalusian bath. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1056} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1057}

Granada has kept hardly any of its religious buildings. The church of the Salvador holds remains of the court of the great mosque of the Albaicin, which an old description gives nine aisles on eighty-six marble columns; a thirteenth-century minaret serves the church of San Juan de los Reyes; and of the [[building/madrasa-of-granada|madrasa]] built in 750/1349 only the prayer hall is left, much restored. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1057} The hospital has been destroyed. Of the many merchants' inns one stands, the Corral del Carbon, with a two-storey court and a richly decorated monumental doorway. A few houses, and garden pavilions outside the walls such as the Cuarto Real de Santo Domingo, show the same plan and ornament as the palaces of the Alhambra, which the article takes as proof that luxury in building was not the rulers' alone. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1058}

## In the Pleiades gazetteer
Pleiades, the open gazetteer of ancient places, lists this place under the title «Iliberri» (Pleiades no. 265921), classed as a settlement, with activity attested from 330 BCE to 2100 CE. It describes an ancient settlement to the west of modern Granada whose precise location is unknown. {cite:pleiades 265921}
