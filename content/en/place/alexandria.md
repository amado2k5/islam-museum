## Overview
Alexandria, in Arabic al-Iskandariyya, was the principal seaport of Egypt and, in Ptolemaic times, the second city of the world. It lies on the Mediterranean at the western angle of the Nile Delta and is one of the few important ports on the African shore of that sea. The Encyclopaedia of Islam says it was founded in 332 BC by [[person/al-iskandar-al-maqduni|Alexander the Great]], and that when it passed into Arab hands its glory had diminished but it was still a great and splendid city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 145}

Before [[place/al-fustat|Fustat]] was founded it was the seat of rule in Egypt, and al-Maqrizi reports that it stayed a house of learning and a seat of wisdom until the Muslims took it. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.422}

## The Arab takeover
The city was surrendered to the Arabs in 21/642, and a considerable number of Greeks left it. The new rulers did not molest the inhabitants. The encyclopaedia judges that the well-known story of the burning of the great library on the order of the caliph [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar ibn al-Khattab]], told by an Arabic author of the 7th/13th century, cannot be accepted as historical. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 145}

The article says the Arab newcomers were at first overwhelmed by the city, whose buildings and monuments must have seemed the work of superhuman power. Tradition held that the city shone so brightly at night that a tailor needed no lamp to thread his needle; the geographer Yaqut, writing in the 12th century, denied this, saying the town was as dark at night as any other and that its houses gleamed only because they were white and their facades and thoroughfares were of marble. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 145}

The Arabic accounts of the 3rd/9th to 7th/13th centuries, pieced together, give only a general description, and the article says they are quite insufficient for reconstructing the plan. It still judges it fairly certain that the city kept its overall layout through the Middle Ages: straight streets crossing at right angles, giving a chessboard pattern. The new rulers also used the riches of antiquity. In a monetary crisis early in the 2nd/8th century the governor of Egypt allowed a copper statue to be melted down for coin, and under the Sultan al-Nasir Muhammad ibn Qalawun lead from an old underground tunnel was used in building the canal of Alexandria. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 145}

## Houses, water and walls
A feature of medieval Alexandria taken over from antiquity was that houses were built on columns, rising above one another in as many as three tiers. The city thus made full use of its land and secured its water through a planned underground system of canals, cisterns and wells. It had, and still has, considerable winter rainfall, and in summer the waters of the Nile were directed and stored there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 145}

The city was well fortified, though the article says there is no precise information on the origin of the medieval defences. Reports say that [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn al-As]], meeting stiff resistance when besieging the city after the invasion of Manuel in 25/645, swore to destroy its walls after retaking it. The article doubts such reports: a soldier as prudent as Amr could hardly have wished to leave so important a frontier city without a wall. Later rulers are said to have built walls, among them the Abbasid caliph [[person/al-mutawakkil|al-Mutawakkil]], [[person/ahmad-ibn-tulun|Ibn Tulun]], [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Saladin]], [[person/baybars|Baybars]] and al-Ashraf Shaban, but since the Arabic word for build also meant restore, the article draws no firm conclusion. It prefers to think that these rulers added improvements gradually, and that the walls themselves were pre-Islamic. It notes that the walls attributed to al-Mutawakkil enclosed about half the area of the Hellenistic-Roman circuit. About a hundred towers were built along the walls in the Middle Ages and fitted out, cannon included, and a moat lay before them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 145}

## Harbours and the lighthouse
The medieval port had an eastern and a western harbour, separated by the causeway of seven stadia, the Heptastadium, that joined the island of Pharos to the mainland. On the north-eastern point of the island stood the Pharos, the great lighthouse begun in the time of Ptolemy Soter, which the article calls the prototype of all lighthouses and one of the wonders of the ancient world. It survived the Arab conquests by several centuries. Arab writers call it the manara, manar or fanar, and al-Balawi left a precise description of it dating from 561/1165. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 145} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 146}

After earthquakes destroyed the Pharos, the port was watched from a knoll, Kom al-Nadura, where the arrival and departure of ships were noted. In 882/1447, under Sultan [[person/qaytbay|Qaytbay]], a tower bearing his name was built on the ruins of the Pharos. A second lighthouse, begun under Qalawun or his son al-Nasir Muhammad and finished in 767/1365, was added for closer watch over the eastern harbour. The western harbour was closed by an iron chain. For reasons of security the eastern harbour was reserved for Christian shipping and for vessels from the dar al-harb, and the western one for Muslim ships. Entering the eastern harbour called for careful navigation past submerged rocks; the harbour authorities had pilots and launches to guide the great Frankish ships to their anchorage, and a wooden landing stage linked the anchorage to the shore. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 146}

## Gates and royal buildings
Only the 7th/13th-century traveller al-Abdari describes the city gates in detail. He admires their hewn stone, each doorpost, lintel and step a single block, and their leaves clad in fine iron. The city had four main gates. Bab al-Bahr led to the Heptastadium and the eastern harbour, and Bab Rashid, the Rosetta gate, was the eastern gate on the road to Rosetta and Fuwwa. The southern gate, Bab Sidra, was used by caravans from the Maghrib and the Egyptian hinterland. The fourth, Bab al-Akhdar, led to one of the three large cemeteries, was opened once a week on Fridays for visitors, and was surrounded by many places of pilgrimage and the graves of scholars and pious men. In the west of the city lay royal buildings such as the Dar al-Sultan, a magnificent complex going back to antiquity, the Dar al-Adl, the Dar al-Imara, the Qasr al-Silah and the Dar al-Tiraz. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 146}

## Population
The article says an estimate is hard. At the Arab conquest some 40,000, or by other reports 70,000, Jews lived in the city, and Ibn Abd al-Hakam gave figures for the Greeks that the article calls unreliable. Better figures come from later times. Benjamin of Tudela, in the 12th century, put the Jewish residents at 3,000, which E. Ashtor reads as counting only taxpayers, with a true total near 9,000. A traveller of 886/1481 found about sixty families, and another a few years later about twenty-five. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 146} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 147}

The whole population was put at 65,000 in the 13th century, then fell sharply in the middle of the 8th/14th century. Between 748/1347 and 751/1350 the plague killed one to two hundred a day, and seven hundred at its height; the Dar al-Tiraz and the Dar al-Wikala closed for lack of labour and trade, and markets and customs houses stopped. The city survived, and by 786/1384 visitors estimated 60,000 and 50,000. The article stresses that Alexandria's growth depended chiefly on its trade, and says it was returning to prosperity by 788/1386. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 147}

## Government, law and the courts
In the earlier Middle Ages Alexandria held a special position, as before the Arab invasion, but later its governors were appointed by the central administration. The city remained either a polis, a self-contained administrative area, or part of the western coastal area of Egypt, to which Libya sometimes belonged. The governor of Egypt came to reside there for part of the time. The treasury was usually run by a Muslim, though financial and civil administration was often left to Copts, and documents of the first century of Arab rule show Copts named as governors; the Christian Theodosius was so appointed by the caliph Yazid ibn Muawiya. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 147}

Under Ahmad ibn Tulun, from 256/870, Alexandria was independent, and the article says its special status lasted into the 3rd/10th century, reflecting the Roman-law position by which a polis lay outside the province. Under the [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimids]] its governor extended his authority over the province of Buhayra. The article rejects the view that the Crusades so diminished the city that a posting there signalled royal disfavour. After the Fatimids the city lost its independent position but regained commercial and strategic importance as a market for East and West. Until about the 3rd/10th century public meetings were occasionally held there on accepting government precepts or choosing a Coptic Patriarch, and the Coptic Patriarch later moved his seat to Cairo. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 147}

The governor was a military official, while the qadi was a civil official and a religious judge, sometimes called ra'is al-madina and, in crises, sometimes governing the city himself. In Mamluk times the governor held the rank of amir tablkhana; after the attack of Peter of Lusignan in 766/1365 the government paid the city more attention and gave its governor the rank held in Tripoli, Safad and Hama. The Maliki school prevailed, because of nearness to North Africa and the settling there of Maghribis and Spanish Muslims, and the chief qadi was almost always a Maliki, with occasional Shafii choices; the three schools, Maliki, Shafii and Hanafi, were all represented in the courts. Several sultans ensured legal protection of persons and goods for foreign merchants, and al-Nasir Muhammad is said to have backed a Frank against his own judge in 735/1334. The muhtasib supervised the markets, though his powers were in practice limited to smaller transactions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 147}

## Revenue and textiles
Alexandria was an important source of revenue for those in power, from the high duties paid by Karimi and foreign merchants, from nearly every transaction and shop, and from the mint. Money-changers, sailors, brokers, interpreters, auctioneers and donkey-drivers paid high taxes, and caravan leaders paid a tax outside the city. The article gives a few figures: according to the Qadi al-Fadil the annual duty revenue was 28,613 dinars; the sultan's diwan took some 50,000 dinars from arriving ships in 721/1321; and al-Maqrizi writes of one Frankish vessel that paid 40,000 dinars in 703/1303. The article thinks about 100,000 dinars a year from foreign trade no exaggeration. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 147} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 148}

The city was always a centre of cloth manufacture, with products reaching India, and looms for linen, silk, wool and cotton. The Dar al-Tiraz made brocade for the court, including official gifts and the yearly covering of the Kaaba. It was burnt in a Crusader raid in 767/1365 and restored. One report puts the looms at 14,000 at the start of the 9th/15th century and 800 by 837/1434, as cheaper cloth from Flanders and England was imported. In 788/1386 Egypt sent an Alexandrian weaver to Yemen, at the request of its government, to help expand silk production. The article says the medieval sources do not yet show that glass was made there, that excavations at Kom al-Dikka show imported ceramics, and that the state monopoly on alum, exported through the city, and on natron, was important to its economy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 148}

## Trade and markets
The article says it is hard to establish the city's international trade before the 5th/12th century, but that in that century it is plain: Benjamin of Tudela names 28 Christian cities or countries with representatives there, and William of Tyre calls it the emporium of East and West. Ibn Battuta wrote that he had seen no harbour to equal it save a few ports in India, in the land of the Turks and in China. The article says neither the Crusader states nor the Mongol empire affected its place in world trade. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 148}

The article describes close control at the gates and harbour: travellers such as Ibn Jubayr were registered and their goods examined, foreign Christians were identified by their consul, and merchants lodged in the funduk assigned to their nation. These funduks were run by officials or consuls of friendly states under Egyptian protection, and the foreign merchants enjoyed extraterritorial privileges. [[polity/republic-of-venice|Venice]], the leading commercial power, gained a second funduk in the 13th century. Muslims came from Spain, North Africa, Mesopotamia, Syria and the neighbourhood of India. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 148}

The customs house, with thirty storerooms, also served for public auctions. The Dar al-Wikala, a public administration for the head of state, sold imported goods to Muslim entrepreneurs. Each trade had its own market, among them the Suq al-Attarin, the pepper and spice market and probably the centre of the Karimi merchants, and the Suq al-Murjaniya, one of the most important coral markets of the Mediterranean, where worked coral sold for three or four times the price of raw. Other markets dealt in linen, slaves, grain, candles, wax, wood and second-hand goods, and the Suq al-Ajam served Iranian merchants dealing in silk. Women took part in commerce too, and one was known as Sitt al-Tujjar. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 148} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 149}

State trade, the Matjar al-Sultani, was already active under the Ayyubids and Mamluks, importing slaves, wood and metals and offering monopoly goods such as alum, natron, corn and flax, and later spices, sugar and soap. The main line was pepper, and before the rounding of the Cape of Good Hope Venetian vessels loaded about 1,500,000 pounds a year. In 737/1337 Alexandrian money-changers lent the sultan 10,000 dinars. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 149}

## The revolt of 727/1326
The article holds that in Alexandria the state ruled in its own interest rather than that of the community, and offers as proof the revolt of 727/1326, caused by heavy taxes and put down with great severity. Karimi merchants, silk dealers and producers were fined and had goods confiscated, in all some 260,000 dinars, and the leaders were punished. It adds that where the ruler was weak and the army thin, soldiers and mercenaries sold protection to merchants, and that the city came to concentrate on long-distance trade. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 149}

## Religious and scholarly life
The article divides the religious and scholarly life of the city into two periods. In the early Middle Ages Christian and Jewish elements were supreme: after the Arab conquest the Greek Patriarch had to leave and the Coptic Patriarch entered, and the Copts supported the Arabs against Byzantium. The article says this period prepared the ground for Islamic science, with a centre for translating works of antiquity. In purely Islamic learning, which al-Sakhawi said began with [[person/al-silafi|al-Silafi]], Alexandria depended on Fustat and Cairo, and students travelled there for the Quran and hadith. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 150}

The vizier Ridwan ibn al-Walakhshi founded a Sunni madrasa there in 531/1137, where the jurist Awf taught hadith. Not long afterwards al-Adil ibn al-Sallar founded a second for al-Silafi, with whom Saladin later studied the Muwatta of Imam Malik. Saladin also had a school, hospital and hostel built for Maghribis, with free lodging, teachers, medical care and financial help. Sources often mention ribats, and the literature on the merits of Alexandria stresses its strategic importance to Islam as a border harbour. Wealthy merchants also built mosques and schools; the article names Abd al-Latif ibn Rushayd al-Takriti, died 714/1314, whose hadith and Shafii school is today the Masjid Abi Ali, and the Karimi family of Kuwayk, said to be able to pay for a mosque or school from a day's profit. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 150}

The article lists many scholars and Sufis of the city, among them Ibn Ata Allah, al-Shatibi and [[person/al-busiri|al-Busiri]], and names the Masjid al-Umari, formerly the Church of Theonas, and the Masjid al-Juyushi or al-Attarin, formerly the Church of St Athanasius. It also names the Ribat al-Wasiti, east of the mosque of [[person/abu-al-abbas-al-mursi|Abu al-Abbas al-Mursi]], and the Ribat Siwar, the ribat of al-Hakkari near the Rosetta gate, and a khanqah built toward the end of the 7th/13th century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 150}

## Alexandria in al-Maqrizi
Al-Maqrizi says that Alexander son of Philip the Macedonian renewed the city, and that the seat of the kingdom moved to it from Memphis, so that it became the seat of rule in Egypt. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.446}

He quotes Abu al-Salt Umayya ibn Abd al-Aziz al-Andalusi, who says that it was "the house of learning and seat of wisdom" until the Muslims took it in the days of Umar ibn al-Khattab. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.422}

When Amr ibn al-As came with the Muslim armies and took Alexandria, the seat of the kingdom moved from Alexandria to Fustat in Egypt, and Fustat became the seat of rule of Egypt after Alexandria. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.446}

## Other geographers and historians
Gollius, citing Ibn Abd al-Hakam and Abu al-Fida, reports that Alexandria was a triple city, each quarter with its own wall, and that its streets were ordered like a chessboard. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 402} He also reports that the Romans resisted Amr ibn al-As for nine months and retook the city after a time. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 403}

He adds that the Franks besieged Alexandria in 562 AH and that Saladin and his uncle Asad al-Din Shirkuh repelled them after four months. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 403}

## For Scholars
Alexandria is an Egyptian port on the Mediterranean, Egypt's seat of kingship before Fustat, and it remained the home of learning and seat of wisdom until the Muslims opened it. Al-Maqrizi reports that Alexander son of Philip the Macedonian renewed it, the royal seat moving from Memphis to make it the seat of kingship in Egypt; he quotes Abu al-Salt Umayya ibn Abd al-Aziz al-Andalusi that it 'was the home of learning and seat of wisdom' until the Muslims opened it in the days of Umar ibn al-Khattab — when Amr ibn al-As arrived with the Muslim armies and opened it the royal seat moved to Fustat, which became the seat of kingship in Egypt.
