## Overview
France is a country in Western Europe whose capital is Paris, according to the place card. This page gathers what four books read for the museum say about it: al-Qazwini's *Athar al-Bilad wa Akhbar al-Ibad* (he died in 682 AH), which has an entry for "Afranja" with Paris as its capital; Ali Muhammad al-Salabi's *al-Dawla al-Uthmaniyya* (2001), which shows France as an ally of Sultan Sulayman, then as the invader of Egypt, then as a haven for Arab and Turkish opposition societies; the Arabic *Hadir al-Alam al-Islami* with the notes of Shakib Arslan, who describes French policy in Algeria, Morocco and Syria in the early 1930s; and Hasan Ibrahim Hasan's *Tarikh al-Islam*, which tells of Muslim campaigns in Gaul centuries before the modern state. These are twentieth- and twenty-first-century authors, and each judgement below is attributed to its author. {cite:athar-al-bilad-qazwini p.575} {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.203} {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.166} {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim 1 p.261}

## Afranja in al-Qazwini's Athar al-Bilad
Zakariyya al-Qazwini, who died in 682 AH, has an entry "Afranja": a wide land at the far western end of the sixth clime. Citing al-Mas'udi he says it holds about a hundred and fifty cities, that its capital is Paris, and that it is a month's journey long and wider than that. He calls it infertile, with poor ploughland, little vine and no trees; its people are the Ifranj, Christians and men of war by land and sea, patient in battle and never fleeing, since death is easier for them than defeat, and they live by trade and crafts. {cite:athar-al-bilad-qazwini p.575} This is a thirteenth-century Arab geographer's description of the land of the Franks, and the page is attached to France because the entry names Paris as its capital. See [[place/paris|Paris]].

## Muslims in Gaul
Hasan Ibrahim writes that Musa ibn Nusayr did not carry out his plan of conquering southern Europe, so the conquests stopped at the Pyrenees until Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz appointed al-Samh ibn Malik al-Khawlani over al-Andalus. Al-Samh crossed the Pyrenees, advanced on Septimania and Provence, raided Aquitaine and besieged Toulouse; Eudes, duke of Aquitaine, met him with a large army, al-Samh and most of his men were killed, and Abd al-Rahman ibn Abd Allah al-Ghafiqi took command and withdrew to [[place/narbonne|Narbonne]]. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim 1 p.261}

In 103 AH Anbasa ibn Suhaym al-Kalbi took Septimania, reached the Rhone basin and Burgundy as far as Lyon, and was killed on the way back in 107 AH; the advance then stopped for four years because of unrest in al-Andalus, until [[person/abd-al-rahman-al-ghafiqi|Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi]] took office and, with eight thousand men, seized the duchy of Aquitaine. By the author's account the duke of Aquitaine sought help from the Franks, and Charles Martel gathered a huge army and met the Muslims near Poitiers at the place called Balat al-Shuhada, the battle known as the battle of Tours. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim 1 p.261} See [[event/balat-al-shuhada|the battle of Balat al-Shuhada]].

He reports that after eight days of skirmishing the fighting lasted until sunset; the Muslims attacked again the next day and victory was nearly theirs, until a rumour that the enemy had seized their booty sent the troops hurrying to protect it, the ranks fell into disorder, Abd al-Rahman was struck by an arrow and died, and the army withdrew in the dark; Charles did not pursue for fear of a trap. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim 1 p.261} {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim 1 p.262}

The author judges the battle of Tours one of the decisive battles of history, says the Arabs did not again try to take the land of the Franks and fell back to al-Andalus, keeping only Septimania beyond the Pyrenees, and notes that Charles won the title "the Hammer" (Martel). This is his assessment; he also writes that the quarrels between Arabs and Berbers, and then between Mudar and Yaman, grew so bitter that some Muslims called on the Franks against their co-religionists. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim 1 p.262}

## France and the Ottoman state
Al-Salabi describes the start of relations. King Francis I had been captured at Pavia in 1525, and his mother, the regent, began negotiations with the Ottoman state by sending an envoy with letters from herself and the captive king asking for an attack on the Habsburgs and his release. After he was freed under the Treaty of Madrid in 1526, the king sent his secretary Jean de la Forest in 941 AH (1535) to [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Sultan Sulayman]] to conclude an alliance in the form of a treaty later called the Franco-Ottoman Capitulations. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.203} See [[person/francis-i-france|Francis I]] and [[event/capitulations-941|the Capitulations]].

He lists among its terms free movement and navigation in armed and unarmed ships; the right of French subjects to trade in all parts of the empire; customs paid once; the same taxes as Ottoman subjects; consular representation with immunity; the consul's jurisdiction in civil and criminal cases between Frenchmen; freedom of worship; and a ban on enslaving the king's subjects. Among its results he names closer co-operation between the two fleets and Ottoman attacks on the coasts of the kingdom of Naples, which belonged to Charles V; in 1543 the two fleets together attacked Nice, held by the Duke of Savoy, Charles V's ally. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.203} {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.204}

He holds that France gained militarily, economically and politically: it reached the eastern trade without the Portuguese monopoly that followed the Cape route, and won the right to protect the subjects of other Western states under its flag. Al-Salabi judges that the Ottomans did not benefit as France did and calls the treaty "the first wedge" in the state's coffin; he adds that in its last years the Christian powers of Europe intervened in its affairs under the Capitulations and the protection of Christians, above all in Syria. These are his judgements. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.204} {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.205}

He says the Ottoman state entered a formal alliance with France in 943 AH (1536) in answer to a Spanish counter-attack on Tunis, and that [[person/khayr-al-din-barbarossa|Khayr al-Din Barbarossa]] made [[place/marseille|Marseille]] the base of his fleet and sold his booty there. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.221} {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.231} He adds that the French fleet commander placed his forces under Khayr al-Din, who attacked Nice, drove out the Duke of Savoy and returned it to the French king, then settled his fleet at Toulon, which most of its inhabitants had left on the king's order, and stayed there until 1544; hostile propaganda spread across Europe as a result. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.232} On the failure of [[person/charles-v|Charles V]]'s expedition against Algiers he writes that the French rejoiced because the fall of Algiers would, in his view, have meant the fall of France, and that Francis I hastened to conclude treaties with the sultan. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.228}

## From Ottoman reform to the Egyptian campaign
Al-Salabi reports that Sultan Ahmad sent Ottoman ambassadors to Vienna and Paris "for the first time" and envoys to France to see its factories, and that Sultan Mahmud I engaged a French military adviser, Count Alexandre de Bonneval, to revive the artillery. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.312}

He then tells the French campaign in Egypt. Britain sent Admiral Nelson, who destroyed the French fleet in Abu Qir bay and cut off reinforcements; Bonaparte followed what al-Salabi calls a "national Islamic policy" of showing respect for religion and custom, trying to detach the Egyptians from the caliphate and forming a native government of notables, and al-Salabi judges that it failed, citing the resistance. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.332} See [[person/napoleon-bonaparte|Napoleon Bonaparte]] and [[event/french-campaign-egypt-1213|the French campaign in Egypt]].

On the first revolt of [[place/cairo|Cairo]], 21 to 23 October 1798 as the book gives it, he reports from al-Jabarti and from French accounts that soldiers entered al-Azhar on horseback and that the French afterwards took revenge on Cairo and its suburbs. He adds that the Porte declared war on France and ordered the French chargé d'affaires and French subjects jailed, and allied with Russia on 25 December 1798 and with Britain on 5 January 1799. Bonaparte's campaign in Syria, February to June 1799, failed to take Acre, and he later left Egypt in secret, leaving command to Kleber. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.333} The Convention of al-Arish of 24 January 1800 provided for French evacuation; the British government blocked it for fear the besieged army would return to European battlefields. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.334} The book lists the reasons the French left: the destruction of their fleet, British naval control, the Ottoman state joining their enemies, division within the expedition and Egyptian resistance. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.336}

## France in the nineteenth century
Al-Salabi writes that [[person/muhammad-ali-pasha|Muhammad Ali]] sent [[person/rifaa-al-tahtawi|Rifa'a al-Tahtawi]] to France for five years, 1826 to 1831, and that after his return Tahtawi spread national and social ideas he had met in France; this is the author's assessment, and he sees in them a distance from Islamic thought. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.349} On the journey Prince Abd al-Hamid made to Europe with Sultan Abd al-Aziz from 21 June to 7 August 1867, the delegation visited France, England, Belgium and Austria-Hungary; the book names Napoleon III among the monarchs of the time and says the prince concluded that France was "a country of pleasure". {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.400} See [[person/abd-al-hamid-ii-uthmani|Abd al-Hamid II]].

He writes that France occupied Tunis in 1299 AH (1881) "in return for" Britain's occupation of Cyprus, and that Britain occupied Egypt in 1300 AH (1882), declaring the occupation temporary. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.416} See [[place/tunisia|Tunisia]] and [[event/french-conquest-of-tunisia|the French conquest of Tunisia]].

## Algeria, Morocco and Syria in Hadir al-Alam al-Islami
In Arslan's notes Algeria had been an Ottoman province since 1516; European states bombarded Algiers in the seventeenth century to curb its fleet; in 1827 the dey Husayn quarrelled with the French consul Deval and struck him with a fan; France bombarded the coast and took [[place/algiers|Algiers]] in 1830 under Charles X. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.166} {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.167}

He adds that a body called the "African Committee" debated keeping only the coast against full conquest; conquest was preferred in 1834 and a governor-general appointed; the French took Oran, Mostaganem, Annaba and Bejaia, took Constantine on a second campaign in 1837 and reached Biskra in 1844. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.168} Resistance in the west lasted longer under [[person/abd-al-qadir-al-jazairi|Amir Abd al-Qadir]], who was given the oath of allegiance on 21 November 1832, took [[place/mascara|Mascara]] as capital, and signed the Desmichels treaty with the French on 26 February 1834 and the Tafna treaty on 30 May 1837. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.168} {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.169}

On Morocco Arslan writes that the French had occupied Fashoda in the Egyptian Sudan and Britain objected, and that the two states then made a secret agreement, which he dates to 1902, by which France ceded Egypt, the Egyptian Sudan and Uganda to Britain and Britain ceded Morocco and North Africa to France; he calls this "among the most appalling things history has recorded". He goes on to the Algeciras conference, the French entry into Wajda and Casablanca, and Sultan Abd al-Hafiz's acceptance of the French protectorate after long refusal. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.115} He adds that Germany objected and the German emperor came to Tangier. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.116} See [[place/morocco|Morocco]].

On the "Berber decree" of 16 May 1930, Arslan calls it a policy of recklessness whose aim, as he puts it, was to detach the Berbers from Islam. He reports that the fifth section of the League for the Rights of Man in Paris asked its central committee to approach the French government in Morocco to release four exiles and to protest against the policy of assimilation, and he hopes the French government will withdraw it; this is his opinion. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 p.337} He adds that the French foreign ministry consulted scholars, among them the Dutch orientalist Snouck Hurgronje. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 p.338}

Arslan also accuses French officials in Syria, since their occupation of it in 1918, of stirring hatred between Muslims and Christians and between Christians and Druze, and says they did the same between Arabs and Berbers in Algeria; this is the accusation of the author of a note. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.340} See [[place/syria|Syria]] and [[place/lebanon|Lebanon]].

## A refuge for Arab and Turkish societies
Al-Salabi reports that Namik Kemal, Ziya and Ali Suavi joined the Egyptian prince Mustafa Fazil in France and formed the "New Ottomans"; that Ahmed Riza went to Paris in 1889 for its international exhibition and declared he would not return, staying about six years until he issued his newspaper in 1895; and that the military and civilian wings of the opposition agreed in Paris to act against the sultan. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.451} {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.454} {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.455} In 1329 AH "al-Arabiyya al-Fatat" was formed in Paris by students studying there, and in 1331 AH the Reform Society in Beirut and the Lebanese Renaissance Society abroad sent the French government a joint letter asking it to occupy Syria and Lebanon, by the book's account. The Arab Congress was held in Paris in 1332 AH (1913); the French arranged the venue, pinned great hopes on it and published its resolutions. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.465} {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.466} See [[place/paris|Paris]] and [[event/arab-congress-paris-1332|the Arab Congress in Paris]].

## Note on the sources
The three sources treat France only through its dealings with the Muslim world, so they do not replace a history of France itself. Al-Salabi's page numbers are the printed ones; the numbers given for Hadir al-Alam al-Islami are the printed page numbers read from each page's running head. Arslan's date of 1902 for the secret Anglo-French agreement has not been checked here.
