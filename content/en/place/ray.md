## Overview
Ray was a great and ancient city of the Jibal, east of Qazvin, near present-day Tehran. Yaqut gives it a long article in the Mu'jam al-buldan, collecting how its name is pronounced and explained, how it is described, how it was conquered and rebuilt in the Abbasid period, and what he saw of its ruin himself. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.116}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut vocalises the name with a fatha on the first letter and a doubled second, and says that if it is Arabic it comes from the verb "rawaytu". He calls it a famous city, one of the mother cities of the land, rich in fruit and good things, a halting place for pilgrims on the main road and the chief town of the Jibal. He gives the distance to Nishapur as 160 farsakhs and to Qazvin as 27. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.116}

For the origin of the name he passes on several reports. A Persian history says that Kaykhusraw carried the wheel built by Kaykavus as far as the site, where the people said he had come "bi-ri", ri being the Persian word for the wheel, and he ordered a city to be built there. He cites al-Imrani that Firuz son of Yazdegerd built it and named it Ram Firuz, and Ibn al-Kalbi that it was named after a man called Ray of the Banu Shaylan. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.116} {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.117}

Yaqut describes the city as he saw it: built of ornamented brick painted blue, with a bare mountain above it where nothing grows. He says he passed through its ruins in 617 AH, fleeing the Tatars, and saw walls standing and pulpits still in place. He reports an account from one of its educated men about why it fell: the people were Shafi'is, Hanafis and Shi'a, factional feeling and wars between them continued until the groups had destroyed one another and only the Shafi'i quarter, the smallest, remained, while surviving followers of the other two schools hid their allegiance and built their houses underground. Yaqut gives this as what he was told and does not verify it. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.117}

He quotes al-Istakhri that it was the most flourishing city of the East after Baghdad, one and a half farsakhs in each direction, built mostly of wood and clay, with large villages each yielding thousands of men, and districts including Qasran, Bahzan and Damavand. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.117}

On the conquest he reports that Umar ibn al-Khattab wrote to Ammar ibn Yasir, his governor at Kufa, to send Urwa ibn Zayd al-Khayl al-Ta'i to Ray and Dastaba with eight thousand men; the Daylam rallied against him and he defeated them, in 20 AH or, by another report, 19. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.117} {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.118}

He records that al-Mahdi, on reaching Ray in the caliphate of al-Mansur, built the city in which people then lived, with a surrounding ditch and a congregational mosque, through Ammar ibn Abi al-Khasib, finished in 158, and named it al-Muhammadiyya. He adds that the city's tribute was twelve million dirhams until al-Ma'mun passed through on his return from Khurasan; the people complained to him and he remitted two million. Among older sayings he quotes al-Asma'i that Ray was the bride of the world and a trading place for people. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.118}
