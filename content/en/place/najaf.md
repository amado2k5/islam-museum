## Overview
Najaf is a city in Iraq, where the shrine of Ali ibn Abi Talib is. Ibn Battuta mentioned it on his way to Iraq. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958} The coordinates recorded for it come from GeoNames, for a modern town of the same name {cite:geonames-gazetteer}. Matching it to the ancient site needs an additional source.

## In the iDAI.gazetteer
The iDAI.gazetteer of the German Archaeological Institute records this place as no. 2104369, of type populated place, with the preferred name «Nadschaf», placed in its hierarchy under «Irak». Recorded names include Najaf, Meshed ’Ali and Nedjef. {cite:idai-gazetteer 2104369}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut places Najaf behind Kufa, where two springs in the Furat branch, one called al-Rabd and the other al-Najaf, water twenty thousand palm trees. He describes al-Najaf as a dyke that keeps the water from rising over Kufa and its cemeteries. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.271}

Near it lies the grave of Ali ibn Abi Talib, which the poets often mention. Yaqut quotes verses by al-Hammani al-Kufi on the Najaf and its land, and verses by Ishaq al-Mawsili in praise of al-Wathiq that describe the place. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.271}

## In The Oxford Dictionary of Islam
The Oxford Dictionary of Islam counts Najaf among the two holiest cities of Iraq, the other being Karbala. It says the city was reputedly founded in 791 by Harun al-Rashid, caliph of the Abbasids, and describes it as a Shii religious centre six miles to the west of Kufa and south of Baghdad, where the tomb of Ali ibn Abi Talib, the first imam of the Shiis, stands. According to the Dictionary, Kufa kept its importance as the focus of Shii activity down to the fifteenth century, after which Najaf took over, and around the shrine arose schools, hospices, libraries and Sufi convents. Qom took over from Najaf as the centre of Shii learning in the late nineteenth century, a shift the Dictionary says was undone by the rise of Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr (died 1980) and Ayatollah Khomeini (died 1989). {cite:oxford-dictionary-of-islam e1704}
