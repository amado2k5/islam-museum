## Overview
The Punjab is a region in the north-west of the Indian subcontinent, described on the place card today as a province of Pakistan. In the sources at hand it is a country that routes pass through from India to Kabul and Ghazna: Hasan Ibrahim Hasan says caravans of the Punjab carried large quantities of goods across the Afghan plateau to Kabul and Ghazna and from there to Khurasan and Bukhara. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim 2 p.260} Arslan calls it "the forefront of India", which Sabuktigin raided. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.287} Its name appears in Ibn Battuta's Rihla as "Panj Ab", which means the five waters. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.2} This page rests on older Arabic accounts and on Arslan's twentieth-century notes, and describes the region through what they wrote about it.

## "Panj Ab" in Ibn Battuta's Rihla
Ibn Battuta relates that at the opening of Muharram 734 AH he reached the valley of the Indus known as Panj Ab, "the five waters", one of the greatest valleys in the world, which floods in the heat so that the people of the country sow on its flood as the people of Egypt do on the Nile; this was the first province of the sultan Muhammad Shah, king of India. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.2} He says the news-writers reported their arrival to Qutb al-Mulk, amir of Multan, that the chief of the amirs of Sind at that time was a mamluk of the sultan named Sartiz, and that Sivistan and Multan are ten days apart. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.2} He says the newcomer stays in Multan, the capital of the lands of Sind, until the sultan's order about his arrival and his hospitality comes. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.3} He adds that Imad al-Mulk Sartiz lived in Multan and gathered troops from it against rebels at Sivistan. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.6} See [[place/multan|Multan]], [[place/sind|Sind]] and [[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]]. This is a traveller's description of the Delhi sultanate in the eighth Hijri century, not a delimitation of the Punjab.

## Ghazna and the Punjab
Hasan Ibrahim Hasan relates that Mahmud of Ghazna added the lands of the Punjab to his kingdom, that according to Browne he raided India twelve times, that his campaigns between 392 and 415 AH took on the character of jihad, and that Jaipal, king of the Punjab, who had fought Mahmud's father Sabuktigin, was still a considerable power. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim 3 pp.94-96} He says that in 392 AH Mahmud raided northern India and defeated and captured Jaipal and then released him; went on to Waihind and then to the region of Multan, which he calls a famous pilgrimage centre for Indians in the south of the Punjab; took Bhatiya in 395 AH and moved on Multan in 396 AH. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim 3 p.97} In 398 AH Mahmud crossed the Indus and met at Waihind in the Punjab a great army made up of the forces of Indian rajas, and then besieged the fortress of Bhimnagar. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim 3 pp.97-98} Anandpal undertook to pay yearly tribute and not to bar Mahmud's passage through his land, and Mahmud attacked Multan in 400 and 401 AH. {cite:tarikh-islam-hasan-ibrahim 3 p.98} See [[person/mahmud-ghaznawi|Mahmud of Ghazna]], [[person/sabuktigin|Sabuktigin]], [[polity/ghaznavid-empire|the Ghaznavid state]] and [[polity/hindu-shahi|the Hindu Shahis]].

Arslan relates that Sabuktigin raided the Punjab, the forefront of India, until the rajas of Lahore, Delhi, Ajmer, Kannauj and Kalinjar mustered and he defeated them; that Mahmud began his campaigns with the conquest of Multan in 1005, then beat the raja of Lahore and his allies on the plain of Patandah, and that the Abbasid caliph al-Qadir bi-llah gave him the title Yamin al-Dawla. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 pp.287-288} He adds that after Mahmud's death the Seljuks appeared in Khurasan, the sons of Sabuktigin moved from Ghazna to Lahore in India, and the Ghurids then overcame them. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.290} See [[place/lahore|Lahore]] and [[place/kabul|Kabul]].

## The Ghurids, the Mongols and the sultans of Delhi
Arslan quotes René Grousset that the Ghurids inherited the Ghaznavids' realm and so advanced into India, and quotes Subh al-A'sha that Muhammad ibn al-Husayn al-Ghuri took Lahore in 547 AH, correcting that Ibn al-Athir gives 579 AH, which he thinks sounder, and that he granted his mamluk Qutb al-Din Aybak the city of Delhi. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.291} He adds from Grousset that the seat of the sultanate was moved from Lahore to Delhi because Lahore secured only the Punjab while Delhi invited the rule of the Ganges plains and the Deccan, and that the Ghaznavids at Lahore were like the rajas of one province of India, whereas the Ghurids at Delhi and their heirs were sultans of all India. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.292} In Iltutmish's time, he says, the amir Jalal al-Din Mankubirti the Khwarazmian sought refuge with him; Iltutmish drove back the Mongol raid on the Punjab and did not venture to restore Jalal al-Din's realm. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.292} In 1297 a hundred thousand Mongols from beyond the river marched on the Punjab and Ala al-Din Khalji met and beat them near Lahore. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.293} See [[polity/delhi-sultanate|the Delhi Sultanate]] and [[place/delhi|Delhi]].

## The Punjab in Mughal times and after
For the Mughal age Arslan says Babur raided the Punjab in 1525, Humayun came back down to it with fifteen thousand horsemen, and Akbar was crowned in the Punjab and was chased back to it by Hemu. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 pp.296-299} Of the Sikhs he says, in Arslan's account, that they were a sect built on the teaching of Nanak, born in 1469, a pupil of the reformer Kabir, who sought to cleanse the religion of the Indians of idol worship, many gods and caste and bring it nearer to monotheism; that they rose against the Mughal sultans and fought long wars with them, and that they took Lahore and the Punjab in 1764 once Delhi had weakened. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 pp.311-312} See [[person/guru-nanak|Guru Nanak]] and [[polity/mughal-empire|the Mughal Empire]].

## In the early twentieth century
Arslan notes that Amir Amanullah Khan of Afghanistan seized the moment when disorder spread through the Indian Punjab and declared war on Britain. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.268} In counting India's Muslims in his own day he says Muslims are more than half the population of the Punjab and 93 per cent of that of the North-West Frontier. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.317} These are Arslan's figures for his time and do not describe the present.
