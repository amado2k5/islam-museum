## Overview
Buda is the western bank of present-day Budapest, and, the place card says, a former independent city in the kingdom of Hungary. In Arabic books on Ottoman history it appears as a city named alongside the fortress of Neuhäusel and the city of Pest: the armies of Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent enter it in 1526 CE, and later, in Ali al-Salabi's account, it passes to Austria in the years around 1097 and 1099 AH. The page rests on two books: al-Salabi's al-Dawla al-Uthmaniyya, and Hadir al-Alam al-Islami with Shakib Arslan's notes. See [[place/budapest|Budapest]] and [[place/hungary|Hungary]].

## Suleiman enters Buda in 1526
On the fighting with Hungary and the siege of Vienna, al-Salabi says that the king of Hungary resolved to cancel the undertakings his predecessors had given the Ottoman sultans, and went as far as killing Sultan Suleiman's envoy, who was demanding the yearly tribute laid on Hungary. Suleiman answered in 1521 CE with a great campaign, and the battles went on until the Turks won their great victory at Mohács in 1526; [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Suleiman the Magnificent]] entered Buda on 11 September of that year. Hungarian resistance went on all the same, he adds, and the sultan kept up the pressure until his armies reached the walls of [[place/vienna|Vienna]], capital of the Holy Roman Empire, in 1529; but the length of his lines of communication, and Charles V's turn from fighting Francis I to making terms with him in order to concentrate on the Ottomans, made taking Vienna impossible for Suleiman, who withdrew, and the struggle for control of the kingdom lasted until his death. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.202} See [[place/mohacs|Mohács]].

## Buda and Pest in the Ottoman decline
Al-Salabi mentions the grand vizier Mehmed Köprülü, who died in 1072 AH, 1661 CE, and who restored the state's standing, and says his son Ahmad Köprülü followed his course, refused peace with Austria and Venice, led an army against Austria and took the fortress of Neuhäusel, east of Vienna, on 25 Safar 1074 AH, 28 September 1663 CE. He then says that with Ahmad Köprülü's death the Ottoman order weakened and Austria attacked Hungary and seized the fortress of Neuhäusel and the cities of Pest and Buda; the king of Poland raided Moldavia, and Venetian ships raided the coasts of the Morea and Greece and occupied Athens and Corinth in 1097 AH. The scholars and statesmen, he writes, agreed to depose Sultan Mehmed IV, who was deposed in 1099 AH and succeeded by his brother Sulayman II; the decline went on in the latter's reign and Austria seized positions and towns, [[place/belgrade|Belgrade]] among them, in 1099 AH. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.308}

Al-Salabi gives no separate year for the loss of Buda and Pest. He places it in a sentence linked to the events of 1097 and 1099 AH, in a summary history of the state, not a detailed account of a siege of Buda.

## Budapest in Hadir al-Alam al-Islami
In a chapter titled "European fanaticism or Islamic fanaticism" the book follows the popes' attempts to unite the Christian kings against the Turks in the days of Sultan Selim I and Suleiman, and says that the Turks took Budapest and all that remained of Hungary, which stayed in their hands for a hundred and forty-seven years, besieged Vienna so that Charles V had to seek an alliance with the king of France out of fear of the Turks, and that the Turks made peace with Austria in 1533. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 p.230} The book here uses the modern name Budapest, the name of the two cities after they united, where al-Salabi says Buda and Pest. See [[place/ottoman-empire|The Ottoman Empire]].

In an exchange Arslan recounts with two men from English embassies who came to him in Berlin, arguing that Eastern peoples should not be denied independence on the ground of better administration elsewhere, he gives as an example that the Romanians who were in Hungary were more advanced than the Romanians of Romania itself, and that the government of Budapest was many times better than that of [[place/bucharest|Bucharest]]. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.67} This is a political argument from the early twentieth century, put in Arslan's name; it is not a description of the city.

## What these sources do not say
The books read give no account of Buda's buildings, baths, mosques or people in Ottoman times, and do not give the year it left Ottoman hands, so the page keeps to what is above. The place card describes Buda as the western part of Budapest and a former independent city, on Wikidata's authority.
