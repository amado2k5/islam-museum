## Overview
Jerusalem, in Arabic al-Quds, is a city holy to Jews, Christians and Muslims. In Islamic tradition it was the first of the two qiblas and the end of the Night Journey, and it was handed over to the Muslims in 637 CE. The Encyclopaedia of Islam article on al-Quds divides its Islamic history into three periods. In the first six hundred years the city was contested between Islam and Christianity and between many Muslim princes and factions; the period opens with a bloodless and poorly recorded surrender, is marked by the building of the [[building/dome-of-the-rock|Dome of the Rock]], reaches its climax in the Crusades, and closes with the devastations of the first half of the 7th/13th century. The next six hundred years were comparatively uneventful, with Jerusalem living as an out-of-the-way provincial town that nevertheless profited from its holy character under the Mamluks and Ottomans. The modern period begins with the conquest by Ibrahim Pasha in the early 1830s. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 360}

The article stresses that Jerusalem lived on the care lavished on it from outside rather than on any political, administrative or cultural weight of its own. For that reason it says the city's history has to be told as a series of highlights rather than a continuous account. It also notes that [[person/mujir-al-din-al-ulaymi|Mujir al-Din al-Ulaymi]], who wrote his history of Jerusalem and Hebron in 900/1494-5, observed that before him little useful had been written beyond praises of the city, accounts of Umar's conquest and stories about the Dome of the Rock. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 360}

## Names
In early Islam the city's full name was Iliya, city of the Temple; in practice Iliya, from the Roman Aelia, or more commonly Bayt al-Maqdis was used. Bayt al-Maqdis comes from an Aramaic word for the Temple, and was soon transferred to the city, while the Temple area came to be called al-Haram. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 360} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 361}

The now common name al-Quds is absent from [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]], [[person/al-baladhuri|al-Baladhuri]] and other early classics, appears once in a text of 355/966 and often in [[person/al-muqaddasi|al-Muqaddasi]] around 375/985, and was reported by [[person/nasir-khusraw|Nasir-i Khusraw]] in 439/1047 as the local people's usage. The article derives it from the Aramaic kudsha, understood as "city of the sanctuary" rather than "city of holiness", and notes that Karaite writers in early tenth-century Jerusalem used al-Quds for the Temple area. It also records that al-Suyuti listed seventeen Arabic names for the city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 361}

## Jerusalem in the Qur'an and the first qibla
The article states that Jerusalem is not named in the Qur'an. It says the city was nevertheless known to the Prophet, and that the opening of the sura of the Night Journey is taken by the prevailing Muslim tradition to refer to the sanctuary of Jerusalem. It records the contrary arguments: that no building stood on the Temple site at the time, that the Holy Land is called the "nearest" rather than the farthest, and that the verse reads as an account of an ascension to a heavenly sanctuary. The author concludes that by the time the verses were combined, the identification with the Jerusalem Temple was already dominant. This is the article's own reasoning, and the tradition itself rests on the [[event/isra-and-miraj|Night Journey and the Ascension]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 361}

On the [[concept/qibla|qibla]], the article holds that Islamic tradition naming Jerusalem as the first direction of prayer is genuine, since a holy city of the monotheists was the natural original orientation. It says no political motive such as winning over the Jews should be assumed, because Muslims at Medina simply prayed toward the direction of the People of the Book, and that the change to [[place/mecca|Mecca]] followed naturally once Islam became a separate religion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 361}

## The conquest
After the battle of Ajnadayn in 13/634 southern Palestine lay open, and the patriarch Sophronius lamented the raids in his sermons of that winter. Jerusalem fell early in 638, at the end of 16 or the start of 17 AH, after the battle of the [[event/battle-of-yarmuk|Yarmuk]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 361}

The article sorts the accounts of the surrender into groups. The oldest and, in its judgement, most trustworthy says that the capitulation was arranged with a little-known tribal commander, Khalid ibn Thabit al-Fahmi, on condition that the open country belonged to the Muslims and the city would be left alone while its people paid tribute. No treaty text is mentioned in that version. Later versions give a short treaty, then add conditions, including in some Christian authors a clause that no Jew should live in the city. The article traces that clause in al-Tabari to Sayf ibn Umar, whom it calls unreliable, and says it was invoked in later times of tension. A still later version adds conditions of the legendary Covenant of Umar. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 361} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362}

## Early Muslim presence
The article calls the stories of a visit by the caliph [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] legends. In one, the Jewish convert [[person/kab-al-ahbar|Ka'b al-Ahbar]] suggests that Umar pray behind the Rock so that both qiblas would face him, and Umar refuses because Muslims turn toward the Kaaba alone; in Christian versions the caliph declines to pray in a church so as to prevent later claims on it. Because the terms safeguarded the churches, the article reasons that the largely unoccupied Temple area probably served for Muslim prayer from the start, on Umar's order. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362}

Early settlers were mostly Medinese, among them a nephew of Hassan ibn Thabit named Aws and the Companion [[person/ubada-ibn-al-samit|Ubada ibn al-Samit]], recorded as the city's first Muslim judge. Yemeni auxiliaries were also stationed there. The article suggests that a saying linking the building of Jerusalem to the destruction of Medina may have begun as a quip on this movement of people. It adds that [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]] is reported to have given the revenue of the gardens of Siloam to the poor, and that the conquest left the Christian community in disarray, yet Jerusalem kept largely its Christian character: al-Muqaddasi says the Christian holidays set the rhythm of the year for Muslims too. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362}

## The Umayyads
[[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya]] governed Syria and Palestine for forty years. Two events in his career took place in Jerusalem: a pact in 38/658 with [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn al-As]] that, in the article's words, decided the contest with [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] in his favour, and the homage paid to him as caliph in 40/660. The article reads his reported prayers at Christian sites on that occasion as a sign of Islam entering into the inheritance of earlier monotheistic religions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362}

The article argues that the Muslim place of worship on the Temple area took shape under Mu'awiya and that the plan for the Dome of the Rock may also have been laid in his long reign, though its inscription bears the year 72/691-2 under [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik]]. It discusses Goldziher's theory that Abd al-Malik built the Dome to divert the pilgrimage from Mecca, then held by his rival [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Ibn al-Zubayr]]. The article rejects the theory, pointing out that the major historians and geographers make no such allusion, that Abd al-Malik's soldiers took part in the pilgrimage in 68/687-8, and that a standing ceremony held in other cities was a participation in the pilgrimage, not a substitute for it. In its view the impulse was religious. It names [[person/raja-ibn-haywa|Raja ibn Haywa]] as having charge of the building and describes the mosaics as echoing prophetic descriptions of the future Jerusalem and the inscriptions as stressing the prophethood of Jesus while denying his sonship. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 363}

The Umayyad period also produced the [[building/al-aqsa-mosque|al-Aqsa mosque]] and the governor's residence, new city gates and a repaired road with Arabic milestones. Excavated Umayyad foundations south of the Aqsa suggest to the article that Jerusalem might have become an inland administrative centre; instead [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad]] prince Sulayman, later caliph, founded [[place/ramla|Ramla]] as capital of the province, which in the long run harmed Jerusalem. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 363} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 364}

## The Abbasid period
The end of Umayyad rule brought tribulations: the walls were pulled down after a rebellion against Marwan II, and earthquakes followed. The [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid]] caliph al-Mansur visited in 140/758 and again in 154/771, and al-Mahdi also prayed there; but [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]] and later Abbasid caliphs did not come, a change the article links to a new piety wary of the legends about Jerusalem. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 364}

Christian life drew support from Western Europe; the article mentions buildings for pilgrims founded by Charlemagne and his successors, while remaining agnostic about the reported embassies with Harun. It lists hermits on the Mount of Olives praying in Greek, Syriac, Latin, Georgian, Armenian and Arabic. Around 800 the Jewish high council moved from Tiberias to Jerusalem, where it was challenged by the Karaites, who made the city their centre. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 364}

The ninth century brought famine under al-Ma'mun and a peasant revolt in Syria at the end of al-Mu'tasim's reign, during which the rebels' leader entered Jerusalem, its people fled and places of worship were plundered. In 264/878 [[person/ahmad-ibn-tulun|Ahmad ibn Tulun]] took Palestine. By the tenth century the belief that Jerusalem would host the Last Judgement had spread, and the article notes that rulers such as the Ikhshidid founder and the viceroy [[person/kafur-al-ikhshidi|Kafur]] were buried there. It also records friction between communities: a mosque built on half the Holy Sepulchre's outer court, an attack on a Palm Sunday procession in 938, and in 355/966 the pillage of churches and the killing of the patriarch under a Berber governor. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 364} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 365}

## Fatimids, Turkomans and Seljuks
Jerusalem passed to the [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimids]] but shared little in their prosperity and was harassed by bedouin chieftains. The caliph [[person/al-hakim-bi-amr-illah|al-Hakim]] ordered the destruction of the Holy Sepulchre on 28 September 1009 as part of a persecution of Christians and later Jews; the article rules out explanations based only on the caliph's state of mind and suggests a shift in Isma'ili religious policy. The dome of the Rock collapsed in an earthquake in 407/1016. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 365} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 366}

From the mid-fifth/eleventh century Jerusalem replaced Ramla as the country's main city. The Turkoman commander [[person/atsiz-ibn-uwaq|Atsiz]] took it in 463/1071 and, after a rising, retook it, killing many; in 470/1078 [[person/tutush|Tutush]] brought it into the [[polity/seljuk-empire|Seljuk]] realm and gave it to Artuk of the [[polity/artuqid-dynasty|Artuqid]] line. In 491/1098 the Fatimid vizier [[person/al-afdal-ibn-badr-al-jamali|al-Afdal]] besieged it and took it back. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 366}

## Life in the fourth and fifth centuries
From geographers and some hundred Geniza letters the article reconstructs the city before the Crusades. Al-Muqaddasi praises its beauty, markets and baths. Quarters existed for Christians, Armenians, Jews and the Karaites, and the [[group/karramiyya|Karramiyya]] had built khanqahs. The article distrusts the population figures: Nasir-i Khusraw's 20,000 reflects a favourite round number, and the 70,000 killed by the Crusaders in 492/1099 cannot indicate the number of inhabitants, as many had fled into the city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 366}

## The Crusaders and the Ayyubids
Several pages of the OCR, covering the Crusader capture of 1099, are unreadable here, so only the article's closing summary can be reported. It cautions that Geniza letters by eyewitnesses qualify the chroniclers: there was a bloodbath, but not as all-embracing as the summary reports suggest. Jerusalem became a Christian city, and the kingdom of Jerusalem took its name from it. It flourished as a capital with a largely European population. Muslims and Jews were later allowed to visit; [[person/usama-ibn-munqidh|Usama ibn Munqidh]] describes praying on the Temple area. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 368}

After the victory at Hattin in 583/1187 [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Saladin]] besieged Jerusalem and [[event/saladin-jerusalem-583|recovered it]]: after long negotiations an agreement in Ramadan 583/November 1187 let the inhabitants ransom themselves. Muslim shrines were restored to their purpose, and the convent of St Anne became the Salahiyya madrasa. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 368} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 369}

In 616/1219 the [[polity/ayyubid-dynasty|Ayyubid]] ruler of Damascus, [[person/al-malik-al-muazzam|al-Mu'azzam]], fearing a new Christian advance, ordered the city dismantled except for the Temple area, the Holy Sepulchre and the citadel. In 626/1229 his brother [[person/al-malik-al-kamil|al-Kamil]] ceded it to Emperor Frederick II for ten years. The article reports widespread Muslim protests against both acts. In 642/1244 the Khwarazmians took the city, plundering and killing, and it then stayed under the rulers of Egypt until the Ottoman conquest. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 369} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 370}

## The sanctity of Jerusalem in Islam
The article says that Islam took over Jewish and Christian notions of the city's holiness. The tradition ranking Jerusalem as the third sanctuary after Mecca and [[place/medina|Medina]] was formulated in the first Islamic century and generally accepted in the second, after dispute about whether a place so far from the Hijaz was foreign to Islam. It reports that both reverence and objection deepened later. The prophets who lived there and the Night Journey were accepted by all, whereas the belief that it would be the scene of the Resurrection, and the legends built on it, drew criticism as local inventions to attract visitors. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 369}

Books on the merits of the city were few before the sixth/twelfth century, and its conquest by the Crusaders aroused little reaction for decades; the article reads this as showing that veneration was not yet a spiritual force. After Zanki's conquest of Edessa in 539/1144 the courts of Zanki and [[person/nur-al-din-mahmud-ibn-zangi|Nur al-Din]] took up the theme of jihad for Jerusalem, and it reached its height with Saladin. Works on its merits became abundant, at least thirty in the Mamluk period. [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]]'s objections were directed at innovations in its cult, and its status as third sanctuary was never questioned. The article quotes [[person/sayyid-qutb|Sayyid Qutb]], who saw the Night Journey as joining the sites of the monotheistic religions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 369} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 370}

## Mamluk Jerusalem
At the start of the [[polity/mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk]] period the city was largely in ruins and deserted, and the Mongols reached Hebron and Gaza in 658/1260 before the Mamluk victory at [[event/ayn-jalut-658|Ayn Jalut]]. From 778/1376 it was a separate district under a governor answerable to [[place/cairo|Cairo]], while the Haram was supervised by an official styled superintendent of the two holy sites. The history of the age was one of rebuilding, with madrasas, zawiyas, khanqahs and tombs erected by sultans, amirs and private persons. Because of its isolation, Jerusalem was the commonest place of exile for dismissed Mamluk officers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 370}

The article pictures a city of Muslim scholars living on pious foundations, who moved often between Jerusalem, Cairo and Damascus, and of leading families such as the Banu Ibn Jama'a who held the richest offices; it notes that nepotism was rife. Mujir al-Din counted about twenty Sufi convents, and the article reports tensions between scholars and Sufis. A Franciscan monastery was founded on Mount Zion in the 1330s; and the article gives the case of the synagogue demolished in 879/1474, whose closure a Shafi'i judge ordered against instructions from Cairo, after which the sultan punished those involved and had it rebuilt. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 370} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 371}

## The first Ottoman period
After the [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottoman]] conquest of 1516-17, [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Sulayman the Lawgiver]] left the walls built between 944/1537 and 948/1541, the renovated Dome of the Rock, public fountains and endowments, including a soup kitchen founded by his wife Khurrem. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 371} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 372} Ottoman archives show about 4,000 inhabitants at the start of the period and a tripling under his reign; the largest revenue was the toll on visitors to the Holy Sepulchre. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 372}

Insecurity, the article says, hampered development until well into the nineteenth century. The city was treated as a source of income for the governor of Damascus rather than administered closely, and local families such as the Khatib, Khalidi, Husayni and Nashashibi rose through religious office, tax-farming and waqf administration. Risings occurred, including in 1808 after a fire at the Holy Sepulchre and in 1825 against heavy taxes. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 372} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 373}

## 1831 to 1917
The article describes this as a time of radical change. The conquest by Ibrahim Pasha, stepson of [[person/muhammad-ali-pasha|Muhammad Ali]], relieved non-Muslims of special levies and allowed church building, which angered many Muslims; a rising drove out his garrison in 1834-5 before he put it down. After the Ottomans returned, European consulates opened from 1838, the Latin patriarchate was revived in 1847, and Christian and Jewish numbers grew, with Jews forming the majority by about 1880 according to the article. The telegraph reached the city in 1865, a carriage road in 1868 and the railway in 1892, and suburbs grew outside the walls from about 1860. General Allenby entered the city on foot in December 1917. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 373} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 374}

## After 1917
This final section deals with contested matters; the article's account is summarised here with attribution and without a verdict. Under the British mandate the city was seat of the government and of Jewish, Muslim and Christian bodies. The article says the clash of Arab and Jewish national aspirations touched Jerusalem more than any other city in Palestine, with violence in 1920 and 1929 and a general uprising from 1933; the mufti [[person/amin-al-husseini|Amin al-Husayni]] led the Supreme Muslim Council, and the article records his rivalry with [[person/raghib-al-nashashibi|Raghib al-Nashashibi]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 374} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 375}

The article reports that the United Nations General Assembly proposed on 29 November 1947 that Palestine be divided and Jerusalem internationalised; that fighting followed, the Jewish Quarter of the Old City was left by its inhabitants on 27 May 1948, and the ceasefire divided the city. It says that Jordan annexed its area and Israel proclaimed Jerusalem its capital in December 1949, and notes that, by its reading, both acts contradicted the resolution; it adds that Israel's step was not recognised internationally. In 1967 the Old City came under Israeli control after three days of fighting in Jerusalem. According to the article, the Israeli authorities speak of reunification, whereas United Nations bodies and Muslim organisations called for a return to the earlier situation. It also says the Muslim religious institutions kept control of the mosques of the Temple Mount, and that a fire damaged the Aqsa mosque on 21 August 1969, which the article attributes to a deranged tourist from Australia; the Muslim council afterwards carried out repairs over several years. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 375} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 376}

## Pages 201 to 388: Gollius's notes on Jerusalem
Gollius records that the Rock was built on the order of Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan, begun in 66 AH and completed in 73, and that the mosque of Jerusalem was measured at 660 cubits long and 406 cubits wide, printed figures. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 379} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 380} He adds that al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah ordered the demolition of the Church of the Resurrection in 398 AH. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 381} The Crusaders under Godfrey entered on 15 July 1099, corresponding to 25 Sha'ban 492 AH, and Saladin recovered the city on 27 Rajab 583. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 382}

## For Sprouts
Jerusalem is a blessed and historic city, home to the sacred al-Aqsa Mosque and the golden Dome of the Rock.

## For Explorers
Jerusalem is a sacred city of profound status in Islam, being the first Qibla toward which early Muslims prayed and the destination of the Prophet's Night Journey. It houses the sacred al-Aqsa compound and the Dome of the Rock, and Caliph Umar entered it with a celebrated peace treaty guaranteeing religious freedom and safety for its inhabitants.

## For Scholars
A city of deep spiritual sanctity and rich history spanning millennia. In Islamic tradition, it stands as the first Qibla, the third holiest sanctuary, and the terrestrial terminal of the Prophet's Night Journey, encompassing the al-Aqsa compound and the Dome of the Rock. Surrendered peacefully in 16 AH (637 CE) to Caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab under the historic 'Covenant of Umar' guaranteeing Christian religious liberties, the city experienced continuous stewardship under the Umayyads, the Ayyubids under Salah al-Din, and subsequent Mamluk and Ottoman administrations.
