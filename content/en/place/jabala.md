## Overview
Jabala is a coastal town on the coasts of Syria. According to al-Baladhuri it was a fortress of the Romans, who left it when Homs was conquered. Then the Muslims took by force a town two farsakhs from it, and it was ruined and its people left it. [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Muawiya ibn Abi Sufyan]] founded Jabala and garrisoned it. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.182}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut makes Jabala (vowelled with movement) the name of several places. Among them is Shi'b Jabala: a red plateau in Najd between al-Sharif (a water of Banu Numayr) and al-Sharaf (a water of Banu Kilab), where the famous day was fought between Banu 'Amir and Banu Tamim, 'Abs, Dhubyan and Fazara; Tamim and its allies were routed and Laqit ibn Zurara was killed there. On Abu Ahmad's authority he reports it one of the greatest days of the Arabs, fifty-seven years before Islam and seventeen before the Prophet's birth as transmitted, and Abu Ziyad describes the plateau as a day's march long and half a day wide with only two ways up (Sal'a on the sunrise side and al-Khalif on the west). {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.104} He returns to it under the entry Shi'b Jabala: a day on which most of the Arab tribes gathered and victory went to Banu 'Amir, with verses of Labid. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.347} Among them is a Jabala to which al-Hazimi attributed the hadith man Sulayman ibn 'Ali al-Jabali residing in Mecca, as a place of the Hijaz; Yaqut says he saw no one else name a place called Jabala in the Hijaz, and Abu al-Fadl Muhammad ibn Tahir, on 'Abd al-Ghani's authority, placed the man at the Syrian Jabala and judged that correct. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.104; 2 p.105} Among them is the famous fortress on the Syrian coast in the district of Aleppo near al-Ladhiqiyya: on Ahmad ibn Yahya ibn Jabir's authority, when 'Ubada ibn al-Samit had finished with al-Ladhiqiyya in year 17 he came among his troops to a town called Balda two farsakhs from Jabala, took it by force, and it fell ruined and its people left; Mu'awiya then founded Jabala — a Roman fortress whose people had evacuated at the conquest of Hims — garrisoned it and built a fortress outside the old Roman one, whose dwellers had been monks. The Byzantines took it in year 357, a year after Sayf al-Dawla's death, and held it until year 473, when its judge Ibn Duli'a rose against it with the help of Jalal al-Din ibn 'Ammar, lord of Tripoli; the Franks then took it in year 52 as printed, until Salah al-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub recovered it by treaty in year 584, and in Yaqut's time it was in Muslim hands. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.105} Among them is a fortress at the end of Wadi al-Sitara in Tihama toward Dhara on Abu Zayd's authority, a village of Dhara said to be the first village built in Tihama on 'Arram ibn al-Asbagh's authority (which Yaqut supposes al-Hazimi may have meant), a village of Banu 'Amir ibn 'Abd al-Qays in al-Bahrayn, and Dhu Jabala, a city of Yemen under Mount Sabir also called Dhat al-Nahrayn, which 'Abdallah ibn Muhammad al-Sulayhi founded in year 458 and which 'Amara says was named for Jabala, a Jew who sold pottery on its site. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.106} He also has al-Jubayla, the diminutive of Jabala: the chief town of the villages of Banu 'Amir ibn al-Harith ibn Anmar al-'Abqasis by the sea. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.110}

## The museum's identification
The record's Jabala is the Syrian coastal city, and it is Yaqut's "famous fortress on the Syrian coast near al-Ladhiqiyya" (about 21.4 km from the museum's al-Ladhiqiyya point); his Ibn Jabir account agrees with the record's al-Baladhuri source for Mu'awiya's founding. What is new is his sequel: the Byzantine capture of 357, Ibn Duli'a's recovery of 473, the Frankish rule — whose year is printed as "52" and left as printed — and Salah al-Din's recovery in 584. His other Jabalas (the Najdi ravine, Dhara in Tihama, al-Bahrayn, the Yemeni Dhu Jabala and al-Jubayla) are distinct entries and are not to be conflated with the Syrian city.
