## Overview
The land of Wabar is the land known as the sands of 'Alij, where Wabar ibn Umaym and his people settled, and perished because of their transgression, as al-Mas'udi relates from the akhbaris (historians of reports). {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.456}

## The account of the akhbaris
According to the akhbaris, it was among the most fertile lands of God, abundant in trees and good in fruit. The jinn lived in their homes and protected them from the humans who came to it. They would throw dust in his face and stir whirlwinds, and sometimes lead him astray or kill him. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.456}

They report that today only jinn and wild camels inhabit it, and that the wild camels are descended from the camels of the jinn. Al-Mas'udi mentions that this place is false in the view of many people of understanding. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.457}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut gives Wabar as a name of the pattern of "Qatam" and "Hadham", which may come from "wabar", the wool of camels, hares and the like, or from "tawbir", the effacing of a trace. He then sets out several statements about its place: that it is the land of Wabar ibn Iram ibn Sam ibn Nuh, who moved there at the confusion of tongues and built a dwelling, a wide land between al-Shihr and Sana'a of about three hundred farsakhs square; that, in al-Layth's words, it was among the abodes of Ad between the sands of Yabrin and Yemen, which God left to the jinn when Ad perished; that, from Muhammad ibn Ishaq, it is a land inhabited by the nasnas, while some place it between Hadramawt and al-Subub; and that the book of Ahmad ibn Muhammad al-Hamadhani puts it in Yemen between Najran and Hadramawt and between the land of Mahra and al-Shihr. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.356} {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.357}

He relates that it was the most fertile of lands, richest in water, trees and fruit, so that its tribes multiplied and grew wealthy and arrogant and did not acknowledge the grace given them; God then altered their form and made them nasnas, each with half a head, half a face, one eye, one hand and one leg, and the ants of their land became as large as great dogs. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.356} {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.357}

He quotes Hisham ibn Muhammad that Wabar was a village of the Banu Wabar, one of the first nations, cut off between the sands of Banu Sa'd and al-Shihr and Mahra; those who claim to have reached it say they come on a land of lofty palaces, palms and water with no one in it, and it is said that jinn dwell there and that no human enters it without losing his way. For this he cites a line of al-Farazdaq that likens a man's straying to the straying of one who seeks the road to Wabar. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.356} {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.357}

He also reports what the scholars of the Arabs say: that when God destroyed Ad and Thamud he let the jinn settle in their homes and they guard them against anyone who approaches, throwing dust in the face of one who comes near, bewildering him and sometimes killing him, and that the camels called hawshiyya, said to have been covered by the camels of the jinn, are found there. He tells the story of a man of Yemen who followed a stallion from his herd to the spring of Wabar. In the same entry he gives Abu Zayd al-Ansari's list of sayings of the form "I left him at the spring of Wabar", which name places no one can identify, and then reads a line of al-Nabigha that mentions the palms of Wabar as showing that it was an inhabited, known land with date palms. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.356} {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 5 p.357}

## The museum's identification
The record and Yaqut agree on the substance: the land of Wabar, whose people perished, which the jinn settled and guard, and where the hawshiyya camels are found, as al-Mas'udi gives it in the record from the akhbaris. Yaqut, however, does not place Wabar in the sands of Alij: they appear in the same entry (p. 358) only in a traveller's tale of losing his way in the sands of Alij and meeting a half-bodied nasnas, not as a placing of the land. He sets the place in several different statements, between al-Shihr and Sana'a, between Najran and Hadramawt, or between the sands of Banu Sa'd and al-Shihr and Mahra, and does not choose among them. The record's coordinates, the Wabar crater site in the Rub' al-Khali, come from the museum's geocoding pass and are not Yaqut's; they should not be read as a fixing of the land he describes.
