## Overview
Samarkand, in Arabic Samarqand, is an ancient city of [[place/transoxiana|Transoxania]], the Arabic Ma wara' al-Nahr, on the southern bank of the Zarafshan river, also called Nahr Sughd. The Encyclopaedia of Islam says that in early Islamic times it was the first city of the region in extent and population, even when, as under the [[polity/samanid-dynasty|Samanids]], [[place/bukhara|Bukhara]] was the administrative capital. Its standing came from its position where trade routes met, from Afghanistan through [[place/balkh|Balkh]] and [[place/tirmidh|Tirmidh]], and from Persia through [[place/marw|Marw]], routes that ran on north and east into the Turkish steppes and along the Silk Road to eastern Turkistan and China. Above all it flourished because of the fertility of [[place/sughd|Sughd]], the heavily irrigated Zarafshan basin, which could support a dense farming population. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1105}

## Name and ancient history
The second part of the name holds the Eastern Iranian word kand, "town", while the first part has not been satisfactorily explained. The city first appears in accounts of Alexander's campaigns in the east, as Marakanda, and its site at Tepe Afrasiyab has produced Hellenistic evidence. Alexander occupied it several times during the fighting with Spitamenes and, according to Strabo, razed it, while Arab legend makes him, and also the Tubba king Shamir Yur'ish, its founder. After the partition of 323 BC it was the capital of Sogdiana within the satrapy of Bactria, and it passed out of Seleucid hands with Bactria when Diodotos declared independence and the Graeco-Bactrian kingdom arose. From then until the Muslim conquest it stayed historically and economically apart from Persia, though cultural contact with the west went on. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1108}

The firm information comes from Chinese sources. The kingdom of K'ang-Kü is named from the Han period, and the Tang Annals identify its chief territory, K'ang, with Samarkand. The Annals of the Wei, compiled in 437, say that a dynasty related to the Yüe-či had reigned there since before the Christian era, and the Buddhist pilgrim Hiuen-tsang visited and briefly described the city about 630. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1108}

## Arab arrival and the rulers of Sughd
The article says the Arabs appear for certain in the affairs of Samarkand only in the time of the governor of Khurasan [[person/qutayba-ibn-muslim|Qutayba ibn Muslim]]. It doubts the tradition that the Prophet's cousin [[person/qutham-ibn-al-abbas|Qutham ibn al-Abbas]], whose supposed tomb at Afrasiyab is the Shah-i Zinda, was in Samarkand in 56/676, and judges that the cult must have arisen later as a family cult begun by the Abbasids after they took power in 132/750, possibly adapting an older cult on the same site. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1108}

The Iranian ruler of Samarkand in Qutayba's time was [[person/tarkhun|Tarkhun]], probably a title rather than a personal name, called malik Sughd or malik Samarkand in the Arabic sources and first mentioned in 85/704 fighting Qutayba at Bukhara. In 91/710 Qutayba sent his brother Abd al-Rahman to collect tribute, which Tarkhun paid; the anti-Arab party then deposed him, and he was either killed or took his own life. Another Soghdian prince, [[person/ghurak|Ghurak]], replaced him and ruled for some twenty-seven years until his death in 119/737 or 120/738, with an Arab garrison in his city. His policy towards the Arabs swung between conciliation and appeals for help to the Chinese emperors, as nominal suzerains over Central Asia, or to the Turks. The Turgesh appeared in Sughd in 102/721, and in 110/728 Ghurak joined a general Soghdian rising against the Arabs with Turkish help, so that the Arabs were for a time reduced to their garrisons at Samarkand and Dabusiyya. Arab authority was firmly re-established only in the late 730s, by the governor [[person/nasr-ibn-sayyar|Nasr ibn Sayyar]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1108}

Qutayba had built a mosque in Samarkand, but the article holds that Islamisation beyond the Arab garrison must have been slow. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1108}

## The faiths of the city
The city held adherents of many faiths. Hiuen-tsang found only two abandoned Buddhist monasteries, and Buddhism had almost certainly vanished a century or so later. There was probably a Nestorian Christian bishopric in the sixth century, raised to a metropolitan see in the early eighth. Clavijo still found many Christians there at the start of the ninth/fifteenth century, and the community seems to have ended shortly afterwards, in the reign of Ulugh Beg. In the mid-fourth/tenth century [[person/ibn-hawqal|Ibn Hawqal]] described a Christian community with monastic cells on the hill of Shawdhar south of the city, including Christians from Iraq. Not long afterwards the Hudud al-alam mentions a Manichaean convent, whose members the article takes to be Manichaeans who had fled Iraq for fear of persecution in the time of al-Muqtadir. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1108}

## Abbasid rule and the Samanids
Under the early Abbasids the Zarafshan valley was deeply affected, in the caliphate of al-Mahdi, 158-69/775-85, by the movement of the "wearers of white" led by [[person/al-muqanna|al-Muqanna]], which the article calls Neo-Mazdakite. The governor of Samarkand, Jibra'il ibn Yahya, helped put it down in his area. [[person/abu-muslim-al-khurasani|Abu Muslim]] is said to have built the city's outer wall, and [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]] to have repaired it after decay. [[person/rafi-ibn-layth|Rafi ibn al-Layth]] began his revolt in Samarkand in 190/806 by killing the governor and seizing the city, and held it until he surrendered to al-Ma'mun in 193/809. The first Islamic coins struck at Samarkand date from this period, beginning in 142-4/759-62. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1108}

About 204/819, on al-Ma'mun's order, the governor of Khurasan [[person/ghassan-ibn-abbad|Ghassan ibn Abbad]] gave the four sons of Asad ibn Saman-Khuda governorships in Transoxania and eastern Khurasan, and [[person/nuh-ibn-asad|Nuh]] received Samarkand. When he died in 227/842 the city passed to his brother Ahmad, who died in 250/864 and whose copper fulus were struck there from 244/858. As [[polity/tahirid-dynasty|Tahirid]] authority in Khurasan collapsed under [[polity/saffarid-dynasty|Saffarid]] attack, [[person/nasr-ibn-ahmad|Nasr ibn Ahmad]] became virtually independent ruler of Transoxania with his capital at Samarkand. His brother and eventual conqueror [[person/ismail-i-samani|Ismail]], forefather of all later Samanid amirs, made Bukhara the Samanid capital, but Samarkand stayed the commercial centre of Transoxania in the following centuries. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1108} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1109}

## Trade, paper and learning
Samarkand was one of the principal markets for Turkish slaves brought from Inner Asia, and Ibn Hawqal says slaves trained there were the best in Transoxania. One of its most famous products, exported across the Islamic world, was paper, brought in by the Chinese artisans captured at the [[event/battle-of-talas-133|battle of Talas]] in 133/751. The city was also a centre of scholarship. The great [[group/hanafi-madhhab|Hanafi]] theologian [[person/abu-mansur-al-maturidi|al-Maturidi]], who died about 333/944, came from the Maturid quarter, and his tomb in the city was still shown in the ninth/fifteenth century. Another Hanafi theologian and Qur'an commentator, Abu 'l-Layth al-Samarkandi, died towards the end of the fourth/tenth century. The local history by [[person/umar-al-nasafi|Abu Hafs Umar al-Nasafi]], who died in 537/1142-3, the Kitab al-Qand fi ma'rifat 'ulama' Samarqand, dealt with the shrines and graves of local scholars and with some events in the city's history, but survives only in an abridgement of a Persian translation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1109}

## The city in the tenth century
The geographers al-Istakhri, Ibn Hawqal, [[person/al-muqaddasi|al-Muqaddasi]] and the author of the Hudud al-alam describe Samarkand in the Samanid heyday. They show the tripartite plan typical of Iranian towns: a citadel, the town proper and the suburbs, named here from south to north. The citadel stood on high ground south of the town and held the administrative offices and the prison. The town, with houses of clay and wood, lay on a hill, ringed by a deep ditch dug to supply earth for the surrounding wall. Running water reached the whole town by an underground aqueduct, a lead-lined channel, bringing it from the south to the central square called Ra's al-Tak. The aqueduct seems to go back to pre-Islamic times, since Zoroastrians supervised it and were excused the poll-tax in return, and it watered the town's extensive gardens. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1109}

The town had four main gates: the Bab al-Sin to the east, recalling the old silk-trade link with China; the Bab Bukhara to the north; the Bab al-Nawbahar to the west, a name which, as at Bukhara and Balkh, points to a Buddhist monastery; and the Bab al-Kabir or Bab Kishsh to the south. The lower suburbs ran down towards the Zarafshan inside a wall with eight gates and held most of the bazaars, caravanserais and warehouses, which were rare in the town itself. The Samanid government offices and the Friday mosque stood in the town. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1109}

## Karakhanids, Kara Khitay and the Mongols
Samarkand and Bukhara were taken by the incoming [[polity/qarakhanid-dynasty|Karakhanids]] in 382/992, and with the defeat of the last Samanid, Ismail ibn Nuh al-Muntasir, in 394/1004 they passed finally under Turkish control. In the second quarter of the fifth/eleventh century Samarkand became the capital of the western khanate of the Karakhanids, covering Transoxania and western Farghana, first under Ali ibn Harun Boghra Khan and then under a parallel line descended from Ilig Nasr. Under rulers such as Shams al-Mulk Nasr ibn Tamghach Khan Ibrahim it became, later in the century, a splendid cultural and artistic centre, and a regular Karakhanid mint. After the battle of the Katwan Steppe in 536/1141, where the Seljuk sultan [[person/sanjar|Sanjar]] and his vassal Mahmud ibn Muhammad Khan were decisively beaten by the pagan Kara Khitay, Samarkand and Bukhara formed a reduced Karakhanid principality under Kara Khitay overlordship. The city still prospered commercially, and about 1170 the Spanish Jewish traveller Benjamin of Tudela is said to have found 50,000 Jews there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1109}

The last Karakhanid at Samarkand, Uthman Khan ibn Ibrahim, was executed in 608/1212 by the Khwarazm-Shah Ala al-Din Muhammad, and the city was occupied by the [[polity/khwarazmian-empire|Khwarazmians]]. Soon afterwards the Mongols of [[person/genghis-khan|Chingiz Khan]] reached Transoxania and, after taking Bukhara, came to Samarkand, the gathering point for the Khwarazm-Shah's forces, in the spring of 617/1220. The city fell and was devastated, though some citizens were allowed to return on payment of a ransom of 200,000 dinars. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1109} For a century and a half it was a shadow of its former self. The Taoist traveller Ch'ang-ch'un, who journeyed in Western Asia in 1221-4, reports 100,000 families before the sack and only a quarter of them after it, and in the mid-eighth/fourteenth century [[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]] found the population much reduced and the city ruinous and without a wall. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1109}

## Timur and his successors
Prosperity returned when [[person/timur-lang|Timur]], supreme in Transoxania after about 771/1369, made Samarkand the capital of his growing kingdom and began to adorn it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1109} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1110} In 808/1405 the Spanish envoy Ruy Gonzalez de Clavijo saw it in its new glory. He gives Cimesquiente as the local name and explains it as "large village"; the article reads this as an echo of a Turkish corruption based on a popular link with the word for "thick". Timur's grandson [[person/ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]], who died in 853/1449, embellished the city with his palace Cihil Sutun and built his famous observatory there. The memoirs of [[person/babur-mughal|Babur]] give a very full description of the city in Timur's day, which the article calls justly classical. Babur captured Samarkand in 903/1497 and held it for some months; in 906/1500 it was taken by his rival, the Ozbeg Shibani Khan. With Safawid help Babur took it again in 916/1510 but had to withdraw the next year. Under the Ozbegs Samarkand was only the nominal capital and fell well behind Bukhara. In the eighteenth century it declined severely and was virtually uninhabited in the middle years. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1110}

## Russian rule and the modern city
Russian troops under General K.P. Kaufmann occupied Samarkand in November 1868 and imposed a treaty of vassalage on the amir of Bukhara, in whose lands it lay. The city was cut off from the nominally independent khanate and ruled directly as part of the Russian Governorate-General of Turkestan. After 1871 a new Russian town grew up west of the old city, with a station on the Trans-Caspian line to Tashkent. The anti-Russian rebellion of Turkestan in 1916, provoked by Tsarist attempts to conscript local people for labour service, began in the Samarkand oblast. Under the Soviets the oblast joined the Turkestan Autonomous SSR in 1918 and the Uzbek SSR in 1924, of which Samarkand was first the capital until [[place/tashkent|Tashkent]] replaced it in 1930. Since 1990 it has been in the Republic of Uzbekistan. The modern city, a centre of food processing and industry, had 257,000 inhabitants in 1970. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1110}

## Architecture: Afrasiyab and the Timurid city
Archaeologists call the ruins of old Samarkand Afrasiyab, after the city's destruction by Chingiz Khan in 617/1220; a museum on the site keeps fragments of stucco and ceramics. Under Timur and his successors the earlier southern suburbs became the new Samarkand, known for its ceramic revetment and its modular architecture. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1110}

The [[building/shah-i-zinda|Shah-i Zinda]], "the living prince", the shrine of Qutham ibn al-Abbas, survived on the southern slopes of Afrasiyab. Excavations, notably those led by N.B. Nemtseva in 1962, found the base of a fifth/eleventh-century minaret in the north-west corner of the shrine, an earlier mausoleum, an underground mosque and a semi-underground chamber. A line of tombs with portals and domed rooms runs along an old north-south alley, with the shrine at its northern end. The article compares the mausolea to scattered jewels in blue and turquoise glaze, and dates the two main entrance arches to the ninth/fifteenth century. Among the tombs it describes are the mausoleum of Khwaja Ahmad of the 1350s, that of Shad-i Mulk Aka, who died in 773/1371, built by her mother Turkan Aka, Timur's elder sister, and holding the best-preserved tilework of the ensemble, and the double-domed mausoleum of Shirin Bika Aka, dated 787/1385-6, with the earliest double-shell dome. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1110} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1111} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1112}

Of the great Timurid foundations the article describes the mosque of [[building/bibi-khanum-mosque|Bibi Khanum]], 801-8/1398-1405, begun on Timur's return from India. Its courtyard measures 87 by 63 metres and its entrance portal rises to 41 metres. Built too fast, it soon began to decay, and in the eleventh/seventeenth century the Tilla Kari mosque replaced it; the earthquake of 1897 hastened the fall of its domes, and long restoration began in the 1970s. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1112} The Gur-i Mir, "the World Master", is an octagonal mausoleum Timur had built after the death of his favourite grandson Muhammad Sultan in 805/1403, with a turquoise melon-shaped double dome 37 metres high. Timur was buried there in 807/1405, followed by later Timurids, and Ulugh Beg brought the dark nephrite stone of his cenotaph from Inner Asia in 828/1425. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1112} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1113}

The Registan was a crossroads where six main roads met in the eighth/fourteenth and ninth/fifteenth centuries. Of Ulugh Beg's buildings there only his [[building/madrasa-ulugh-beg-samarqand|madrasa]] survives, with inscriptions dated between 820/1417 and 823/1421. Facing it the Sher Dor madrasa was built from 1028/1619 to 1045/1636 under the Janids, and between them stands the Tilla-kari, 1056-70/1646-60, which combines a college with a Friday mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1113} The [[event/ulugh-beg-observatory-samarqand|observatory of Ulugh Beg]], according to [[person/abd-al-razzaq-samarqandi|Abd al-Razzaq al-Samarqandi]], was raised in 823/1420, a circular building 48 metres wide, faced with glazed bricks, north-east of the town. Excavation has uncovered at its centre a deep stepped trench marked in degrees, part of the gigantic sextant used to record the movements of planets and stars. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1113}

The article also describes the [[building/ishrat-khana-mausoleum|Ishrat-khana]], a mausoleum of 869/1464 built by Habiba Sultan Begum, wife of Sultan Abu Sa'id, for a daughter, the unfinished Ak Saray of the 1470s, and the tomb of the Naqshbandi leader [[person/khawaja-ahrar|Khwaja Ahrar]], who died in 896/1490. It closes with the gardens of the Timurid period, among them the Dilgusha, built in 799/1396 east of the town, a walled garden with a tiled gate on each side, pigeon towers at the corners and a domed pavilion at the centre. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1113} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1114}

## The siege and the peace
Samarqand is the great city of the Sughd. Sa'id ibn Uthman besieged it, and swore that he would not leave until he conquered it and threw down its citadel (qahandiz). Its people fought him for three days, and his eye and the eye of al-Muhallab were gouged out. Then its people feared for a palace in which were the sons of their kings and nobles, so they made peace with him for seven hundred thousand dirhams. They pledged with him fifteen of the sons of their kings, some say forty, and some say eighty. Then he threw at the citadel, and the stone stuck in its corner. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

## What the city exported
Al-Muqaddasi mentions among its exports the incomparable Samarqand paper. He mentions the Simkan and Samarqandi garments, large copper cooking pots, tents, and brocade carried to the Turks. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p.246-248}

Golius states that Ulugh Beg placed Samarqand in his tables and that it is the chief town of Transoxania, and he quotes Yaqut's description of it with twelve gates and a wide wall, and with water brought to it by a lead pipe. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 414} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 415}

Golius denies that Alexander built it, as Yaqut says, because Strabo records that the cities Alexander founded in Sogdiana were ruined and left nothing. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 414} Timur made it the seat of his kingdom. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 414}

## For Sprouts
Samarqand is an ancient historic city on the Silk Road, famed for its turquoise domes, grand mosques, and scholars.

## For Explorers
Samarqand is one of Central Asia's oldest cities and a jewel of the Silk Road in Transoxiana. It thrived through trade and scholarship, serving as a vital center of papermaking. Famous for the magnificent Registan square with its blue-tiled colleges, it also housed Ulugh Beg's advanced astronomical observatory.

## For Scholars
A premier historic metropolis of Transoxiana (modern Uzbekistan), situated along the fertile Zarafshan River basin at the heart of the Silk Road linking China, the Middle East, and the Mediterranean. Integrated into the Islamic realm under Qutayba ibn Muslim in 93 AH (712 CE), it became the pivotal center that introduced Chinese papermaking technology to the Islamic world. Reaching architectural brilliance as the capital of the Timurid Empire, it is famed for the Registan square ensembles, Bibi-Khanym Mosque, Shah-i Zinda necropolis, and Ulugh Beg's observatory, and was home to foundational thinkers like Abu Mansur al-Maturidi.
