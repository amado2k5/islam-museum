## Overview
Samarqand is the great city of the Sughd. Sa'id ibn Uthman besieged it, and swore that he would not leave until he conquered it and threw down its citadel (qahandiz). Its people fought him for three days, and his eye and the eye of al-Muhallab were gouged out. Then its people feared for a palace in which were the sons of their kings and nobles, so they made peace with him for seven hundred thousand dirhams. They pledged with him fifteen of the sons of their kings, some say forty, and some say eighty. Then he threw at the citadel, and the stone stuck in its corner. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

## What the city exported
Al-Muqaddasi mentions among its exports the incomparable Samarqand paper. He mentions the Simkan and Samarqandi garments, large copper cooking pots, tents, and brocade carried to the Turks. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p.246-248}

## For Sprouts
Samarqand is an ancient historic city on the Silk Road, famed for its turquoise domes, grand mosques, and scholars.

## For Explorers
Samarqand is one of Central Asia's oldest cities and a jewel of the Silk Road in Transoxiana. It thrived through trade and scholarship, serving as a vital center of papermaking. Famous for the magnificent Registan square with its blue-tiled colleges, it also housed Ulugh Beg's advanced astronomical observatory.

## For Scholars
A premier historic metropolis of Transoxiana (modern Uzbekistan), situated along the fertile Zarafshan River basin at the heart of the Silk Road linking China, the Middle East, and the Mediterranean. Integrated into the Islamic realm under Qutayba ibn Muslim in 93 AH (712 CE), it became the pivotal center that introduced Chinese papermaking technology to the Islamic world. Reaching architectural brilliance as the capital of the Timurid Empire, it is famed for the Registan square ensembles, Bibi-Khanym Mosque, Shah-i Zinda necropolis, and Ulugh Beg's observatory, and was home to foundational thinkers like Abu Mansur al-Maturidi.
