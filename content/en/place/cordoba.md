## Overview
Cordoba: a Spanish city, which was the capital of the emirate and then of the Umayyad caliphate in al-Andalus, and in which is the congregational mosque. {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari}

## In the books of biography and routes
al-Maqqari quotes al-Hajari in al-Mashhab that Cordoba, in the Marwanid state, was the dome of Islam, the seat of the generous and the mine of scholars, and that in al-Andalus it stood as the head stands to the body. He mentions the famous mosque and the bridge over the greatest river. {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari p.189-190}

According to Ibn Sa'id in al-Maghrib, the sultans of al-Andalus turned between three poles: Cordoba, al-Zahra and al-Zahira. He reports, on the authority of al-Shaqundi, that Cordoba was the greatest of the kingdoms of al-Andalus in knowledge and the most abundant in merit. {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari p.491-492}

Ibn Bashkuwal, as transmitted by al-Maqqari, mentions that it had seven gates and that its suburbs reached twenty-one suburbs. {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari p.501-502}

According to al-Baladhuri, Musa ibn Nusayr ordered Tariq ibn Ziyad not to go beyond it in his raid. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

## For Sprouts
Cordoba is a historic city in al-Andalus, famous for its Great Mosque, grand libraries, gardens, and beautiful monuments.

## For Explorers
Cordoba was the capital of the Umayyad Caliphate in al-Andalus and one of the world's greatest cities during the Middle Ages. Situated on the Guadalquivir River, it was famous for paved and illuminated streets, vast libraries, its university at the Great Mosque, and the palace-city of Madinat al-Zahra.

## For Scholars
The premier metropolis of al-Andalus and capital of the Umayyad Emirate and Caliphate, located in southern Spain along the Guadalquivir River. Reaching its cultural zenith in the 4th century AH (10th century CE) under Abd al-Rahman III and al-Hakam II, it rivaled Baghdad and Constantinople as the foremost center of learning, arts, and philosophy in Europe. Renowned for its Great Mosque, extensive palace libraries containing hundreds of thousands of codices, the royal city of Madinat al-Zahra, and street lighting, it was home to monumental figures like Ibn Rushd (Averroes) and Ibn Hazm, serving as a primary conduit of classical and Arabic science into Western Europe.

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-Buldan
Yaqut vocalises Cordoba with a damma on the first letter, a sukun on the second and a damma on the ta and the ba. He thinks it a foreign, Roman word, though in Arabic it could come from qurtaba, hard running, and he quotes al-Asma'i that qartabahu means he threw him down, with verses. He calls it a great city of al-Andalus in the middle of the country, the seat of its kings and its capital, home to the Umayyad rulers and a mine of the learned, five days from the sea. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.324}

From Ibn Hawqal, who travelled those lands around 350 AH, he reports that it is the greatest city in al-Andalus and has no equal in the Maghrib for its population and extent; it is said to be like one side of Baghdad. It is fortified with a stone wall with two gates, includes al-Rusafa, the common people live in its suburb, its inhabitants are wealthy, and a mule can cost five hundred dinars. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.324}

Yaqut, speaking in his own voice, says this was its state until about 440 AH, when the Umayyad era and that of Ibn Abi Amir ended, usurpers rose, and the power of the Banu Abbad and others grew; Cordoba was left without an authority to turn to and fell into ruin through the injustice done to it, while Seville under the Banu Abbad was built up and became the seat of rule in al-Andalus, and Cordoba became like one of the middling cities. He notes that people lamented it at length and gives verses of longing for it by the judge Muhammad ibn Abi Isa al-Laythi, then lists a number of scholars attributed to it, among them the reciter and grammarian Abu Bakr Yahya ibn Sa'dun al-Azdi al-Qurtubi, who died at Mosul in 567. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 4 p.324}
