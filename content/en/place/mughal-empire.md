## Overview
The Mughal sultanate in India was the state founded by Babur after the battle of Panipat in 1526 and ruled from Delhi until its last sultan, Bahadur Shah, was removed after the revolt of 1857. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.313} The source of this page is the chapter Prince Shakib Arslan wrote as commentary on Stoddard's book in the Arabic *Hadir al-Alam al-Islami*, titled "History of the Indian Islamic kingdoms". It was written in the twentieth century, decades after the state ended, so every judgment and figure in it is Arslan's account and what he quotes from European and Arab authors, not the museum's. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.296} Arslan lists the Mughal sultans: Babur, Humayun, Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan, Aurangzeb, Muhammad Shah, Shah Alam, Muhammad Akbar and Bahadur Shah. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.313} See [[polity/mughal-empire|the Mughal Empire]] and [[place/india|India]].

## Babur and Panipat
Arslan relates that Babur, whose title was Zahir al-Din Muhammad, was a descendant of Timur and son of Umar Shaykh, ruler of Fergana, his mother being of the line of Chaghatay and his father's side tracing to Genghis; that the rule of Fergana came to him in 1494 at the age of twelve, that he added Transoxiana in 1497 and then lost to Muhammad Shaybani Khan the Uzbek and fell back on Afghanistan, and that he gained the principality of Kabul in 1504 and Kandahar in 1507. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.296} When Sultan Ibrahim II Lodi of Delhi quarrelled with his uncle Alam, who took refuge in Kabul, Babur raided the Punjab in 1525 with some 13,000 fighters and guns not yet known in India; Ibrahim had a hundred thousand fighters and a thousand elephants, and the armies met on the plain of Panipat on 21 April 1526, Friday 8 Rajab 932 AH. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.296} See [[person/babur-mughal|Babur]], [[event/battle-of-panipat-932|the battle of Panipat]] and [[place/panipat|Panipat]].

He says Babur set barriers of chained carts with guns between them against the elephants, and had gunners skilled in the Ottoman manner of the sixteenth century; Ibrahim perished with about twenty-five thousand men, Babur entered Delhi and was proclaimed king of kings of India in its great mosque, and his son Humayun took Agra. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.297} The Hindu rajas then rallied at the call of Rana Sanga of Chittor; the battle near Agra ended in their defeat, Babur besieged and took Chanderi in 1528, and then went after Mahmud Lodi in Awadh, who fled to Bengal, whose ruler Babur forced to submit. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.297} Babur died in 1530; Arslan describes him as joining sword and pen, writing his memoirs himself, which were translated into European languages, and he quotes Renan's praise of their truthfulness. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 pp.297-298} He adds that Babur was a poet with a Turkish divan and wrote the Baburnama. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.298} See [[place/delhi|Delhi]], [[place/agra|Agra]] and [[place/kabul|Kabul]].

## Humayun and Sher Khan
Arslan says Humayun overcame Mahmud Lodi in a battle named in the text "Lucknow" in 1531, but could not overcome another Afghan commander, Sher Khan of the fortress of Chunar, who took Bengal, Bihar and Awadh. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.298} Humayun was beaten by Sher Khan at Kannauj in 1540, lost his guns, men and treasure and fled to the mountains of Afghanistan; he took refuge with the shah of Persia, who helped him with an army to take Kandahar in 1545 and Kabul in 1550. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.299} When Sher Khan died and his sons quarrelled, Humayun came down into the Punjab with fifteen thousand horsemen, met Sikandar Shah's army on the plain of Sirhind between Delhi and Lahore, won, and returned to Delhi; he died soon afterwards and was buried in a domed tomb at Delhi which the text counts among the wonders of the world. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.299} See [[person/humayun-mughal|Humayun]].

## Akbar and the consolidation of the state
Humayun was succeeded by his son Akbar Abu al-Fath Jalal al-Din Muhammad, not yet fourteen, born in Sind on 15 October 1542, with an experienced vizier, Bayram. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.299} In 1556 Hemu, commander of an Afghan king, retook Delhi and chased Akbar to the Punjab; the armies met at Panipat, Hemu fell, his men scattered, and Akbar entered Delhi and took Awadh and Bihar from the Afghans. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.299} Arslan holds that Akbar dealt with the Rajput princes by winning their hearts and taking the stubborn ones by the sword; he took Gwalior in 1558, Ajmer in 1560, Chittor in 1568 and Ranthambore in 1569, and the rajas of Marwar and Bikaner submitted in 1570. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.300} He says the defenders of Chittor killed their women and children as its fall drew near. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.300} Akbar relied on the raja of Amber and on Raja Todar Mal, whom he made finance minister and then governor of Bengal, encouraged intermarriage between Mughals and Rajputs, and himself married the sister of Raja Bhagwan Das. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.301}

Akbar then joined Gujarat in 1573, Bengal in 1580, Kashmir in 1586 and Sind in 1592, took Ahmadnagar in 1600 after the death of its queen, and died in 1605. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.301} Arslan describes his government as built on Persian-Mughal principles of great exactness, with a vakil, a vizier, a khan-khanan and a sadr, eighteen great provinces, civil administration in Persian hands, an army of Mughals and Indians, and a standing force of 140,000. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 pp.301-302} He praises Akbar's just treatment of the Hindus; he notes that European historians criticised what they saw as Islam's demand to humble the unbeliever, answers that justice is God's command and not limited to Muslims, and contrasts this with the conduct of European states in their colonies in his own day. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 pp.302-304} See [[person/timur-lang|Timur]].

## Akbar's beliefs in Arslan's account
Arslan relays from European historians that Akbar stayed far from the traditional rulings, leaned to Sufism, drew Shia scholars close, ordered Indian books translated into Persian, invited the Portuguese priests of Goa in 1580 and let the Jesuits open schools. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 pp.305-307} He quotes the French Encyclopaedia of Islam that Akbar left Islam and set up a creed he called divine monotheism, that he advised making the sun and fire symbols of the Deity, and that eighteen men of his court entered their names in the register of believers in his call. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.307} Arslan comments that in his view the man was confused on the question of the Divine, that those who followed may have done so to curry favour, and he rejects excusing Akbar by saying the sun and fire were mere symbols, since singling out a symbol may lead to worshipping it. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.309} These are the views of Arslan and those he quotes, not the museum's judgement. He says Akbar's son Salim, called Jahangir, who succeeded in 1605 and ruled to 1627, dropped what his father had followed and held to the school of the Sunna and the community, while keeping Persian as the official language. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.309} See [[person/jahangir-mughal|Jahangir]].

## Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb
Arslan relates that Khurram, son of Jahangir, rose against his father with the commander Mahabat Khan, that peace followed, and that he took the throne after his father as Shah Jahan, sending his son Aurangzeb to the Deccan to put down rebels. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 pp.309-310} A dispute among Shah Jahan's sons over the throne ended with Shah Jahan confined and Aurangzeb victorious in 1659, ruling alone until 1707. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.310} He says Aurangzeb took the kingdoms of Golconda and Bijapur in 1685 and captured Sambhaji, son of Shivaji, king of the Marathas, and added Assam in 1660 and Arakan in 1666. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.310} He holds that the Mughal state never reached such might and breadth as in Aurangzeb's days, its frontiers running from Kabul to Arakan and from the Himalaya to the Karnatic. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.311} He adds that European historians report harshness in his taxation and in pressing Brahmins to adopt Islam, the turning of some of their temples into mosques and the renaming of Benares as Muhammadabad, and also that he lived as an ascetic, fasting and watching over his afterlife. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.311} See [[person/shah-jahan-mughal|Shah Jahan]], [[person/aurangzeb-mughal|Aurangzeb]], [[place/golconda|Golconda]] and [[place/bijapur|Bijapur]].

## Fragmentation and the end
After Aurangzeb, Arslan says, many kingdoms split from Delhi: the state of Hyderabad of the Deccan in 1724, the state of Bengal in 1707 and the state of Awadh in 1724, leaving the Mughals only Delhi, Agra and the Doab, while the Sikhs and Marathas became independent. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.311} He adds that the Sikhs took Lahore and the Punjab in 1764 as Delhi weakened. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.312} He cites the Encyclopaedia of Islam that Nadir Shah's invasion of India in 1739 broke the back of the Mughal state, and that Ahmad Durrani then took Delhi and the Punjab and defeated the Marathas at the third battle of Panipat on 7 January 1761. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 pp.312-313} See [[person/nadir-shah-afshar|Nadir Shah]], [[person/ahmad-shah-durrani|Ahmad Shah Durrani]], [[place/lahore|Lahore]], [[place/punjab|the Punjab]], [[place/hyderabad|Hyderabad]] and [[place/awadh|Awadh]].

He relates that Shah Alam was forced after a war with the English to leave the diwani, the collection of revenue in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, to the English, was blinded in 1788 by one of his officers, and was allotted 90,000 rupees a month with only the name of sultan left to him; he died in 1806. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.313} Muhammad Akbar followed from 1806 to 1837, then Bahadur Shah from 1837 to 1857, neither holding more than the name of sultan, with an allowance from the English East India Company. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.313} Bahadur Shah took part in the revolt of 1857, was seized by the English and held at Rangoon, where he died in 1862, the last of the sultans of Delhi in Arslan's account. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.313} See [[person/shah-alam-ii|Shah Alam II]], [[person/bahadur-shah-zafar|Bahadur Shah Zafar]] and [[person/muhammad-shah-mughal|Muhammad Shah]].
