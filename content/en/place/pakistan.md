## Overview
Pakistan is the name of a state that came into being after the age of the sources this page rests on, so the old geographies and travel books do not use it, and it does not appear in Hadir al-Alam al-Islami, the survey by Lothrop Stoddard with Shakib Arslan's commentary, written in the 1920s and 1930s. They name the lands that lie within its borders today by their own names: Sind, Multan and Makran, then the Punjab and Lahore. This page gathers what each source says in the voice of its own time, and begins with the picture Arslan drew of the Punjab and the north-west frontier of India. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 scan p.322}

## Sind and the conquest of Muhammad ibn al-Qasim
Speaking of the Hindu Kush and the mountains round it, Arslan says that from them the Arab commander [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-qasim|Muhammad ibn al-Qasim al-Thaqafi]] came down in early Islam, raided India, conquered Sind and reached the borders of Multan, dating this to 712 CE. He adds that Sultan Mahmud ibn Sabuktigin of Ghazna came down from the same mountains in the early eleventh century CE, and that the rulers of India massed against him in a battle he calls Patindah. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 scan p.202} For the early chronicles' account of the conquest of Sind see [[event/conquest-of-sind-89|the conquest of Sind]]; the Gregorian date here is the one Arslan gives.

## Multan in al-Qazwini
Zakariyya al-Qazwini, who died in 682 AH, has an entry on [[place/multan|Multan]] in Athar al-Bilad wa Akhbar al-Ibad. He calls it the last city of India toward China, a great, strongly fortified city held in esteem by the people of China and India; its people are Muslims and unbelievers, it lies within the Muslim state, and the unbelievers have in it the great dome and the chief idol-house, with the congregational mosque adjoining the dome. He takes this from Mis'ar ibn Muhalhil. {cite:athar-al-bilad-qazwini p.120}

From al-Istakhri he quotes that it was the seat of the king and the gathering place of the army, and that its king was a Muslim who entered the city only on Fridays, riding an elephant, to attend the Friday prayer. He then describes an idol the Indians revere and travel to from the farthest parts of their country, offering great sums every year; its house is a palace with a dome in the middle, and he gives, from Mis'ar ibn Muhalhil, the measurements of the dome and of the idol. He reports that the king of Multan does not abolish the idol because the wealth brought to it goes to him, and that when Indians march against them in war the Muslims bring the idol out and make as if to break or burn it, whereupon the Indians turn back. {cite:athar-al-bilad-qazwini p.120-121} All of this is description he takes from earlier geographers; he does not claim to have seen it himself.

The same entry says that Harun ibn Abd Allah, a client of the Azd, was from Multan, a brave man and a poet, and tells how, in a war between the Indians and the Muslims, his clash with an elephant armed with a sword became the cause of the Indians' defeat, as the entry relates it. {cite:athar-al-bilad-qazwini p.121-122}

## Mansura and the river Mihran
Al-Qazwini has an entry on "al-Mansura" in [[place/sind|Sind]]: a famous and bountiful city built, he says, by al-Mansur Abu Ja'far, the second of the Abbasid caliphs; governors reside there; a channel from the river Mihran encircles it so that it sits in the middle like an island, very hot and full of gnats; and it has two fruits found in no other city. {cite:athar-al-bilad-qazwini p.123-124} He describes the Mihran as wide as the Tigris or wider and, quoting al-Istakhri, says it appears at Multan, passes Mansura and falls into the Persian Sea below Sind, with crocodiles as in the Nile. {cite:athar-al-bilad-qazwini p.124}

Of Makran he says only that it is a district between the land of Sind and the land of Tiz, with large towns and villages, and he repeats from the book Tuhfat al-Ghara'ib a wonder about a one-piece stone bridge there; it belongs to the marvel-lore he passes on without claiming to have seen it. {cite:athar-al-bilad-qazwini p.272} See [[place/makran|Makran]].

## Ibn Battuta in the land of Sind
[[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]] came to Sind on his way to India. In passing he says that the river valley they call the Sind river is known as "Panj Ab". {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.191} Elsewhere he calls Multan the capital of the land of Sind. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.385} He then describes Multan as the seat of the land of Sind and the residence of its chief amir; on the road to it lies a large river that can be crossed only by boat and where travellers' baggage is searched. When he arrived the levy was a quarter of what merchants brought and seven dinars on each horse; two years after reaching India the sultan lifted these levies and ordered that only zakat and the tenth be taken. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.414}

He describes the court of Multan's amir, Qutb al-Mulk, and the muster of troops there, where archers and horsemen were tested and ranked by their marksmanship before being enrolled in the army. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.415} He says that visitors from Khurasan and beyond the Oxus lodged there awaiting permission, that the sultan's chamberlain arrived two months after their own arrival to receive one of them, and that between Multan and the sultan's court was forty days of continuous settlement. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.416} The first town they entered after Multan was Abuhar, which he calls small, fine, well built and watered by streams. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.418}

Before Multan he passed through Siwistan, a large city with desert and sand outside it and no trees except the umm ghaylan; its people eat millet and peas, from which they make their bread, and also dairy and fish. He says its preacher showed him a letter from the Commander of the Faithful Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz to his remote ancestor appointing him to preach in the city, and that the family has inherited the post since. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.409} He afterwards tells of an uprising there by some amirs against the governor the sultan had appointed, and of the severe punishments inflicted by the governor of Sind, and describes what he saw of its aftermath. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.410-411}

He describes Lahari as a fine city on the shore of the great sea where the Sind river meets it, with a great harbour visited by people from Yemen and Persia, which swelled its revenues. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.412} He reports from its governor, the amir Ala al-Mulk, that its yearly revenue was sixty lakhs, and describes a plain near it strewn with stones shaped like people and animals, which, its governor told him, historians hold to be the remains of a city whose people were turned to stone. He then went to Bhakkar, a fine city cut by a channel from the Sind river. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.413} From there he went to [[place/uch|Uch]], a large city on the Sind river with good markets, where he met Shaykh Qutb al-Din Haydar al-Alawi, who invested him with the Sufi cloak. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-ihya-al-ulum-1987 p.414}

## The Punjab and Lahore: Ghaznavids, Mongols, Sikhs
In its chapter on the history of the Indian Muslim kingdoms, Hadir al-Alam al-Islami says that Mahmud of Ghazna began his campaigns with the taking of Multan in 1005 CE, entered [[place/lahore|Lahore]] in 1024, deposed its ruler and annexed the whole Punjab, which was, in the book's phrase, "the first station on the road of Islam to India". {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 scan p.293} After Mahmud's death the Seljuks appeared in Khurasan and cut into the Sabuktigin dynasty's realm, so its princes moved from Ghazna to Lahore, until the Afghan Ghurids overcame their rule. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 scan p.295}

The book says that a hundred thousand Mongols from beyond the Oxus marched on the Punjab in 1297 and that Ala al-Din defeated them near Lahore, and that Timur entered the Punjab in 1398 on his way to Delhi. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 scan p.298-299} Babur raided the Punjab in 1525. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 scan p.301} It says Akbar was born in Sind on 15 October 1542 and that Sind was joined to the Delhi sultanate in his reign, in 1592. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 scan pp.304, 306} It says the Sikhs seized Lahore and the Punjab in 1764 when Delhi grew weak. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 scan p.317}

Among Mughal buildings it lists the mosque of Lahore built in Jahangir's time and the Wazir mosque at Lahore. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 scan p.325-326}

## Muslim population in the 1920s
Arslan says the highest share of Muslims among Indian provinces was on the north-west frontier, where Muslims were ninety-three percent of the population, and that in the Punjab they were more than half. This describes British India as its author saw it, not a state then in existence. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 scan p.322}
