## Overview
When [[person/faraj-ibn-barquq|Faraj]] moved his father [[person/barquq|Barquq]]'s body to [[building/farag-ibn-barquq-complex|his khanqah]] in the Eastern Cemetery (1400-1411), he began the urbanisation of its northern end, and mausolea, madrasas and khanqahs followed there throughout the fifteenth century. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.202} Yeomans thinks the motive was not only Faraj's example but the belief that those buried near the holy shaykhs would share their blessing. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.202}

## A living quarter
As in the Southern Cemetery, the shaykhs' tombs drew pilgrims, and the annual route to Mecca passed through the area, so mosques, madrasas and khanqahs went up with lodgings for visitors, students and Sufis; the necropolis became more like a residential suburb, and it is still a thriving community where tombs stand beside housing, shops and streets. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.202} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.204} Yeomans insists it was never a "city of the dead", as Europeans called it, but a living quarter, as the generous lodgings of the Inal and Qurqumas complexes show. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.213} Lodgings were also needed for patrons and visitors at the saints' feasts, the mawlids. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.217}

## Monuments
It holds the [[building/farag-ibn-barquq-complex|khanqah of Faraj ibn Barquq]], the [[building/khanqah-mausoleum-of-sultan-barsbay|Barsbay complex]] with the tomb of Ganibak al-Ashrafi, the [[building/funerary-complex-of-sultan-inal|Inal]] and [[building/emir-qurqumas-complex|Qurqumas]] complexes, and the [[building/qaytbay-complex-cairo|Qaytbay complex]]. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.203}
