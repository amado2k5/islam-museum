## Overview
The Deccan, which Hadir al-Alam al-Islami writes al-Dakan and al-Dakhan, is a region of southern India, and on the museum's card a plateau. This page rests on what that work says: an Arabic translation of Lothrop Stoddard's survey with notes by Shakib Arslan, in the edition the museum's source record dates to 1934, in its chapter on the history of the Indian Muslim kingdoms, where Arslan says he relied for most of this account on the French historian René Grousset. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.295} It does not describe the plateau or its physical character, so this page says nothing beyond what is said of the region and its kingdoms. It is a twentieth-century account of earlier history.

## The Deccan and the Delhi sultanate
The book says the seat of the sultanate was moved from Lahore to Delhi because Lahore holds only the lands of the Punjab, while Delhi calls to the rule of all the Ganges plains and the provinces of the Deccan. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.292} It says no conqueror before had reached the Deccan: neither Alexander, nor Mahmud of Ghazna, nor Muhammad of Ghur, none of whom got there in their campaigns. It says that in 1308 Ala al-Din Khalji sent his commander Malik Kafur to conquer the kingdom of the Deccan, that the Maratha raja withheld tribute and his land was invaded, and that the kingdom of Telingana was invaded, its capital Warangal taken by storm and its king's treasuries seized. In 1310 the kingdom of Mysore was invaded and the great city of Halebid sacked, and on the return to Delhi the Maratha raja, who had rebelled again, was killed and the Marathas were annexed to the sultanate of Delhi. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.293} See [[person/ala-al-din-khalji|Ala al-Din Khalji]] and [[person/malik-kafur|Malik Kafur]].

It then says that the Turkish Tughluqs took power in 1321, that one of them, Muhammad, became known for harshness and oppression and angered Hindus and Muslims alike, and that each amir withdrew to a kingdom of his own: one king in the Deccan, one in Malwa (the volume's Malfa), one in Bengal, one in Gujarat and one in Oudh, leaving the government of Delhi only a land the text writes as Dawab, and the Punjab. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.293} The book then lists the Muslim kingdoms of India as the sultanate of Delhi and the kingdoms of the Deccan, Bengal, Jaunpur, Malwa and Gujarat. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.294}

## The Bahmani kingdom
The book says the greatest kingdom to split from the Delhi empire was the Deccan, founded by the Bahmanis, whom it calls Afghans, with its capital at Kulbarga, that is Gulbarga, near Hyderabad, and containing what is now the Nizam of Hyderabad's domain together with Berar and the Maratha country. Beside it lay two strong Hindu kingdoms, Warangal and Vijayanagar at the southern end of the Deccan, so that the sultanate of the Deccan was, in its words, a constant jihad against them. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.294} {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.295} It adds that Ahmad I the Bahmani, whose name the page reads as Ajad, took Warangal in 1424 and destroyed it, and that Bahmani power peaked under Muhammad II, from 1463 to 1482, through his vizier Mahmud Gawan, who took Goa from the kingdom of Vijayanagar and the coasts of Sirkar from the raja of Orissa; in his days the authority of the Deccan stretched from the Bay of Bengal to the Arabian Sea. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.295} See [[place/gulbarga|Gulbarga]], [[place/bahmani|the Bahmanis]] and [[person/ahmad-shah-i-bahmani|Ahmad Shah I Bahmani]]; the identification of the name as Ahmad is the page author's estimate, not the text's.

## The five states
The book says that after the death of Muhammad II the sultanate split into five independent states. The first was the Barid Shahis at Bidar, from 1490, whose kings, it says, were of Georgian origin. The second was the Imad Shahis at Berar, from 1484 to 1572, of Hindu origin who became Muslims. The third was the Nizam Shahis at Ahmadnagar, from 1496 to 1600, whose founders were also converted Hindus. The fourth was the Qutb Shahis at Golconda, from 1512 to 1687, of Persian origin. The fifth was the Adil Shahis at Bijapur, from 1489 to 1686, whose founder Yusuf Adil is said to have been a son of the Ottoman sultan Murad II, carried by fate after a long stay in Persia to India, where he was devoted to the Persians and to Shi'ism, unlike his Ottoman family, spread Persian letters and made Shi'ism the state religion, followed in this by his son Ismail. The book reports this with the wording "it is said", and the writer of this page has not checked it. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.295} See [[place/ahmadnagar|Ahmadnagar]], [[place/golconda|Golconda]], [[place/bijapur|Bijapur]], [[place/deccan-sultanates|the Deccan sultanates]] and [[person/ali-adil-shah-i|Ali Adil Shah I]].

Arslan compares the five quarrelling states to the party kings of Andalusia, to the kings of Egypt and Syria after Saladin and to the kings of Italy before its unification, and says wars among them almost never ceased, which helped their Hindu neighbours and strengthened the raja of Vijayanagar, who once overran Ahmadnagar on the pretext of helping Bijapur. The two quarrelling kings made peace, the kings of Bidar and Golconda joined them, and the four marched on Vijayanagar, killed its raja at the battle of Talikota and destroyed his capital in 1565. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.295} See [[event/battle-of-talikota-1565|the Battle of Talikota]].

## The Deccan and the Mughals
The book says that Emperor Akbar resolved in 1595 to take Ahmadnagar, after absorbing what remained of the Muslim kingdoms of India, and that the Deccan was still divided among five kingdoms until Ahmadnagar annexed Bidar in 1572 and reduced the number to four. At the head of Ahmadnagar stood a sultana whom the book calls Shanda and nicknames the White of the Deccan, praising her resolve and daring; she drove Akbar back defeated, and he could not take Ahmadnagar until after her death, annexing it in 1600, whereupon the other kings of the Deccan, alarmed, hurried to Delhi with submission. Akbar's reign ended in 1605. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.301} See [[person/chand-bibi|Chand Bibi]] and [[person/jalal-al-din-akbar|Akbar]]; linking the sultana the book calls Shanda to Chand Bibi is the page author's estimate, not the text's.

## Hyderabad Deccan in the 1930s
In the first volume Arslan describes the principality of Hyderabad Deccan: thirteen million people, mostly Hindus, though the capital is mostly Muslim and its ruler is a Muslim called the Nizam, with a resident minister from the British whose influence is limited. It has an army of thirty thousand, mostly Arabs of Hadramawt, pays Britain a small tribute and has almost complete internal independence. He also mentions a dispute in recent years between the Nizam and Britain over a large province the Nizam claims as his, and says he does not know how it ended. He says the Nizam is the wealthiest of Muslim rulers, that he settled on Caliph Abd al-Majid, whom the Kemalists drove into exile in Europe, three hundred pounds a month, and that ties between them grew in early 1932 with the marriage of the caliph's daughter to the Nizam's son. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.335} See [[place/hyderabad-deccan|Hyderabad Deccan]].

## Hadramis in the Deccan
In the part of volume three headed "Information on the Hadramis" the writer says Hadramis settled in Indian towns including Hyderabad Deccan and Bijapur, and that the first of them to enter India did so in the year 617, the text not naming the calendar. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 p.162} He adds that among the main factors in founding the kingdom of Malik Ambar were the counsels of al-Habib Ali ibn Alawi al-Haddad, and that Amir Johar Sakhrati was a student of Shaykh Shaykh ibn Abd Allah al-Aydarus and was buried in the cemetery of the sayyids and Arabs below the city of Bijapur. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 p.177} He mentions Sayyid Umar ibn Abd Allah Ba Shayban al-Alawi, who died in 1066 at Belgaum in India, to whom Adil Shah had granted the revenue of a village near Belgaum, after he had lived for a while beside Malik Ambar, king of the Deccan. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 p.178} These are the section's reports on the genealogies and influence of the Alawis, and are attributed to its author, not to the museum.

## A note on the source
Hadir al-Alam al-Islami is in origin a survey of the Muslim world of the 1920s and early 1930s; Arslan's notes are his own view, and its Gregorian dates in this chapter are taken from a European historian whose book was not read. The date of the end of the Barid Shahis came through the scan in an unreliable form and is not written here, and names that were not clear were left out. The page does not describe the Deccan plateau as geography and does not go beyond 1605 for the Deccan.
