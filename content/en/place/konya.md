## Overview
Konya, the Iconium of antiquity, stands on the edge of the plateau of [[place/anatolia|Anatolia]], on the diagonal line that joins the Dardanelles to the Taurus passes into Syria. Its great age was under the [[polity/rum-seljuk-sultanate|Seljuks of Rum]], who made it their capital, and it is the city of [[person/jalal-al-din-rumi|Jalal al-Din Rumi]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 287}

## Before the Seljuks
In the centuries of the Arab raids Konya was a Byzantine military base, which the attackers seem to have avoided on purpose in favour of other routes; the article's author, Cl. Cahen, takes this to explain why it is so seldom named in the military histories. No archaeological work covers the period, but the wide reuse of older materials in the Seljuk monuments suggests that many buildings lay in ruins and that the town had been rebuilt only in part. The [[event/crusaders-pass-konya-490|Crusaders who passed in 1097]] found no usable fortifications, though they drew on the gardens around the town. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 287}

## The Seljuk capital
The site was well watered, unlike the desert near it, and it commanded the southern route at a time when the [[polity/danishmendid-emirate|Danishmendids]] barred the Seljuks from the northern one. Its real growth dates from the reign of [[person/masud-i-rumi|Mas'ud]], 512-50/1118-55, who [[event/konya-masud-capital-512|resolved to make it a capital]] and built a mosque and other monuments there. It was already a city of note when the German army of [[person/frederick-barbarossa|Frederick Barbarossa]] passed through in 1190. By then it had, besides the chief mosque, smaller mosques, madrasas, Sufi lodges, busy markets and rebuilt walls. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 287}

Cahen describes a town in which rank showed in the size and height of houses, not in separate quarters for each people. There were Greeks, with a monastery said to be dedicated to Plato, Armenians and some Jews. The records speak less of Turks, a word kept for the Turcomans of the plains, than of Muslims: local Turks, much Iranised in such a town, and Iranian immigrants who came above all after the Khwarazmian and Mongol invasions and who sometimes rose to the highest civil posts. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 287}

He singles out three groups. The igdish were a militia recruited from the sons of local converts and Turkish mothers. The [[group/akhi-associations|akhis]] were a Turkish form of the futuwwa, important above all under the Mongols and their first successors. Among the Sufis there were several orders; from the middle of the thirteenth century the standing of Rumi must have drawn many disciples, though they did not become an organised order, the [[group/mawlawiyya|Mawlawiyya]], until the fourteenth. The walls were extended and strengthened under the great sultan [[person/kayqubad-i|'Ala' al-Din Kayqubad]], with the forced help of the senior amirs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 288}

## Decline under the Mongols
The Mongol protectorate over Seljuk Anatolia in the second half of the thirteenth century harmed Konya, slowly and in part. In the rivalry of the brother sultans 'Izz al-Din Kayka'us and Rukn al-Din Qilij Arslan, Konya was the headquarters of those who wished to resist the Mongols and who leaned on the Turcomans of the southern and western frontiers. As central authority weakened, the Turcoman principalities of the borders gained, and political life moved away from the city. The first sign was the [[event/konya-jimri-karamanid-675|occupation of Konya in 675-7/1277-8]] by the Karamanids of the western Taurus, who set up there a false Seljuk known as Jimri. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 288}

Its cultural and religious standing and the size of its population kept the town alive, as the buildings and pious foundations of Karamanid princes and dignitaries show. Bayezid Yildirim occupied it, and in 880/1475 it was [[event/ottoman-annexation-konya-880|annexed for good to the Ottoman state]] with the rest of the Karamanid lands; Ottoman archives show that the endowments and institutions of the Seljuks and Karamanids were in general kept up. In so large an empire Konya could be no more than a provincial centre. It was still under its walls, at the end of 1832, that the Egyptian army of [[person/ibrahim-pasha-misri|Ibrahim Pasha]] [[event/battle-of-konya-1832|crushed the Ottoman army]] of Reshid Pasha. The population, about 50,000 at the end of the nineteenth century, was 119,841 in 1960. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 288}

## The citadel and the great mosque
Some eighty monuments survive, the article's second part records, many so heavily restored that only fragments of the first decoration remain. The city walls were destroyed within living memory; travellers recorded the antique and Seljuk carving built into them, especially near the 108 towers. The ruined kiosk of Qilij Arslan II, once a square chamber clad in ceramics above a vaulted hall, stands in the citadel area. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 288}

Beside it is the Alaeddin mosque. The oldest part is the area around the mihrab, dated by an inscription of Sultan Mas'ud to about 1155; the western half of the prayer hall was finished in 617/1220, and the eastern pillared hall was added in 1235. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 288} Joined to the mosque is a twelve-sided tomb of 616-17/1219-21, which from 1192 was the mausoleum of the dynasty, except for Kayka'us I, who is buried at [[place/sivas|Sivas]]. The courtyard wall has two marble portals in a style developed from that of twelfth-century Damascus. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 289}

## The shrine of Rumi
The article calls the [[building/mevlana-celaleddin-rumi-tomb|Mevlevi complex]] the city's greatest spiritual memorial. The lodge began in 628/1231, and the earliest hall, which holds the poet's cenotaph, is dated 672/1273-4, the year [[event/rumi-dies-konya-672|Rumi died]]. The wooden cenotaph of 674/1277 is covered in inscriptions and lies under a gold-embroidered cloth given by [[person/abd-al-hamid-ii-uthmani|Abdulhamid II]]. Most of the complex is of the sixteenth century: Bayezid II restored the tombs and added a minaret in 910/1504, and [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Suleyman I]] rebuilt the mosque and the hall of the ritual dance in 973/1565. The nearby Selimiye mosque, begun by Suleyman and finished by [[person/selim-ii-uthmani|Selim II]], has been wrongly attributed to [[person/mimar-sinan|Sinan]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 289}

## Madrasas
The [[building/sircali-madrasah|Sircali Madrasa]] of 640/1242-3 keeps a fine faience mihrab. The [[building/karatay-madrasa|Karatay Madrasa]] of 649/1251-2 keeps its marble portal and a dome patterned with stars over the pool of its hall, and now houses Seljuk ceramics. The [[building/ince-minaret-medrese|Ince Minareli Madrasa]] of about 663/1264-5 lost two thirds of its brick minaret in 1317/1899; of its portal, wrapped in giant bands of inscription, the article remarks that heavy decoration hiding the structure was the weak point of later Seljuk architecture. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 289} Around the city stand caravanserais of the thirteenth century, each with a portal, a court and a hall, all partly ruined. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 290}

## In the Pleiades gazetteer
Pleiades, the open gazetteer of ancient places, lists this place under the title «Iconium/Claudiconium» (Pleiades no. 648647), classed as settlement and archaeological site, with activity attested from 9000 BCE to 2100 CE. Recorded names include «Iconium», «Claudiconium» and «Qūniya». Its description calls it a city of importance in Lycaonia, later part of the Roman province of Galatia. {cite:pleiades 648647}
