## Summary
Central Asia and the Caucasus form a region whose Muslims are mostly Sunni and Hanafi, according to The Oxford Dictionary of Islam. {cite:oxford-dictionary-of-islam e414}

## In The Oxford Dictionary of Islam
The Oxford Dictionary of Islam groups Central Asia with the Caucasus and says the two regions have much in common. For the Caucasus it lists Abkhazia, Georgia, Dagestan, Chechnya, Azerbaijan and Armenia; for Central Asia in the strict sense, Turkmenistan, Tajikistan, Kyrgyzstan, Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan. Its account is that the Muslims of both regions are largely Sunnis of the Hanafi legal school, that Shiism appears among the Tats, Ironis and Azeris of Dagestan, that most of the population is Turkic, and that the Tajiks are Indo-Iranian in descent and speech. In its telling, Arab armies had established Muslim rule over the east Caucasus by about the year 650; merchants then carried the faith further between 800 and 1200; and by about 1550 the Crimea, the steppes of southern Russia and Kazakhstan, and western Siberia were governed by Islamic law. Russian conquest began in the 1500s. Sufi brotherhoods, the Dictionary adds, preserved the faith while Russia ruled and in many districts directed the resistance; from the Qadiri brotherhood came several militant Sufi organizations that fought Russian authority by force until the Soviet collapse and saw that fight as jihad. {cite:oxford-dictionary-of-islam e414}
