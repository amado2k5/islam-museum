## Overview
Tiberias, in Yaqut al-Hamawi's Mu'jam al-buldan, is a small town looking over the Lake of Tiberias, at the edge of a mountain with Mount Tabor above it, part of the district of [[place/jordan|Jordan]] at the end of the Ghawr; it lies three days from [[place/damascus|Damascus]], the same from Jerusalem and two days from [[place/akka|Acre]], and stretches along the lake with little breadth. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 v.4 p.17} He places it in the third climate, at longitude 57 degrees 45 minutes from the west and latitude 32 degrees. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 v.4 p.17}

For the conquest he reports that Shurahbil ibn Hasana took it in 13 AH by treaty, for half of the people's houses and churches; another account has him besiege it for some days and then make peace on their lives, property and churches except what they left, reserving a place for the Muslims' mosque. They then broke the agreement in 'Umar's caliphate and a body of Byzantine stragglers joined them, so Abu 'Ubayda sent 'Amr ibn al-'As with four thousand men, who took it on the same terms as Shurahbil's treaty; all the towns of Jordan were opened on this treaty without fighting. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 v.4 p.17}

Yaqut quotes Abu al-Qasim that a Roman king named Tabara first built it and it took his name, and that it has hot salt springs with bathhouses that need no fuel; he mentions the hot spring of Sulayman ibn Dawud between it and Baysan, which the local people hold to cure every ailment. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 v.4 pp.17-18} From Abu 'Abdallah ibn al-Banna he quotes a description of it as the capital of Jordan, narrow and stifling in summer and unhealthy, about a farsakh long, with eight bathhouses and a large congregational mosque in the market, a great bridge below it on the Damascus road, drinking water from the lake, and plenty of fish and boats; he also passes on a local saying that its people spend two months of the year each on fleas, bugs, wasps, heat, sugar cane and mud. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 v.4 pp.17-18} The relational adjective is Tabarani, irregular, to tell it from Tabaristan; the best known person so named is the hadith master Sulayman ibn Ahmad Abu al-Qasim al-Tabarani, born in Tiberias in 260 AH, who reached Isfahan in 290 AH and stayed there until his death in 360 AH, and whose books include the Mu'jam al-Kabir, al-Awsat and al-Saghir. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 v.4 pp.18-19}

Among the visited graves Yaqut lists those the townspeople say are of Sulayman ibn Dawud, Luqman the Wise, Abu 'Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah and Abu Hurayra, among others; for most he notes that other places also claim the same person's grave, and leaves the true answer to God. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 v.4 pp.18-19}
