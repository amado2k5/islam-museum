## Overview
Edirne, the Adrianople of the classical and Byzantine world, lies at the confluence of the Tunca and the Arda with the Meriç, also written Maritsa. It was the capital of the [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottomans]] after [[place/bursa|Bursa]], and the Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose article this page follows, calls it the traditional centre of Turkish, now Eastern, Thrace. Its importance comes from its position on the main road from Asia Minor to the Balkans, where it is the first major staging point after [[place/istanbul|Istanbul]]. It guards the eastern entrance to the corridor between the Rhodope and Istranca mountains, and it was once the starting point of river traffic down the [[place/maritsa|Meriç]] to the Aegean. The article calls it particularly rich in Ottoman monuments, and says its standing fell when the capital moved to Istanbul and again when the Russians took it in 1829. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 710}

## Setting, names and people
The city lies inside a bend of the Tunca on ground that rises to 75 metres at the hillock carrying the Selimiye mosque; the lower slopes have often flooded. It has two parts: Kale-içi, the walled district to the west, whose walls have almost gone and which was rebuilt on a geometric plan after a fire at the end of the nineteenth century, and Kale-dışı to the east, the centre of the modern city. Ottoman sources write the name Edrinaboli, Endriye, Edirne or Edrine, and use honorific names such as Abode of Divinely-Aided Victory and Abode of the Sultanate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 710}

The article puts the population at over 100,000 in the middle of the nineteenth century and 87,000 at the start of the twentieth, of whom about 47,000 were Turks, 20,000 Greeks, 15,000 Jews, 4,000 Armenians and 2,000 Bulgarians. It fell to 34,528 in 1927 and 29,400 in 1945, and has risen since. Since the Balkan Wars Edirne has been a Turkish frontier city; it was briefly under Bulgarian occupation in 1913 and Greek occupation from 1920 to 1922. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 710}

## Before the Ottomans
The article says Thracian tribes probably settled the site first, that the Macedonians took it and named it Oresteia, and that the Emperor Hadrian rebuilt it as Hadrianopolis. It saw Constantine defeat Licinius in 323 and the Goths defeat Valens in 378, and it was besieged by the Avars in 586, taken by the Bulgars in 914 and besieged by the Pechenegs in 1049 and 1078. Turks from Asia Minor appear in 1342-3, when Umur Bey of Aydin fought as an ally of Cantacuzenus and defended [[place/didymoteicho|Dimetoka]] against the local lord of Edirne. In 754/1353 the Ottoman prince Süleyman Pasha joined Cantacuzenus in Edirne after defeating a Bulgar and Serb army. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 710}

## Conquest and forward capital
The [[event/conquest-of-edirne-763|conquest]] was carried out under [[person/murad-i-uthmani|Murad I]] by Lala Shahin Pasha, who defeated the lord of Edirne at Sazlıdere. The lord fled by boat, and in Ramadan 763/July 1362 the townspeople surrendered on condition that they could live there freely. Murad I left the administration to Lala Shahin and for a time held court at Bursa or Dimetoka, but Edirne almost at once became the forward base of Ottoman expansion in Europe. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 710} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 711}

Bayezid I set out from Edirne to besiege Constantinople. After his defeat at [[event/battle-ankara-804|Ankara]], his son Süleyman moved the treasury there and took the throne; he lost the city to Musa Çelebi, who also ruled from it and struck coins there. Mehmed I spent most of his reign in Edirne and died there. The Pretender Mustafa was executed there in 825/1422 after his defeat by [[person/murad-ii-uthmani|Murad II]], under whom the city and its surroundings prospered; he received ambassadors and directed campaigns from it, and the circumcision feasts of his sons were held on the island in the Tunca. A mutiny of the [[group/janissaries|Janissaries]] set off by a fire was settled by raising their pay. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 711}

[[person/mehmed-ii|Mehmed II]] returned only to plan the siege of Constantinople, drawing up the plans there and testing the siege guns nearby. After the [[event/conquest-constantinople-857|conquest]] he again held court there, and [[person/selim-i-uthmani|Selim I]] did too. Süleyman the Magnificent often stayed, and the city's greatest mosque was built under his successor, though mutinies in 994/1586 and 1003/1595 disturbed it. From the time of Ahmed I it was known for royal hunts and festivities, most brilliantly under [[person/mehmed-iv-uthmani|Mehmed IV]], the Hunter. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 711}

## Decline
The article ties the city's later life to Ottoman defeats. In the [[event/edirne-incident-1703|Edirne incident]] of 1115/1703, [[person/mustafa-ii-uthmani|Mustafa II]], holding court there, was deposed in favour of [[person/ahmed-iii-uthmani|Ahmed III]] by malcontents from Istanbul. The [[event/edirne-fire-1158|fire of 1158/1745]] burned some sixty quarters, and an earthquake followed in 1164/1751. Albanian troops mutinied in 1801 against the reforms of [[person/selim-iii-uthmani|Selim III]], and a second incident came in 1806; the abolition of the Janissaries caused only minor difficulties. After the Russian occupation of 1828-9, which the article says deeply affected the Muslims, many Muslims left and Christians from nearby villages replaced them. [[person/mahmud-ii-uthmani|Mahmud II]] visited for about ten days and ordered a bridge over the Meriç, finished in 1842 under [[person/abd-al-majid-i-uthmani|Abd al-Majid]]. The [[event/russian-occupation-edirne-1878|occupation of 1878-9]] and the Balkan wars brought further devastation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 711}

## Castle and palaces
Of the castle, whose four towers and nine gates are known by name, only the Clock Tower, once the Great Tower, remains. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 711}

Murad I found the lord's palace inadequate and built the Old Palace outside the castle, moving in during 767/1365-6. [[person/evliya-celebi|Evliya Çelebi]] says it was walled, rectangular and without gardens of its own, and later a barracks for recruits; in Süleyman's Hungarian campaigns it could house 6,000 pages. Murad II built the New Imperial Palace in 854/1450 on an island in the Tunca, partly with marble from ruins near Salonica. Mehmed II planted the island with trees and bridged it to the main buildings, and Süleyman added a bridge to the city and extensions. By the end of the eleventh/seventeenth century it had 18 pavilions, 8 small mosques, 17 large gates, 14 baths and 5 courts, and six to ten thousand people lived in it. It decayed in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries despite restorations. The Russians camped in its gardens in 1829, and the second occupation ended it: the Ottomans fired its ammunition stores before leaving and quarried the ruins for stone on their return. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 711}

## Mosques
The first Friday prayers were held in a converted church in the castle, later called the Halebiye. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 711} The oldest surviving mosque is Yıldırım's, of 801/1399; in 1878 the Russians stripped its tiles and the two marble rings that gave it the name Ear-ring Mosque. The [[event/eski-cami-edirne-804|Eski Cami]], the Old Mosque, was begun in 804/1402 by Emir Süleyman and finished in 816/1413 under Mehmed I. A stone said to come from a corner of the Kaaba was set in the window beside its mihrab and has been venerated since. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 712}

Murad II built the [[event/muradiye-edirne-839|Muradiye]] first as a house for Mevlevi dervishes and then turned it into a mosque. The [[event/uc-serefeli-edirne-841|Üç Şerefeli]], the Three-Balconied Mosque, was begun in 841/1437-8 and finished in 851/1447-8; Evliya Çelebi says it was paid for from the booty of [[place/izmir|Izmir]]. A great dome rests on six columns, its courtyard is regarded as the first built by the Ottomans, and its three-balconied minaret is called the first of that kind. The article adds that Fakhr al-Din Ajami publicly condemned there the Hurufi followers of Fadl Allah Tabrizi, who were believed to enjoy the sympathy of Mehmed the Conqueror. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 712}

[[person/bayazid-ii|Bayezid II]] built beside the Tunca a mosque, baths, a hospital, a madrasa and an almshouse, a [[event/beyazidiye-edirne-890|complex]] dated 893/1488 and paid for from booty taken at Akkerman. The mosque is simple, its dome resting on the four walls. The hexagonal hospital had rooms for isolating patients, who, Evliya Çelebi says, were regularly made to listen to music. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 712}

The article names the architect [[person/mimar-sinan|Sinan]] as author of the most beautiful sixteenth-century monuments of the city. Three of his mosques still stand, including that of Shaykhi Çelebi and the [[event/selimiye-edirne-977|Selimiye]], called the glory of Edirne and its last royal mosque. The Selimiye was built between 972/1564-5 and 982/1574-5, at a cost Evliya Çelebi gives as 27,760 purses from the booty of Cyprus. The article says its dome, on eight columns, is six cubits higher than that of Hagia Sophia. Four three-balconied minarets stand at its corners, and it forms one whole with a madrasa, a Qur'an reciters' house, a school and a clock-house. The madrasa is now a museum of antiquities and the reciters' house an ethnographic museum. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 712}

## Learning, markets and government
Edirne was an important centre of Islamic learning with an independent course, like Istanbul and Bursa, and had madrasas beside those of its mosques. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 712} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 713} Markets were built largely to support pious foundations, among them the covered market of Mehmed I and the Arasta that Sinan built for Murad III to support the Selimiye. Sinan also built khans for Rüstem Pasha and Sokollu. Trades included dyeing, tanning, soap-making, attar-of-roses distilling and carriage-building, and the city had its own style of bookbinding. Water came by the aqueduct of 937/1530, and the oldest of its bridges, that of Ghazi Mikhal, dates from 823/1420. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 713}

A qadi and a subaşı first governed the city. Edirne was a crown domain of the sultans, yielding nearly two million aspers in the early tenth/sixteenth century, and was the seat of a Greek Orthodox metropolitan and a chief rabbi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 713}

## Sufis and poets
With more than fifty lodges, Edirne produced many dervish shaykhs, among them the [[group/mawlawiyya|Mevlevis]] Jalal al-Din and Jamal al-Din under Murad II. The article lists poems praising its beauty, including a refrain poem by the local poet Hayali and an ode by [[person/nef-i|Nef'i]]. Hibri of Edirne wrote a history of the city for 1359-1633 in 1636, still unpublished when the article was written. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 713}

## From capital to frontier city
The Ottomans took Edirne under Murad I and made it their forward base for expansion in Europe; the plans for the siege of Constantinople were drawn up there and its great guns tested nearby {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 pp. 710-711}. The city kept its standing after the capital moved to Istanbul: sultans still founded imperial mosques and complexes there, the last and greatest being Sinan's Selimiye of 1569-75 {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p. 73}.
