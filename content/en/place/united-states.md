## Overview
The United States is a country located mainly in North America, according to the place card. It appears in two books read for the museum, Ali Muhammad al-Salabi's *al-Dawla al-Uthmaniyya* (2001) and the Arabic *Hadir al-Alam al-Islami* with the notes of Shakib Arslan, in scattered guises: a land beyond the ocean named in the reports of the Ottoman navy; a source of missions in Syria and of societies at the Cairo missionary conference; a colonial power ruling Muslims in the Philippines; a state that the books bring into Muslims' talk of world politics; and a place of Muslim communities and newspapers. These are the statements of later authors, attributed to them. No entry for the United States was found in Yaqut's *Mu'jam al-Buldan* or al-Qazwini's *Athar al-Bilad*. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.368} {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.363}

## The Americas in Ottoman naval reports
Al-Salabi says that [[person/khayr-al-din-barbarossa|Khayr al-Din Barbarossa]] passed the Strait of Gibraltar and fell on Spanish and Portuguese ships returning from the American lands loaded with gold and silver, which shook the Christian world. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.221}

He says that Piri Reis, commander of the Ottoman navy and a geographer (1465 to 1554 according to the book), drew two maps, one of Spain, West Africa, the Atlantic and the eastern coasts of the Americas, which he presented to Sultan Selim I in Egypt in 1517 and which is kept in the Topkapi museum, and the other of the Atlantic coasts from Greenland to Florida. He calls Piri Reis's map of America the oldest map of it, and says that a radio symposium held at Georgetown University in the United States on 26 August 1956 had its geographers agree that it was an extraordinary discovery. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.503} {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.504} He adds that Piri Reis wrote in his *Kitab-i Bahriye* that the continent was found in 870 AH (1465), some twenty-seven years before Columbus. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.504} These are the author's assertions; neither Piri Reis's date nor the symposium has been checked here.

## Missions and missionary societies
In al-Salabi's account Ibrahim Pasha opened the door in Syria to French and American missions, and some writers count 1834 as a turning point, when American missions expanded, the American mission press was moved from Malta to Beirut and a girls' school was founded in Beirut by Eli Smith and his wife. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.368} This is what the author relates while criticising Ibrahim Pasha's policy.

Arslan's notes summarise the missionary Zwemer's call for united missionary effort and say that the first step towards it was the Cairo conference of 1906, at which sixty-two missionaries and as many invited guests sat on behalf of twenty-nine societies of Europe and America whose aim was the evangelising of Muslims, followed by two further conferences, at Lucknow in India and in Edinburgh. This is Arslan's account of what Zwemer wrote. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.279}

## The Philippines under American rule
The Philippines take up a whole section of Arslan's notes. He says the Filipinos rose against Spain in 1896 and again with the help of the United States, that the American government fought Spain and took the Philippines, Cuba and Puerto Rico, and that the Philippines then came under United States authority and rose against it in turn. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.358} {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.360}

Arslan says the Americans, it seems, wanted to win over the Muslims of the Philippines to strengthen themselves against the Catholics; a former governor came to Istanbul about twenty years earlier and asked the Ottoman government to send guides to teach the Philippine Muslims, and the Islamic shaykhate sent one of its officials, Wajih Efendi Zayd al-Kilani al-Nabulsi, whom the Muslims received with joy and whom the Americans helped; he then fell ill, was brought back to Istanbul, and died later. He says Wajih put the number of Muslims there at one to two million. This is Arslan's account, from a friend. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.358} {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.359}

Arslan also relays, from the French *Revue du Monde Musulman* and John Foreman's English book *The Philippine Islands*, that the Americans on taking the islands did not touch Islamic beliefs or try to break Muslim law although they disliked the Muslims' arrangements of government; that the American government held that the people of the Muslim province should govern themselves, and was distinguished from Spain by training the people in democratic principles; and that it took care to organise Muslim affairs and found municipalities for them. He gives the occupation force on Sulu alone as 4,839 men and 294 officers, and in the province forty-one schools with 2,114 pupils, fifteen American teachers, fifty native Christian teachers and nine Muslim teachers, with 240 Muslim children waiting for places. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.363} See [[place/philippines|the Philippines]] and [[place/sulu|Sulu]].

In the third volume he quotes the writer Chatelier in the *Revue du Monde Musulman* that when the Americans took the Philippines, milder rulers than the Spanish, the movement of the Muslim province of Sulu changed to trade from what it had been; the printed text has the unclear word "debauchery" and the sentence before speaks of the pirates of Sulu. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 p.367}

## The United States in Muslims' talk of the world
In Arslan's notes the Allies asked Turkey only for neutrality, to which Mr Morgato, as printed, the American ambassador in Turkey at the outbreak of the war, bore witness in his memoirs according to Arslan. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.330} {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.331}

In an interview in Cairo with a Muslim scholar from [[place/china|China]], imam of Tientsin and lodging at the Kulshani lodge, reproduced from *al-Ahram* and signed by three journalists, he was asked what his people felt towards foreign states. He said Japan was strong and inclined to swallow them, so they disliked it, whereas the state truly beloved among them was the United States, on whose model they were weaving everything and whose dealings with them had been in words and writings rather than war or seizure of their land. This is the speaker's view alone. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.268} {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.269}

In the chapter on Egypt, the talks between Lord Milner and Saad Zaghlul in the autumn of 1920 produced, by the newspapers' account, proposals including that Egypt's independence be equal to Cuba's in relation to the United States. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.134}

## Muslim communities and the press
The book quotes Chatelier's article in the *Revue du Monde Musulman*, continued from the passage cited above in its third volume, that there are Syrian Arabs, Muslim and Christian, in the United States and Argentina, and that there are Muslim communities in Australia, Brazil and Vancouver. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 3 p.368} In a passage on the Islamic press it says that newspapers are also published in the United States, Brazil and Argentina in Syrian Arabic and Albanian, and that the tone of the Islamic papers of the East, of America and of Berlin is marked by sharp hostility, according to the text. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.313}

## Turkey and the United States after the Second World War
Al-Salabi says that after the Second World War Turkey drew closer to the United States, entered treaties with her and America set up military bases on Turkish soil, and that the Democratic Party adopted American policy and Celal Bayar became president of the republic; the book prints 1374 AH for this, a year that has not been checked here. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.479}

## Note on the sources
This page shows the United States from the angle of the Muslim world as two authors of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries saw it; it does not replace a history of the United States or of Muslims there. The page numbers of *Hadir al-Alam al-Islami* are the printed ones, read from each page's running head. Al-Salabi's claim about the accuracy of Piri Reis's map of Antarctica is left unrelayed, as a large assertion that has not been checked.
