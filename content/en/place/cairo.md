## Overview
Cairo is the capital of Egypt {cite:geonames-gazetteer 360630}. The [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimids]] founded it beside Fustat, and the museum's data date its founding to 969 CE {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1001} {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1030}. [[person/al-maqrizi|Al-Maqrizi]] says that the commander [[person/jawhar-al-siqilli|Jawhar]] reached its site with the troops of [[person/al-muizz-li-din-allah|al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah]] in Sha'ban 358 AH, settled there and laid out the palace, and ran a wall of mud brick around the palace and the mosque {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1030} {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1067}. He reports another account that it was laid out in Jumada II 359 AH {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1030}. Jawhar meant it to be a fortress between the Qarmatians and the city of Misr {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1030} {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1031}. He called it al-Mansuriyya, until al-Mu'izz arrived from the Maghrib and named it al-Qahira {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1067}. Its mosque was called "the mosque of Cairo" and [[building/al-azhar|"al-Jami' al-Azhar"]] {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1032}.

Al-Maqrizi counts it as the fourth place in Egypt to which the seat of rule moved in the Islamic period, after Fustat, al-Askar and al-Qata'i' {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1001}. It became the caliphal residence, where the caliph lived with his household and his closest people; after the Fatimids, Saladin and those who ruled after him lived there, until al-Kamil moved from it to the Citadel of the Mountain, and it became a city of residence after it had been a fortress {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1001}. Al-Suyuti says that Jawhar built in it the seat of government for al-Mu'izz, known as the Two Palaces {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p. 620-621}. Golius writes that people came to the new city from every side and it grew, until it became, as it were, the seat of all Egypt and a market for East and West {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 398}.

## Founding and the Fatimid city
Jawhar, the commander of al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah, reached the site of Cairo on 17 Sha'ban 358 AH, settled there and laid out the palace, and built the first wall, of mud brick, around the palace and the mosque. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1030} {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1067}

Jawhar called that first wall al-Mansuriyya, until al-Mu'izz arrived from the Maghrib and named the city al-Qahira. It is said that the astrologers chose an hour in which Mars was "the conqueror of the sphere" (qahir al-falak), and that this gave the city its name. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1067}

Accounts differ on the date of the laying-out. Al-Maqrizi gives Jawhar's arrival as 358 AH and reports another account that the city was laid out in Jumada II 359 AH. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1030}

Al-Maqrizi counts Cairo as the fourth place in Egypt, in the Islamic period, to which the seat of rule moved, after Fustat, al-Askar and al-Qata'i. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1001}

Cairo became the caliphal residence, where the caliph lived with his household and his closest people. After the Fatimids, Saladin and his sons and the rulers after them lived there, until al-Kamil moved from Cairo to the Citadel (Qal'at al-Jabal). Cairo then became a city of residence, after it had been a fortress in which one was held. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1001}

## For Sprouts
Cairo is Egypt's historic capital, the city of a thousand minarets, home to the noble al-Azhar Mosque and vibrant heritage.

## For Explorers
Cairo is an ancient city on the banks of the Nile River in Egypt, founded by the general Jawhar al-Siqilli in the Fatimid era in 358 AH near historic Fustat. Called the City of a Thousand Minarets for its abundant mosques and colleges like al-Azhar and Sultan Hasan, it flourished under the Ayyubids and Mamluks as a hub of commerce and scholarship.

## For Scholars
The historic capital of Egypt and one of the supreme metropolitan centers of Islamic civilization, situated along the eastern bank of the Nile. Founded as 'al-Qahira al-Mu'izziyya' in 358 AH (969 CE) by the Fatimid commander Jawhar al-Siqilli north of earlier settlements at Fustat and al-Qata'i. Following the fall of Baghdad in 656 AH, Cairo became the preeminent political and intellectual capital of the central Islamic lands under the Ayyubids and Mamluks, hosting the symbolic Abbasid caliphate, al-Azhar University, and monumental stone-carved colleges and hospitals controlling Mediterranean-Red Sea trade.
