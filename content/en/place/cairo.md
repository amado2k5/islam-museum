## Overview
Cairo is the capital of Egypt {cite:geonames-gazetteer 360630}. The [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimids]] founded it beside Fustat {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1001} {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1030}; the museum's data date its founding to 969 CE, a year not on al-Maqrizi's pages. [[person/al-maqrizi|Al-Maqrizi]] says that the commander [[person/jawhar-al-siqilli|Jawhar]] reached its site with the troops of [[person/al-muizz-li-din-allah|al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah]] in Sha'ban 358 AH, settled there and laid out the palace, and ran a wall of mud brick around the palace and the mosque {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1030} {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1067}. He reports another account that it was laid out in Jumada II 359 AH {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1030}. Jawhar meant it to be a fortress between the Qarmatians and the city of Misr {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1030} {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1031}. He called it al-Mansuriyya, until al-Mu'izz arrived from the Maghrib and named it al-Qahira {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1067}. Its mosque was called "the mosque of Cairo" and [[building/al-azhar|"al-Jami' al-Azhar"]] {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1032}.

Al-Maqrizi counts it as the fourth place in Egypt to which the seat of rule moved in the Islamic period, after Fustat, al-Askar and al-Qata'i' {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1001}. It became the caliphal residence, where the caliph lived with his household and his closest people; after the Fatimids, Saladin and those who ruled after him lived there, until al-Kamil moved from it to the Citadel of the Mountain, and it became a city of residence after it had been a fortress {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1001}. Al-Suyuti says that Jawhar built in it the seat of government for al-Mu'izz, known as the Two Palaces {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p. 620-621}. Golius writes that people came to the new city from every side and it grew, until it became, as it were, the seat of all Egypt and a market for East and West {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 398}.

## Location
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, article al-Kahira, calls Cairo the capital of Egypt and one of the most important centres of religious, cultural and political life in the Muslim world. The city lies on both banks of the Nile, about 20 km south of the delta, where the Muqattam hills almost reach the river. The site had been inhabited from early times, and it became of first importance at the Arab conquest of 22/643, when [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr b. al-As]] laid the foundations of a permanent encampment at [[place/al-fustat|al-Fustat]]. The article derives the present name from Misr al-Qahira, a town founded in 359/970 by the Fatimid caliph [[person/al-muizz-li-din-allah|al-Mu'izz]], which gradually absorbed the places around it. The remains of al-Fustat lie in the quarter of the modern city called Old Cairo. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 445}

Its approximate coordinates are 30.0626 N and 31.2497 E {cite:geonames-gazetteer}.

## What this page covers
This page gathers what the museum links to the place: events, people and buildings. It is not a full historical account of the place; a description of the place and its history from original sources will be added in later batches.

Golius is reported to say that Jawhar, the Fatimid commander, built Cairo in 360 AH as the seat of the Fatimid state north of Fustat. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 397} The Fatimids named it al-Qahira, intending a link to Fatima, the daughter of the Prophet. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 398}

In 564 AH Shawar ordered a fire set in Fustat and Misr to keep the Franks from taking it, and the Franks demanded a million dinars for peace. When Alexandria and Damietta were taken back from the Franks, Saladin ringed Cairo with a stronger wall. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 398}

## Topography and growth
The article stresses that Cairo grew horizontally, spreading outward rather than piling layer on layer as other Middle Eastern cities did. The first enclosure of al-Qahira stood well north of al-Fustat, al-Askar and al-Qata'i. It was meant as a seat of government away from the main districts of habitation, and it held a palace complex, the barracks of the Fatimid armies and the new congregational mosque of [[building/al-azhar|al-Azhar]]. For a time old walled Cairo, with Qasr al-Sham' and Babalyun, and al-Fustat stayed important as industrial centres, with the chief river port and the main tenement blocks. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 445}

The Nile shifted steadily westward and al-Qahira drew people as the seat of government, so the population moved north. The mud-brick walls of Jawhar, begun in 358/969, were enlarged twice: by [[person/badr-al-jamali|Badr al-Jamali]] between 480/1087 and 484/1091, and by Baha' al-Din Qarakush under [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Salah al-Din]] between 572/1176 and 589/1193. The first enlargement, said to have been prompted by fear of an attack by the Saljuq Atsiz, was the smaller. The second took in wide areas between Jawhar's walls and the Nile, now recalled only in quarter names such as Bab al-Luq and Bab al-Khalq. By the middle of the 6th/12th century much of al-Fustat lay abandoned. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 445}

The Citadel, begun for Salah al-Din in 572/1176, was the first major expansion outside the Fatimid enclosure. The article says it was built not to fortify the city but as a place of refuge. Its water came from the Nile by an aqueduct whose present form dates from the reign of [[person/al-nasir-muhammad-ibn-qalawun|al-Nasir Muhammad]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 445}

Under the Bahri Mamluks building went on mostly outside the Fatimid walls, while foundations inside them were almost all funerary or royal. The great Mamluk thoroughfares date from this time, among them the Darb al-Ahmar running from the Citadel to [[building/bab-zuwayla|Bab Zuwayla]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 445}

Under the Ottomans growth turned west of the walled city, above all to Bulaq, which became the principal port. Residential districts followed at Giza and Imbaba, at Shubra, where Muhammad Ali built a palace by 1850, and then at Abbasiyya, Garden City and Heliopolis. The walled area of al-Qahira stayed the seat of government and, once the Ezbekiyya quarter received a European-style plan in the 19th century, the centre of commerce as well. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 446}

## Founding and the Fatimid city
Jawhar, the commander of al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah, reached the site of Cairo on 17 Sha'ban 358 AH, settled there and laid out the palace, and built the first wall, of mud brick, around the palace and the mosque. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1030} {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1067}

Jawhar called that first wall al-Mansuriyya, until al-Mu'izz arrived from the Maghrib and named the city al-Qahira. It is said that the astrologers chose an hour in which Mars was "the conqueror of the sphere" (qahir al-falak), and that this gave the city its name. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1067}

Accounts differ on the date of the laying-out. Al-Maqrizi gives Jawhar's arrival as 358 AH and reports another account that the city was laid out in Jumada II 359 AH. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1030}

Al-Maqrizi counts Cairo as the fourth place in Egypt, in the Islamic period, to which the seat of rule moved, after Fustat, al-Askar and al-Qata'i. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1001}

Cairo became the caliphal residence, where the caliph lived with his household and his closest people. After the Fatimids, Saladin and his sons and the rulers after them lived there, until al-Kamil moved from Cairo to the Citadel (Qal'at al-Jabal). Cairo then became a city of residence, after it had been a fortress in which one was held. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1001}

## The Fatimid palace, walls and mosques
The Fatimid palace was the political, religious and administrative centre of al-Qahira from the 4th/10th to the 6th/12th century. It has vanished completely. Ravaisse made a tolerable reconstruction from al-Maqrizi's description, and the article judges that it almost certainly consisted of several separate dwellings, each built around a qa'a, a long hall with two axial iwans and a sunken central area. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 449}

Jawhar's mud-brick walls had disappeared by al-Maqrizi's day, which the article puts down to pressure of population on the enclosure rather than to the weakness of the material. The Fatimid fortifications that remain, the article says, date from the time of the caliph al-Mustansir. They include [[building/bab-al-futuh|Bab al-Futuh]] and [[building/bab-al-nasr|Bab al-Nasr]] of 480/1087 and Bab Zuwayla of 484/1091. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 449}

The earliest mosque of al-Qahira was al-Azhar, dated 359-61/970-2 and laid out on much the same lines as the mosque of [[building/masjid-ibn-tulun|Ibn Tulun]]. It was not founded as a teaching institution, but a little more than a year after its foundation it had become the centre for spreading the Fatimid da'wa. The article sees this change of function, together with the growth of population inside the enclosure, as the likely reason for the founding of the [[building/mosque-of-al-hakim|mosque of al-Hakim]], begun by al-Aziz in 380-1/990-1 and finished by al-Hakim no later than 403/1012. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 449} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 450} Almost every important ruler from al-Hafiz to [[person/qaytbay|Qa'itbay]] reorganised or added to al-Azhar, and [[person/qansuh-al-ghawri|al-Ghawri]] gave it a second minaret. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 450}

Of the other Fatimid mosques, al-Akmar, of 519/1125 and the work of the vizier al-Ma'mun al-Bata'ihi, has the earliest decorated facade in Cairo. Its facade follows the street rather than the line of the qibla, the first case of what became standard Cairene practice. The mosque of al-Salih Tala'i, of 555/1160, was built outside the walls of Badr al-Jamali as a shrine for the head of al-Husayn. The head was instead placed in a shrine inside al-Qahira, on the site of the present mosque of Sayyidna al-Husayn, the work of a 19th-century architect. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 450}

The Fatimid caliphs were buried inside the palace, and their tombs were destroyed when the dynasty fell. More characteristic of the period are the mashhads of the late 5th/11th and 6th/12th centuries in the southern cemetery, built over supposed graves of descendants of Ali and meant as centres of pilgrimage. The most precisely dated is that of Sayyida Ruqayya, of 527/1133. The mashhad of al-Juyushi on the Muqattam, of 478/1085, is the mausoleum of Badr al-Jamali. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 450}

## The Ayyubids
The Citadel was the largest Ayyubid fortification ever undertaken, set on a spur of the Muqattam. Al-Malik al-Adil strengthened it in 604/1207-8 with three great square towers. The article gives its purpose as internal defence, against a possible Fatimid counter-attack or a rising of the people of al-Qahira. It adds that al-Maqrizi says the stone came from the pyramids at Giza, though most of it is soft Muqattam limestone cut on the spot. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 450} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 451} A second fortress with the same purpose, the Qal'at al-Rawda, was built on the island of Rawda by [[person/al-malik-al-salih-ayyub|al-Salih Najm al-Din Ayyub]] in 638/1240-1. There he lodged the garrison of Mamluks known as the Bahri, who eventually supplanted the Ayyubid dynasty. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 451}

No Ayyubid mosque survives in Cairo. The article suggests that the Ayyubids relied more on the [[concept/madrasa|madrasa]] to counter the Fatimid da'wa, and it notes that the mosque of Amr was the only one allowed to hold the Friday sermon in their time, which it reads as an attempt to reduce the importance of al-Azhar as a centre of Fatimid propaganda. One of Salah al-Din's first foundations was a Shafi'i madrasa beside the grave of the Imam [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]], begun in 572/1176-7. Only its cenotaph of 574/1178-9 remains. The present [[building/mausoleum-of-imam-al-shafii|mausoleum]] of 608/1211, with its wooden dome, was built by al-Malik al-Kamil. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 451}

The best preserved Ayyubid madrasa is the double madrasa of al-Salih Najm al-Din Ayyub on the main street of Fatimid Cairo, of 641/1243-4. Its mausoleum, completed in 648/1250, is described as the first conspicuous funerary foundation of Cairo, built as part of the street facade. This approach became so standard in Mamluk Cairo that where the qibla and the street facade conflicted, the facade often won. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 451}

## The Mamluks
The earliest Bahri Mamluk mosque is that of [[person/baybars|Baybars]] in the Maydan al-Zahir, of 665-7/1266-9, with a very large square maqsura. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 452} On the Citadel, al-Nasir Muhammad built a large mosque, with a foundation inscription of 718/1318, and a palace, the Qasr al-Ablaq, destroyed in 1824. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 451} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 452} From 715/1315 amirs, not sultans, founded most of the new mosques, chiefly along the roads running from the Fatimid walls toward the Citadel. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 452}

The mosque of [[building/masjid-sultan-hasan|Sultan Hasan]], begun in 757/1356, is described as outstanding for its scale and height. It is a vast cruciform madrasa for the four madhhabs with a palatial tomb chamber behind the qibla iwan. The article reports that its porch is now confirmed as inspired by the Saljuq Gok Medrese at Sivas. The building was left unfinished at Hasan's death. [[building/al-muayyad-mosque-cairo|Al-Mu'ayyad Shaykh]] copied its facade for his mosque at Bab Zuwayla and took its great bronze doors. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 452}

The article holds that Mamluk madrasas in Cairo were almost always funerary foundations, the founder's mausoleum being attached and usually placed to face the street. They did not train officials as the metropolitan madrasas of Ottoman Istanbul did. The first cruciform madrasa built for all four rites was that of al-Nasir Muhammad, of 695/1295 to 703/1304. The two surviving hospitals are those of [[person/qalawun|Qalawun]], of 683/1284, and of al-Mu'ayyad, of 821-3/1418-20, which was turned into a mosque in 825/1421-2. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 453} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 454}

Cairo was rich in quasi-monastic foundations: rawiyas, khanqahs and ribats. The earliest khanqah in Egypt was Ayyubid, of 569/1173-4. The article records that the khanqah of Baybars al-Jashankir required its Sufis to give up salaried posts. From the time of al-Mu'ayyad Shaykh the stress shifted to communal dhikr and daily attendance. The author notes that the sources say little about which tariqa a given khanqah served. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 454}

Of the Burji period, the article rejects the common view of decline from the Bahri age. It sees a change of taste toward richer surface ornament, technical ingenuity in making up for scarce marble, and an apogee of Mamluk stonework in the domes of Faraj b. Barquq's khanqah. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 455} Qa'itbay, the most considerable Burji builder, built no mosques that were not mainly some other institution. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 453} Al-Ghawri placed his mausoleum so that it reached from the street to the qibla wall, and he also built a market hall that became the [[building/khan-al-khalili|Khan al-Khalili]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 455} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 456}

Great houses and palaces were built around a qa'a with wind-catchers facing north, and were often added to over time. Of the Mamluk palaces the best preserved is that of the amir Yushbak. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 455} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 456} Khans lodged foreign merchants; the Wikalat al-Nakhla, bearing al-Ghawri's name, is the best preserved, and wikalas were organised by trade and by the nationality of their merchants. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 456}

## The Ottoman period
The article calls most claims that the Ottoman conquest disrupted Cairo's architecture prejudiced or based on slight acquaintance. In its view the Mamluk tradition persisted strongly, and the Istanbul style appeared only now and then. It explains the scarcity of large Ottoman foundations by a tax fixed under the governor Khadim Sulayman Pasha, which left the governors little money, and by their preference for founding great complexes in Istanbul. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 456}

The most common commemorative foundation of the period was the sabil-kuttab, a public fountain with a Qur'an school above it. The best known is that of Abd al-Rahman Katkhuda, of 1157/1744, which the article calls an elegant Mamluk pastiche. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 457} The mosque of Sulayman Pasha on the Citadel dates from 935/1528, and that of Malika Safiyya from 1019/1610 is the closest to the mosques of Istanbul. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 456} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 457} Tekkes of the Mawlawiyya and the Rifa'iyya, and a Bektashi convent at the foot of the Muqattam, also survive. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 458}

[[person/muhammad-ali-pasha|Muhammad Ali]] built on the Citadel a mint in 1227/1812, the palaces of al-Jawhara and the Harim, and finally the mosque of Muhammad Ali, finished in 1265/1848. The article says its plan rests on that of the Yeni Cami in Istanbul, despite frequent claims to the contrary. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 457}

## The cemeteries
The great southern cemetery, al-Qarafa al-Kubra, had been the main burial ground of Cairo since the conquest by Amr. In Fatimid times it was the centre of a considerable cult, which was made acceptable to Sunni orthodoxy when a madrasa was founded at the tomb of al-Shafi'i soon after the Ayyubid conquest. It holds the shrines of [[building/sayyida-nafisa-mosque|Sayyida Nafisa]] and Sayyida Ruqayya, the mausoleum of Shajar al-Durr, of about 648/1250, and the enclosure and mausoleum of the Abbasid caliphs, possibly as early as 640/1242-3. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 446} The northern or north-eastern cemetery, called misleadingly the Tombs of the Caliphs, holds large Burji foundations such as the complex of Faraj b. Barquq, of 803/1400 to 813/1411, and the funerary complex of Qa'itbay, of 877-9/1472-4. The article sees the growth of these cemeteries as a result of pressure on building space in the city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 449}

After the Ottoman conquest the development of the cemeteries ended. The Istanbul custom of small cemeteries beside pious foundations was not taken up, and most Turks who died in Egypt were buried in existing Mamluk enclosures. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 449}

## Restorations
Almost all the standing monuments have been restored, most recently under the Comité de conservation des monuments de l'art arabe. The article notes that many earlier restorations were not recorded, and it explains the rarity of restoration inscriptions by a Mamluk preference for founding a new building in one's own name over mending another's. It also lists major earlier repairs, among them the restoration of the mosque of Ibn Tulun by Lajin in 696/1296 and repairs after the earthquake of 702/1303. Its closing judgement is that concern for the monuments of others was not a pronounced Cairene trait. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 458} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 459}

## The modern city
The article says modern Cairo took shape under Muhammad Ali, who ruled from 1805 to 1849. It treats the reign of Khedive Ismail, from 1863 to 1879, as a watershed for town planning, the press and Egyptian nationalism. The railway to Alexandria was built in 1856 and extended to Suez in 1858. The Suez Canal opened in November 1869, an Opera House was built, and new quarters such as Ismailiyya were laid out on a Parisian model. From 1874 the ruler left the Citadel for a new palace. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 462} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 463} Heliopolis arose in 1906 and Maadi from 1907. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 463}

After the First World War Egyptian nationalism became a popular movement; a measure of independence was proclaimed in 1922 under King Fu'ad, and the first state university opened in Cairo in 1925. The Free Officers' revolution of 23 July 1952 abolished the monarchy. After the British withdrawal the large Midan al-Tahrir was laid out, and the Nile corniche gave the city a riverside facade for the first time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 462} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 464}

The article cites an estimate of 245,000 inhabitants at the end of the 18th century, 678,433 in 1907, 1,070,857 in 1927 and 3,348,799 for the whole metropolitan area in 1960. It attributes the rapid rise to migration from the countryside. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 464} Culturally it lists the University of Cairo, Ain Shams, the Arabic Language Academy founded in 1932, over twenty museums, among them the Museum of Islamic Art in its present building since 1903, and new mosques since 1952. It adds that a law of 1961 reshaped al-Azhar, giving it secular faculties alongside its older ones. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 465}

The article describes the city as dual in character, truly Egyptian and at the same time cosmopolitan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 463} It warns that a lack of suitable legislation was letting historic sites be swamped by housing. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 464}

## For Sprouts
Cairo is Egypt's historic capital, the city of a thousand minarets, home to the noble al-Azhar Mosque and vibrant heritage.

## For Explorers
Cairo is an ancient city on the banks of the Nile River in Egypt, founded by the general Jawhar al-Siqilli in the Fatimid era in 358 AH near historic Fustat. Called the City of a Thousand Minarets for its abundant mosques and colleges like al-Azhar and Sultan Hasan, it flourished under the Ayyubids and Mamluks as a hub of commerce and scholarship.

## For Scholars
The historic capital of Egypt and one of the supreme metropolitan centers of Islamic civilization, situated along the eastern bank of the Nile. Founded as 'al-Qahira al-Mu'izziyya' in 358 AH (969 CE) by the Fatimid commander Jawhar al-Siqilli north of earlier settlements at Fustat and al-Qata'i. Following the fall of Baghdad in 656 AH, Cairo became the preeminent political and intellectual capital of the central Islamic lands under the Ayyubids and Mamluks, hosting the symbolic Abbasid caliphate, al-Azhar University, and monumental stone-carved colleges and hospitals controlling Mediterranean-Red Sea trade.
