## Overview
Iznik is the ancient and Byzantine Nicaea, a town in north-western Anatolia that the Encyclopaedia of Islam says is called Nikiya by Ibn Khurradadhbih and al-Idrisi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 309} Administratively it is now the centre of a district in the province of Bursa; see [[place/bursa-province|Bursa province]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 309} Its place in this museum rests on three things the article treats: its changing hands between Muslims, Byzantines and Crusaders, its early Ottoman monuments, and its pottery.

## From Byzantium to the Ottomans
According to the article, the Arabs besieged Nicaea in vain in their first campaigns against Byzantium in 99/717 and 107/725. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 309} See [[event/siege-of-nicaea-727|the siege of Nicaea in the 720s]]. At the beginning of 1081, in the middle of 473 AH, it fell to the Seljuk [[person/sulayman-ibn-qutlumush|Sulayman, son of Qutlumush]], who made it his residence. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 309} The article reports that the first Crusaders, under Walter Sans-Avoir, were severely defeated before Nicaea in 489/1096 by Sulayman's son and successor, whom it calls Alp Arslan. The next year the town could not withstand the Crusaders under [[person/godfrey-of-bouillon|Godfrey of Bouillon]] and surrendered on 5-6 Rajab 490, 19-20 June 1097, to the Byzantines allied with the Crusaders, in whose hands it remained until the Ottoman invasion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 309} See [[event/siege-of-nicaea-1097|the siege of Nicaea in 1097]]. The article on Anatolia adds that after the Fourth Crusade conquered Constantinople in 1204 the Byzantines under Theodore Lascaris founded a Greek counter-empire in western Anatolia with Nicaea as its capital. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 488}

The Ottoman sultan Uthman is said to have attacked Nicaea, but it was Orkhan who took it, after a prolonged siege, in 731/1331, and he moved his capital there for a time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 309} In 804/1402 it was taken and devastated by a raiding body of Timur's troops, but it soon recovered and is described as flourishing at the time of Prince Mustafa's rebellion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 309} Bayezid II is said to have intended, after the death of Mehmed II, to renounce the throne and retire to Nicaea. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 309}

## Decline and the present town
The article dates the decline of the town from about the middle of the 11th/17th century. It gives the population as about 10,000 according to Grelot, about 1,500 by the end of the 19th century, and 6,290 in 1960. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 309} The present town occupies a small part of the area inside the ancient walls. The best preserved ancient buildings are the Roman and Byzantine walls, a double rampart with monumental gateways and 238 towers according to Texier. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 309} The Byzantine part of the defences dates from Leo III the Isaurian, who built it after the Arab invasion of 726; Michael III in 858 and later Theodore Lascaris completed and improved them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 309}

## Early Ottoman monuments
The article calls the town very rich in Islamic monuments, some dating from early Ottoman times. Orkhan converted the church of Hagia Sophia into a mosque soon after the conquest, and its qibla wall was covered with faience tiles when it was redecorated in the early 11th/17th century; see [[building/hagia-sophia-iznik|Hagia Sophia, Iznik]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 309} Orkhan also built next to it a madrasa, which the article calls the first in the Ottoman Empire; the museum's record of a madrasa at Iznik is [[building/suleyman-pasha-madrasa-iznik|the Süleyman Pasha madrasa]], and the article does not itself say that the two are the same. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 309} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 310}

Another mosque from Orkhan's reign is the Hajji Ozbeg mosque, the earliest Ottoman mosque whose original dating inscription survives, giving 734/1333; see [[building/haci-ozbek-mosque-iznik|the Hajji Ozbeg mosque]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 310} Excavations in 1963 and 1964 showed that a small mosque outside the walls bearing Orkhan's name dates from 735/1334, not from before the conquest as once believed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 310} The mosque and tomb of Hajji Hamza Beg were built in 746/1345 and 750/1349. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 310} None of these early mosques had minarets. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 310}

The later mosques the article lists are the Green Mosque of 780/1378 to 794/1391, the mosque of Qutb al-Din of about 821/1418, the mosque of Mahmud Celebi of 846/1442 and the mosque of Eshrefzade-i Rumi of 874/1469, all with minarets in the Seljuk style. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 310} Of these the article thinks the Green Mosque the most significant: it has faience tile decoration inside and on the minaret, and its carved marble mihrab is the earliest of its kind in Ottoman mosque architecture. It says Khayr al-Din of the Candarli family had it built and names the architect as Hajji Musa. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 310} See [[building/yesil-mosque-iznik|the Green Mosque]]. Among the few surviving secular buildings the article mentions the Hajji Hamza bath of the late 8th/14th or early 9th/15th century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 310}

## Pottery
The article says Iznik was once a flourishing pottery centre. Excavations between 1963 and 1966 established that the so-called Miletus ware was made there, with a red clay body and designs in blue, turquoise-green and purple. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 310} This red ware was suddenly replaced, apparently under outside influence, by a refined white-bodied faience resembling porcelain, which Arthur Lane divided into three groups: the earliest, of the late 9th/15th and early 10th/16th centuries; a second with purple and green added; and a third, from about the middle of the 10th/16th century, in which a lively red appears. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 310} Excavated shards, kiln wasters and kilns show that all three groups were made locally. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 310} Tiles and pottery were still being made in 1736, after which production ended and was forgotten, and potters seem to have moved to Kutahya. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 310} See [[concept/iznik-pottery|Iznik pottery]].

## What the sources leave open
The articles read give no coordinates for the town and no population figure earlier than the 11th/17th century. The approximate coordinates on this record are not from the Encyclopaedia of Islam and should be replaced from a gazetteer.
