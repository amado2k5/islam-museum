## Overview
Tunis, in Arabic also Tunus or Tunas, is the capital of modern [[place/tunisia|Tunisia]]. Like ancient [[place/carthage|Carthage]], it lies at the base of a large gulf, sheltered from northerly and north-westerly winds, where the western and eastern Mediterranean meet, and it stands at the crossing of natural routes serving the different regions of the country. The Encyclopaedia of Islam stresses that the two cities are often confused but were built on distinct sites. Carthage, founded in the ninth century BC by seafarers from Tyre, lay on the coast. Tunis, founded by the Arab conquerors at the end of the seventh century AD, sits inland, beside a shallow lagoon, on a hill that slopes gently to the east and rises above the Sedjoumi salt flat to the west. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 680}

## The older city of Tunes
The article says Arab Tunis was not made from nothing. It replaced an older city, Tunes, and kept its name, which is generally held to be Berber and to mean a halt or encampment. If so, the article reasons, the town was probably founded by the indigenous population, and it finds credible the idea that Tunes is older than Carthage, although its earliest evidence comes from a time when Punic Carthage was already great. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 680}

Tunes is attested from the beginning of the fourth century BC. According to Diodorus, in 395 BC two hundred thousand Libyans rose against Carthaginian rule and seized it. Greek and Roman historians name it repeatedly, as occupied by Agathocles in 310, Regulus in 256, the Libyans of Matho in 240, Scipio Africanus in 203 and Scipio Aemilianus in 146 BC. It drew strength from its position and fortifications, and the article thinks it logical to place it on the hill later occupied by the Arab citadel, the kasba. Having sided with Carthage, it was destroyed at the end of the Third Punic War, but a smaller city rose again under Roman rule. It appears on the fourth-century map known as Peutinger's Table as Thuni, perhaps a copyist's slip for Thunis, and as a Christian city it was a bishopric. The article concludes that for all its antiquity Tunes never played more than a modest role in the shadow of its great neighbour. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 680}

## Foundation as an Arab city
At the end of the seventh century the Arabs completed their conquest of eastern Barbary by taking Carthage. They did not settle there. Fearing that a Byzantine fleet might suddenly return, they demolished its walls, cut its aqueducts, filled in its harbours and ruined its farmland, and settled instead at the base of the lagoon, at the edge of Tunes. The small town, filled with troops and new buildings, soon looked like an Arab foundation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 681}

Arab historians, the article says, date its birth to the year 80 of the Hijra, which it places in 699, shortly after the fall of Carthage in 698, and credit [[person/hassan-ibn-al-numan-al-ghassani|Hassan ibn al-Nu'man]] with founding it. On the orders of the Umayyad caliph [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan]], Hassan set up an arsenal there, and a thousand Coptic labourers from Egypt were soon employed building ships, so that the Byzantines could be opposed on land and sea, as al-Bakri reports. The article cautions against reading the Arab claim that Hassan or a successor brought the sea to Tunis as a canal through the lake, a work realised only at the end of the following century. It judges it likely that he dug a channel through the coastal strip between lake and sea at Halk al-Wadi, today La Goulette, so that ships built in the arsenal could reach open water. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 681}

## A garrison city under Kairouan
In its early centuries Arab Tunis had a military role. Troops were kept there permanently to face a landing or to put to sea and raid Christian shores, and several of the many eighth-century naval expeditions sailed from it. Arabs mixed there with Berbers who had accepted Islam, willingly or under pressure, and a new people formed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 681}

For a long time Tunis stayed secondary to [[place/qayrawan|al-Kayrawan]], the capital and seat of the governors named by the [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad]] and [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid]] caliphs and, from the ninth century, of the [[polity/aghlabid-emirate|Aghlabid]] amirs. The Tunis militia, the djund, did not always obey. More than once an ambitious chief led it in revolt, only to be crushed by forces sent from al-Kayrawan, the most important rising being that of Mansur al-Tunbudhi in the first half of the ninth century. The article attributes to these revolts the city's reputation as a factious one, a phrase it takes from al-Bakri. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 681}

## Spanish and Ottoman rule
The readable text resumes in the sixteenth century. In 976/1569 the beylerbey of Algiers, Ali, took Tunis. In the autumn of 981/1573 Don Juan of Austria expelled the Turks and left 8,000 men, who built a new fortress, Nova Arx, between the city walls and the lakeshore, with six bastions and an area above ten hectares. The next year the Turks returned in force and besieged La Goulette and Tunis, and both garrisons surrendered in Rabi' II 981/August 1574. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 684} The museum records these as [[event/capture-of-tunis-1569|the capture of 1569]], [[event/capture-of-tunis-1573|the capture of 1573]] and [[event/ottoman-recovery-of-tunis-981|the Ottoman recovery]].

Ifriqiya then became a province of the [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottoman empire]], governed by a pasha with a Turkish militia of 3,000. Power passed within a few years from the pasha to the militia chiefs in 1591 and to a dey in 1595, who acknowledged Ottoman sovereignty but ruled absolutely. The bey, who twice a year led a mobile camp, the mahalla, to collect taxes inland, steadily gained strength. Murad Bey passed his office to his son and so founded the Muradi dynasty, which in the later seventeenth century displaced the deys. Through these changes the city of the pasha, dey and bey remained the capital. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 684}

In the seventeenth century the population was reshaped. The militia of Turks recruited from Ottoman provinces grew from 3,000 to 4,000 men concentrated in the capital, and the [[group/janissaries|Janissaries]] formed the bulk of the bey's camp. Converts from Christianity chose to live among them as Turks. More numerous were the Moors expelled from Spain in 1609 by Philip III. Some settled inland and made the land prosper, while others took part in the crafts and trade of Tunis and formed Andalusian clusters in the Madina and the northern suburb. Jews from Livorno, mostly of Spanish origin and driven out by the Inquisition, were allowed to settle by the Grand Duke of Tuscany. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 684}

## The beys and the eighteenth century
The next page of the scan is lost, and the text resumes with the decline of privateering. The beys signed treaties of peace and trade with several European powers and the United States, who had chosen to pay a kind of tribute to the Barbary states. Privateering was then turned against the Italian states and revived strongly in the 1790s while Europe was at war. After the congresses of Vienna in 1815 and Aix-la-Chapelle in 1818 the powers forced Tunis to give it up. The capital of the Husaynid beys remained the country's chief commercial hub, with all imports passing through La Goulette, improved by the Dutch engineers Homberg and Frank, and caravans linking it with the Maghrib, Mecca and black Africa. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 686}

The article finds the city's structure unchanged in the eighteenth century, but each bey lent his name to new buildings, mostly in the central Madina. Al-Husayn ibn Ali, founder of the Husaynid dynasty, gave it the New Mosque, finished in 1139/1726, now recorded as [[building/el-jedid-mosque-tunis|El Jedid Mosque]], with three madrasas and a tomb. Ali I added four colleges and his mausoleum. Ali II built a college, a hospice for the sick and destitute of both sexes, and the monumental Turbat al-Bey, burial place of all later beys. Hamuda ibn Ali II enlarged the Dar al-Bey, created the Suk al-Bey for costly fabrics and built five barracks. Turks and Andalusians lived in the upper city, minorities in the lower city, and the Jews in the hara. Christians crowded near the Gate of the Sea, and Spanish Trinitarians opened a hospital for captives in 1723. The article judges that Tunis remained essentially a triptych of the Madina and its two suburbs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 686}

## Walls and modernisation
At the turn of the nineteenth century Hamuda renovated the ramparts under Homberg, giving the second perimeter wall the outline it kept almost to the end of the protectorate; it was left open to the east, where the lake was thought sufficient defence. The Bardo to the north had become a small walled town of palaces and gardens. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 687}

After 1830, the article says, Tunisia opened to European influence, successive beys set out to modernise it, and foreign colonies grew, so that Europeans may have numbered 15,000 on the eve of the French protectorate. The first Tunisian printing press and an Arabic newspaper date from 1277/1860, and the first detailed map of the city from 1859. Old buildings took new uses: a palace became the Tribunal of the Shar' in 1856, and the Sadiki College, founded in 1875 by the minister [[person/khayr-al-din-al-tunisi|Khayr al-Din]], was installed in a former barracks. The Europeans built eastward in the lower city, a new French consulate went up in 1861, and the ramparts around the Madina were soon demolished. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 687} Restoration of the Zaghouan aqueduct in 1862 improved the water supply, a gasworks gave the first public lighting, and railways reached La Goulette, La Marsa, the Bardo and the Algerian frontier. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 688}

## The protectorate and after
The French protectorate of 1881 was a turning point. In 1911 the article counts some 70,000 Europeans and no more than 85,000 indigenous people, 65,000 Muslims and 20,000 Jews, about 150,000 inhabitants in all. A modern port, completed in 1897 and joined to La Goulette by a ten-kilometre canal, exported phosphates and ores. The city became what the article calls a double city, with an old Arab town beside a planned European one centred on Marine Avenue. In the inter-war years it grew from 171,600 to 219,500, and a plan of the 1930s tried to protect the Arab town's character. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 688}

The Second World War brought serious damage. Between 1936 and 1956 the Tunisian Muslim population rose from 97,300 to 338,400, mostly rural families seeking work, and shanty-towns ringed the old city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 689} After independence, the article says, the second wall and the old kasba were demolished, unhealthy quarters cleared and old cemeteries turned into gardens, while a plan to drive a road through the Madina was, in its author's view, fortunately dropped. Greater Tunis passed a million inhabitants, 1,082,000 in 1984 and 1,259,000 in 1994. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 690}

## Its founding
The source relates that Hassan ibn al-Nu'man founded it in the year 82 AH as a naval base, to prevent repeated Byzantine attacks on Carthage, and that he dug a canal connecting it to the sea. It is mentioned that Abd al-Malik sent to it from Egypt two thousand Coptic craftsmen to establish shipbuilding. {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 p.20-21}
