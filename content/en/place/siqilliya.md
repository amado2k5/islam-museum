## Overview
Sicily is an island in the sea. According to al-Baladhuri, Mu'awiya ibn Hudayj al-Kindi was the first to raid it, in the days of Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan, and it was raided ever after. The house of al-Aghlab ibn Salim took more than twenty cities from it, which remained in Muslim hands. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.329}

## Al-Idrisi's description
Al-Idrisi calls Sicily "unique in its age for excellence and virtues, and singular among lands for its goodness and dwellings". He says that travellers from every region came to it in earlier times and all agreed on its excellence. {cite:nuzhat-al-mushtaq-2002 p.588}

## The Frankish conquest in al-Idrisi's account
Al-Idrisi says that Roger ibn Tancred began to conquer its lands "in the year four hundred and fifty-three" after the Hijra. He took all of it over a period of thirty years, and was succeeded by his son. {cite:nuzhat-al-mushtaq-2002 p.589}

## In the Pleiades gazetteer
Pleiades, the open gazetteer of ancient places, lists this place under the title «Sicilia (island)» (Pleiades no. 462492), classed as an island, with activity attested from 750 BCE to 2100 CE. Recorded names include Sicilia, Sicily and Trinacria. It describes Sicily as the largest island of the Mediterranean, inhabited since about 8000 BCE, and lists Punic, Carthaginian, Greek, Roman, Vandal, Ostrogothic, Byzantine, Arab and Norman settlement among its cultural layers. {cite:pleiades 462492}

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-buldan
Yaqut records Siqilliyya as a fertile island rich in towns, villages and provincial cities, eleven stages long from Trapani to Messina and three days wide. He read, in Ibn al-Qatta' the philologist's hand on the back of a copy of the History of Sicily, a marginal note in a version of the island's sira that it held twenty-three cities and thirteen fortresses beyond count of estates; and from Abu Ali al-Hasan ibn Yahya the jurist's History of Sicily, citing Qadi Abu'l-Fadl, eighteen cities — Balarm among them — and more than three hundred and twenty fortresses, the island never ceasing to be held by one ruler obeying none around it for its strength and ample revenue. He describes its gushing springs, flowing rivers and wondrous resorts, its mountain called Qasr Yanih bearing a great towering city, and the Mountain of Fire in which flame never ceases — none can approach it, and a brand taken from it dies in the hand once removed; its livestock abundant, no lions, snakes or scorpions in it; mines of gold, silver, copper, lead and mercury, alum, kohl, vitriol, iron and sal-ammoniac carried from the Fire Mountain to al-Andalus; saffron growing in its soil. It was thinly settled before Islam; when the Muslims took Ifriqiya its people fled there and built it up. Its conquest came under the Aghlabids at the hand of the qadi Asad ibn al-Furat: the island's lord was a patrician named Constantine, who killed Euphemius on a report; Euphemius's party seized a part of the island until the lord of Constantinople sent an army that drove him out, and he fled to Kairouan appealing to Ziyadat Allah ibn Ibrahim ibn al-Aghlab, then its governor on al-Ma'mun's behalf, who made the venture easy for him and levied the people, appointing Asad ibn al-Furat, qadi of Kairouan, over them; the fleets gathered and made for Sicily in 212 with nine hundred horse and ten thousand foot, routed the Byzantines, and Asad gained the whole island by movement, dying in 213 — a righteous learned man who had met Malik ibn Anas. It stayed in Muslim hands a while, most of its people becoming Muslims and building mosques, until the unbelievers prevailed and took it, and it is in their hands today, in his words. Ptolemy in the Book of the Prophecy gives longitude forty degrees, latitude thirty-five. From Abu Ali the jurist, author of the History of Sicily, the Mountain of Fire: a mountain overlooking the sea at the strait between Catania and Messina near Taormina, three days round, with great trees, many buildings and remains of the ancients — its fighting men in King Taormina's day were said to be sixty thousand — and at its top vents giving fire and smoke, sometimes lava flowing out that burns all it crosses and turns to iron slag where nothing grows; it is today a bare scar the people call al-Akhbath, clouds and snow never leaving its summit; the Byzantines claimed many early sages travelled to gaze on its wonders, and it was said to hold a gold mine, so they called it the Mountain of Gold; in some years the fire ran to the sea and the people of Taormina lit their nights by its glow. From Ibn Hawqal the merchant a chapter on the island's description: a triangle of equal sides, seven days by four; its famous city Balarm, seat of Sicily on the sea's neck, of five adjoining quarters — Balarm itself, al-Khalisa, the quarter of the Slavs, the quarter of the Mosque called Ibn Siqlab, and the New Quarter — with over three hundred mosques, the mills standing on the Wadi al-Abbas; he was there in 362. Then comes Ibn Hawqal's account of the people's character — their uncleanness and neglect of purification, in his words — which the author says he has soiled his book by repeating, God knowing best. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 3 p.417-419}

## The museum's identification
The record's Sicily is Yaqut's island, whose seat is Balarm. The entry makes its conquest an Aghlabid venture led by the qadi Asad ibn al-Furat in 212, not a direct caliphal campaign, and its loss to "the unbelievers" a report of his own time — the Normans — carried on his wording. Ibn Hawqal's censure of the inhabitants is transmitted under his name, the author excusing himself for repeating it; the Mountain of Fire described near Taormina is the island's volcano on the museum's reading, its two names — al-Akhbath and the Mountain of Gold — both reports.
