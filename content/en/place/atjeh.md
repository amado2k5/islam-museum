## Extent and divisions
Atjeh, Achin, lies at the most northerly part of the island of Sumatra. It was once the seat of a powerful Muslim empire and is now a province of the Indonesian Republic. Under Dutch rule its southern limit was formed by the residencies of Tapanuli and the east coast of Sumatra, now the province of North Sumatra, but in earlier times the political sovereignty of Atjeh extended much farther south. Pagan chiefs in the Batak regions received their rank from the princes of Great Atjeh. Only the district to the north-west, with the Atjeh river and the port of Atjeh, the former residence of the princes, was reckoned as Atjeh proper; the Dutch called it Great Atjeh and its capital Kuta Radja, "fort of the prince". The port of Sabang, on the island of Pulo Weh, dates only from the beginning of the twentieth century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 789}

The other districts on the west, north and east coasts were under Dutch rule known as the Dependencies. The inhabitants of the coast, the Baroh, differ in many respects from the people of the interior highlands, the Tunong. Under Dutch rule the Dependencies developed into a thriving country, in spite of the decline of the traditional pepper culture. In 1942 the rice export surplus was estimated at 45,000 tons, and betel nut, copra, rubber and livestock were exported. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 789-790}

## The highland Gayo and Alas
High mountain chains separate the coastal plain from the Gayo country, which is divided by transverse chains into four tablelands. The northernmost, with the great Tawar lake and the sources of the river Peusangan, is held by the Urang Laut ("people of the lake"); the plain to its south by the Urang Doröt ("people of the land"); the table-land of Serbodjadi lies to the south-east, with the sources of the Peureula'; and the southern Gayo Luos ("wide Gayo") contains the bed of the Tripa. The Alas country lies south of this. The princes of Atjeh appointed four chiefs, the kedjuruns, as mediators between the Gayo and Atjeh; two of them governed round Lake Tawar, one among the Doröt, and one in Gayo Luos. Two kedjuruns represented Atjeh among the Alas. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 790}

## People, language and sources
Little is known of the origin of the people of Atjeh. They belong linguistically to the Malay-Polynesian peoples, though slaves from Nias and other foreigners, such as merchants from Hindustan, affected the population. Atjeh has many dialects; the literary language is closest to the idiom of the Baroh district. Gayo is an independent language, and Alas is a northern Batak dialect. In the nineteenth century Malay was almost unknown in Atjeh except in the sea-ports, but earlier it was the language of the court, and of letters, official documents and theology. The earliest Achehnese adaptations of Arabic and Malay works date from the seventeenth century. Snouck Hurgronje's study of the people in 1891-1892 is the basic source, and J. Kreemer's Atjeh (1922-1923) adds ethnographic detail. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 790}
