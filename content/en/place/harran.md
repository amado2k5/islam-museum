## Overview
Harran is a city of [[place/jazira|al-Jazira]]. Its people made a peace agreement with Iyad ibn Ghanm on the same terms as Edessa. It is reported that they moved to it before his arrival, and made peace for their city while they were in it. There was rivalry between the Harranians and the Christians over making the peace, according to al-Baladhuri. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.239-241}

In it are Tall Afra, the land of Tall Mudhaba, and the prayer ground. In its outskirts are lands granted to al-Ghamr ibn Yazid ibn Abd al-Malik. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.239-241}

Ibn Fadl Allah al-Umari reports from al-Bakri that on the gate of Harran is an inscription in Syriac attributed to Plato, and that the rest of the temples of the Sabians is a house known as Mu'allaniyusha, which for them is the temple of Azar, the father of Abraham. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.225-226}

See [[event/conquest-of-harran|the conquest of Harran]].

## In Yaqut's Mu'jam al-Buldan
Yaqut gives the name with a doubled r and offers two possible derivations: a form from the verb used of a horse that will not be led, or a form from heat, since a thirsty man is called harran. He gives the relative adjective as Harrani, and notes that some say Harnani, against the rule. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.235}

He calls it a great and famous city of the Jazira of Aqur, the chief town of the lands of Mudar, a day from al-Ruha and two days from al-Raqqa, on the road to Mosul, Syria and the Byzantine lands. He passes on two sets of coordinates, one from Ptolemy and one from Abu Awn's astronomical tables, which differ. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.235}

On the name, he reports that it was called after Haran, brother of Abraham, who is said to have built it first, the name then being Arabised to Harran, and that some say it was the first city built on earth after the Flood. It was the home of the Sabians, the Harranians whom the books on religions and sects describe. He adds that commentators on the Qur'an applied two verses to Harran, one on Abraham's emigration and one on Lot's deliverance. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.235}

He quotes a line by the poet Sudayf ibn Maymun about a tomb at Harran, which he takes to mean Ibrahim ibn Muhammad ibn Ali ibn Abdallah ibn al-Abbas, whom Marwan ibn Muhammad held at Harran until he died there of the plague after two months; he adds that some say he was killed. He also relays, on a chain through an Egyptian poet, Ibn al-Nabih, a story of that poet riding with the Ayyubid prince al-Ashraf past Harran's cemeteries on a very hot day and improvising verses comparing its air to something thick and scorching. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.235}

On the conquest, Yaqut says Harran was taken in the days of Umar ibn al-Khattab by Iyad ibn Ghanm, who camped before it ahead of al-Ruha. Its leaders came out and said they would not resist, but asked him to go on to al-Ruha and promised the same terms as its people received. Iyad agreed, made peace with al-Ruha, and then made peace with Harran on the same pattern. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.236}

He says many scholars are named after it and that it has a history, and lists the hafiz Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Allan al-Harrani, author of a history of the Jazira, who died on the day of the Eid al-Adha in 355 and whom he calls a trustworthy, noble hafiz, and the hafiz and imam Abu Uruba al-Hasan ibn Muhammad al-Harrani, also author of a history of the Jazira, who died in Dhu al-Hijja 318 at ninety-six. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.236}

He then lists other places of the same name: a village of Aleppo, two villages in al-Bahrayn called Greater and Lesser Harran that belonged to Banu Amir ibn al-Harith of Abd al-Qays, and a village in the Ghuta of Damascus. These are not the city of this record. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995 2 p.236}
