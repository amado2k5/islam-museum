## Overview
Bengal is a historical region of the eastern Indian subcontinent, divided today between [[place/bangladesh|Bangladesh]] and West Bengal in India, according to the record's data. Ibn Battuta writes the name Bunjala; he reached it from the Maldive islands, and described its cheap prices, its sultan Fakhr al-Din and his war with Ali Shah, and then his journey to the Kamru mountains to meet Shaykh Jalal al-Din al-Tabrizi {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.147} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.148}. The page numbers given here are from the second volume of the edition, whose numbering begins again after the first volume.

## Arrival and prices
Ibn Battuta sailed forty-three nights from the Diba al-Mahal islands, the Maldives, and then came to the land he writes as Bunjala, which he vocalises with a fatha on the ba, a sukun on the nun, a soft jim and a fatha on the lam. He calls it a wide country rich in rice, and says he had seen nowhere in the world with cheaper prices, though it is gloomy; the people of Khurasan, he says, call it "dozakh-pur ni'mat", meaning hell full of bounties {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.147}.

Among the prices he records: rice at twenty-five Delhi rotls for a silver dinar, the silver dinar being eight dirhams; a milch cow for three silver dinars; fattened hens at eight for a dirham and young pigeons at fifteen for a dirham; a fat ram for two dirhams; sugar at four dirhams the rotl; a fine cotton cloth thirty cubits long for two dinars; and a pretty slave girl for one gold dinar. He tells of a pious Maghribi, Muhammad al-Masmudi, who had long lived in the country and died in Delhi, and who bought the year's food for himself, his wife and a servant for eight dirhams. Ibn Battuta bought a slave girl called Ashura, and one of his companions bought a boy called Lu'lu' for two gold dinars {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.147}.

## The city of Sudkawan
The first city he entered in Bunjala was Sudkawan, a great city on the shore of the great sea, where the Ganges, to which Hindus make pilgrimage, and the river Jun meet and flow into the sea; the people have many boats on the river with which they fight the people of Lakhnauti {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.148}.

## The sultan of Bengal
He describes the sultan, Fakhr al-Din, called Fakhra, as a worthy ruler who loves strangers, above all the poor and the Sufis. The country had belonged to Sultan Nasir al-Din, son of Sultan Ghiyath al-Din Balban, who had placed his son Mu'izz al-Din on the throne in [[place/delhi|Delhi]]; Nasir al-Din marched to fight him, they met at the river, and the meeting was called the Meeting of the Two Auspicious Stars. He left the kingship to his son and returned to Bengal, where he stayed until his death. His son Shams al-Din succeeded him, then Shams al-Din's son Shihab al-Din, until his brother Ghiyath al-Din Bahadur Bur overcame him; Shihab al-Din asked Sultan Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq for help, and Tughluq gave it and took Bahadur Bur captive. Tughluq's son Muhammad later released him on condition that he share his kingdom, but he broke the agreement and Muhammad fought him until he was killed; Muhammad placed a son-in-law over the country, whom the soldiers killed, and Ali Shah took the rule, then in Lakhnauti {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.148}.

Fakhr al-Din, who had been a client of Nasir al-Din's house, saw that the kingdom had passed from his sons, rebelled in Sudkawan and his own country of Bengal and took the rule for himself. Strife grew between him and Ali Shah: in the winter and mud season Fakhr al-Din raided Lakhnauti by sea, where he was strong; when the dry days came, Ali Shah raided Bengal by land, where he was strong {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.148}.

Ibn Battuta tells that Fakhr al-Din made one of the poor, Shayda, his deputy in Sudkawan; while the sultan was away fighting his enemy, Shayda rebelled, meaning to seize the throne and to kill the sultan's only son. The sultan turned back, Shayda fled to the fortified city of Sunurkawan, the sultan's army besieged it, its people grew afraid and seized Shayda, and the sultan ordered that his head be sent; many of the poor were killed because of him {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.148}. Ibn Battuta says that when he entered Sudkawan he neither saw nor met its sultan, who was in revolt against the king of India; fearing the outcome, he left {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.148}.

## The Kamru mountains and Shaykh Jalal al-Din
He set out for the Kamru mountains, a wide range joined to China and to Tibet, where the musk deer live; he says its people resemble the Turks, are strong at service, and are reputed for practising sorcery. His aim was to meet Shaykh Jalal al-Din al-Tabrizi, whom he calls one of the great saints and a man of great age. The Shaykh told him he had reached the Abbasid caliph al-Musta'sim in Baghdad and was there when he was killed; the Shaykh's companions later told Ibn Battuta that he died aged one hundred and fifty, that for about forty years he had fasted continuously, breaking the fast only after ten days, on the milk of his one cow, and that the people of those mountains had accepted Islam at his hands, which is why he lived among them. Ibn Battuta relates marvels he attributes to him, among them the story of a cloak the Shaykh gave him {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.148} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.149} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.150}.

After taking leave of the Shaykh he travelled to Habanq, one of the largest and finest cities, divided by the river that flows from the Kamru mountains and is called the Blue River; one can travel on it to Bunjala and to Lakhnauti, and it has water-wheels, gardens and villages on both banks, as on the Nile of Egypt. Its people are unbelievers under protection, who pay half of what they sow. He travelled fifteen days on this river and reached Sunurkawan, the city whose people had seized Shayda {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-1958 2 p.150}.

## Notes
This page rests on Ibn Battuta alone and does not treat the history of Bengal beyond him; it gives no dates for these events because the text read gives none. Ibn Battuta names Lakhnauti separately from Bunjala and as an opponent of its sultan, so this page does not merge the two.
