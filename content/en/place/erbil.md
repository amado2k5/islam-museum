## Overview
Irbil, written Erbil today, is described by Yaqut al-Hamawi as a strong citadel and a large city on a wide, level plain, counted among the districts of Mosul and lying between the two Zab rivers, two days' journey from Mosul. The citadel stands on a high earth mound with a broad top and is ringed by a deep moat; it holds markets, dwellings for the townspeople and a congregational mosque, and Yaqut likens it to the citadel of Aleppo, only larger and wider. In his own time a large town had grown up at the foot of the citadel, built up by the emir Muzaffar al-Din Kukburi ibn Zayn al-Din, who raised its wall, markets and covered markets; strangers settled there until it became a great city. Yaqut writes of the emir that he gave lavishly to strangers and ransomed captives from the hands of those he calls the unbelievers, while by Yaqut's own judgement he was harsh on his subjects in taking their money. Most of the people of the town and its countryside were Kurds who had taken up Arabic; the road to Baghdad took seven days for caravans. It had no orchard and no running river; most crops grew on underground channels, and people drank from sweet wells. Yaqut adds that he found no one there of note for learning except Abu al-Barakat al-Mubarak ibn Ahmad, known as al-Mustawfi. He also weighs where the name comes from, suggesting the rabl, a kind of tree, or an abundance of growth. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.177} {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p.176}
