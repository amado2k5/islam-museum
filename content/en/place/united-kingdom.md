## Overview
The United Kingdom is a country in north-western Europe, according to the place card; the island itself has a separate page, [[place/britain|Britain]], which treats the period before the modern age. This page covers the role of the British state in the Muslim East as told by two books read for the museum: Ali Muhammad al-Salabi's *al-Dawla al-Uthmaniyya* (2001) and the Arabic *Hadir al-Alam al-Islami* with the notes of Shakib Arslan. Both are works of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries and both pass judgements on British policy; those judgements are attributed to their authors here and are not the museum's own. No entry under this name was found in Yaqut's *Mu'jam al-Buldan* or al-Qazwini's *Athar al-Bilad*. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.332} {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.125}

## From Abu Qir to the alliance with the Ottomans
Al-Salabi relates that Britain followed French ambitions in Egypt closely, and when the French expedition arrived she sent a fleet under Admiral Nelson, which surprised the French fleet in Abu Qir bay and sank it; he lists the results as a heavy French loss, British command of the seas, a blockade of the Egyptian coast and French dependence on the country's own resources. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.332} He says the Ottoman state entered negotiations with England that produced an alliance on 5 January 1799. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.333} When news of the al-Arish talks reached London, he writes, the British government took a position that would block the agreement, fearing the besieged French army would return to the battlefields of Europe; it preferred that the French stay or surrender as prisoners, and on 15 December 1799 issued explicit orders to that effect. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.334} For the French withdrawal he names the joint British and Ottoman attack and British naval command. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.336} See [[event/french-campaign-egypt-1213|the French campaign in Egypt]] and [[person/napoleon-bonaparte|Napoleon Bonaparte]].

## The nineteenth century
In al-Salabi's account the policy of the powers, except Russia and France, turned in the first quarter of the nineteenth century on preserving the Ottoman empire, and Britain was foremost in holding to this, until it became possible to fill the vacuum left by Ottoman retreat from the Balkans and she and the others abandoned the principle. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.373}

On the confrontation with [[person/muhammad-ali-pasha|Muhammad Ali]] he says that the London convention of 1840 limited the Egyptian presence in Syria to Muhammad Ali's lifetime. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.368} He then says the British came into open conflict with Muhammad Ali's forces and, with the support of the people of Syria, defeated them and took the Syrian ports, and that Muhammad Ali was forced under their pressure to sign a treaty by which he gave up Syria, kept Egypt hereditary for himself and his sons, limited the Egyptian army to eighteen thousand and built no ships for a fleet. The author uses harsh terms of Muhammad Ali; that is his judgement. The author's wording on the two pages does not agree exactly on what the settlement said about Syria, so both are given as printed. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.371}

Al-Salabi reports that Britain obtained from the Ottoman state the right to occupy and administer Cyprus in June 1878, the island staying Ottoman, in return for Britain's undertaking to defend the empire's Asian possessions against Russian threats on condition that the sultan introduce reforms in consultation with her, and that she undertook to withdraw if the Russians withdrew from what they had occupied. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.413} At the Congress of Berlin, he says, Abd al-Hamid persuaded Britain to stand by him. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.414} He adds that France occupied Tunis in 1299 AH (1881) in return for Britain's occupation of Cyprus, and that Britain occupied Egypt in 1300 AH (1882), declaring the occupation temporary. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.416}

Writing of Abd al-Hamid's reign he says that from the first quarter of the nineteenth century Britain began to incite the Kurds against the Ottoman state, {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.435} and that the state worked to expose English plans to break the empire up in the name of the freedom of nationalities. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.436} This is the author's account of matters whose accuracy has not been checked here.

## The Great War and the mandates
Al-Salabi says that the English, through the Hussein and McMahon correspondence, drew the Arabs to the Allies' side, and that the Allies agreed to divide the Ottoman lands in the secret Sykes-Picot treaty. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.466} Among its terms he lists southern Iraq to Britain. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.467} He counts the Balfour Declaration of 2 November 1917, making Palestine a national home for the Jews, among the results of this phase, and says the English occupied Istanbul and that the British High Commissioner and the Allied commander-in-chief there held the real sovereignty. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.467} See [[person/hussein-bin-ali-al-hashimi|Sharif Hussein ibn Ali]].

## Britain in Hadir al-Alam al-Islami
In Arslan's notes the Allies did not at first accept Egypt's offer to enter the war on their side, since it would have obliged them to evacuate Egypt afterwards; they allied with Sharif Hussein in 1915 only when the war grew long; and after the war they treated the Arabs as enemies and divided their lands. This is Arslan's assessment. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.330} The main text says Britain declared at the start of the war that Egypt had become a protected country, and that a new agreement between Britain and Iran, appearing just before the Versailles conference ended, made Iran, at least in effect, protected too. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.318} Its chapter on Egypt adds that Britain declared the protectorate over Egypt alone in 1914 and that the Versailles conference refused to hear the Egyptian delegation. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.125}

It relates that Lord Milner and Saad Zaghlul Pasha reached basic rules in the autumn of 1920, by the press and the official statement: lifting the protection and declaring Egypt independent on terms equal to Cuba's towards the United States, full self-government, withdrawal of the garrison and civil officials, an alliance treaty, a military and naval position for Britain to guard the Suez Canal, and the Sudan left open; but it was not put into effect at once. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.134} See [[person/saad-zaghloul|Saad Zaghloul]].

Arslan says France and Britain bombarded Algiers in the seventeenth century to curb its corsairs, and that the English fired on it in revenge after the Aix-la-Chapelle agreement. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.167} In a note on Fashoda he describes a secret Anglo-French agreement, which he dates to 1902, by which France ceded Egypt, the Egyptian Sudan and Uganda to Britain. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.115} See [[place/algiers|Algiers]].

The fourth volume has a chapter Arslan titles "The Europeans' deceit of the Arabs and Muslims", which by its table of contents holds three documents signed in the name of the King of Britain on respect for Arab independence and the Islamic religion. One of them says His Majesty's government has decided to make a free Arabian peninsula one of the main terms of peace, that the English have respected Islam, and that it allowed grain to be shipped to Jeddah after German and Turkish officers seized what had been sent. The second communiqué, dated 4 Dhu al-Qa'da 1335 AH, says Britain does not intend military action against Arab lands or ports except to protect Arabs from Turkish or other attack or to support those who wish to free themselves from the Turks. The chapter title is Arslan's own verdict on these promises and is attributed to him. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.284} {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.285} The second volume carries copies of letters sent by Lord Kitchener, Sir Henry McMahon and General Maxwell to Sayyid Ahmad al-Sharif al-Sanusi. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.129}

## Note on the sources
The sources vary the name: "Britannia", "Inglatira", "Inklatira" and "the English" in al-Salabi, "Britaniyya", "Inklitra" and "the English" in *Hadir al-Alam al-Islami*. The page numbers of that book are the printed ones, and Arslan's date of 1902 for the secret agreement has not been checked.
