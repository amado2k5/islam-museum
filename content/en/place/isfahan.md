## Overview
A city in central Iran, where Ibn Sina spent his last years at the court of Ala al-Dawla. The Encyclopaedia of Islam treats Isfahan, in Arabic Isbahan, as both a town and a province. It reports that Hamza al-Isfahani took the name to mean "the armies". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 106}

Because of its central position, the province lived through most of the upheavals Persia has known since the Arab conquest. The article nevertheless calls its population remarkably uniform. The exceptions are the mountain districts of Firaydan and Chahar Mahall, home mainly to Bakhtiyari tribes, and small Jewish and Christian minorities, chiefly in the town. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 107} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 108}

## Name and early notes
Golius is reported to say that Isfahan was originally four villages that merged, that Shah Ismail made it a seat of government, and that Shah Abbas then moved his own seat there from Qazvin. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 459} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 460} The same source adds that the Zayandeh Rud reaches the city from Damavand by a channel Shah Abbas dug after much effort, as Olearius and Della Valle also record. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 461}

Golius also derives the name from "sipah", meaning soldiers, and attributes this to al-Biruni, while setting out a less likely account that ties it to the sons of Phalunjius. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 457}

## The province and its waters
In Sasanian times Isfahan was an important province in a central position. The Persian commander Hurmuzan, consulted by [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar ibn al-Khattab]] about further conquests, advised marching on Isfahan, which he likened to a head whose fall would bring down the two wings, Azerbaijan and Fars. After the conquest it belonged to the province of the Jibal, later called Iraq-i Ajam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 106}

The province ranges from the pastoral mountains of Firaydan and Chahar Mahall, through oasis farming on the plateau, to the fertile river plain. The town itself receives about five inches of rain a year, between November and April, and the article gives a mean August maximum of 36.1 degrees Celsius and a January minimum of minus 2.2. Cultivation is irrigated, outside Firaydan and Chahar Mahall, from river, kariz channels or wells. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 107}

The Zayandeh Rud rises on the slopes of the Zarda Kuh, flows south-east through the town and vanishes in the Gavkhani marsh. Ibn Rusta credited the original sharing of its water to Ardashir son of Babak, while tradition places the modern division under Shah Abbas. The river had twelve permanent bridges, and one of its dams, the Band-i Marwan, dates from Umayyad times. In 1954 a tunnel from the Karun at Kuhrang increased its flow, a plan first conceived under [[person/tahmasp-i-safavi|Shah Tahmasp]] and begun, but abandoned, under Shah Abbas. The early geographers praise the climate and the harvests, and the province also produced textiles, armour, brasswork and carpets. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 107}

## The city and its people
The city is an old foundation, centred first on the village of Jay, also called Shahristan. Two miles to the west lay Yahudiyya, where Jews are said to have been settled by Nebuchadnezzar or under Yazdigird I, and which later became the centre while Shahristan turned into a suburb. Legend, passed on by Ibn Rusta, credits the citadel to Kay Ka'us. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 107} Benjamin of Tudela put the Jews of the town at 15,000 in the twelfth century. Armenians settled in a suburb to the south that became known as [[place/new-julfa|New Julfa]], and by 1889 only about 2,000 remained there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 108}

Medieval writers describe the people of Isfahan as vigorous, quick-witted and skilled craftsmen. Al-Qazwini praises their learning in law, letters, astronomy and medicine. The article records recurrent strife between [[group/shafii-madhhab|Shafi'is]] and [[group/hanafi-madhhab|Hanafis]], and [[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]], who visited in 727/1326-7, found a fine city largely in ruins from the feud between Sunnis and Rafidis. After the Safavids adopted Shi'ism the Shafi'i-Hanafi strife ended, and a new rivalry between Haydaris and Ni'matis arose. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 108}

## Conquest and the early caliphates
The article gives two accounts of the Muslim capture. The Kufan school places it in 19/640, when Abd Allah ibn Itban marched on Jay and its commander capitulated on terms that replaced the poll tax with an annual tribute. The Basran school puts it in 23/644, under [[person/abu-musa-al-ashari|Abu Musa al-Ash'ari]] or his lieutenant. [[event/conquest-of-isfahan|The conquest]] is dated 21/641-2 by al-Tabari. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 108}

Isfahan was governed from Basra and Iraq, and in 68/687-8 the [[group/azariqa|Azariqa]] branch of the Kharijites besieged it and was beaten. [[person/al-hajjaj-ibn-yusuf|Al-Hajjaj]] appointed its governors from 75/694, and Arab tribes settled in its districts. In 127/744-5 the Alid rebel Abd Allah ibn Mu'awiya held the city for about two years. Under [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]] the district of Qumm was split off in 189/804-5. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 108} The [[group/khurramiyya|Khurramiyya]] movement reached the city about 218/833 and an army sent by al-Mu'tasim suppressed it, and the Banu Dulaf governed from 253/867 until 282/895-6. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 109}

## The Buyid period
After years of turmoil, in which [[person/mardawij-ibn-ziyad|Mardawij]] took the city in 315/927 and [[person/rukn-al-dawla|Rukn al-Dawla]] and his brother Ali ibn Buya later occupied it, Rukn al-Dawla ruled Isfahan until his death in 366/976. [[person/ala-al-dawla-ibn-kakuya|Ala al-Dawla]] Muhammad ibn Dushmanziyar governed it intermittently until 433/1041-2. In 420/1029 the [[polity/ghaznavid-empire|Ghaznavid]] Mas'ud [[event/masud-takes-isfahan-423|took it]], and when the townspeople killed his governor he returned and massacred many of them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 109}

The article judges that the city became flourishing and extensive in this age, especially under the vizier Isma'il ibn Abbad. The Buyids added the Tabarak quarter, and in 429/1037-8 Ala al-Dawla raised a wall around the city. [[person/ibn-hawqal|Ibn Hawqal]] notes its silk and textile exports and says that no city between Iraq and Khurasan except Rayy traded more. Al-Mafarrukhi's figures for daily slaughter lead the author to a conservative estimate of over 100,000 inhabitants. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 109}

## Seljuk capital
[[person/tughril-beg|Tughril Beg]] besieged the city twice, and it fell after nearly a year in Muharram 443/1051. He appointed a young man from Nishapur to govern it and ordered that no taxes be demanded for three years. [[person/nasir-khusraw|Nasir-i Khusraw]], visiting in 444/1052, found it the most populous and flourishing city he had seen in Persian-speaking lands, with a large Friday mosque, many bazaars and a strong wall. Tughril moved his capital there from [[place/rayy|Rayy]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 110}

Under [[person/malikshah|Malikshah]] and his vizier [[person/nizam-al-mulk|Nizam al-Mulk]], Isfahan reached great heights and became an important Sunni centre. The city was exempted from extraordinary dues, the Gulbar quarter and new mosques were added, the old Friday mosque was enlarged, and a fortress at Shahdiz held the sultan's armoury and treasury. Nizam al-Mulk built a Nizamiyya madrasa there and endowed it with property yielding over 10,000 dinars a year. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 110}

After Malikshah's death in 485/1092 the city declined, though as capital it remained worth fighting for. [[person/barkyaruq|Barkyaruq]] and [[person/muhammad-tapar|Muhammad Tapar]] contested it, and a Batini movement took [[building/shahdiz-castle|Shahdiz]] and was crushed there only in 500/1107. Isfahan stayed the main centre of the empire until Muhammad's death in 511/1118. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 111}

## Mongols, Ilkhans and Timurids
The Khwarazmshah [[person/ala-al-din-tekish|Tekish]] took Isfahan in 590/1194. In 625/1228 [[person/jalal-al-din-khwarazmshah|Jalal al-Din]] defeated Mongol forces outside it, but the city fell about 638/1240-1 through treachery within the walls. The article says the Isfahanis accepted Mongol rule with difficulty. By the eighth/fourteenth century the city had regained some prosperity. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 111}

In 789/1387 [[person/timur-lang|Timur]] marched on Isfahan. After a riot in which his tax collectors were killed, he massacred 70,000 inhabitants, according to Nizam al-Din Shami. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 111} The Venetian Barbaro estimated only 50,000 inhabitants in 879/1474-5. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 111}

## The Safavid capital
[[person/ismail-i-safavi|Shah Ismail]] took Isfahan in 908/1502-3, and [[person/abbas-i-safavi|Shah Abbas]] made it his capital in 1005/1596-7, replanning and largely rebuilding it. European embassies, trading agents and religious orders visited and often stayed. Chardin counted 162 mosques, 48 colleges, 1,802 caravanserais and 273 baths, and population estimates range from 600,000 to 1,100,000. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 112}

Administration was highly centralised, with the kalantar linking the people and the government and the darugha keeping order. Isfahan again became a religious centre, now of Twelver Shi'ism. Shah Abbas and his early successors tolerated other faiths, but persecution began under Shah Sulayman and, under [[person/sultan-husayn-safavi|Sultan Husayn]], also touched Sunnis and Sufis. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 112} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 113}

## Fall of the Safavids and after
Afghan forces beat the Safavid army at Gulnabad and besieged the city in 1134/1722. It fell after six months, with some 20,000 killed in fighting and an estimated four times as many dead of starvation and disease. [[person/nadir-shah-afshar|Nadir]] entered the city in 1141/1729, and in 1148/1736 he moved the capital to [[place/mashhad|Mashhad]]. [[person/karim-khan-zand|Karim Khan Zand]] took the city in 1172/1758-9, and [[place/shiraz|Shiraz]] became capital in 1180/1766-7. Under the Qajars the capital moved to [[place/tehran|Tehran]]. Visitors of 1796 and 1811 put the population at only 50,000 to 60,000. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 113}

In the nineteenth century Muhammad Husayn Khan Nazim al-Dawla, a native of the city, helped it prosper again, but famine in 1288/1871-2 reversed this. Zill al-Sultan governed from 1874, and a census of 1882 counted 73,654 inhabitants. Isfahan took a prominent part in the Constitutional Revolution. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 114}

## Monuments
The article calls the Islamic monuments of Isfahan one of the most significant architectural complexes preserved at the heart of a modern city. Most date from the Safavid period, the oldest parts from the Seljuk age. The [[building/jame-masjid-isfahan|Great Mosque]] keeps traces of Malikshah's time, brick-decorated Seljuk domed halls and the stucco mihrab of [[person/oljaitu|Oljaytu]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 115}

Shah Abbas planned the Maydan-i Shah, 510 metres long and 165 wide, with the Chahar Bagh avenue begun in 1006/1598. Around it stand the [[building/masjid-al-shah-isfahan|Masjid-i Shah]], probably begun in 1021/1612-13, the Masjid-i Shaykh Lutf Allah, begun in 1012/1603, and the [[building/ali-qapu|Ali Qapu]] palace. The [[building/chehel-sotun|Chihil Sutun]] palace was started about 1006/1598, and the Mader-i Shah madrasa dates from 1118/1706 to 1126/1714. The article calls the city a museum the size of a city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 115} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 116}

## For Sprouts
Isfahan is a historic city in Iran, famed for its grand squares, blue-tiled mosques, and magnificent stone bridges.

## For Explorers
Isfahan is a historic jewel in central Iran, renowned for its architectural splendor and celebrated as 'Half the World'. Flourishing under the Seljuks and later as the Safavid imperial capital, it is famed for the grand Naqsh-e Jahan Square, the Shah Mosque, and the Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque, where scholars like Ibn Sina spent years writing and teaching.

## For Scholars
A premier historic metropolis of central Iran along the Zayandeh River and the Zagros plateau, ideally situated along major trans-Eurasian trade routes. Ascending to prominence under the Great Seljuks in the 5th century AH through vizier Nizam al-Mulk and Sultan Malikshah, it hosted scholars such as Ibn Sina during his later productive years. Isfahan achieved legendary urban brilliance under Safavid Shah Abbas I, who made it his capital in 1006 AH (1598 CE), constructing the magnificent Naqsh-e Jahan complex with the Shah Mosque, Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque, Ali Qapu Palace, and the monumental bridges crossing the Zayandeh.
