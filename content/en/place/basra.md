## Overview
Basra, in Arabic al-Basra, is a city of lower Mesopotamia on the [[place/shatt-al-arab|Shatt al-Arab]], about 420 km south-east of [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]]. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose account this page follows, distinguishes two sites: Old Basra, marked today by the village of Zubayr, and New Basra, a later settlement near ancient [[place/ubulla|al-Ubulla]] from which the modern town grew. The article reports that the rapid growth of the modern town followed the discovery of oil west of Zubayr. It divides the history into two parts, the city until the Mongol conquest of 656/1258, and modern Basra. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1145}

## Foundation as a military camp
The article says the Muslim town can be treated as a new construction, although it probably stood on the site of ancient Teredon and more certainly on that of a Persian settlement called Vahishtabadh Ardashir. The Companion [[person/utba-ibn-ghazwan|Utba ibn Ghazwan]] camped in 14/635 on the ruins of an old Persian post that the Arabs called al-Khurayba, "the little ruin". In 17/638, on the orders of [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar ibn al-Khattab]], he chose this place for the military camp that became the town. The article suggests that the name probably derives from the nature of the soil. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1145}

The camp lay about fifteen km from the Shatt al-Arab. The article gives it three purposes: to control the route from the Persian Gulf, Iraq and Persia; to serve as a base for later expeditions east of the Euphrates and Tigris; and to help settle the Bedouin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1145}

## From huts to a town
The first dwellings were simple huts of rushes gathered from the nearby marshes. They were later strengthened with low walls and, after a fire, rebuilt in crude brick. Only under [[person/ziyad-ibn-abi-sufyan|Ziyad ibn Abi Sufyan]] were baked bricks introduced and the place given the look of a real town, with a new Great Mosque and a residence for the governor. The rampart with its ditch was not built until 155/771-2. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1145}

Drinking water was always a serious problem. Several canals were dug and the bed of the ancient Pallacopas was used to give the town a river port, yet the inhabitants still had to go as far as the Tigris for water. The article argues that this, together with the harsh climate, would by itself have kept the camp from becoming a great city, but political, economic and psychological factors held the Basrans there. It names the foundation of Baghdad, followed by the weakening of central power and political anarchy, as the forces that ended this, bringing a decline as complete as the growth had been rapid. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1145}

## The early conquests and the civil wars
In its first years Basra supplied contingents to the Arab armies. Its men took part in the battle of [[event/battle-of-nihawand|Nihawand]] in 21/642 and in the conquests of [[place/istakhr|Istakhr]], [[place/fars|Fars]], [[place/khurasan|Khurasan]] and [[place/sijistan|Sijistan]], the last in 29/650. As booty flowed in, the men of Basra grew aware of their importance, and the town became the stage for the first great armed conflict between Muslims, the [[event/battle-of-the-camel|battle of the Camel]] of 36/656. The inhabitants had been divided in their loyalties before the fight, and the victory of [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]] only added to their disorder. The article observes that the population remained more Sunni than Shi'i, in contrast to Alid [[place/kufa|Kufa]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1145}

In 37/657 men of Basra fought in the ranks of Ali at [[event/battle-of-siffin|Siffin]], yet a considerable number of the first [[group/khawarij|Kharijites]] were also recruited in the city. In 41/662 [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Muawiya]] reasserted Umayyad authority there and in 45/665 sent Ziyad, whom the article regards, to a degree, as the architect of the town's prosperity. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1145}

## Tribes and newcomers
Basra was divided into five tribal departments, called akhmas: the people of the high district of the Hijaz, Tamim, Bakr ibn Wa'il, Abd al-Qays and Azd. These Arab groups formed the military aristocracy. They absorbed, in the rank of mawali or slaves, the relatively few indigenous inhabitants and a host of immigrants, among them Iranians, Indians, people from Sind, Malays and Zanj. The article says these newcomers took up their masters' quarrels, and that the old tribal solidarity was slow to fade. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1145} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1146}

## Umayyad and Abbasid decades
The local situation worsened under the governor [[person/ubayd-allah-ibn-ziyad|Ubayd Allah ibn Ziyad]]. After his death in 64/683 serious disturbances broke out, and following a period of anarchy the [[polity/zubayrid-caliphate|Zubayrids]] held the city until 72/691. The [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyads]] were then chiefly concerned with putting down uprisings, the most important being [[event/revolt-of-ibn-al-ashath|that of Ibn al-Ash'ath]] in 81/701. A period of calm followed until the revolt of the Muhallabids in 101-2/719-20 and some lesser seditions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1146}

The town passed to the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasids]] without great difficulty, but the nearness of the new capital soon took away its character as a semi-independent metropolis. It became a provincial town, periodically threatened by revolts that the article calls more social than political. These were the rising of the [[group/zutt|Zutt]], who spread terror in the region from 205 to 220/820-35; the Zanj, who seized power in 257/871; and the Qarmatians, who plundered the city in 311/923, as the [[event/qarmatian-sack-of-basra-311|record of that event]] also describes. Soon after, the Baridids held it, and the [[polity/buyid-dynasty|Buyids]] retook it in 336/947. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1146}

## Late medieval decline
The article mentions a new rampart built in 517/1123, two km inside the old one. It also reports that tribes, in particular the Muntafik, took advantage of the depredations, and that a copyist of Ibn Hawqal states that from 537/1142-3 a number of buildings were destroyed. Of the old metropolis, the article says, nothing remains in our time but a building known as the Masjid Ali and the tombs of Talha, al-Zubayr, [[person/muhammad-ibn-sirin|Ibn Sirin]] and [[person/al-hasan-al-basri|al-Hasan al-Basri]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1146}

## A complete metropolis
The article describes Basra at its height, around the beginning of the third/ninth century, as fully developed and greatly populated. Surviving figures vary from 200,000 to 600,000, but it was a very great medieval city and a "complete metropolis". It was a commercial centre, a halting place for caravans, with a river port, al-Kalla', that took fairly large ships; a financial centre, thanks to Jewish and Christian communities and to bourgeois of non-Arab stock; an industrial centre with its arsenals; an agricultural centre with many varieties of dates; and the home of intense religious and intellectual activity. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1146}

## A centre of learning
The article quotes the scholar Louis Massignon on Basra as the crucible in which Islamic culture took its classical form between the first and fourth centuries of the Hijra. It lists what began or flourished there. Arabic grammar was born and made illustrious by [[person/sibawayh|Sibawayh]] and [[person/al-khalil-ibn-ahmad-al-farahidi|al-Khalil ibn Ahmad]]. Mu'tazilism developed with [[person/wasil-ibn-ata|Wasil ibn Ata]], [[person/amr-ibn-ubayd|Amr ibn Ubayd]], Abu al-Hudhayl, [[person/al-nazzam|al-Nazzam]] and others; see the [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazila]]. Scholars such as [[person/abu-amr-ibn-al-ala|Abu Amr ibn al-Ala]], [[person/abu-ubayda-mammar|Abu Ubayda]] and [[person/al-madaini|al-Mada'ini]] collected verses and historical traditions that fed later writers. In religion, [[person/al-hasan-al-basri|al-Hasan al-Basri]] and his disciples founded mysticism, according to the article. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1146}

In poetry the city could claim the great Umayyad poets and the modernists [[person/bashshar-ibn-burd|Bashshar ibn Burd]] and [[person/abu-nuwas|Abu Nuwas]]. The article adds that Arabic prose was born there, with [[person/ibn-al-muqaffa|Ibn al-Muqaffa]], Sahl ibn Harun and [[person/al-jahiz|al-Jahiz]]. After the third/ninth century it sees intellectual decline as less marked than the political and economic decline. Through Ibn Sawwar the town gained a library of lasting fame, and the [[group/ikhwan-al-safa|Ikhwan al-Safa]] and [[person/al-hariri|al-Hariri]] helped keep up its prestige. Yet Arab culture in general was by then in decline, and Baghdad and other provincial capitals tended to replace Basra completely. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1146}

## Modern Basra
The article says Basra, already much reduced in size and vitality in the fifth to seventh centuries AH, was weakened further and faster by the destruction, near-anarchy and neglect that followed the Mongol leader Hulegu's visit to Iraq in 656/1258 and the setting up of an [[polity/ilkhanate|Ilkhanid]] government, for which Basra was the remotest province. In the mid-eighth/fourteenth century [[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]] found the city largely in ruins. The town was drifting toward a move from its original site to a new one, about a dozen miles away, on or near the site of Ubulla, a move the article attributes partly to security and partly to the deterioration of the canals. The great date belt of the Shatt al-Arab remained the wealth and pride of the Basrans. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1146} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1147}

Cultural and economic life declined through the Jalayirid and Turkoman periods, from 740/1340 to 914/1508. In 914/1508 Basra fell with all Iraq to the Persian power of Shah Ismail, who held it for a brief generation until 941/1534. The article describes it as then a provincial town of little interest apart from its seaport, its gardens and its taste for local independence. The Ottoman conquest of Iraq in 941/1534 strengthened the Sunni element in the population, but the article says it had little effect on the city's status: the Turkish pasha of Baghdad was content with minimal respect and tribute. When Basra's independent airs became too offensive in 953/1546, two expeditions from central Iraq restored some appearance of the sultan's authority. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1147}

A longer attempt at quasi-independence, under merely nominal imperial authority, was made by a local notable of unknown origins, Afrasiyab, then by his son and grandson. The article says the dynasty opened Basra and its waterways to European representatives and merchant fleets, and that it survived against the pasha of Baghdad, the threats of the Safavid shah and local rivals, although restoration to the empire was incomplete for a further generation of tribal dominance and plague. In the next two centuries Basra remained the metropolis of southern Iraq, the country's only port though poorly equipped, the centre of the date trade and the gateway to the tribes and princes of Arabia, Khuzistan and the [[place/persian-gulf|Persian Gulf]]. It was repeatedly at the mercy of tribal raiders, notably the Muntafik group. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1147}

The article records episodes of the eighteenth century: the threat and siege during the campaigns of [[person/nadir-shah-afshar|Nadir Shah]]; the slow growth of British, French and Italian trading posts, consulates and missions; and the siege and occupation of the city in 1189-1194/1775-79 by Persian forces under Sadiq Khan, brother of [[person/karim-khan-zand|Karim Khan Zand]]. It also records threats from the fleet of the Imam of [[place/muscat|Muscat]] in 1213/1798, which came to nothing, and the great plague of 1247/1831 that weakened the port. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1147}

## The twentieth century
The period from 1248 to 1332/1832-1914 was one of slow development with better commercial links to Europe and America, and Basra became a wilayet in 1267/1850. After the British occupation of Iraq from 1333/1914 and the Mandate, 1339-1351/1920-32, the town was transformed quickly: a modern port was built, a deep channel dredged at the mouth of the Shatt al-Arab, and new roads, buildings and services were added. It became the southern terminus of the Iraq railways and a growing air centre. With its suburbs of Makil and Ashar it held about 200,000 people in 1955, and was by far the leading port of the Persian Gulf and Iraq's indispensable outlet. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1147}

Oil changed the region's economy. A major field was found near Zubayr in 1368/1948, with others, notably al-Rumayla, to follow. Oil export by pipeline to Fao began in 1371/1951, and a small refinery was finished at Muftiyya in 1372/1952. The industry became Basra's largest source of employment, technical education and wealth. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1147}

## For Sprouts
Basra is a historic city in southern Iraq, surrounded by rivers and palm groves, famous for grammar, language, and libraries.

## For Explorers
Basra is an ancient city in southern Iraq near the Shatt al-Arab, founded by Utba ibn Ghazwan under Caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab in 14 AH. As the first garrison city established outside Arabia in Islam, it thrived through maritime commerce, emerging alongside Kufa as the cradle of Arabic grammar, literature, and jurisprudence.

## For Scholars
The earliest garrison city established by Muslims outside the Arabian Peninsula, founded in 14 AH, about 636 CE, by Utba ibn Ghazwan under the instruction of Caliph Umar at the northern head of the Persian Gulf along the Shatt al-Arab. Evolving rapidly from a military base into a trans-regional maritime trade entrepôt linking the Middle East with the Indian Ocean, it emerged alongside Kufa as the preeminent intellectual cradle of Arabic linguistics. Basra gave birth to the prestigious Basran school of grammar codified by Sibawayh and al-Khalil ibn Ahmad, hosting luminaries in literature, philosophy, and sciences such as al-Jahiz and Ibn al-Haytham.
