## Overview
Lahore is the principal city of the [[place/punjab|Punjab]], on the left bank of the Ravi about 700 feet above sea level. Its position in the fertile alluvial plain of the upper Indus made it again and again a frontier stronghold, and later a capital; since 1947 it has belonged to [[place/pakistan|Pakistan]], of which it is the second largest city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 37}

## Name and first mention
Popular etymology ties the city's foundation to the mythical Lava, son of Rama, and scholars have proposed the older forms Lohawar and Lavapura; the spelling Lahanur appears in the thirteenth-century Qiran al-sa'dayn of Amir Khusraw. The first certain mention is in the Hudud al-'alam of 372/982, which describes a city inhabited only by Hindus yet subject to the Qurayshite ruler of [[place/multan|Multan]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 37}

## Ghaznavid capital
[[person/mahmud-ghaznawi|Mahmud of Ghazna]] took Lahore at a date that is not known and built a fortress there; the report that he renamed it Mahmudpur is doubted by the article's author, P. Jackson. Under his successors it became the administrative centre of the Indian provinces and in effect the second capital of the [[polity/ghaznavid-empire|Ghaznavid empire]]. The governor Ahmad Yinaltigin rebelled in 424/1033, and in 435/1043-4 a confederacy of Hindu princes besieged the city for a long time without success. After [[place/ghazna|Ghazna]] itself was lost in 558/1163, Lahore was the dynasty's capital until the [[polity/ghurid-dynasty|Ghurids]] captured it in 583/1187 and ended Ghaznavid rule. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 37}

## Under the Delhi sultans
When the Ghurid Mu'izz al-Din Muhammad ibn Sam was murdered in 602/1206, Lahore was for a time the capital of the Indian lands ruled by his slave [[person/qutb-al-din-aybak|Qutb al-Din Aybak]]. After Aybak's death the former Ghurid officers Qubacha, Yildiz and [[person/iltutmish|Iltutmish]] disputed it; Iltutmish's forces took it in 613/1217, and it probably fell to him for good shortly before Qubacha's overthrow in 625/1228. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 37} Under Iltutmish's weaker successors its governors were often in revolt, and in 639/1241 the Mongols took and sacked the city, then abandoned it at once. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 37}

Lahore reappears as part of the [[polity/delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]], and Sultan Balban restored the city soon after his accession in 664/1266. For some decades it remained a frontier district exposed to regular Mongol attack, and Deopalpur seems to have replaced it as the administrative centre. It regained prominence early in the fourteenth century under the governor Ghazi Malik, who took the throne of [[place/delhi|Delhi]] in 720/1320 as [[person/ghiyath-al-din-tughluq|Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq]]. In the reign of his son [[person/muhammad-ibn-tughluq|Muhammad]] the district was ravaged by the Chaghatay khan Tarmashirin about 729/1329, and a few years later a Mongol chief allied with the Khokars occupied the city; [[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]] mentions the harsh reprisals of the sultan's forces against the townspeople. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 38}

Shaykha Khokar, governor for the Tughluqs, submitted to [[person/timur-lang|Timur]] in 801/1398 and was then put down by an army under Timur's grandson. Timur conferred Lahore on Khidr Khan, who seized Delhi in 817/1414 and founded the [[polity/sayyid-dynasty-delhi|Sayyid dynasty]]. In 845/1441 the Sayyid sultan granted the city to [[person/bahlul-lodi|Bahlul Lodi]] to check the Khokars; ten years later Bahlul supplanted the Sayyids at Delhi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 38}

## The Mughal city
The Lodi governor of the Punjab, Dawlat Khan, encouraged [[person/babur-mughal|Babur]]'s designs on Hindustan, and the Mughals occupied Lahore in 930/1524. [[person/humayun-mughal|Humayun]] fled there before [[person/shir-shah-suri|Shir Shah Sur]] and abandoned the Punjab to him in 947/1540; Shir Shah is said to have regretted on his deathbed that he had not razed a city so useful to an invader from the north-west. Humayun reoccupied it in Rabi' II 962/February 1555. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 38}

With the Mughal restoration Lahore entered the period of its greatest prosperity, to which its chief monuments belong. [[person/jalal-al-din-akbar|Akbar]] made it his headquarters for the expeditions against Kashmir, Sind and Qandahar in 992-1006/1584-98, and received Portuguese Jesuit missionaries there in 1595. [[person/jahangir-mughal|Jahangir]] made it his capital from 1031/1623, and it remained so for most of the reign of [[person/shah-jahan-mughal|Shah Jahan]], who was born there, founded a carpet manufactory and renovated the palace. The city also served pretenders as a base: Khusraw in 1015/1606, Shahriyar in 1037/1628 and [[person/dara-shikoh|Dara Shikoh]] in 1068/1658. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 38}

Its importance declined under [[person/aurangzeb-mughal|Aurangzeb]], who lived there less than his predecessors, though he built its congregational mosque. The French traveller Bernier, visiting in 1665, gives the impression of a city already decaying, with large areas in ruins. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 38}

## Afghans, Sikhs and British
[[person/bahadur-shah-i-mughal|Bahadur Shah I]] came to Lahore in 1123/1711 on campaign against the first Sikh rising and died before finishing it. The governor Zakariyya Khan submitted to [[person/nadir-shah-afshar|Nadir Shah]] in Shawwal 1150/early 1738. [[person/ahmad-shah-durrani|Ahmad Shah Durrani]] occupied the city in Muharram 1161/January 1748, and after his invasion of 1165/1751-2 the Mughal emperor signed a treaty that passed Lahore into the Afghan sphere. Ahmad Shah installed his son [[person/timur-shah-durrani|Timur Shah]] there; Sikhs and Marathas expelled him in 1171/1758. After each later Afghan invasion the Sikhs simply reoccupied the city, and they secured it in 1181/1767. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 38}

For about thirty years three Sikh chieftains governed Lahore. Ranjit Singh took it from them in Safar 1214/July 1799 and was proclaimed maharaja of the Punjab in 1802; as his capital the city recovered some of its lost standing, and he repaired its walls and built widely. By the two treaties of Lahore in 1846 his successor Dalip Singh had to accept a British garrison and a British Resident, and in March 1849 the city was formally annexed to the British empire. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 39}

At the partition of 1947 the city and most of its district were allotted to Pakistan. From 1955 it was the capital of West Pakistan, and since 1970 of the province of the Punjab. Its population, under a quarter of a million in 1901, reached 1,296,477 at the census of 1961. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 39}

## The Fort
The architectural history of Lahore can be traced in substance only from the Mughal period; the article's second author, P. A. Andrews, notes that the older tombs have been so much altered that their interest lies mainly in their inscriptions. Among them are the graves of [[person/al-hujwiri|Abu al-Hasan Ali Hujwiri]], called Data Ganj-bakhsh, and of Qutb al-Din Aybak. Burnt brick is the typical material of the city for want of local stone, usually covered with cut plaster or tilework. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 39}

Excavations begun in 1959 showed that the Fort rests on levels going back to the Ghaznavid occupation. Akbar began the present structure about 974/1566, extending the site north to the bank of the Ravi and enclosing a rectangle of about 340 by 427 metres; he also built the city wall with its thirteen gates. Jahangir completed the early phase in 1027/1618 with a courtyard of private apartments. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 39} Between 1034/1624-5 and 1041/1631-2 the north and west walls were clad in mosaics of cut glazed tile showing courtiers, hunting, elephant fights and battle. Shah Jahan ordered the Diwan-i 'Amm of forty columns in 1037/1628 and built new private apartments, among them the Shish Mahall of white marble with pietra dura inlay and a ceiling of convex glass set in plaster. The [[building/moti-masjid-lahore|Moti Masjid]] of about 1645 was the first of the three Mughal Pearl Mosques, and Aurangzeb added the Alamgiri Gate in 1084/1673-4. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 40}

## Mosques, tombs and gardens
The mosque built by Jahangir's mother in 1023/1614 has a five-arched prayer hall and, Andrews writes, the finest floral painting in Pakistan. The [[building/wazir-khan-mosque-lahore|Mosque of Wazir Khan]] of 1044/1634 sets flat panels of cut mosaic tile, with flowers, trees and inscriptions, against a robust brick structure, and its octagonal minarets appear to be the first such towers on a Mughal mosque. The [[building/badshahi-mosque|Badshahi Mosque]] of 1084/1673, which Andrews calls Aurangzeb's finest building, joins the local octagonal minarets and recessed panelling to the plan and bulbous domes of the congregational mosque of Delhi; its brick is faced throughout in red sandstone, with white marble for the domes. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 40}

[[building/jahangir-tomb|Jahangir's tomb]] at Shahdara, completed about 1046/1637, is a square mass of red stone inlaid with white and black marble, with tall octagonal corner towers; the tomb of his empress [[person/nur-jahan|Nur Jahan]] nearby repeats the form at half the size. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 40} The tomb of [[person/muhammad-iqbal|Muhammad Iqbal]] is a severe rectangular cell whose white marble interior is carved with lines from his Zabur-i 'Ajam. Of the gardens, the Shalimar, completed in 1052/1642, was entered at its lowest terrace and led up past cascades to a platform set in a tank and 450 fountains. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 41}
