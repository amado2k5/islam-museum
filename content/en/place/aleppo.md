## Overview

Aleppo, in Arabic Halab, in Turkish Halep, is a city in northern Syria, among the oldest inhabited cities in the world. It flourished in the Hamdanid, Ayyubid, Mamluk and Ottoman periods. {cite:geonames-gazetteer} The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose account this page follows, calls it the most important town of Syria after Damascus. It stands at about 390 metres above sea level, on the north-western edge of the inland Syrian plateau, beside the small river Kuwayk, which comes down from the last foothills of the Taurus. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 94}

## Setting and resources

The town lies in a wide chalky plain with a healthy but severe sub-desert climate and low, irregular rainfall. The article lists wheat, cotton and sheep as the basic resources of the region, with olives, figs and vines, market gardens along the river and the pistachio trees for which the town has long been known. These local goods gave Aleppo commodities to trade and the base for manufacturing, chiefly textiles and soap. It also served as a market for the nomadic Arabs of the northern steppe, who brought sheep, alkalis and salt. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 94}

## Before Islam

The article holds that no other place still inhabited and flourishing can claim a comparable history. The city is first mentioned in the twentieth century BC under almost its present name, in conditions that imply a long earlier past, and it is thought to have grown from a prehistoric village whose rocky height, where the citadel still stands, made it one of the strongest defensive positions in northern Syria. Its early kingdom dealt with the Hittites, who at first were friendly and later, under King Mursil, destroyed the town. Aleppo then fell to the Mitannis and, about 1430 BC, again to the Hittites. After the Hittite kingdom collapsed the settlement seems to have shrunk to a small rural town. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 94}

Its recovery began with Alexander's conquests. Between 301 and 281 BC Seleucus Nicator founded on the site a Macedonian colony called Beroia, laid out with straight streets crossing at right angles, square ramparts and canals bringing water from the springs of Haylan. The article says this layout was kept in the Islamic town and in part survived into modern times. Under Rome, in the province formed in 64 BC, the city enjoyed a long peace and received an agora and a colonnaded avenue. A Christian community settled early, and in the Byzantine period many Jews lived there, as did Arabs of the Tanukh tribe in a caravan suburb outside the walls called al-Hadir. In 540 AD the Persian king Khosroes I burned the town, although the citadel held out, and Justinian later rebuilt its defences and built a cathedral. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 94}

## The Muslim conquest and the early caliphates

The article dates the arrival of Muslim troops before Aleppo to 16/636, under [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]]. The Arabs of the suburb surrendered at once and the rest of the townspeople soon followed. [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda]] signed a pact guaranteeing their lives, their fortifications, their churches and their houses in return for tribute. The first mosque was made by walling in the bays of the monumental arch at the entrance of the colonnaded street. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 94}

Attached first to the military district of Hims and then to that of Qinnasrin, Aleppo had no administrative or political role under the [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad caliphate]]. The Christian community stayed large, and it was more than a century before the Muslim population justified a monumental Great Mosque. The article says it is not known whether al-Walid I or his brother Sulayman built it. The mosque remained the chief place of worship until modern times. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 94} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 95} Under the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid caliphate]] the city was a provincial centre of no political weight. It passed to [[person/ahmad-ibn-tulun|Ahmad ibn Tulun]], was besieged by the Qarmatians in 290/902-3 and from 325/936-7 came under [[person/muhammad-ibn-tughj-al-ikhshid|Muhammad al-Ikhshid]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 95}

## The Hamdanids and the tenth century

In 333/944 the Hamdanid amir [[person/sayf-al-dawla|Sayf al-Dawla]] took Aleppo from the [[polity/ikhshidid-dynasty|Ikhshidids]]. For the first time since the coming of Islam it became the capital of a state and the seat of a ruler. The article notes the admiration that tradition gives to this prince for his campaigns against the Byzantines and for the literary life around the palace he built outside the walls, where [[person/al-mutanabbi|al-Mutanabbi]], [[person/abu-firas-al-hamdani|Abu Firas al-Hamdani]], [[person/ibn-jinni|Ibn Jinni]] and [[person/ibn-nubata-al-misri|Ibn Nubata]] were among those who gave the court a brilliance the article calls unique at the time. It adds that the administrative methods seem to have done little for economic life. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 95} In the winter of 351/962 Nicephorus Phocas appeared unexpectedly, took the town by storm after a siege and left it a ruin after a week of pillage and fire, killing or carrying off its people. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 95} The museum has a record of the [[event/byzantine-sack-of-aleppo-351|Byzantine capture]].

The recovery was slow. Sayf al-Dawla went to Mayyafariqin, and under his son the article sees the darkest period since the conquest: ambitious regents, neighbouring amirs, Byzantine invasions, Bedouin raids and repeated [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]] attempts on the town produced about fifty years of disorder. Even the Fatimid occupation of 406/1015 brought no real improvement. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 95}

## Mirdasids, Seljuks and the Crusader threat

In 414/1023 the Syrian Bedouin divided the country among themselves, and Aleppo went to Salih ibn Mirdas of the Kilab. His descendants, the [[polity/mirdasid-dynasty|Mirdasids]], held it for slightly over fifty years under the nominal overlordship of the Cairo caliphs, though family quarrels let the Byzantines and Fatimids intervene again and again. Turkish bands began to enter Syria in this period. In 462/1070 Mahmud had the khutba said in the names of the Abbasid caliph and the Seljuk sultan Alp Arslan, against the wishes of the townspeople, most of whom the article says had been Imami Shi'is since Hamdanid times. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 95}

After the last Mirdasid gave the town up to the Uqaylid Muslim ibn Quraysh in 472, Aleppo was contested for years. In 479/1086 Malik Shah sent Aq Sunqur as governor, and later [[person/tutush|Tutush]] and then his son [[person/ridwan-ibn-tutush|Ridwan]] held it. The article describes this small Seljuk line as locally important only: its territory was small and impoverished, it rivalled the Seljuk line of Damascus, and Shi'i and Isma'ili townspeople made its authority precarious. Its princes could not stand up to the Crusaders, who attacked the town in 493/1100 and 497/1103 and forced it to pay tribute. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 95} In the following decades Il-Ghazi, then Balak, and the qadi Ibn al-Khashshab figured in the town's defence, and the article says the Crusaders desecrated sanctuaries on its outskirts. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 95} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 96}

## The Zangids and the Ayyubids

In 523/1129 the sultan gave Aleppo to [[person/imad-al-din-zangi|Imad al-Din Zangi]], whose campaigns freed it from the Crusader threat. His son [[person/nur-al-din-mahmud-ibn-zangi|Nur al-Din Mahmud]] rebuilt the walls, the citadel, the Great Mosque and the markets, repaired the canals and founded the first madrasas, which the article presents as part of his effort to restore Sunni orthodoxy. It counts six madrasas of his in Aleppo, among them the Halawiyya, on the site of the former Byzantine cathedral, and the Shu'aybiyya, on the site of the first mosque. He invited Hanafi and Shafi'i jurists from Iraq and Upper Mesopotamia to teach in them, and Sufi convents were built beside the madrasas. He also founded a hospital and a hall for public judicial hearings. The article reads the failed Shi'i coup of 552/1157 as showing that this Sunni policy was bearing fruit. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 96}

After Nur al-Din's death [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Salah al-Din]] marched on Aleppo, but the loyal townspeople held out and even asked the Isma'ilis for help. He took the city only in 579/1183. He gave it to his son Ghazi, who ruled as al-Malik al-Zahir and was the first [[polity/ayyubid-dynasty|Ayyubid]] to take the title of sultan. Ghazi, his wife Dayfa Khatun and the mamluk Toghril kept Aleppo in the hands of Salah al-Din's direct descendants and made it the capital of a strong and prosperous state, second only to Egypt. The article calls this the high point of medieval Aleppo: new suburbs for the Turkish cavalry, trade with the Venetians under treaties of 1207, 1225, 1229 and 1254, a fully rebuilt citadel, a larger canal system and expanded markets. New madrasas and Sufi convents were built, among them the Zahiriyya and the Firdaws, and its intellectual life included [[person/shihab-al-din-al-suhrawardi|Shihab al-Din al-Suhrawardi]], [[person/kamal-al-din-ibn-al-adim|Kamal al-Din Ibn al-Adim]] and Ibn Shaddad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 96}

## Mongols and Mamluks

Under Yusuf II the dynasty reached its height and its collapse. Attacked by the Khwarazmians and then by the Mongols of [[person/hulagu|Hulagu]], Aleppo was abandoned by its ruler and part of its people and taken by assault on 8 Safar 658/24 January 1260, then sacked. Yusuf II was taken prisoner and put to death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 96} The museum has a record of the [[event/mongol-sack-aleppo-658|sack]]. After the battle of [[event/ayn-jalut-658|Ayn Jalut]], Aleppo was occupied by the Mamluks, retaken by the Mongols and recovered again, and it stayed [[polity/mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk]] until the Ottoman conquest as capital of a province second only to Damascus, guarding the northern frontier. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 96} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 97}

Recovery was slow: fear of another Mongol attack left the town half empty for nearly half a century, and the Black Death of 1348 and then [[person/timur-lang|Timur]] completed the damage. From the early fifteenth century the fall of the Armenian kingdom of Cilicia and of the Genoese posts on the Black Sea gave Aleppo an advantage as the starting point of caravans bringing silk from Gilan, sold on to the Venetians. Large khans and suburbs along the caravan routes arose, with mosques and Sufi lodges, and one suburb housed the Christians who acted as brokers and interpreters for European merchants. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 97}

## The Ottoman centuries

The Ottomans, after the battle of [[event/marj-dabiq-922|Marj Dabiq]], occupied Aleppo without fighting and made it the capital of a province, under the [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottoman empire]]. The article follows the struggles of the local Janissaries and the rival Ashraf faction, which lasted into the mid-nineteenth century. Despite heavy taxation the city became at one stage the principal market of the Levant. The Venetians had a consulate there from 1548, the French from 1562, the English from 1583 and the Dutch from 1613. Trade then declined, which the article attributes to Smyrna's rise, the wars with Persia, shifts in world trade, corrupt administration and the earthquake of 1822. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 97}

The article names the Ottoman khans and the endowments of Dukagin-zade Mehmed Pasha and Ibrahim-Khan-zade Mehmed Pasha, and judges that Aleppo possesses the most beautiful markets in the Muslim world. It lists the mosques of Khusraw Pasha and Bahram Pasha and the Ahmadiyya, Sha'baniyya and Uthmaniyya madrasas. It also describes new suburbs of craftsmen, and a Christian community, many of whom became Roman Catholics through European missions. Its verdict is that the first half of the Ottoman period was the culminating point of the city's history. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 97} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 98}

## The nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Egyptian occupation from 1831 to 1838 placed a heavy burden on the people, and the revolt of 1266-7/1850 is called the last spectacular act of a doomed social order. Under European influence new schools, newspapers, a new legal system, the tomato, kerosene and machines arrived, and western-style districts grew outside the old town. Railways reached it from Hama and Damascus in 1906 and from Istanbul and Baghdad in 1912. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 98} Joined to Syria after 1918, it suffered an economic crisis from the new frontiers, then became an industrial town and a political and intellectual centre second only to Damascus. The article gives over 450,000 inhabitants at its writing, among them 320,000 Muslims, 130,000 Christians and a few thousand Jews, and regrets that growth lacked town planning. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 98}

## Notes from other sources

One of the museum's other sources notes that Aleppo lies at latitude 36 degrees 46 minutes, with Alexandretta as its port on the gulf of that name. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 512} It says Hulagu took the city in 658 after a siege of ten days, ruined it and killed its people, and that in 922 it passed from Qansuh al-Ghawri to the Ottoman Selim. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 517} It also records several explanations of the name, from milk, from a building of giants, and the view of Aleppo's Jews that it was the Aram Zobah beaten by David. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 517} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 518}

## For Sprouts
Aleppo is an ancient city in northern Syria, one of the oldest inhabited cities in the world. It is famous for its great citadel on a hill in its centre and its long covered markets, and through the ages it was a great trading city on the caravan routes.

## For Explorers
Aleppo is a city in northern Syria, among the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world; its beacon is the famous citadel on a hill in its centre. It flourished in the Hamdanid, Ayyubid, Mamluk and Ottoman eras, and was a crossroads between the Mediterranean, the Euphrates, Anatolia and Mesopotamia, so its covered markets gathered the goods of East and West. The sources record that Hulagu took it in 658 AH after a short siege, and that the Ottoman Selim I took it from the Mamluks in 922 AH. Several stories are told about its name: from milk, from the giants' building, and others.

## For Scholars
Aleppo (Halab al-Shahba; Greek Beroea) is a city in northern Syria, among the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world, dominated by its stone citadel on a central hill. In Islamic history it appeared as the Hamdanid capital in the tenth century under Sayf al-Dawla, a court for poets and philosophers, then passed among Ayyubids, Zengids, Mamluks and Ottomans. The sources record that Hulagu took it in 658 AH (1260 CE) after a ten-day siege and destroyed it, and that Selim I took it in 922 AH (1516 CE) from Qansuh al-Ghawri. A trade junction between the Mediterranean, the Euphrates, Anatolia and the Jazira, its long covered souks gathered eastern silk and western goods; it was famous for textiles and soap. Several etymologies of its name are told: milk, the giants' building, and its Jews' view that it is Aram-Zobah. In modern times it suffered heavy bombardment and destruction in the Syrian war from 2012, then rebuilding began. Historians read it as a model of a routes-city layering civilizations.
