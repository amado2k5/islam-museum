## Overview
A city in northern India. It was the capital of the Delhi Sultanate from the thirteenth century CE, and then the capital of the Mughals. {cite:geonames-gazetteer}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam, which spells the name Dihli, places the city on the west bank of the Yamuna. It says Delhi was the capital of the earliest Muslim rulers of India from 608/1211 and stayed the capital of the northern dynasties until the deposition of [[person/bahadur-shah-zafar|Bahadur Shah]] in 1858. A few rulers preferred [[place/daulatabad|Dawlatabad]], [[place/agra|Agra]] or [[place/lahore|Lahore]] as their centre. From 1911 Delhi was the capital of British India, and after 1947 of independent India. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 269}

The usual Romanised form, Delhi, rests on the commonest early Muslim spelling, Dihli. Urdu and Punjabi spell the name Dilli, and the article dates that form certainly to the Prithi Raj Rasa of the seventh/thirteenth century. The origin of the name is obscure. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 269}

## The successive cities
Popular speech counts seven cities of Delhi, but the article finds that the centres of government in the area numbered nearer double that number, and it describes them roughly in order of time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 269}

The earliest settlement was Indrapat, the Sanskrit Indraprastha, a mound where the Purana Qila now stands. Legend ascribes it to the Pandavas, and potsherds from as early as the fifth century BCE and Kushan fragments of the first and second centuries CE have been found there. After a long gap came a ninth or tenth century Tomar town at Suradi Kund, with a large masonry tank. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 269} After the conquest, [[person/qutb-al-din-aybak|Qutb al-Din Aybak]] built a mosque in 588/1192. The early governors and monarchs then refortified and extended the old Hindu walls to form the first Muslim city, known by the name of its former occupant as Qil'a Ray Pithora. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 270}

The Khalji sultan [[person/ala-al-din-khalji|Ala al-Din]] repaired and extended the citadel of Lalkot. As protection against the Mongols he set up a camp on the plain of Siri to the north, then entrenched it, and about 703/1303 walled it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 270} [[person/ghiyath-al-din-tughluq|Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq]] chose a site about eight kilometres east of Qil'a Ray Pithora for Tughluqabad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 271} [[person/muhammad-ibn-tughluq|Muhammad ibn Tughluq]] walled in the suburbs that had grown between Qil'a Ray Pithora and Siri to make Jahanpanah, whose walls, some twelve metres thick, have almost entirely fallen. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 272} Another city, Firuzabad, extended from Indrapat to the Kushk-i Shikar and is now largely covered by the later Shahjahanabad; all that survives is the citadel known as Firuz Shah Kotla. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 272} The article lists it among the cities of [[person/firuz-shah-tughluq|Firuz Shah]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 276}

The Timurid sack ended Delhi's standing as a capital for a time. The Sayyid sultan Khidr Khan held court at Khidrabad and [[person/mubarak-shah-sayyid|Mubarak Shah]] at Mubarakabad, both on the Yamuna. The [[polity/lodi-dynasty|Lodis]] moved their seat to Agra, and Delhi became, in the article's phrase, little more than a vast necropolis, with the plains between Siri and Firuzabad covered by tombs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 272}

After the Mughal invasions of the early tenth/sixteenth century, [[person/humayun-mughal|Humayun]] settled at Delhi and in 940/1533 began a citadel called Dinpanah on the mound of Indrapat. [[person/shir-shah-suri|Shir Shah]] dispossessed him and completed it as the citadel of a new city; only its northern and southern gates remain. His son, popularly called Salim Shah, built the fortress of Salimgarh on the Yamuna about 957/1550. Humayun's return added nothing to the city's buildings, and the next two Mughal rulers preferred Agra and Lahore. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 273}

[[person/shah-jahan-mughal|Shah Jahan]], after eleven years at Agra, moved to Delhi, and on 12 Dhu al-Hijja 1048/16 April 1639 founded a new fort, the Red Fort, as the citadel of his city Shahjahanabad. The fort was finished after nine years, the walling went on at the same time, and many buildings followed, among them the congregational mosque begun two years after the fort. Shahjahanabad remained the Mughal capital, except for [[person/aurangzeb-mughal|Awrangzib]], who spent much time in the Deccan. Humayun's tomb complex, Nizamuddin and the shrines of Roshan Chiragh-i Dihli and Qutb al-Din Kaki at Mihrawli served as burial places for the later Mughals, and at Mihrawli stands a small summer palace used by the last of them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 273}

With the fall of the Mughal dynasty in 1858, the destruction of many buildings by the British during and after the mutiny, and the transfer of the capital to Calcutta, Delhi became a lesser town, a local administrative seat and a garrison. British expansion lay north of Shahjahanabad, where the Civil Lines were laid out, and the capital was moved there in 1911; the new city, first called Raisena, became New Delhi. Later growth went westward and southward. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 273}

The article warns of a confusion of names. Lalkot and Qil'a Ray Pithora were already called Old Delhi in Timur's time, but since New Delhi was built the phrase has often been wrongly applied to Shahjahanabad. After the Red Fort was built, the older fort of Humayun and Shir Shah was known as the Old Fort. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 273}

## Early monuments of the sultans
Because Delhi holds the earliest monuments of a settled Islamic power in the subcontinent, the article says, the first characteristic Indian Islamic styles developed there and spread widely. Its earliest phase, as elsewhere, reused material taken from Hindu temples. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 273}

The first mosque built in India, the Quwwat al-Islam, stands at Qil'a Ray Pithora on a temple plinth. Its columns came from some twenty-seven Hindu and Jain temples, and a great arched screen was added in 595/1199, carved with floral motifs and lines of naskh. In the courtyard stands an iron pillar of the Gupta period, which the article takes to be placed there by the builders as a relic and a sign of triumph. Qutb al-Din also began the minaret known as the Qutb Minar. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 274} See [[building/quwwat-ul-islam-mosque-delhi|Quwwat al-Islam Mosque]]. [[person/iltutmish|Iltutmish]] extended the colonnades and the screen, and added three storeys to the minaret, bringing it to 69.7 metres by about 626/1229. Its lower storeys carry bands of Arabic inscription, including panegyrics and verses of the Qur'an. His own tomb, of about 632/1235, is a square chamber whose interior is banded with arabesques and Qur'anic inscriptions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 274}

The tomb of his son Nasir al-Din Mahmud at Malikpur, locally called Sultan Ghari, is the first monumental tomb in India. The article adds that the tomb of the sultan [[person/ghiyas-ud-din-balban|Balban]], who died in 686/1287, is the first to use the true voussoired arch, a technical advance and a strengthening of Islamic building tradition against that of the Hindu craftsmen. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 274} See [[building/tomb-of-balban|Tomb of Balban]]. Ala al-Din Khalji planned a huge extension of the Quwwat al-Islam mosque, of which a few columns, the foundations of the north gateway and the first storey of a gigantic minaret remain. His southern gateway is the most notable part. The Jama'at Khana mosque at the shrine of [[person/nizam-al-din-awliya|Nizam al-Din]] is the first in India built of specially quarried materials. His tomb and madrasa, now much ruined, show for the first time in India domes on corbelled pendentives. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 274}

## Tughluq monuments
Ghiyath al-Din's work is the city of Tughluqabad and his own tomb, a red sandstone building faced with white marble and with strongly sloping walls. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 276} See [[building/tomb-of-ghiyath-al-din-tughluq|Tomb of Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq]]. Muhammad ibn Tughluq's Jahanpanah includes the Sat Pulah, a seven-span sluice regulating a lake within the walls, and near Begampur stands the Bijay Mandal, which the article reports as supposed to be the remains of his Qasr-i Hazar Sutun, with the first intersecting vaulting in India. His transfer of Delhi's elite to Dawlatabad scattered the northern craftsmen, and a rubble-and-plaster phase followed under his successor Firuz Shah, who reigned 752-90/1351-88. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 276}

Firuz Shah's many building and restoration projects are listed by the chroniclers Afif and Firishta and in his own Futuhat-i Firuz Shahi. Strict economy governed them, and red sandstone and marble were no longer used. Within Firuz Shah Kotla stand the ruined [[building/jami-masjid-firuzabad-delhi|congregational mosque]] and a three-storeyed pyramid carrying an Ashoka pillar brought from the Meerut district. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 276} Half a dozen mosques of 766-76/1364-75 show the period's style. They include the large [[building/begumpur-mosque|Begampur mosque]] in Jahanpanah, with a courtyard of 68.0 by 75.3 metres, the Sanjar or [[building/kali-masjid-nizamuddin|Kali mosque]] at Nizamuddin, the [[building/khirki-masjid-delhi|Khirki mosque]] and the [[building/kalan-masjid-delhi|Kalan mosque]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 276} Firuz built a madrasa at the Hawd-i Khass, where his own tomb stands in the south-east corner; see [[building/hauz-khas-madrasa|Hauz Khas Madrasa]]. His minister Khan-i Jahan Tilangani has the first octagonal tomb at Delhi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 276} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 277}

The shrine of Nizam al-Din has an entrance gate dated 780/1378-9 and a large stepped well. The shaykh's tomb dates from Akbar's time, replacing an earlier one by Firuz; Akbar Shah II added the dome in 1823. Among the graves to its south are those of Jahanara, daughter of Shah Jahan, and of Muhammad Shah, who died in 1161/1748. The shrine of Nasir al-Din Chiragh-i Dihli, who died in 757/1356, has an east gate of 775/1373, and Muhammad Shah built the wall around it in 1142/1729. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 277} See [[building/dargah-nizamuddin|Nizamuddin dargah]].

## Sayyid and Lodi tombs
The Sayyid and Lodi dynasties produced mainly tombs, concentrated at Khayrpur, Mubarakpur and Mujahidpur, in two types, square and octagonal, with a large central dome. The earliest octagonal example is that of Mubarak Shah, who died in 838/1434. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 277} The tomb of Sikandar Lodi, built about 924/1518, stands in a fortified enclosure; see [[building/tomb-of-sikandar-lodi|Tomb of Sikandar Lodi]]. The Moth ki Masjid, built about 911/1505 by Sikandar's wazir, is described by the article as one of the liveliest buildings in the whole of Islamic art in India. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 278}

## Mughal monuments
Babur and the early Humayun added little. [[building/purana-qila-delhi|Purana Qila]] was mostly Shir Shah's work; within it stand the octagonal [[building/sher-mandal-delhi|Sher Mandal]], which Humayun used as a library and from which he fell to his death, and a mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 278} The first major Mughal building in Delhi is [[building/humayun-tomb|Humayun's tomb]], whose foundations his widow laid in 976/1568-9, with the Persian architect Ghiyath. Sayyid Ahmad Khan dates it to 973/1565. It stands in a square garden, 340 metres on a side, the first charbagh in India to keep its original plan, and has a bulbous double dome. Near it are the [[building/tomb-of-isa-khan-delhi|tomb of Isa Khan]] and the [[building/nila-gumbad-delhi|Nila Gumbad]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 278} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 279} The tomb of [[person/abd-al-rahim-khan-i-khana|Abd al-Rahim]], Khan-i Khanan, who died in 1036/1626-7, is a smaller and simpler relative. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 279}

The main Mughal phase was the building of Shahjahanabad and the Red Fort in 1048/1638. Within the palace enclosure lie the Diwan-i Amm, gardens, and a range of palaces along the eastern wall, watered by the canal Nahr-i Bihisht. The Diwan-i Khass once held the Peacock Throne, which Nadir Shah took to Persia in 1152/1739 and had broken up. The fort originally had no mosque, and Awrangzib added the Moti Masjid in white marble. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 279} See [[place/red-fort|Red Fort]], [[person/nadir-shah-afshar|Nadir Shah]] and [[building/moti-masjid-delhi|Moti Masjid]]. The great [[building/jama-masjid-delhi|Jami Masjid]], the Masjid-i Jahan-numa of 1648-50, rises on an outcrop of the Aravalli ridge, with a sanctuary of 79 by 27.5 metres and three white marble domes. The [[building/fatehpuri-masjid-delhi|Fathpuri mosque]] at the end of Chandni Chawk is nearly contemporary. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 279} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 280}

The latest Mughal phase includes the tomb, madrasa and mosque of Ghazi al-Din Khan, father of Asaf Jah, outside the Ajmer gate, built in 1122/1710, where an Arabic school is still maintained, and the tomb of Safdar Jang, who died in 1166/1753, in what the article calls the last great Mughal garden. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 280} See [[building/mazar-safdarjang|Safdarjang's Tomb]]. The article closes by noting that the vast buildings of New Delhi show occasional echoes of Mughal glory but have no further Islamic significance. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 280}

## In Ibn Battuta's Rihla
In the second part of his Rihla [[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]] gives Delhi sections on its description, walls, gates, congregational mosque, shrines and scholars, and on the kings who held it. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.568} He describes the sultan's palace there, "Dar Sara", with its many gates; at the first sat players of trumpets, horns and shawms who sounded them whenever an amir or notable arrived, and outside it were benches where the executioners sat. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.355}

He relates that Sultan Muhammad Shah moved its people to Dawlatabad, forty days away, after they had written insulting notes and thrown them into the audience hall at night: he bought their houses and ordered them to move, they left their belongings behind, and the city was left "empty"; then he wrote to people of other towns to move there and repopulate it, which ruined their towns while Delhi was not refilled "because of its vastness and size, for it is one of the greatest cities in the world". {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.385} He says that when he entered it he found it empty but for a little habitation. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.385} There the sultan made him a judge of the Maliki school. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.412}
