## Overview
Tabriz is the traditional capital of the Persian province of [[place/azerbaijan|Azerbaijan]] and is now the administrative centre of its eastern province, at an altitude of about 1,340 metres. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose article this page follows, calls it in general one of the most important cities between Istanbul and India. Its history, as the article tells it, is one of frequent changes of master, earthquakes and rebuilding, with a high point under the Mongol Il-Khans. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 51}

## Setting and climate
The city lies in the eastern corner of an alluvial plain sloping towards Lake Urmiya. The chief stream, the Aji Chay or "bitter river", rises on Mount Sawalan and passes the north-western suburbs, and one of its tributaries runs through the town. Wild mountain country lies to the north, and the great Sahand massif fills the land between Tabriz and [[place/maragha|Maragha]]. For this reason the article judges the site to be the only suitable pass for communication between east and north, and says traffic between Transcaucasia and the south must also pass through it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 51}

Winters are severe, with heavy snow; in summer the nearby Sahand and the many gardens temper the heat. The article calls frequent earthquakes a feature of the city, and lists the worst as those of 244/858, 434/1042, 1641, 1727 and 1780. The shock of 434/1042 was noted by [[person/nasir-khusraw|Nasir-i Khusraw]] in his travel book, and the museum records it as [[event/tabriz-earthquake-434|the great earthquake at Tabriz]]. The town's fortifications were levelled under Nasir al-Din Shah, and the city has since absorbed its old suburbs and tends to spread west and south-west. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 51} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 52}

## The name
The article reports that Yaqut gives the pronunciation Tibriz, on the authority of a pupil of [[person/abu-l-ala-al-maarri|Abu l-Ala al-Maarri]] who spoke the local Iranian dialect. Today the name is pronounced Tabriz, or Tarbiz in the Turkish dialect now dominant in the region, and Armenian sources confirm the form with a. A popular Persian etymology explains it as "making fever run", but the article suggests the name may rather mean "that which makes the heat disappear", perhaps in connection with volcanic activity of the Sahand. The Armenian spelling reflects a northern Pahlavi form, which leads the author to think the name could go back to a period before the Sasanians. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 52}

## Early history and Arab rule
Identifying Tabriz with an ancient city of Media has caused much discussion. An Armenian historian of the fourteenth century says it was founded as an act of revenge on the first Sasanian king; the article notes that no ancient source has the story and explains it as popular etymology. When the Arabs conquered Azerbaijan, about 22/642, their effort was directed chiefly against [[place/ardabil|Ardabil]], and the article thinks Tabriz had become a mere village. It reads the later legend that Zubayda, wife of [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]], built the town in 175/791 as perhaps resting on her receipt of another estate in Azerbaijan. According to al-Baladhuri and Ibn al-Faqih, the town was rebuilt by the family of al-Rawwad al-Azdi, particularly his sons, who built its walls. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 52}

An earthquake destroyed the town in 244, and it was rebuilt before the end of the reign of the caliph [[person/al-mutawakkil|al-Mutawakkil]]. The local rulers seem to have been practically independent, since the history of the [[polity/sajid-dynasty|Sajids]] shows no trace of their intervention. After the Sajids, Azerbaijan became the arena of many struggles, among them those of the Daylami [[polity/musafirid-dynasty|Musafirids]]. The Rawwadids, successors of the Musafirids, can be traced at Tabriz down to 446/1054, when the Seljuk [[person/tughril-beg|Tughril]] received the submission of its lord. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 52}

Earlier geographers mention Tabriz only among the small towns of Azerbaijan. [[person/al-muqaddasi|Al-Muqaddasi]] already praises it, and [[person/ibn-hawqal|Ibn Hawqal]], about 367/978, calls it the most prosperous town of the province, with busy trade. [[person/ibn-miskawayh|Miskawayh]] describes a noble city with a strong wall, surrounded by woods and gardens, and Nasir-i Khusraw gives its area in 438/1046 as 1,400 by 1,400 paces. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 52}

## Seljuks and Atabegs
Tabriz is rarely mentioned in the history of the Great [[polity/seljuk-empire|Seljuks]], though Tughril is said to have celebrated his marriage with the caliph's daughter near the town. Under the Seljuk branch of Iraq, Azerbaijan played a larger part: in 514/1120 Sultan Mahmud spent some time in Tabriz to calm the inhabitants, who were alarmed by Georgian raids. After Mahmud's death his son Dawud settled in Tabriz and ruled from it, between 526 and 533/1132-9, a great fief made up of Azerbaijan, Arran and Armenia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 52} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 53}

The Atabeg Ildegiz then founded the line of [[polity/ildigizid-atabegs|Ildegizid]] Atabegs, who ruled the province until 622/1225. Tabriz passed to them only in 570/1174, when Pahlawan b. Ildegiz took it and gave it to his brother [[person/qizil-arslan|Qizil Arslan]]. During Qizil Arslan's time as Atabeg, 582-87/1186-91, Tabriz definitely became the capital of Azerbaijan. The article adds that the Ildegizids lived in great style, as odes by Nizami and Khaqani suggest, but that only their remains at Nakhchivan are known. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 53}

## The Mongol conquest
The [[polity/mongol-empire|Mongols]] first appeared before Tabriz in the winter of 617/1220, and the Ildegizid Atabeg Ozbeg bought them off with a heavy ransom; they returned the next year and left for a second ransom. In 622/1225 the Khwarazm Shah [[person/jalal-al-din-khwarazmshah|Jalal al-Din]] entered the town, which Ozbeg had abandoned, and held it for six years. In 628/1231 he left Azerbaijan and the Mongols conquered the whole province. Ibn al-Athir, quoted by the article, calls Tabriz the very heart of the country. The Mongol commander summoned the notables, levied a heavy indemnity, ordered the weavers to produce cloth for the great king and fixed an annual tribute. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 53}

## Capital of the Il-Khans
After the fall of Baghdad in 654/1256, [[person/hulagu|Hulegu]] settled at Maragha. Under [[person/abaga-ibn-hulagu|Abaqa]], 663-80/1265-82, Tabriz became the official capital of the [[polity/ilkhanate|Il-Khans]] and kept that position until the coming of Oljeytu. Its greatest splendour came under [[person/ghazan-khan|Ghazan Khan]], who entered it in 694/1295. The article says he ordered temples, churches, synagogues and fire-altars destroyed, and that the orders are reported to have been revoked the next year after an appeal by the Armenian king Hethum. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 53}

From 699/1299 Ghazan began a series of buildings at Sham, west of the town, meant as his resting place. The article lists a domed mausoleum taller than the tomb of Sultan Sanjar at Marw, a mosque, two madrasas, one Shafi'i and one Hanafi, a hostel for sayyids, a hospital, an observatory like that at Maragha, a library and archives, with endowments said to yield 100 tumans of gold. In the city itself he replaced the old wall, 6,000 paces long, with one of 25,000, enclosing the gardens and further quarters. On the slopes inside it the vizier [[person/rashid-al-din-hamadani|Rashid al-Din]] built fine buildings, and the quarter was called Rab-i Rashidi. The article adds that gold and silver coinage and measures across the empire were standardised on the standards of Tabriz. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 53}

At this time Tabriz was also an important centre of literature and mysticism, praised by Rumi in his Mathnawi. The Sufi poet Mahmud Shabistari lived and worked there among other places, and another notable Sufi master was Khwaja Muhammad b. Sadiq Qujuji, whose descendants later served as shaykh al-islams. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 53} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 54}

Ghazan was buried at Sham in 703/1304. In 705/1307 his successor conceived of a new capital at Sultaniyya, though the inhabitants were not easily moved. In 717/1317, under [[person/abu-said-ilkhan|Abu Said]], Rashid al-Din went to Tabriz and left the next year to meet his fate; his property was confiscated and Rab-i Rashidi sacked. After the battle of 736/1336 the family's property was again plundered by the people of Tabriz, and valuable collections and books disappeared. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 54}

## Jalayirids, Muzaffarids and Timur
Out of the anarchy of 736/1336 rose the Jalayirid dynasty, whose fortunes the article ties closely to Tabriz. Ashraf, the Chobanid, ruled from the city as the real power until Jani Beg Khan of the Blue Horde defeated him and displayed his head over a mosque door in 756/1355. The Muzaffarid [[person/mubariz-al-din-muhammad-muzaffar|Mubariz al-Din Muhammad]] seized Tabriz in 758/1357 and left after two years, and [[person/uways-jalayiri|Uways]] Jalayir then reoccupied it. Despite their intermittent rule, the article says the Jalayirids gained the sympathy of the people of Tabriz. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 54}

In 788/1386 [[person/timur-lang|Timur]] drove out Sultan [[person/ahmad-jalayir|Ahmad Jalayir]] and levied an indemnity on the people. In 795/1392 the "fief of Hulegu" was granted to Miran Shah, with Tabriz as capital. After Timur's death a long struggle between his grandsons troubled the city, until in 809/1406 Shaykh Ibrahim of Shirwan handed it back to Ahmad Jalayir amid great joy. Clavijo, envoy of Henry III of Castile, stayed in Tabriz in 1404 and 1405 and found it, despite its trials, very busy with considerable trade, praising its streets, markets and buildings. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 54} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 55}

## Safavids and Ottomans
The OCR of the article's passages on the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries is too damaged to read, and this page does not summarise them. What can be read concerns the long struggle between Ottomans and Persians. After twenty years of Ottoman occupation, the article says, the inhabitants killed many Turks in a revival of Shi'i feeling, and [[person/abbas-i-safavi|Abbas I]] invited the people to remove every trace of Turkish rule. In 1019/1610 an Ottoman army under the grand vizier Murad Pasha appeared before the town but withdrew after five days, short of supplies. This hastened the building of a new fortress in the Rab-i Rashidi quarter, and a treaty of 1022/1612 restored the earlier frontier. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 56}

Sultan [[person/murad-iv-uthmani|Murad IV]] entered Tabriz on 12 September 1635; the article judges that the aim was plunder rather than conquest, and says he ordered the town destroyed before withdrawing. The museum records this as [[event/capture-of-tabriz-1635|the capture of Tabriz in 1635]]. The treaty of 1049/1639 gave Persia a frontier that, the article says, largely survives. Haji Khalifa reported that the old ramparts had vanished, and of Sham-Ghazan only the mosque of [[person/uzun-hasan|Uzun Hasan]] was left. Yet travellers a few years later, the article reports, found a splendid revival: [[person/evliya-celebi|Evliya Celebi]], in 1057/1647, gave detailed statistics of its madrasas, caravanserais, houses and gardens. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 56}

## The Qajar period and the revolution
Under the [[polity/qajar-iran|Qajars]] Tabriz was the residence of the heir to the throne, and until the accession of Muhammad Shah foreign diplomatic missions spent most of their time there; their transfer to [[place/tehran|Tehran]] marked the definite move of the political capital to that city. On 8 July 1850 the Bab was executed in Tabriz at the entrance to the arsenal; the museum's page on the [[group/babis|Babis]] covers the movement. A census of 1842 recorded 100,000 to 120,000 people, and in 1895 the estimate was 150,000 to 200,000, including 3,000 Armenians. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 58}

The article stresses the part played by the Turkish-speaking people of Tabriz in the Persian constitutional movement. Open rebellion began there on 23 June 1908, with [[person/sattar-khan|Sattar Khan]] and Baqir Khan closely associated with the defence. Government troops blockaded the city completely from February 1909, and a Russian force entered on 30 April 1909. Ottoman troops occupied Tabriz in June 1918. A railway to Julfa, opened in May 1916, was the first on Persian soil. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 58} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 59}

From the second city of Persia in the nineteenth century, the article says, Tabriz had become the fourth by 1980, with some 600,000 people, and 1,088,985 in the 1991 census. Its industries include carpet-weaving, textiles and leatherworking. From December 1945 it was the capital of an autonomous Democrat Party regime under Ja'far Pishawari, supported by Soviet occupation but also voicing local grievances, including discrimination against Azeri Turkish; Persian forces entered the city on 12 December 1946. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 59}

## Monuments
Only part of the article's section on architecture is legible. The best surviving example of Kara Koyunlu patronage, it says, is the Blue Mosque, named for its extraordinary blue tile revetment. It was part of a complex erected in 870/1465 by Khatun Jan, wife of the ruler [[person/jahan-shah-qaraqoyunlu|Jahanshah]], which included a Sufi hospice and was intended as a mausoleum for the queen and her family. The mosque has a domed square hall 16 metres across, enclosed on three sides by a U-shaped corridor of nine domes, with a smaller domed mihrab hall behind it. The author judges the tilework unequalled in variety and technical skill. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 60}

## Tabriz in Yaqut
Yaqut, in the museum's earlier record, says Tabriz was once a village until Ruwad al-Azdi settled there in the days of al-Mutawakkil, after which his son and brothers built palaces and a wall. In Yaqut's time it was the capital and largest city of Azerbaijan, a fine place with strong walls, flowing rivers and orchards, whose people made terms with the Tatars in 618. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p. 766} {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut p. 767}
