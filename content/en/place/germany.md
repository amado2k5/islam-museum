## Overview
Germany is a country in Central Europe, according to the place card. It appears in two books read for the museum, Ali Muhammad al-Salabi's *al-Dawla al-Uthmaniyya* (2001) and the Arabic *Hadir al-Alam al-Islami* with the notes of Shakib Arslan, in three roles: one of the lands that took part in European campaigns against the Ottomans in the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries; the Germany of Kaiser Wilhelm and Bismarck, towards which the Ottomans leaned in their last decades; and a member of the Central Powers in the First World War. The books also mention German orientalists and the poet Goethe. No entry for Germany was found in Yaqut's *Mu'jam al-Buldan* or al-Qazwini's *Athar al-Bilad*, so this page relays the statements of modern authors, each judgement attributed to its author. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.66} {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.317}

## European campaigns and the Holy Roman Empire
Al-Salabi writes that King Sigismund of Hungary and Pope Boniface IX called for a European coalition against the Ottoman state which reached 120,000 fighters of several nationalities, among them Germany, France, England, Scotland, Switzerland, Luxembourg, the southern Low Countries and some Italian principalities; the campaign set out for Hungary in 1396 (the book also prints 800 AH, which does not match that year) and ended with most of the Christians beaten by Bayezid at Nicopolis. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.66} See [[event/battle-of-nicopolis-1396|the battle of Nicopolis]].

On Khayr al-Din Barbarossa he says Charles V was emperor of the Holy Roman Empire, which then comprised Spain, Belgium, Holland, Germany, Austria and Italy and defended Christian Europe against the Ottoman danger towards eastern and central Europe. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.215} He adds that [[person/charles-v|Charles V]] attacked north-eastern France and was defeated, then had to go to Germany, where the Protestant rebellion against Catholicism in general and against him in particular had taken on serious proportions. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.232}

## Germany under Abd al-Hamid II
Al-Salabi relates Prince Abd al-Hamid's journey to Europe in 1867, naming among the monarchs of the time Wilhelm I in Germany, and says the delegation visited France, England, Belgium and Austria-Hungary; the prince, he says, came away judging France "a country of pleasure", England a country of wealth, agriculture and industry, and Germany "a country of order, military and administration", and his admiration for Germany was great, so that as sultan he entrusted the training of the army to it. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.400} See [[person/abd-al-hamid-ii-uthmani|Abd al-Hamid II]].

On the Congress of Berlin he writes that Chancellor Bismarck called it, fearing that British resistance to Russia would bring a general European war and threaten the German union, and invited the great powers, England, France, Germany and Austria, to Berlin to revise the Treaty of San Stefano. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.415} The book cites some historians that behind the scenes Bismarck offered to partition the Ottoman empire, Egypt to Britain, Tunis and Syria to France, Bosnia and Herzegovina to Austria and the Straits to Russia, and that these offers did not enter the congress's resolutions. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.415} He holds that Abd al-Hamid created friction between Russia and Germany, and quotes the memoirs of Wilhelm II, in which a Russian general says the Congress of Berlin was a great mistake of Bismarck that destroyed Russian trust in Germany. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.414} The congress's date is printed in the book as 1305 AH (1887), which does not agree with the war of 1877 that precedes it, so no year is relied on here.

Al-Salabi describes Wilhelm II as a friend of Abd al-Hamid and "the only ally of the Ottomans in Europe", the book's own description. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.446} Turkey then entered the First World War on the side of the Central Powers, Germany and Austria, while the Arabs, by the book's account, were drawn to the Allies. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.466}

## Germany in Hadir al-Alam al-Islami
In the main text, Muslim leaders understood that Turkey had become a pliant instrument in Germany's hand, obeying its orders; their wise men saw no reason to warm themselves at Germany's fire or to endorse its far ambitions for world domination, since Muslims would gain nothing, and preferred that the West be left to fight itself so that the Muslim world could gather its strength. This is the book's reading, not the judgement of this page. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.317}

In Arslan's notes the German emperor came to Tangier and declared that nobody could touch Morocco's independence; Germany sent a warship to the roads of Agadir when the quarrel with France arose, and drew back because Britain had promised France that the British fleet would be at its disposal; Arslan counts this among the causes of the war of 1914. This is the opinion of the author of the note. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.116} See [[place/morocco|Morocco]].

## German orientalists and Goethe
The book reports from Dermenghem, page 273 of his work as it cites it, that Goethe, "the greatest of the German poets", said that if this is Islam, are we not all Muslims. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.92} Arslan's notes summarise what the German orientalist and physician Max Meyerhof says in his book *The Islamic World*, among it that the exaggerations of Arab poets do not suit Western taste while Arab metre, order and gift of expression are very sweet. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.113} See [[person/shakib-arslan|Shakib Arslan]].
