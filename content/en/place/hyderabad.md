## Overview
Hyderabad of the Deccan is a city and Muslim-ruled principality in southern India, described on the place card today as the capital of Telangana. In Prince Shakib Arslan's twentieth-century account, written as commentary on Stoddard's book, it is the first of the principalities that stood under British protection in India: ruled by a Muslim called the Nizam, with about 13 million people, mostly Hindus, and a capital that is mostly Muslim. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.335} Everything on this page is Arslan's account and what he quotes, with figures from his time, and is not a description of the city today; the museum sources at hand do not give the founding date of the city itself.

## The Deccan before Hyderabad
Arslan relates that the greatest kingdom to split from the empire of Delhi was the Deccan, founded by the Bahmanis, with its seat at "Kulbarga" near Hyderabad, and covering what is today the realm of the Nizam of Hyderabad plus Berar and the Maratha country. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 pp.294-295} He says Ahmad I Bahmani took Warangal in 1424 and that the Bahmanis were at their strongest under Muhammad II through the efforts of his vizier Mahmud Gawan, so that Deccan rule stretched from the Bay of Bengal to the Arabian Sea, then after his death divided into five independent states. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.295} Among them he names the Qutb Shahi state at Golconda, from 1512 to 1687, whose kings were of Persian origin, according to Arslan. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.295} He says the states of Ahmadnagar and Bijapur made peace and were joined by the kings of Bidar and Golconda, and the four marched on Vijayanagar and killed its raja at the battle of Talikota in 1565. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.295} See [[polity/bahmani-sultanate|the Bahmani Sultanate]], [[polity/deccan-sultanates|the Deccan sultanates]], [[place/golconda|Golconda]] and [[place/bijapur|Bijapur]].

After Aurangzeb took Golconda and Bijapur in 1685, by Arslan's account, he says the Mughal empire came apart after Aurangzeb's death, and "the state of Hyderabad of the Deccan" arose in 1724 beside the states of Bengal and Awadh. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 pp.310-311} See [[person/aurangzeb-mughal|Aurangzeb]] and [[polity/mughal-empire|the Mughal Empire]].

## With the English
Arslan states that Lord Cornwallis stirred up the Nizam of Hyderabad on one side and the Marathas on the other against Tipu Sultan, so that the English, the Marathas and the Nizam's army attacked him from four directions in 1790, and it ended with a peace in 1792 in which Tipu gave up part of his land and paid an indemnity. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.319} He says most Muslim princes in India backed the English, those who helped them most being the Nizam of Hyderabad, the ruler of Bhopal and the ruler of Rampur, and that many were rewarded with new grants when rule passed from the Company to the Crown. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.313} See [[person/tipu-sultan-mysuri|Tipu Sultan]] and [[place/mysore|Mysore]].

In his count of Muslim-ruled principalities in India he gives Hyderabad of the Deccan 13,374,000 people and a yearly income of 1,760,000 English pounds, and says its relations were directly with the British Indian government, a privilege no other principality had. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 4 p.316} Elsewhere he describes it as having an army of 30,000, mostly Arabs from Hadramawt, a resident minister from the English whose influence was limited, near-complete internal independence and a small tribute to England. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.335} He also says its Muslims numbered about two million against eleven million Hindus. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.336}

## The Nizam and the caliphate
Arslan calls the Nizam of Hyderabad the wealthiest of Muslim kings and one of the richest in the world, who had a generous hand towards the Ottoman house; when the caliph Abd al-Majid was driven out of Turkey and took refuge in Europe almost penniless, the Nizam settled three hundred pounds a month on him. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 p.335} He adds that ties grew in early 1932 with the marriage of the caliph's daughter to the Nizam's son, that the match was ill received in Ankara and in England, and he judges that both sides' fears were misplaced. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 1 pp.335-336} This is Arslan's account and opinion.

## Jamal al-Din al-Afghani in Hyderabad
In his note on Jamal al-Din al-Afghani, Arslan says Goldziher reported that when he was exiled from Egypt to India he was lodged in Hyderabad of the Deccan, where he wrote his "Refutation of the Materialists". {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923 2 p.302} See [[person/jamal-al-din-al-afghani|Jamal al-Din al-Afghani]].
