## Summary
Knowledge for these four was a trust paid for with a lifetime, not prestige bought with it: an imam who gave jurisprudence its grammar, an imam carried to the flogger who did not yield, a knower who taught love without condition, and a poetess who made her tongue an endowment for truth.

## Al-Shafi'i: the founder of legal theory
[[person/al-shafii|Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i]] journeyed from Gaza to Mecca, then to Medina to study under Malik, then to Iraq, Yemen and Egypt, until he joined the jurisprudence of the people of hadith and the people of reason and wrote the "Risala" that laid down the first rules of legal reasoning {cite:britannica-al-shafii}. He said of his travels that no one had travelled for knowledge a journey more profitable than his — and he was issuing rulings before he came of age {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Ahmad: the steadfastness of the inquisition
[[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]] was tried in the [[event/mihna-of-the-quran|inquisition over the createdness of the Qur'an]] in the days of al-Mu'tasim, al-Wathiq and al-Mutawakkil: imprisoned, flogged and released on bail, and he refused to declare the Qur'an created even while declining to debate his opponents {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999}. When the tide turned, his stand in [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] became the honour of the people of hadith and a lesson that knowledge is not bought with comfort {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Rabi'a al-Adawiyya: the free knower
[[person/rabia-al-adawiyya|Rabi'a al-Adawiyya]] was born into bondage and freed; in [[place/basra|Basra]] she made her house a school of renunciation and love, teaching that God is loved for Himself — not out of desire for paradise or fear of the fire — until her words and her stands became a chapter of their own in the books of the Sufis and of women {cite:sifat-al-safwa-tartusi-2012}.

## Al-Khansa: the poetess who offered her sons
[[person/al-khansa|Al-Khansa]] was the greatest Arab elegist of the jahiliyya and of Islam in mourning her brothers Sakhr and Mu'awiya; when she and her sons embraced Islam they went out to [[event/battle-of-al-qadisiyya|al-Qadisiyya]] and all four were killed, and to the condolers she spoke her famous words: praise be to God who honoured me with their martyrdom {cite:alam-al-nisa-kahhala-risala-1984-v1}. Her poetry remains a school in how the word, for its people, is a striving of another kind {cite:muruj-al-dhahab}.

## For Sprouts
Al-Shafi'i travelled between cities until he taught people how to reason; Ahmad was beaten and jailed and did not change his word; Rabi'a taught people to love God without wanting anything; and al-Khansa gave her four sons and praised God.

## For Explorers
Four rooms, four prices: the journey, the lash, the renunciation, the sons. Read each hero's record together with the book or the city that carried their learning.

## For Scholars
Britannica and the Encyclopaedia of Islam for al-Shafi'i; the book of Companion virtues and the broad encyclopaedia for Ahmad; Sifat al-Safwa (Ibn al-Jawzi/al-Tartusi edition) for Rabi'a; Kahhala's A'lam al-Nisa and Muruj al-Dhahab for al-Khansa. See each person's record for fuller sources.

## Notes for researchers
- Ahmad's imprisonment and flogging are established in the biographical and virtue books; the scene's details differ in wording.
- Some of Rabi'a's verses circulate in later Sufi books; attributing them to second-century texts needs continuing verification.
