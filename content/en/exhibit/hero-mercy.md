## Summary
Four people made mercy a form of strength: a preacher who moved hearts rather than frightened them, a woman who sheltered what remained of her household, a sultan who built a house of justice, and an exile who protected his Christian neighbours on the day of the riots.

## Al-Hasan al-Basri: the merciful preacher
[[person/al-hasan-al-basri|Al-Hasan al-Basri]] grew up in the Prophet's household — Umm Salama raised him — and sat teaching in [[place/basra|Basra]] until he became the greatest Successor in sermons and renunciation {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. His lesson went through the heart before the tongue: he softened souls toward their reckoning and gave sinners relief on the condition of repentance, and his saying on the difference between the believer and the hypocrite became the most famous words of the Successors {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim}.

## Zaynab: the lady of the embrace
At [[event/battle-of-karbala|Karbala]] [[person/zaynab-bint-ali|Zaynab bint Ali]] lost her brother al-Husayn and those of her household who perished; on the night of the burning and the captivity she became a mother to the orphans and a guardian to the sick, and she carried the event's testimony into the courts of Kufa and Damascus {cite:momen-1985}. The books of women's biography remember her patience as she answered tyranny with words greater than the sword {cite:nisa-hawl-alrasul}.

## Nur al-Din: the sultan who built the house of justice
[[person/nur-al-din-mahmud-ibn-zangi|Nur al-Din Mahmud ibn Zangi]] was sultan of [[place/damascus|Damascus]]; he founded there a House of Justice where he sat for grievances and gave the weak their due against the strong — even against himself and his own family {cite:shadharat-dhahab}. He united Syria and Egypt into one front that prepared the recovery of Jerusalem, until historians called him the forerunner of the victory Saladin completed {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434}.

## Abd al-Qadir: the exile who shielded a city
[[person/abd-al-qadir-al-jazairi|Abd al-Qadir]] led the Algerian resistance for fifteen years; in his Damascus exile, when the riots of 1860 CE broke out, he went out himself with his Algerian followers to protect the city's Christians and opened his house and stables to the fugitives, so that thousands were saved through him {cite:al-aalam}. French officers and others wrote testimonies of how he rescued them, until his name became a byword for nobility in the West {cite:tdv-bagy}.

## For Sprouts
Al-Hasan was a teacher who softened hearts; Zaynab became a mother to all the children on a terrible day; Nur al-Din built a house where every wrong could be heard; and Abd al-Qadir saved his neighbours in a city far from his home.

## For Explorers
Mercy here is acts, not slogans: a preacher's chair, a captive's tent, a house of justice, an exile's shield. Four rooms and four centuries — read each hero's record together with the city where the deed was done.

## For Scholars
Each room's sources sit in the hero's record: the Encyclopaedia of Islam and the Fihrist for al-Hasan; Momen's book on Shi'ism and "Women Around the Messenger" for Zaynab; Shadharat al-Dhahab and the History of the Caliphs for Nur al-Din; al-Zirikli's al-A'lam and the Turkish encyclopaedia for Abd al-Qadir.

## Notes for researchers
- Abd al-Qadir's stand in Damascus in 1860 CE is also documented in Western sources; the counts of the rescued differ between books.
- Calling Nur al-Din "the forerunner of the victory" is later historians' phrasing, not a claim of consensus.
