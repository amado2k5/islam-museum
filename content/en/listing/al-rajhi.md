## Summary
Alrajhi Bank is a Saudi bank based in Riyadh, formerly known as Al Rajhi Banking and Investment Corporation; 2015 data made it the world's largest Islamic bank by capital. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en} It is one of the Kingdom's largest joint-stock companies and has more than 600 branches. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en}

## Founding and name
The bank dates its founding to 1957. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en} It traded as Al Rajhi Banking and Investment Corporation until 2006, when it changed its name and identity to Alrajhi Bank. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en} That same year it opened in Malaysia, its first step outside Saudi Arabia after some fifty years of operating only at home. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en}

## What it does
It offers deposits, loans, investment advice, securities trading, remittances, cards and consumer finance, and the article states that all its services are offered according to Islamic requirements. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en} In September 2016 it became the first bank in Saudi Arabia to partner with the Ministry of Housing in its part-state-funded mortgage programme; consumer banking then generated about 70% of its assets. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en} It holds 18% of the Kingdom's mortgage market. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en} In 2025 it announced partnerships with Porsche Services Middle East & Africa for vehicle finance, with the AI company Mozn against financial fraud, and with ServiceNow to automate its operations. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en}

## Rankings
It was first on [[award/forbes-middle-east-lists|Forbes Middle East]]'s 30 Most Valuable Banks 2025 and fifth on its Top 100 Companies list for that year. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en} {cite:forbes-middle-east-lists-2025}

## Allegations and outcomes
After the attacks of 11 September 2001 the bank was named in US lawsuits alleging that it had been used for transactions by people and charities with terrorist ties, and in 2002 US officials searched Alrajhi-related charities and businesses in Virginia. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en} In March 2002, under Operation Green Quest, a US Treasury effort to disrupt terrorist financing, agents searched 14 Virginia businesses and charities associated with a foundation set up in 1983 on whose first board three Alrajhi family members sat; federal grand jury subpoenas in 2006 showed that the bank was not directly related to the entities searched. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en} Media reports from 2004 said the Alrajhi name appeared in a document said to list early funders of an armed group, while the 9/11 Commission, in discussing that document, did not name the Alrajhis. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en} The Wall Street Journal reported in 2007 that a 2003 CIA report had described the bank as a conduit for extremist finance. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en}

The bank answered by suing the newspaper for defamation over a 2002 article; the case was settled in 2004 without damages, and the newspaper published a letter from the bank's chief executive and its own statement that it had not intended to allege that the bank supported terrorism. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en} On reports that some of the hijackers banked with it, the article says the bank cannot confirm or deny them without breaching bank-secrecy law, and that the 9/11 Commission found the hijackers held accounts at many banks, including mainstream US banks, and that nothing indicated any suspicious-activity report should have been filed. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en}

In the courts, the US District Court for the Southern District of New York dismissed all claims against the bank in January 2005, and on 30 June 2014 the US Supreme Court left that dismissal intact, with prejudice, together with the claims against its founder and former chairman and its chairman, closing the claims brought against the bank and its officers by victims and survivors of the attacks. {cite:wikipedia-alrajhi-bank-en}
