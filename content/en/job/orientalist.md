## Summary

The müsteşrik is the Western scholar specialised in the East — its languages, religions, sciences, thought, arts and history {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}. The TDV entry traces the word: in 1683 'orientalist' meant a member of the Eastern or Greek church; in 1691 Anthony Wood called Samuel Clark an 'orientalian' for knowing some Eastern languages; the specialist sense appears in English in a 1779 essay on Edward Pococke and in French in 1799; 'orientalisme' entered the Académie française dictionary in 1838 {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}. Turkish renders the field şarkiyat, later doğu bilimi, and Arabic istishrāq, with müsteşrik for the practitioner {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}. The profession passed from church translation to university chairs to the nineteenth century's societies, journals and congresses, and it now carries the critique that tied it to mission and empire {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}.

## From the cloister to the chair

The entry dates the first serious instrument of Western Islamic studies to Peter the Venerable of Cluny, who formed a translation team in Spain; Robert of Ketton finished its Qur'an translation in 1143, and the anthologies of the 'Cluny corpus' followed {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}. The language chairs the churchmen had demanded since the 1250s were decided at the Council of Vienne in 1312 — Arabic, Greek, Hebrew and Syriac at Paris, Oxford, Bologna, Avignon, the Roman Curia and Salamanca — though they were slow to be filled {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}.

## The philologists' century

Guillaume Postel's Arabic chair at the Collège de France dates to 1539; he had come to Istanbul with the first French ambassador, learned the languages and collected manuscripts for the king's library, and in 1538-1539 published the first Grammatica Arabica {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}. Leiden's Arabic chair of 1613 went to Thomas Erpenius, whose own Grammatica Arabica of 1613 counts as the first methodical European work on classical Arabic; his successor Golius produced the Lexicon Arabico-Latinum of 1653, the standard for nearly two centuries, and Golius's pupil Warner gave Leiden some thousand manuscripts {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}.

## Societies and journals

The nineteenth century made the field a profession: the Asiatic Society of Bengal at Calcutta in 1784, Fort William College in 1800, the Société Asiatique at Paris in 1822 and its Journal Asiatique, the Royal Asiatic Society at London in 1823 and its journal of 1834, the American Oriental Society of 1842, the Deutsche Morgenländische Gesellschaft of 1845 {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}. The specialists' shared work was to learn and teach Arabic and Islam's other languages, to build grammars like Sacy's and Wright's, lexicons like Lane's Arabic-English and Redhouse's Turkish-English, to catalogue the manuscripts of the great libraries and to edit and translate the texts {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}. The first Orientalists' Congress met at Paris in 1873 {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}.

## The professional eye

The entry notes that contemporary developments in Muslim societies were long disdained by orientalists and left to economists, journalists, diplomats, soldiers and amateurs {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}. It names Alfred von Kremer of the Vienna Oriental Academy — thirty years a consular adviser in Alexandria, Cairo and Beirut — as the first specialist to treat Islamic history systematically, and Ignác Goldziher's Muhammedanische Studien (1889-1890) as the work that made Islamic studies a religious and cultural system in European science {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}. Christiaan Snouck-Hurgronje resided at Mecca as Abdülgaffâr and concluded Islam was a living, changing reality the Dutch should govern by {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}.

## The critique within the record

The entry also carries the counter-reading: beside those who see orientalism as innocent academic curiosity stand views tying the discipline to Christian missionary work — Paret, Rodinson — and to collaboration with colonialism, the last argued most fully by Edward Said, whose definition of the orientalist the entry quotes at length {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}. Napoleon's failed invasion of Egypt in 1798, which carried a company of orientalists who founded the Institut d'Egypte and produced the Description de l'Égypte, stands in the entry as the emblem of the union between orientalist work and colonial enterprise {cite:tdv-oryantalizm}.
