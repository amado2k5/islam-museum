## Summary

The hammâl moved loads on his back and on poles through the ports, the magazines and the markets {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 161-162, 171-172}. In Evliya's Istanbul the porters were a body with their own patrons and their own bath {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 161-162, 171-172} {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v1 bath list}. In nineteenth-century Alexandria the weigh-station porters' guild petitioned the state for its exclusive right to the porterage and for the rukiyya, the communal division of income, when the weighers began hiring outsiders {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 97-99}.

## The gedek men

The porters attached to the Inspector of the Flour-hall were appointed by imperial diploma, like his clerk, weigher, kyaya, chief porter, seven captains and chaushes, and they enjoyed a kind of fief called the gedek {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 162}. Evliya calls them all strong, stout men, abstemious in their food, who carried loads up to a thousand okkas; when one of them died he was replaced from the porters of Janberdi {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 162}. At Aidinjik, Bandirma, Erdek and Mikhalic, ten men would lift the bags called sighilim, weighing seven to eight Ottoman quintals, onto a single porter's back, while two other porters — the zille hamal — supported him at either side {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 162}.

## The pole-porters of the Custom-house

The porters called Zillehor of the Custom-house and those of the Flour-hall were the most famous in Constantinople {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 171}. The Sirik-hamal, the porters of the poles, lifted by them incredible loads of forty to fifty quintals: when they trod the ground in their heavy battaljizmeh boots the earth shook, and they marched in regular pace under the load crying together 'Ya Hayy' — O all-vivifying {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 171}. Their patrons, Evliya reports, were Ali, the Prophet and Salman the Persian, and in the great procession they passed in this manner, carrying their loads, followed by a kiosk built in imitation of the Custom-house with fountains playing, borne on their backs {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 171-172}.

## Porters of justice, ice and the mint

Beyond the magazines stood porters sworn to other services: the eight hundred porters of justice, the bevâbîn-i şeriat, who took the field with the kazaskers — their patron, Evliya says, was Abu Shayba, girded by Salman, to whose family the Prophet delivered the keys of Mecca {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer §211}. The ice-porters of Modania and the yürük stone-cutters of Bursa carried snow, ice and fresh water down from the mountains of Katirli, Modania and Olympus to the sea, for the chief of the Imperial ice-men and his nine magazines; three hundred boatmen were always on excursion under his direction {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 175-176}. And when the head of the coiners engraved a new die, three seniors of the porters of the Serai and three honest Solis stood present at his working, by the canon of Sultan Selim I {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer §308}. In the procession of the guilds the porters passed bending under the almond chunks and eggs of the bakers' display, carrying loaves the size of millstones and mill-wheels, six porters to a Ramadan dish and ten pair to a column of pie; Evliya counts them as the yamaks, the assistants of the bakers, since there is no bread without them any more than without salt and water {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 124-126, 185-189}. The city even kept a bath for them: the Sort-hammam was the porters' bath {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v1 bath list}.

## The weigh-station porters of Alexandria

In Alexandria in 1871-72 the members of the guild of porters at the weigh stations — the ta'ifat 'attalin qabbaniyya — petitioned the governorate: low-status, poor workers who carried coal and other goods to and from the port weigh stations where goods received an official weight, for which the state levied a tax {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 97}. Their grievance was that the weighers were bringing men from outside the guild to carry goods — cheaper, untaxed and unregulated labour that bypassed the porters' recognized claim to the trade {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 97-98}. Their bolder demand was that a communal division of income, the rukiyya, be carried out within the guild to compensate those who had lost out — the police, closer to the trades' life than other institutions, explained to the governorate that the rukiyya required all income to be divided among the rank and file of the workers {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 98}. The police reported that such rukiyyat still existed in some guilds, practised outside government intervention, and that the guild of brokers and agents was known officially to have agreed on it {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 98}.

## What the guild meant

The porters' claim rested on a principle the state itself still recognized: that only guild members were allowed to practice a particular trade, and that the state's interest above all was taxation {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 98}. Yet guild welfare under pressure was thin: Borg's account found the shoemakers' relief fund the only guild where such practice existed, and the object of the guild, in his words, was 'not benevolence' {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 99}.
