## Summary

The sahafi gathers, edits and publishes news in a paper, magazine or site. Journalism in Islamic lands was not all imported: it is the latest link in an old chain of public speech — the crier in the market, the preacher on the minbar, the secretary in the chancery, the postmaster carrying news between provinces {cite:tdv-matbuat}. But the newspaper did what none of them could: it put the daily printed news in every reader's hand.

## Birth of the Arabic press

The first Arabic paper was Muhammad Ali Pasha's al-Waqa'i' al-Misriyya (Cairo, 1828, Arabic and Turkish), later made all-Arabic under Rifa'a al-Tahtawi {cite:tdv-matbuat}. Khalil al-Khuri issued Beirut's Hadiqat al-akhbar in 1858 with the French 'journal'; Rashid al-Dahdah answered with the broader 'sahifa'; Ahmad Faris al-Shidyaq published al-Jawa'ib at Istanbul in 1860 and named it 'jarida', which spread; and 'majalla' stayed with Ibn al-Yaziji from 1884 as the name of periodicals {cite:tdv-matbuat}.

## The engines of the rise

Printing entered Islamic lands through the presses of Iberian Jews, then Greeks and Armenians; Ibrahim Müteferrika founded the first Turkish press at Istanbul in 1727; printing came to Egypt with the French campaign (1798), then Bulaq (1821) and Iraq {cite:tdv-matbaa}. With that machine the daily paper became possible, and the papers and magazines — the entry says — spread written fusha Arabic and multiplied scholarly, literary, political and social inquiry {cite:tdv-matbuat}.

## Where it stood, and how many

The map widened fast: al-Ahram at Alexandria (1875), then al-Muqattam, al-Mu'ayyad (1890), al-Hilal (1892), al-Adala (1897), Misr (1896), al-Jarida (1907), al-Bayan (1911), al-Sufur (1915). By 1929 Cairo had seen about 550 papers and 500 magazines, Alexandria 123 papers and eighty magazines, Beirut 180 papers and 115 magazines {cite:tdv-matbuat}. And through its editors-in-chief the paper became a school — Muhammad Husayn Haykal raised al-Ahram into a press institution {cite:tdv-matbuat}.

## The journalist and his arts

The trade branched into editor, correspondent, managing editor, editor-in-chief and proprietor, and into every written art: news, report, investigation, leading article and criticism {cite:tdv-matbuat}. It became a door to politics and struggle — the 23 July revolution issued al-Jumhuriyya as its spokesman in 1953 — and a door to the spread of learning, letters and da'wah {cite:tdv-matbuat}.

## Its Islamic wing

Islamic papers arrived early: Thamarat al-Funun of the Society of Arts at Beirut (1875) was the first Islamic paper, followed by da'wah and teaching magazines until every movement, school and al-Azhar had its journalistic tongue {cite:tdv-matbuat}.
