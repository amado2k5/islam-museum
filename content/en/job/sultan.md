## Summary

Sultan — from an abstract word meaning power, authority, dominion — became the standard title of Muslim rulers from the eleventh century {cite:tdv-sultan}. Its cognate saltanat meant both the sultanate itself and the hereditary transmission of power {cite:tdv-sultan}.

## From word to title

The sources say the first political use of the title was by Harun al-Rashid, who conferred it on his vizier Ja'far b. Yahya al-Barmaki; later, when Abbasid authority weakened, provincial governors who had gained real power in their territories, and then the Buyid rulers, were called sultan in practice {cite:tdv-sultan}. The title's wide currency began with Mahmud of Ghazna, who built an independent sovereignty in today's Afghanistan and eastern Iran, and with the Seljuks it became a settled institution {cite:tdv-sultan}.

## On the coins

The first numismatic attestation came in 434/1042-43, when Abu Mansur Faramurz of Isfahan struck Tughril Beg's name as 'al-sultan al-mu'azzam'; Tughril's own Hamadan coin of 435/1043-44 repeated the style, and the caliph al-Qa'im soon confirmed it {cite:tdv-sultan}. In 449/1058 the caliph personally enthroned Tughril in Baghdad and gave him the title 'sultan of East and West' {cite:tdv-sultan}. Later Seljuk rulers compounded it — 'sultan al-'alam' for Alp Arslan, 'al-sultan al-a'zam' for Sanjar — while 'sultanshah', sultan joined to the Persian shah, became a proper name for princes in the Kirman and Syrian branches and among the Khwarazmshahs {cite:tdv-sultan}.

## West and east

From the Seljuks the title spread through the Islamic world: the Khwarazmshahs and the Ayyubids adopted it, thirteenth-century poets sometimes applied it even to petty local rulers, and the Mamluks raised it into 'sultan al-Islam', 'al-sultan al-a'zam' and 'sultan al-salatin' {cite:tdv-sultan}. In Iran the Ilkhanids at first preferred 'ilhan' and 'khan'; Ahmad Teküder, the first Ilkhanid convert to Islam, took 'Sultan Ahmad' on his coins of 681-683/1282-84, and Ghazan Khan, though favouring 'padishah-i Islam', let the full range of sultan titles stand on coinage and inscriptions {cite:tdv-sultan}.

## Padishah and sultan

Epigraphic and numismatic sources show the title among the Jalayirids, Timurids, Qara- and Aq-Qoyunlu; Timurid historians used khalifa, sultan and shah almost as synonyms {cite:tdv-sultan}. When Shah Isma'il founded the Safavid state, 'shah' rose above 'sultan' in Iran, where 'sultan' slid downward to name governors and officers {cite:tdv-sultan}. In Ottoman usage 'sultan' appeared on Orhan Gazi's coins and from the fifteenth century stood before the padishah's name, strengthened into 'sultan al-salatin' and 'sultan of the two lands and the two seas' {cite:tdv-sultan}.

## The caliph's shadow

Classical theory allowed only one caliph, yet Fatimid and Andalusian Umayyad caliphates made three coexist; rulers therefore took 'sultan' while honouring the caliph's spiritual authority — they read the khutba in his name, and he confirmed their sultanates with titles, laqabs and gifts {cite:tdv-sultan}. The sultan was thus the political arm of a shared sovereignty: the caliph's recognition was the investiture, and de facto power — from Tughril's entry into Baghdad — soon made the sultan the senior partner {cite:tdv-sultan}.
