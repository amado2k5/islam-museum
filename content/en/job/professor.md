## Summary

The professor of this record is the holder of a madrasa chair — the müderris as a graded office, not the teacher in general, whom the museum records under job/teacher {cite:tdv-muderris}. The word became official after Nizam al-Mulk founded the Nizamiyya madrasas in the fifth hijri century, and the post carried a term of service, a salary and ceremony {cite:tdv-muderris} {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi}. In the Ottoman state it was a rank in the learned hierarchy reached by graded promotion and examination, opening the road to the judgeship, until the title ended in 1933 {cite:tdv-muderris}. A parallel licence, the dersiam, let a müderris give public lessons in the great mosques of Istanbul {cite:tdv-dersiam}. At al-Azhar in Lane's Cairo anyone competent became a professor by the licence of the sheykh of the mosque {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.

## The Nizamiyya chair

The TDV survey dates the opening of the Baghdad Nizamiyya to 10 Dhu al-Qada 459 — 22 September 1067 — distinct from the start of construction in 457/1065 and from the still earlier Nishapur foundation, whose claims the survey treats with reserve {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi}. The müderris entry makes the word official from these madrasas: appointment was formal, the professor wore a distinguishing dress, and dismissal was a matter of state — in Alp Arslan's reign Ibn al-Sabbagh was removed when Abu Ishaq al-Shirazi was persuaded to return {cite:tdv-muderris}. The Nizamiyyas ran on endowments that funded the teaching posts and the library posts together {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi}.

## The Mustansiriyya's four professors

Guillaume's portrait of the Mustansiriyya, founded at Baghdad in 1234, gives the chair its clearest early shape: four separate law-schools, one for each of the Sunni rites, each headed by a professor who had seventy-five students in his charge and taught them gratis {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}. The four professors drew monthly salaries, each of the three hundred students was assigned a gold dinar a month, and the college ran a kitchen giving daily bread and meat, a library whose rare books students might copy with supplied pens and paper, a bath house and a hospital visited by a physician each morning {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}.

## The Ottoman ladder

In the Ottoman section of the entry the müderris appears as a grade within the ilmiye hierarchy: appointment, an apprenticeship through the ranks, an examination, stated duties, and from the higher chairs a passage into the judgeship {cite:tdv-muderris}. Alongside the madrasa professors stood the dersiam — a müderris licensed to read public lessons in the great mosques, examined after his ijaza; the entry counts the title-holders in 1869 and follows its examinations into the nineteenth century and its place in the early Republic's Diyanet {cite:tdv-dersiam}. The müderris title itself was dropped in 1933 {cite:tdv-muderris}.

## Licence and livelihood at al-Azhar

Lane watched the profession at Cairo in the 1830s: any person competent to the task might become a professor by obtaining a licence from the sheykh of the mosque {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. The professors lived by teaching in private houses and copying books, with occasional presents from the wealthy; the students earned by the same means or by reciting the Qur'an in private houses and at tombs {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. Before the French occupation a sheykh who had studied in the Azhar lived in luxury on two pupils who served him as menial attendants; by Lane's day the profession had fallen so far that only extraordinary talent secured a scanty subsistence {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.

## The ijaza and the degree

Guillaume compares the Muslim professor's ijaza — the licence to teach or repeat the contents of a given document in the professor's name and authority — to the medieval licentia docendi, the earliest form of degree, while warning that the points of resemblance do not prove the European university was moulded on the Islamic pattern {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}. The obligation that no one set up as a teacher without adequate years under an authorised professor is, he writes, too obvious to need a precedent {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}. Al-Azhar still grouped its students in communal quarters and supported them partly by pious benefactions and grants {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}.
