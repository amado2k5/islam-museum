## Summary

The tarraz stitches garments and cloth with coloured silks and gold wire, for the market's counters and for the vezirs' cushions {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 463-464, pp. 221-222}. The hisba guards the customer's garment in his hands, forbidding the embroiderers to shift embroidery between the pieces the fullers and cloth-beaters bring them {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXI, pp. 44-45}. Evliya marches two embroiderer guilds through Istanbul, and in Lane's Cairo the craft also lives in the harem, where women embroider on the menseg frame and sell their work through a female broker {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 463-464, pp. 221-222} {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. VI, p. 187}.

## The garment entrusted

Ibn al-Ukhuwwa's rule is short and precise: repairers may not repair a valuable garment received from a fuller or cloth-beater except in the owner's presence, and the embroiderers and ornament-stitchers must not transfer embroidery from one garment to another among the pieces those trades bring them {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXI, p. 44}. Around the rule sit the fullers' own prohibitions — not to steal from the stuffs entrusted to them, not to wear them, not to pledge them, and to write the owner's name on every article {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXI, pp. 44-45}.

## The price of the stitch

Al-Shayzari prices the embroidery in the cloth merchant's accounting: the man who buys a piece of cloth for ten dirhams and pays a dirham each for bleaching, embroidery and repair may say it cost him thirteen, but if he did the embroidery himself he must say he bought it for ten and worked on it to the value of three {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 21, p. 82}.

## The gold wire of Istanbul

In the procession the gold embroiderers (Nakkashan Zerduzan) are sixty-five men with twenty shops, and a great number of them work at home; their patron is Ebisurkh, buried at Medain, and they work gold-embroidered cushions, sofa-cloths, curtains and cloaks for vezirs and great men, which dazzle the eyes of the beholders with the splendour of the gold wire — a neat handicraft, Evliya calls it {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 463, p. 221}. The handkerchief embroiderers (Nakkashan Yaghlik) are twenty-five men with twenty shops, their patron Senbi ud-din girded by Selman and buried near Damascus; they embroider with variegated silk cushion-cloths, handkerchiefs, towels, shirts and sheets, and pass embroidering, followed by the chief of the embroiderers with his nakibs, sheikhs, chaushes and the music {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 464, pp. 221-222}. Evliya adds a note no other source could give: my mother was famous in this handicraft {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 464, p. 222}. Elsewhere in the procession the work shows itself again: the saddlers adorn their shops with embroidered saddles, and the saddle-cloth makers with embroidered saddle-cloths and trappings {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 390, 395, pp. 207-208}.

## The menseg frame

In Lane's Cairo the craft runs on women's needles: their leisure hours are mostly spent working with the needle, particularly in embroidering handkerchiefs and head-veils upon a frame called the menseg, with coloured silks and gold, and many women, even in the houses of the wealthy, replenish their private purses by ornamenting handkerchiefs in this manner and employing a dellaleh, a female broker, to take them to the market or to other harems for sale {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. VI, p. 187}.

## Today

The embroidery frame and the gold wire both survive, in the ateliers of the old cities and in the workshops that dress ceremonial dress; and the hisba's question — whose garment is this, and whose stitch — is the one any commission embroiderer still answers {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXI, pp. 44-45} {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 463-464, pp. 221-222}.
