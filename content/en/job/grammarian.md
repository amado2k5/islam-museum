## Summary

The nahwi practises nahiv, the science covering the syntax rules of Arabic grammar and the i'rab — the vowel endings of words {cite:tdv-nahiv}. Its founding story tells that Ali gave Abu al-Aswad al-Du'ali a page defining the kinds of words and told him to 'follow this path' (unhu hadha'l-nahw), giving the science its name; the first users of the term were Qur'an teachers who cited 'the nahv of the Arabs' as the correct model for a usage {cite:tdv-nahiv}.

## Why the craft arose

The accounts agree that grammar was made necessary by lahn — errors of speech and recitation — spreading as non-Arabs entered Islam and mixed with the Arabs: on one report, Abu al-Aswad founded the science on the order of the Basran governor Ziyad ibn Abih or his son, or of Umar, after a recitation or speech error; on another, Ali founded it himself after hearing a reader's mistake {cite:tdv-nahiv}. Abu al-Aswad (d. 69/688) himself placed dot vowels at the ends of the Qur'an's words and called his own work 'the i'rab of the Qur'an' {cite:tdv-nahiv}. Because Iraq-Basra was where the corruption of the tongue ran deepest, the language and grammar work was done there {cite:tdv-nahiv}.

## Basra and Kufa

The craft grew in rival schools {cite:tdv-nahiv}. The Basra school, whose founder Abu al-Aswad is also counted the science's founder, ran in tabaqat: Nasr ibn Asim's peers first, the 'second al-Khalil' generation of Isa ibn Umar and Abu Amr ibn al-Ala second, al-Khalil and Yunus third, Sibawayhi fourth, down to al-Mubarrad in the seventh {cite:tdv-nahiv}. The Kufa school was founded by al-Ru'asi and his uncle Mu'adh al-Harra, and al-Farra's line produced its teaching; the two differed even in terminology — Kufan 'khafd' for Basran 'jarr' — and in classing the active participle as a verb {cite:tdv-nahiv}. Scholars who blended the two schools then formed the Baghdad school in the second half of the third century AH, which lasted to the mid-fourth; the Egypt-Syria, Maghreb-Andalus, Iraq and eastern schools that followed mostly kept the Baghdad line with Basran dominance {cite:tdv-nahiv}.

## Sibawayhi's burden

The earliest surviving grammar, Sibawayhi's al-Kitab, was written for teaching but proved so hard — tangled order, unsettled terminology, swollen with non-grammar matter — that it generated an entire auxiliary literature {cite:tdv-nahiv}. Khalaf al-Ahmar already wrote an epitome of the essentials for beginners; al-Jarmi wrote a commentary on its difficult words; al-Mubarrad and al-Sirafi wrote 'introductions to Sibawayhi'; and Zeccac, Ibn al-Anbari, al-Zajjaji and Abu Ali al-Farisi all composed clarifications — a commentary tradition that made the masterwork teachable {cite:tdv-nahiv}.

## The craft criticised

The grammarians drew fire for making language-learning harder: after Aristotle's logical works were translated, hypothetical cases and second- and third-degree causes entered the books, and al-Jahiz complained that students found grammar boring and that the nahwiyyun 'look for i'rab in every poem they see' {cite:tdv-nahiv}. The Cordoban Ibn Mada made the first systematic rejection in al-Radd ala'l-nuhat, calling for dropping the amil-ma'mul theory, the higher-degree causes and hypothetical reconstructions with no practical value {cite:tdv-nahiv}. With the madrasas of the fifth century AH, the inherited grammar entered fixed syllabi and shrank into textbook manuals — al-Zamakhshari's al-Mufassal and the versified epitomes like Ibn Malik's Alfiyya {cite:tdv-nahiv}.

## The modern grammarian

In the twentieth century, Arab and Turkish linguists trained in Europe — Tammam Hassan, İbrahim Anis and others — tried to renew Arabic grammar on functional and structuralist lines, while universities and academies published and studied the classical heritage; Abbas Hasan's al-Nahv al-wafi is named the most comprehensive modern grammar {cite:tdv-nahiv}. Nahiv survives as a school and university subject, and the nahwi's old title persists wherever Arabic syntax is taught {cite:tdv-nahiv}.
