## Summary

The armourer forged the weapons and the defences of the Muslim soldier: swords, lances, daggers, shields, and the body armour of mail, lamellar and quilted cotton {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 251-259, pp. 177-180; elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 Gorelik, pp. 30-33}. Evliya puts the sword-cutlers first among craftsmen — the faith having been established by the sword, every Muslim house needs one — and gives Istanbul a thousand and eight of them in two hundred and five shops {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 251, p. 177}.

## What the armourer made

Gorelik's survey shows the range of the armourer's craft in the early Islamic centuries: the mail shirt (dir' or zardiyya), the lamellar cuirass (jawshan) worn over it, the quilted-cotton garment (the gazakand) sometimes reinforced with mail or layered plates and worn alone or under metal, the favoured round shield, and rounded or conical helmets with aventails of forged metal, mail, leather or cloth {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 Gorelik, pp. 30-32}. Almost no surviving piece of Near or Middle Eastern armour is datable before the thirteenth century, so this craft is reconstructed from written and figurative sources {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 Gorelik, p. 30}.

## Inheriting three traditions

The Muslim armourer worked inside three inheritances: the Byzantine, which gave Syria, Arabia and Egypt the short lamellar and scale coat, the mail shirt and the round shield, the Roman one-piece cuirass never adopted but the Hellenic mail shirt widely so; the Sasanian Iranian, which gave the full harness of helmet joined to an aventail covering neck and face, mail shirt and quilted cotton under a medium shield; and the Central Asian steppe, whose successive waves of nomads brought lamellar cuirasses, long lamellar coats and segmented helmets — the impulse, Gorelik writes, for the most varied types of Muslim armour {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 Gorelik, pp. 30-32}.

## The sword-cutlers

Evliya counts a thousand and eight sword-cutlers in two hundred and five shops; their patron is David, and in the Prophet's time Esir the Indian, girded by Selman, who lived a hundred and twenty years and lies at U'shak {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 251, p. 177}. Their great factory under Mehmed II and Bayazid II stood before the chalk-gate near the lead magazine, where the old Constantinopolitan blades were worked out of the iron dug at Galata-serai; in Evliya's own day it stood opposite the bath of Mahmud Pasha {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 251, pp. 177-178}. The most celebrated cutler was deaf David; Sultan Murad IV, who well understood the sword, used only blades of Isfahan or of deaf David, and made him chief of the sword-cutlers by imperial rescript {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 251, p. 178}. In the procession they showed the named blade types — Senjam, Makrari, Zillehham, Kosh Kadem, Sonkor, Kessed, Suzik, Demirdelissi, Selamieh, Misri — the Syrian work of Mustaqim and the Indian work of Khalkan, polishing swords and playing tricks with naked blades {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 251, p. 178}.

## Cuirass, lance, dagger, shield

The old cuirass-makers were forty men in but four shops — more than a thousand cuirass-makers worked for the army and in the camp instead; their patron was David who first worked his armour preparing to fight Goliath, and in the Prophet's time Moslem Hadadi, whose cuirass the Prophet wore at Uhud before giving it to Sa'dan the Indian {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 255, p. 178}. They fitted their shops on litters with cuirasses from Kabartai and Taghistan and passed clad in armour from head to foot {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 255, p. 178}. The lance-makers were four hundred men in a hundred and five shops, displaying the lances called Sogu, Khisht, Jedah, Cheshetmeli, Kargin, Sunji, Harba and Sinan; the dagger-smiths thirty men in ten shops; the shield-makers (Kalkanjian) thirty men in ten shops, their patron David whose steel shield from Nahjiwan hangs, Evliya reports, in the great mosque at Jerusalem — Hassan Kattal Ghazi's cotton-and-reed shield, presented to the Prophet, began the fame of the shields of Aleppo {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 256-258, pp. 179}. The scabbard-makers added six hundred men in three hundred shops {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 259, p. 180}.

## The armourer's name

Individual armourers do surface: L. A. Mayer's book of Islamic armourers is the standard list, and Flindt notes it records none working at Bukhara — though Persian metalworkers, he adds, were found throughout the dar al-Islam, and Bukhara's bazaars sold mainly Persian blades {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 Flindt, p. 23}.

## Not yet covered

The sources give the guilds, the patrons and the products; they do not give the forging and riveting of mail step by step, wages and prices, or the provincial arsenal beyond Istanbul and Bukhara's bazaar {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 251-259, pp. 177-180; elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 Gorelik, pp. 30-33}.
