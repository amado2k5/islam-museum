## Summary

The lexicographer — the lughawi, the dictionary's scholar — collects the words a people uses to convey feeling and thought and orders them into a book {cite:tdv-sozluk}. Until the end of the fourth century AH such books carried various titles and no common term existed for 'dictionary'; the words lughat, ilm al-lugha, mu'jam and qamus only later became technical terms, with 'qamus' winning out through the fame of al-Firuzabadi's al-Qamus al-Muhit {cite:tdv-sozluk}.

## From questions to word-books

Arabic lexicography began with the Companions' questions to the Prophet about the Qur'an's gharib words, and the answers gathered around Ibn Abbas: the Gharib al-Qur'an compiled by Ata ibn Abi Rabah, the Lughat fi'l-Qur'an on non-Qurashi dialect words, and the Masa'il Nafi' ibn al-Azraq, in which Ibn Abbas answered two hundred questions with proof from old poetry, count as the oldest works {cite:tdv-sozluk}. Later centuries added the gharib al-hadith books to the same shelf {cite:tdv-sozluk}.

## The desert as the source

When trade and conquest mixed non-Arabs with the Arabs and corrupt usage spread, the philologists — Khalil ibn Ahmad, Khalaf al-Ahmar, al-Kisa'i, Nadr ibn Shumayl, al-Asma'i, Abu Zayd al-Ansari, Ibn Durayd, al-Azhari and al-Jawhari — began collecting the sound vocabulary {cite:tdv-sozluk}. They confined their material to the literary texts of the Jahiliyya and early Islamic centuries down to about 150 AH, above all poetry, and to the bedouin themselves, excluding tribes whose speech they judged spoiled by mixing with foreigners {cite:tdv-sozluk}.

## Three stages of the craft

The entry divides the making of Arabic dictionaries into three stages: unordered collecting by hearing; then arrangement by subject, which produced hundreds of single-theme booklets in the second century AH — Kitab al-Nawadir, Lahn al-Amma, Kitab al-Khayl among them; then the whole-language dictionary on a fixed ordering system {cite:tdv-sozluk}. The first of the last kind is Khalil ibn Ahmad's Kitab al-Ayn (d. 175/791), written near the end of his life, which aimed to contain every Arabic word {cite:tdv-sozluk}.

## Schools of ordering

Four orderings competed: by articulation points and permutation, the 'ayn school of Khalil's own Kitab; by rhyme order, crowned by al-Jawhari's al-Sihah, the arrangement whose ease of lookup none of the others matched and which al-Zamakhshari and most later lexicographers followed; by the first letter; and by subject {cite:tdv-sozluk}. Ibn Faris may have first named a dictionary 'mu'jam' with his Mu'jam maqayis al-lugha; Sahib ibn Abbad titled his al-Muhit, Ibn Sida his al-Muhkam wa'l-muhit al-a'zam, and al-Firuzabadi's al-Qamus al-Muhit made 'qamus' itself the word for a dictionary {cite:tdv-sozluk}. The largest Arabic dictionaries grew on this line: Ibn Manzur's Lisan al-Arab and al-Zabidi's Taj al-Arus {cite:tdv-sozluk}.

## Persian and Turkish lexicographers

The craft crossed languages: the entry's Persian section follows the dictionaries written for the Iranian languages, in India and in the Ottoman period, and its Turkish section follows the Turkish dictionaries, where 'lughat' competed with 'kamus' and 'ferheng' until 1932 {cite:tdv-sozluk}. Ottoman lexicography produced translations and epitomes of the classics — Mehmed Vankulu's Lugat-i Vankulu rendered al-Jawhari's Sihah, and Mütercim Asım's Kamus translation of al-Firuzabadi was the most successful Ottoman work of its kind and a madrasa textbook {cite:tdv-sozluk}.
