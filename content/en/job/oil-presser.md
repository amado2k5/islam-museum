## Summary

The zayyat is the man of the oil press and the oil shop {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXI, p. 92}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa sets a trustworthy overseer over the presses and writes the cleanliness rules of the work — scrubbed feet, a close-sleeved coat, a covered mouth {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXI, p. 92}; Lane finds the oilman of Cairo selling butter, cheese and honey with his oil {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}; Evliya marches two oil guilds through the Istanbul procession {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 243-244, p. 175}; and Chalcraft records the Egyptian oil-pressers' petitions against the untaxed European presses of the 1880s {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 147}.

## Inside the press-house

Ibn al-Ukhuwwa requires that a trustworthy man who knows the craft be appointed over the presses {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXI, p. 92}. The sesame may not be pressed until it has been washed, cleansed, roasted and pounded till all the husk has gone, and then ground {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXI, p. 92}. No workman descends to press it until he has cleaned his feet with a scrubbing-stone; about his middle there must be a coat with narrow sleeves for fear he should perspire, and his mouth is covered so that no spittle drops when he speaks {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXI, p. 92}. When work has ceased the troughs are covered with palm-leaf mats {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXI, p. 92}.

## The honest jar

The pressmen's jars must be measured, especially in the summer, for their weight decreases; a jar of standard measure contains twenty-six and a half Egyptian ratls {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXI, p. 92}. Their bottles and measures must be tested {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXI, p. 92}. The adulteration the muhtasib watches for is the pressing of unroasted cotton-seed: raw seed conceals the odour, so the pressers can practise the trick of mixing its oil into sweet olive oil; safflower-seed oil, the manual notes, is harmful to pregnant women {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXI, p. 92}.

## The oilman of the market

In Lane's Cairo the oilman (zeiyat) stood in the market streets selling butter, cheese and honey as well as oil {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}. In Evliya's Istanbul the linseed-oil merchants (Bezrjian) numbered nine hundred and thirty-one men with thirty-five factories; they passed in the procession on wagons pressing linseed oil, their dresses all shining with oil, oiling the young boys among the spectators with their hands, most of them coming from Nikdeh and Kayseri, and all clad in armour {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 243, p. 175}. The olive-oil merchants (Zeyat) were two hundred men with eighty shops, assistants to the grocers, marching likewise armed {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 244, p. 175}. Among the builders' train Evliya also counts the makers of oil presses at three men {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 639, pp. 230-231}.

## The tax on the press

Under colonial rule the pressmen's grievance was the unequal tax: Egyptian oil-pressers complained that European-owned presses were exempt from the oil-pressing tax, and a government memorandum of 1886 noted that the oil-pressers did not cease to formulate complaints, acknowledged the damage to the local industry, and recorded that unequal levies had forced some local-subject oil presses to close {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 147}. The oil-pressers' tax was among the duties abolished by the early 1900s {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 147}.

## Today

The village and market press-house gave way to industrial oil mills, but the craft's two questions remain where the manuals put them: the seed prepared honestly before the pressing, and the jar that holds what it claims {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXI, p. 92}.
