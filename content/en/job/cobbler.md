## Summary

The iskafi is the shoemaker who cuts leather, sews soles and makes the shoes and slippers people wear {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 29, pp. 92-93}. Al-Shayzari gives the craft a short chapter of strict rules on leather, thread and customers' time {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 29, pp. 92-93}, while Evliya Çelebi found the shoemakers of Istanbul one of the largest guilds of the capital — four thousand men whose armed loyalty Sultan Suleiman once invoked against his own Janissaries {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 403, pp. 209-210}.

## The craft and its rules

Al-Shayzari orders that shoemakers should not use too many old pieces of cloth as padding between the inner and outer linings or between the inner sole and the outer; they may stitch the padding for the heels, but must not stitch a sole that split during tanning, nor new leather, nor any leather that has not been tanned {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 29, pp. 92-93}.

## Thread and awl

The thread must be twined well and made no longer than a cubit, because a longer thread frays and grows too weak to pull through the leather {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 29, pp. 92-93}. They should not sew with pig's hair but with palm fibres or fox whiskers, which are better {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 29, pp. 92-93}.

## The customer's shoes

A shoemaker must not keep a customer waiting for his shoes unless a day was fixed in advance, because people are inconvenienced by returning again and again and by the withholding of their property {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 29, pp. 92-93}. The chapter ends with a fashion the muhtasib is told to forbid: paper, felt and the like must not be put into women's slippers to make them squeak as they walk — a taste of the women of Baghdad, al-Shayzari calls it shameful and unbefitting free-born people {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 29, pp. 92-93}.

## The shoemakers of Istanbul

Evliya counts the shoemakers (Papushjian) at four thousand men with three hundred and forty shops; they had seven factories in the Coral market where eight thousand unmarried men of the guild were lodged, and their own officers, appointed by an imperial rescript of Sultan Suleiman, freed them from every other jurisdiction and let them punish their own culprits {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 403, pp. 209-210}. When the Janissaries once refused their soup in discontent, Suleiman swore to break them with the shoemakers' help, and an armed crowd of forty thousand shoemakers gathered before the palace crying "Allah, Allah"; in reward the guild won four privileges, including a better price for a pair of papushes and a separate train with Turkish music in the procession {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 403, pp. 209-210}.

## The guild at the procession

At the procession the shoemakers pass armed but barefoot and bareheaded, their litters dressed with shoes and slippers of every kind — zenane, teleli, serhadli — papushes set inside boots and boots inside shoes, and men's boots of enormous size big enough to hold two men {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 403, pp. 209-210}. Their patron is Rushmad son of Mohammed Ekber, who lived a hundred years and is buried at Jezair in Omman; the guild is served beside by the shoe-merchants, the pashmak-sellers of women's slippers and the instrument-makers who sell lasts, glue, needles, awls, combs and presses near the old Bezestan {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 405-407, pp. 210-211}.

## Today

The iskafi's trade is still living, though transformed by the factory: bespoke shoemakers and repairers still work the suqs, while most footwear is made in workshops far from the Coral market {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 403, pp. 209-210}.
