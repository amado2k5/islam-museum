## Summary

The warraq was the book trade of the Islamic city in one person: copyist, paper-dealer, binder and bookseller together {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 32}. His shop was the rendezvous of the cultured classes, and booksellers and paper-makers had their own sections in the bazaars like the other trades {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 32}. In Evliya's Istanbul procession the booksellers (Sahhafan) pass two hundred men with sixty shops adorned with many thousand precious books {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 452, p. 218}.

## One man, many trades

The warraqun were usually paper-makers and dealers, copyists and bookbinders in one, and they also supplied pens and ink {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 32}. The shop was not only a place of commerce: it was the rendezvous of the cultured classes, just as in ancient Rome the literary connoisseurs met in the booths of the librarii {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 32}. Booksellers and paper-makers had their own sections in the bazaars like the other trades {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 32}.

## Speed and price

A copyist could copy a hundred pages in a day and a night {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 32}. Fantastic prices were paid for valuable literary works, especially autograph copies by the author {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 32}. A gold dinar — for comparison, the amount of a wine-bar's rent for a whole year — seems to have been the average price of one of the better books {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 32}.

## The book market's place

The business of the warraqun flourished on the wealth of private collectors and the grandiose magnificence of Muslim princes who encouraged the foundation of great libraries {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 31-32}. In Iraq and al-Andalus special stress was laid on the binding of books, and Malaga above all was a treasure-house of exquisite leather work {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 31-32}.

## The guild in Istanbul

In Evliya's procession the booksellers (Sahhafan) are two hundred men with sixty shops; their patron is Abu Zar al-Ghifari, surnamed the father of gold on account of his riches {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 452, p. 218}. They adorn their shops with many thousand precious books such as the Multaqa, Shurer and Kashshaf {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 452, p. 218}. Beside them march the stationers, two hundred and five men with two hundred shops, dressed in paper jackets, drawers, cloaks and caps, smoothing and glazing paper as they pass {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 453, p. 218}.

## Today

The warraq's compound trade dissolved with print: the copyist's pen was replaced by the press, while the bookseller and stationer lived on as separate shop trades, and the copying hand survives only in the calligrapher's art {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 32, 97}. In Istanbul the guild memory remains in the Sahhaf bazaar that still bears the trade's name {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 452, p. 218}.
