## Summary

The mathematician worked the sciences called the ulûm-i teâlîm — arithmetic (ilm-i aded), geometry (ilm-i hendese), astronomy (ilm-i felek) and music — which the Muslims named the riyâzî ilimler, the sciences that exercise the mind and prepare it for the divine science above them {cite:tdv-matematik}. The work arose in early ninth-century Baghdad, where the translation of Greek mathematics had reached an advanced level and a new society was asking for astronomy, optics, reckoning and measuring instruments {cite:tdv-matematik}. There al-Khwarizmi wrote the book in which algebra first stood forth as an independent branch {cite:tdv-matematik}, and Carra de Vaux credits the Muslims with making algebra an exact science, laying the foundations of analytical geometry, and founding plane and spherical trigonometry {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}.

## The name of the science

The Greek mathemata — "the middle", "learning, teaching" — named the middle part of the theoretical sciences and was rendered into Arabic as teâlîm, plural of ta'lîm, the four disciplines being called the ulûm-i teâlîm {cite:tdv-matematik}. Under the Platonic influence on the Aristotelian classification the mathematical sciences were seen as a preparation for the divine science above them, and so took the name riyâzî ilimler by likeness to riyâze, "exercise": sciences that exercise and prepare the mind; later all of them came to be called simply riyâziyyât, and in the modernising age riyâziyyât became the name covering every science dealing with number and quantity, as modern Arabic still uses it {cite:tdv-matematik}.

## Baghdad and the new algebra

Râşid traces the beginning to early ninth-century Baghdad: the translations served both the great scholars' answers to the day's mathematical questions and the needs of a forming society in astronomy, optics, arithmetic and measuring instruments {cite:tdv-matematik}. Al-Khwarizmi wrote his Kitab al-Mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa'l-muqabala there, in the Bayt al-hikma milieu, between 197 and 215 (813–830) — the first book in which the term jabr appears {cite:tdv-matematik}. He had a clear aim no one before him had stated: to build a theory of equations solvable by their roots, able to solve every problem of reckoning and geometry and the questions met in commerce, inheritance law and land measurement {cite:tdv-matematik}.

## The school that followed

Ibn Turk followed al-Khwarizmi's proof-by-example and carried it further; Thabit ibn Qurra took Euclid's Elements up again, both to set al-Khwarizmi's proofs on firmer geometric ground and to render the second-degree equations into geometric language — and it is Thabit who first in the history of mathematics separated the algebraic and geometric methods cleanly, showing that the ways of Euclid and of al-Khwarizmi reach the same result {cite:tdv-matematik}. Al-Mahani worked the other way, turning problems of the Elements' tenth book and an Archimedean solid into algebraic equations {cite:tdv-matematik}. Abu Kamil's Kitab al-Jabr wa'l-muqabala is a turning point: he added to the science the chapter now called indeterminate or rational diophantine analysis, tied the theory of equations to stricter proofs, applied the rules of reckoning to fractions, and investigated linear systems of many unknowns and equations with irrational coefficients {cite:tdv-matematik}. The applications of the new algebra — polynomials, counting techniques, numerical analysis, the numerical solution of equations, the geometric interpretation of equations — changed the face of mathematics from the ninth century on {cite:tdv-matematik}.

## The long tradition

After the ninth century the science was no longer the Greek one: conic theory, the theory of parallels, projective-geometry problems, Archimedean methods in area and volume, the isoperimetric problems and geometric transformations were the fields of the foremost mathematicians — Thabit ibn Qurra, al-Quhi, Ibn Sahl, Ibn al-Haytham — who both widened their fields and carried the discipline into new lands {cite:tdv-matematik}. In the fifteenth century al-Kashi took the interpolation problems up again in his Zij-i Khaqani and gave the Miftah al-hisab at Samarkand {cite:tdv-matematik}. Looking over the whole, Carra de Vaux judges that the Muslims taught the use of the ciphers — though they did not invent them — and so became the founders of everyday arithmetic, that they made algebra an exact science and developed it considerably, laid the foundations of analytical geometry, and were indisputably the founders of plane and spherical trigonometry {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}.

## Today

The sciences the mathematician served now stand as the modern university disciplines; the word itself kept the name the early classification gave it — riyâziyyât in Arabic, matematik in Turkish — for the whole family of sciences that deal with number and quantity {cite:tdv-matematik}.
