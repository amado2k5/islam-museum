## The craft

The goldsmith fashioned ornaments and objects from precious metals and stones. The Arabic term comes from ṣavğ, "casting in a mould": siyâgha is casting, and savvâgh/sayyâgh/sâigh the caster — the Ottoman zerger, from Persian "one who works gold" {cite:tdv-kuyumculuk}. Turkish kuyumcu/kuyumculuk itself descends from the root kuymak, "to cast metal" {cite:tdv-kuyumculuk}.

## Antiquity

Jewellery-making is traced to pre-dynastic Egypt's Badari culture (c. 5500–4000 BC) with polished malachite beads; the earliest known bow-drills for piercing stones and bellows-blowers (hamlaç) for melting metal appear on Egyptian wall paintings {cite:tdv-kuyumculuk}. The royal graves of Ur and finds at Alacahöyük and Troy show filigree (telkâri) and granulation (güverse — gold beads soldered to gold surfaces) by the third millennium BC, and repeating-motif casting moulds from fifth-century Uşak-Güre {cite:tdv-kuyumculuk}.

## In the early texts

The entry notes the craft's place in scripture and hadith: the Qur'anic image of the smith purifying molten metal, and the hadith discussions of goldsmiths {cite:tdv-kuyumculuk}. Bukhari opened a chapter on them in "Buyûʿ": it teaches that izhir (a Meccan grass used by goldsmiths) was excepted from the ban on cutting the haram's plants, that Medina's goldsmiths were mostly of the Banu Qaynuqa, and that one of them arranged with Ali to bring izhir by camel for his colleagues — read by Nawawi and Ibn Hajar as proof that trade with non-Muslims is lawful {cite:tdv-kuyumculuk}. The case of Arfaja b. As'ad, who had a nose of silver then of gold made, and Companions and Successors who bound lost teeth with gold wire, show how far the craft had already gone {cite:tdv-kuyumculuk}.

## The Ottoman workshop

Palace goldsmithing was collaborative: zergers worked beside naqqash, hakkâk and zernişancı (gold-inlayers on steel barrels and jade) {cite:tdv-kuyumculuk}. The 1526 ehl-i hiref wage register counts 58 zergers, 22 zernişancı and 9 hakkâks — mostly Tabrizis and Bosnians, with masters and apprentices from Khorasan, Albania, Russia, Georgia, Skopje, Akkerman, Circassia, Herzegovina and the Greeks {cite:tdv-kuyumculuk}. For the palace they made plumes (sorguç), hotoz crowns, earrings, necklaces, rings and archer's rings (zihgir), bracelets, anklets, pazubents, belts and buckles, chains; Qur'an cases, sword, dagger, mace and musket hilts; boxes, lecterns, vessels, cup sets and mirror sets — even the kozak medallions hung on treaties and letters {cite:tdv-kuyumculuk}.

## Guild and festival

Evliya Çelebi — whose father was the palace kuyumcubaşı — reports about 3,000 kuyumcu shops with some 5,000 workers in Istanbul; they took David as their pir for working with anvil, hammer and fire, though their pir in the Prophet's time was Nasr b. Abdullah {cite:tdv-kuyumculuk}. Selim I and Süleyman learned the craft themselves, and Süleyman built the goldsmiths a workplace with fountain, bath, garden and bench-lined rooms {cite:tdv-kuyumculuk}. The guild held a great gathering every twenty years (kānûn-ı Süleymânî) at the royal meadows of Kâğıthane: 5–6,000 tents, about twenty days of festivity, zergeran çavuşları sent a year ahead to summon all the empire's goldsmiths, and new masters took their icâzet with the peştamal binding and the kiss of the pir's hand {cite:tdv-kuyumculuk}. Evliya lists sister trades — wire-drawers, gem merchants, pearl men (incüciyân), jeweller-goldsmiths, polishers (kalciyân), gem sellers (cevherfürûşân), diamond cutters (elmasbür), kalemkârs, sırmakeş wire pullers, acid workers, hakkâks, pot makers and borax sellers — and the romatçıyân who swept up shop dust yearly and sold it to silver-refiners {cite:tdv-kuyumculuk}.
