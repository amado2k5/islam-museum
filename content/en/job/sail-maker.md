## Summary

The yelkenci sewed the sails. Evliya counts them in the shipbuilders' section of the Istanbul procession — three hundred men in ninety shops, making all kinds of sails, and passing on wagons while they sew {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 101, pp. 130-131}. Kunz, five seas away, finds the same craft ashore at Bahrein: pearling boats with home-woven sailcloth and rigging of twisted date-fibre {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 p. 400}.

## Three hundred men, ninety shops

The sail-makers (Yelkenjian) are three hundred men, with ninety shops. They make all kinds of sails, and their oldest patron is Seth — who it was at the Prophet's time, Evliya honestly says, he does not know. They pass on wagons, sewing sails {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 101, p. 131}.

## The shipbuilders' company

The sail-maker does not march alone. Before him go the rope-makers of the arsenal ditch and the hemp-merchants; beside him the mast- and yard-makers (Serenji), two hundred men who sell in their shops at Galata, Top-khanah and Kassim-pasha masts, yards, ribs, planks and all sorts of wood necessary for the construction of a ship, passing on foot rolling masts, yards and oars; and after him the pitch- and tar-makers and the pump-makers {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 99-104, pp. 130-131}. The guild cluster tells the division of labour: one guild spins the cordage, another fells and shapes the spars, and the yelkenci joins cloth to both.

## Homespun sails of Bahrein

At the other end of the Ottoman seas Kunz describes the pearling fleet of Bahrein: boats of one to fifty tons, excellently built by native workmen using local materials, with home-woven sailcloth and rigging of twisted date-fibre {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 p. 400}. The boats are very picturesque with their artistic rigs and spoon-shaped sails, and the larger ones carry a figurehead covered with the skin of the sheep or goat sacrificed at the launching {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 p. 400}. Here the sail-maker's craft reaches back into the homestead: the sailcloth itself is woven at home before it is ever cut to the yard.

## A craft between rope and wood

The sources show the sail-maker's place but not his bench: no account survives in these texts of the loom-width of sailcloth, the seam and bolt-rope work, the tanning or dressing of cotton sails, or what a master charged a ship's nakhoda for a new main-sail {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 101, p. 131; kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 p. 400}.
