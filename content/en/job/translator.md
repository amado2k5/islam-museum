## Summary
The translator carries speech from one language into another. The entry uses tercüman chiefly for the person who interprets by speech, and mütercim for the person who translates books. The word is Arabic. Turkish says tercüman, and modern Turkish has a newer word for the same work. States used it in politics, trade, law and tax collection, and in the translation of books. In the Ottoman divan it became a post with a chief and an office. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.489-492}

## The word
The entry says the forms türcüman and terceman come from the Arabic root trjm, and name the person who carries a phrase from one language into another. It says tercüman came to be used chiefly for interpreting by speech, and mütercim for written translation. Turkish says tercüman. Modern Turkish uses the newer word çevirmen. The word is known in other Semitic languages: Aramaic targmane and targem, Hebrew targum, and Assyrian targumanu. Greek has dragoumanus and Latin dragumanus, and other languages have similar words. Some Turkish dialects used dilmaç. The entry points for these origins to Okiç's article and to the İslâm Ansiklopedisi, which were not read. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.489-490}

The entry says that restating a man's words more clearly for his hearers, even in his own language, was also called translation. In that sense [[person/abdullah-ibn-abbas|Abd Allah ibn Abbas]] was called the interpreter of the Qur'an. It points to Ibn Sa'd's Tabaqat, volume 2, page 366, which was not read. That title is explanation in the same language. It is not the work of carrying speech between languages, so this entry does not give him the translator's occupation. In books arranged by chapter, such as Sahih al-Bukhari, a chapter heading was called a tarjama because it summarizes the meanings of the reports under it. Tercüman al-zaman is a phrase of praise. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.489-490}

## Before the Muslim chanceries
The entry says the Qur'an counts the variety of languages and colours among the signs of power, and it points to [[passage/quran-30-22|al-Rum 22]]. The verse is not typed here. It says God made his message known to his messengers without a translator, spoke to Moses without one, and will speak to people on the Day of Judgment without one. It points to Bukhari, Zakat 9, Riqaq 49, and Tawhid 24 and 36. Those places were not read, and the report is not quoted. It then says that understanding between peoples needed either the learning of a language or a translator, in politics, trade, law and tax collection. Courts customarily kept people called scribes who knew languages. In the story of Esther it points to chapter 3, verse 12, where scribes at the Persian court are said to have known different languages. The text is not copied. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.489-490}

From Rothstein, the entry says that a salaried official at the Sasanian emperor's court handled Arab affairs and also interpreted, and that the ruler of al-Hira paid him. It points to page 130 of that book, which was not read. It says Muhammad Hamidullah thinks this official may have been an envoy, and it points to volume 1, page 389, of his book, which was not read. In the Prophet's time the Sasanian and Byzantine courts had people who knew Arabic. When the Prophet's letter reached the kisra, he had a translator read it, then stopped the translator because the opening address did not match what he expected for his own rank, and tore the letter up. The entry says the Prophet then prayed that the kingdom of Persia be torn as the letter had been torn. It points to page 390 of the same book, which was not read. When Hatib ibn Abi Balta'a brought the Prophet's letter to the Muqawqis, the Muqawqis asked about the Prophet through his translator. The entry points to al-Suyuti's Husn al-Muhadara, volume 1, page 47, which was not read. After the Prophet's envoy had delivered the letter to Heraclius, Heraclius wanted to question a Quraysh delegation then in Jerusalem, Abu Sufyan among them, and he spoke to them through a translator. It points to Bukhari, the beginning of revelation 7 and Tawhid 51, and to al-Tabari's History, volume 2, page 647, which were not read. Al-Baqillani, whom Adud al-Dawla sent to the Byzantines, spoke to the emperor through the palace translator. It points to Qadi Iyad's Tartib al-Madarik, volume 3, pages 595–596, which was not read. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.489-490}

## In the prophetic period and after
The entry says the preaching, and then the conquests, needed a translator if the people of the newly taken lands were to be understood. It is reported that [[person/zayd-ibn-thabit|Zayd ibn Thabit]] wrote the Prophet's letters to rulers and translated what the delegations said. It is reported that he knew Persian, Greek, Coptic and Ethiopic: Persian from the kisra's envoy, Greek from the Prophet's chamberlain, and Ethiopic and Coptic from the Prophet's servants. It is also reported that he learned these languages in Medina from people who spoke them. The entry points to al-Iqd al-Farid, volume 4, page 244, and to al-Mas'udi's al-Tanbih, volume 4, page 161, which were not read. The Prophet asked Zayd to learn Hebrew or Syriac so that he could translate letters that arrived in the Jews' language and that the Prophet did not want others to read. Zayd learned, in a short time, a language of which he already knew a part. The entry points to Ibn Sa'd, volume 2, page 358 onward, which was not read. The Prophet did not take a scribe or a translator from the Jews of Medina. He asked Zayd to learn their language. It points to al-Kattani's al-Taratib al-Idariyya, volume 2, page 278 onward, which was not read. It is reported that Zayd wrote what was sent to the Jews and read what came back from them. The entry says other companions learned Persian, Greek, Ethiopic and further languages, and it does not name them. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.489-490}

The courts needed a translator. Al-Bukhari made a chapter of the judge's use of translation, and of whether one translator is enough. The entry says some scholars held that one is not enough, and that a sound judgment needs two. Abu Hanifa says one is enough. Al-Shafi'i, Ahmad ibn Hanbal, Malik, and the Muhammad whom the entry names with Malik, say two are required, as in testimony. It points to Bukhari, Ahkam 40, which was not read. Nu'man ibn Muqarrin met the kisra's amil in Persia and understood him through a translator. It points to Bukhari, Jizya 1, which was not read. The museum has no person record for Nu'man ibn Muqarrin. It is reported that Umar appointed a translator in the army together with the physician, the qadi and the katib. It points to al-Tabari, volume 2, page 386, which was not read. That is a report of an appointment. It does not say that Umar himself was a translator. The entry says men of state preferred to speak through a translator even when they knew the language, so as not to make a diplomatic mistake. Al-Qalqashandi counts knowledge of a foreign language among the things a katib must know. It points to Subh al-A'sha, volume 1, page 202, which was not read. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.489-490}

## In the chanceries
The Umayyad and Abbasid courts had translators of different languages. At al-Ma'mun's court there were people who knew Greek, Coptic, Nabataean and other languages, and they were called al-Tarjuman. One of them was Abu Umayr Adi ibn Abd al-Baqi al-Tarjuman, who interpreted for envoys Constantine VII sent to al-Muqtadir in 305 AH, which the entry gives as 917, for an exchange of prisoners and for peace. It points to Rashid ibn al-Zubayr's al-Dhakha'ir wa-l-Tuhaf, page 131, which was not read. The envoys were stopped a hundred cubits from the throne, which the entry reckons at about fifty metres. The vizier Ibn al-Furat al-Aquli stood before the caliph, and the translator stood a little behind the vizier. The vizier addressed the translator, and the translator addressed both sides. It points to Ibn Kathir's al-Bidaya wa-l-Nihaya, volume 11, pages 136–137, which was not read. The museum has no person record for Adi ibn Abd al-Baqi. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.489-490}

Under the Seljuks of Anatolia, chancery secretaries and translators stood in the divan beside the sultan or the vizier. The translator also wrote what the divan sent to foreign rulers. In the time of Ala al-Din Kayqubad the divan had four chancery secretaries and two translators. Kayqubad heard, through a translator, the complaints of merchants who came from overseas. Translators were also employed as envoys. Documents survive for the appointment of men described as the tongue of kings and sultans. The entry points to Uzunçarşılı, pages 88 and 89, and to Osman Turan's documents, pages 18–20, which were not read. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.489-490}

## The translation of books
The work was not only diplomatic. Some interpreters also joined in translating books. From the last years of the Umayyads into the first Abbasid period, around the House of Wisdom, many people, a large part of them not Muslims, translated Greek, Syriac, Persian and Sanskrit works into Arabic. Ibn al-Nadim, who saw the House of Wisdom and used its library, counts forty-seven who translated from Greek into Syriac and then into Arabic, or from Greek straight into Arabic, sixteen who translated from Persian, and three from Sanskrit. [[person/ibn-wahshiyya|Ibn Wahshiyya]] translated many books from Nabataean into Arabic. The entry points to the Fihrist, pages 304–305 and 372, which were not read. Abd al-Rahman III asked Constantine VII for a translator for Dioscorides' book of medicine, which the emperor had sent as a gift in Greek. The emperor sent the monk Nicholas to Cordoba. It points to Ibn Abi Usaybi'a's Uyun al-Anba, pages 493–494, which was not read. The museum has no person record for Nicholas. Abd al-Jabbar al-Nu'man interpreted between Timur and Ibn Khaldun in 808 AH, which the entry gives as 1405, and later translated some of Ibn Khaldun's books into Mongolian. The museum has no person record for Abd al-Jabbar al-Nu'man. Al-Jahiz speaks of the difficulty of translating poetry and a philosophical opinion, and of the translator's need to know the fine points of his own language and of the language he translates into. It points to Kitab al-Hayawan, volume 1, pages 75 and 76, which were not read. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.489-490}

## Under the Ottomans
The second part says there is no certain report of when the Ottoman state first used a translator as an official. It is suggested that translators were employed from the years of the state's beginning, in talks with the states whose lands it bordered. A translator may have been present when foreign envoys were addressed, and on missions sent to neighbours. It is said that the official post appeared at the end of the fourteenth century. Another view places official translation in the reign of Mehmed II, when Greek interpreters who knew Turkish were used more often. The density of relations in his reign must have raised the interpreter's standing. Delegations included people who knew the other side's language, and foreign delegations included people who knew Turkish. The entry does not choose between the two views, and no first year is written from them. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.490-492}

The entry lists earlier contacts as worth notice. The Seljuks of Anatolia sent a man named Kyr Alexius to arrange their contacts with the rulers of Cyprus. It does not call him a translator. The original of Murad I's treaty with the Genoese republic in 1386 was in Turkish, and Bartolomeo Langusco translated it into Latin. On 26 October 1389 Hasan Bey, called Casam Bey in the original text, an envoy of Bayezid I, made an agreement at Pera, and earlier agreements from the time of Orhan and Murad were confirmed. A Genoese notary's document of 16 July 1414 says that Cristoforo Picenino, the Turkish interpreter of the people of Chios, translated a Turkish order of Bayezid into Latin. Soon after the conquest a Genoese delegation handed the key of Galata to Mehmed II, and the delegation included a Turkish-speaking interpreter named Nicolo Pagliuzzi. He had taken that post on 5 June 1449 from his predecessor S. Parrisola. The museum has no person record for these men. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.490-492}

After the conquest of Constantinople the sultans began to give diplomatic work to Orthodox subjects who knew languages well. Mehmed II, seeking news of Europe, made use of foreigners. The entry has no record of how they were paid. On 6 July 1454 the rulers of Venice received Ahmed, the chief of the salaried cavalry, and a scribe named Demetrios Grisovergi. The entry does not call them translators. In 1463, when the Ottomans learned that Uzun Hasan was entering Anatolia and would come to terms with Venice, they sent a translator named Nicolo Corner to the Duchy of Milan with an envoy, seeking cooperation. In 1465 an Ottoman envoy who went to Venice on a secret mission had a translator with him in the peace talks. After the war of 1463–1479 a man of standing named Giovanni Dario emerged in Venice and kept close contact with the Turkish envoys. Lutfi presented the text of the 1479 treaty to the Venetian head of state, was received with great ceremony, the people with him were well treated, and he was afterwards called the envoy of the great Turk. The entry does not call him a translator in that sentence. When an envoy named Sinan Bey came to propose an alliance for the Otranto campaign, a translator who was with Dario, whom the entry calls a turcimanno, took part in the talks. At the end of May 1481 a translator named Emanuele brought Venice the news of Mehmed II's death. The entry says that from this the Turkish word, after a change of sense, spread in the West as turcimanno, dragomanno, drugment, drogman, truchement and dragoman, and that the Ottoman bureaux used dragoman. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.490-492}

When Cem Sultan was held in Europe, Muslims who knew foreign languages grew more numerous and were sent abroad. A man named Ali carried to Venice the text of the capitulation that took effect in 1503, and the papers written to settle the questions that arose. While he was to receive the final text he toured the city, and alongside the duty the entry describes as that of a subashi he also took on translation. The gift registers of 909 AH, which the entry gives as 1503, and of 910 AH, which it gives as 1504, name three translators: Ali, also called Alaeddin Ali, Iskender and Ibrahim. Their official title is given as dragoman. This Ali is certainly the man who went to Venice. The Venetian bailo in Constantinople, P. Bragadin, reports that Dragoman Ali died in a plague in 1525. His successor was Yunus Bey. Ali Bey knew Greek, Italian and Latin well, and had some knowledge of other Western languages. Yunus Bey's service ran from 1519 to 1542. In it he learned European politics and wrote a book setting out the Ottomans' political and military strength. The entry thinks the divan's staff of translators probably widened then. The registers name Hasan Bey, Mustafa Bey, Mehmed Bey, Tercüman Ahmed, who is Heinz Tulman, and Murad Bey, a Hungarian who had become a Muslim. The museum has no person record that matches these men. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.490-492}

When Rhodes was taken in 1522 the Turkish delegation that conducted the surrender talks included a translator. The entry does not describe his work. It finds no record of a chief translator in those years, though some names stand out. It is claimed that people of Jewish origin were appointed to this post in the middle of the sixteenth century, and the name of Salomon Ashkenazi is put forward. The entry then says the claim is clearly not right. Ashkenazi was effective, with a part also played by Sokollu Mehmed Pasha, in repairing relations after Lepanto, but he was not officially a translator. After Yunus Bey the name that occurs in political relations is Ibrahim, a man of Polish origin, Joachim Strasz. He carried documents entrusted to him in 1555 and 1567. He became known by his visits to Venice, by Muslim captives he freed, by his correspondence with Michele Membré, a distinguished dragoman of the Venetian republic, and by a Turkish letter in Latin letters, and he was known as a man educated at the University of Padua. In the sixteenth century Kubad Çavuş was effective in this post because of the language he knew, and he carried to the Venetian Senate the document that demanded the surrender of Cyprus without a war. In 1570 Mahmud Bey, a translator of the imperial divan, was stopped on his way to France, passing through Venice, and was imprisoned for a time in Verona. He had carried out serious diplomatic work with the Habsburgs, and he had written a history of Hungary. In this period the names of two translators, Hürrem and Mustafa Bey, occur. The last convert appointed chief translator was Zülfikar, and he served until 1657. The museum has no person record that matches these men. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.490-492}

## The chief translator and the translation office
The entry says the Ottoman translators had their brightest years in the sixteenth century. A great change in the Mediterranean basin at the end of that century then brought their European colleagues forward. The chief reason is that in 1551 the Venetian republic set up a body and a school to teach Eastern languages and to train people for the profession. These young men were called giovani della lingua, and France soon imitated Venice. Venice gave weight to practical teaching, and people called hoca worked there. A large part of the young men who were trained later kept the Turkish documents of the Ottoman period in order and translated them into their own languages. In later centuries they rearranged the school as need and money required. The French trained a class and dragomans of their own. The other states of Europe set up centres that taught their own men, and for this they drew mostly on people of Venetian or French origin. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.490-492}

In the seventeenth century it is established that four translators served in the imperial divan. From the middle of the century the state began to use Greek subjects who knew languages. In 1657 Fazil Ahmed Pasha Köprülüzade appointed Panayoti Nicoussios, a Greek of Chios who had studied medicine in Padua, with the title of chief translator. At the Treaty of Karlowitz in 1699 Alexander Mavrocordato, known as Iskerletzade, was in the company of the representatives Zülfikar and Mehmed Pasha. The entry does not say in that sentence that he was chief translator. It then says that for about a century people of the Orthodox confession in Constantinople were appointed to this post almost as a privilege. They preferred to live in the Fener quarter and so were called Phanariots. This class was steadily criticised in Ottoman society, and also by the embassy staff who met them in Istanbul. The chief translator had with him eight young men who knew languages, called sons of the tongue, and twelve servants. They were Greek, and they were exempt from the jizya. His work was to translate what was said when the grand vizier met the representatives of foreign states, so his equipment was that of a foreign office. He also translated papers that came in, and he submitted to the grand vizier a report of what he had said to the envoys. From the second half of the eighteenth century French, English and Russian influence began to tell on them, and translators were always under suspicion. The suspicion grew in the reigns of Selim III and Mahmud II, and the Greek revolt of 1821 brought the Phanariot translators to an end. Appointment returned, as before, to people of Turkish origin. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.490-492}

Yahya Naci Efendi, known as Bulgarizade, a teacher at the engineering school, became chief translator. One or two divan clerks were attached to him, and the bureau was called the translation office. When this arrangement did not give the result that was wanted, it was decided to appoint a Greek temporarily to the chief post. Stavraki Aristarchi was appointed acting chief translator, and Yahya Efendi was to supervise the work with the clerks. That too did not succeed, and Yahya Efendi was appointed translator, so the post began to be given to Turkish and Muslim officials. After his death the post he had held as deputy was given as a substantive appointment to [[person/hoca-ishak-efendi|Başhoca İshak Efendi]]. The entry gives no year for that appointment. After the Tanzimat the translation office was given a new life. It had become clear that permanent embassies in Europe could no longer be put off, and foreign languages were taught in the office. It did not become clear whether the office was training a civil servant or a diplomat. As the twentieth century began, the founding of modern schools, the civil-service school, and the training of professional men gave foreign policy a new direction. The museum has no person record for Yahya Naci, for Stavraki, for Panayoti or for Mavrocordato. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.490-492}

## Outside the capital
Translators are known to have served in provincial divans as well as at the centre. In the Arab provinces a divan translator was called the Arab translator. In the seventeenth century translators who knew Hungarian served in Buda and Timisoara. Translators were used in the courts, and some institutions employed them. At the end of the eighteenth century a translator was attached to each foreign expert brought in to work in the new military institutions. In the sixteenth century the foreign ambassadors in Constantinople are noticed to have used Turkish and Muslim translators. At the end of the eighteenth century the consuls of various Western states in Ottoman lands employed translators. The entry says their number reached 194, with twenty-four further translators who held a berat. The chief translators in the embassies were Ottoman subjects. It points to the İslâm Ansiklopedisi, volume 12, part 1, pages 180–181, which was not read. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.490-492}

## Not yet covered
The bibliographies of both parts were not read, nor the books named in the notes. The entry gives no year for a first translator, so no first year is written. The two views of when the official Ottoman post began are left as the entry gives them. The museum has no person record for Abu Umayr Adi ibn Abd al-Baqi, for Nicholas, for Abd al-Jabbar al-Nu'man, for Yunus Bey, for Dragoman Ali, for Ibrahim Strasz, for Kubad Çavuş, for Zülfikar, for Panayoti Nicoussios, for Mavrocordato or for Yahya Naci. The museum's record of Sallam the interpreter is a different man, and this entry does not name him, so the occupation is not written from it. Ibn Abbas's title, interpreter of the Qur'an, is another sense, and it is left without this occupation. {cite:tdv-tercuman p.489-492}
