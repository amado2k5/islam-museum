## Summary

The instrument-maker cut and engraved brass into astrolabes, safiha plates and armillary spheres {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 pp. 114-115, 395-396}. Carra de Vaux names al-Fazari as the first Muslim to construct an astrolabe, and al-Zarqali of Toledo as the famous instrument-maker whose safiha treatise generated a whole literature {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 pp. 380, 394-395}. Christie lists the dated astrolabes that survive, from the Isfahan instrument of 984 to the Persian master's of 1715 {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 115}.

## The first makers

The documented history of Arab learning begins with the Abbasids: al-Fazari was the first Muslim to construct an astrolabe, wrote on the use of the armillary sphere and prepared tables according to the years of the Arabs {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 380}. Masha'allah (d. 815) wrote on astrology, on the astrolabe and on meteorology {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 pp. 380-381}.

## The astrolabe and its uses

The astrolabe, an astronomical instrument of ancient Greek invention improved by Ptolemy and perfected by the Muslims, came to Europe some time in the tenth century {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 114}. Its principal use in the East was to determine the hour of prayer and the position of Mecca; to mariners it was invaluable, and its use for nautical observations continued in the West until the seventeenth century {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 pp. 114-115}. A fine astrolabe is a beautiful work of art, made and engraved with amazing care and skill in a form that persisted for centuries without material change {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 115}.

## The dated instruments

The earliest dated astrolabe known is at Oxford: made in 984, it was the joint work of two masters, Ahmad and Mahmud, sons of Ibrahim the astrolabist, of Isfahan {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 115}. An astrolabe made under the superintendence of Ibrahim ibn Sa'id at Toledo in 1066-7 may be compared with another, similar in shape but covered with delicate ornament, the work of the celebrated Persian master 'Abd al-Hamid in 1715 {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 115}.

## Al-Zarqali and the safiha

In Spain Arab astronomy flourished in the eleventh and twelfth centuries: al-Zarqali (Arzachel), who lived c. 1029-1087, is famous as an instrument-maker {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 394}. He invented an astrolabe, a safiha, on which he wrote a treatise out of which a whole literature developed: a Jew of Montpellier translated it into Latin, King Alfonso of Castile made two translations of it into Romance, and Regiomontanus in the fifteenth century published a collection of problems on the 'noble instrument of the safiha' {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 pp. 394-395}. Copernicus quotes Arzachel along with Albategnius {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 395}.

## The observatory bench

The Arab astronomers devoted great attention to the perfecting of instruments; the most important was the armillary sphere, which represented the celestial sphere in three rings corresponding to the meridian, the ecliptic and the colure of the solstices, plus two rings of observation {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 pp. 395-396}. At Maragha, where Nasir al-Din al-Tusi made observations in an observatory founded by the munificence of the Mongol khans and drew up the 'Ilkhanid Tables', the instruments were much admired {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 395}.
