## Summary

The qadi al-qudat — 'qadi of the qadis' — stood at the head of the judiciary in the Islamic states; the title also ran as qadi al-khalifa, qadi al-mamalik, qadi al-afaq and 'the great qadi' {cite:tdv-kadilkudat}. The office began as an honourable name for the capital's judge and grew into the supreme administrator of justice, appointing the provinces' qadis as his own deputies {cite:tdv-kadilkudat}.

## Abu Yusuf in Baghdad

The first qadi al-qudat was Abu Yusuf, appointed by Harun al-Rashid in Abbasid Baghdad; the sources suggest his writ did not yet cover every province — most judges were still named by the caliph — but through his pupils and his standing with the caliph he effectively chose many {cite:tdv-kadilkudat}. The article weighs the claim that the office copied the Sasanian 'mobed-e mobedan' and finds the resemblance imperfect: the Persian chief priest supervised religion as well as judges, while the qadi al-qudat was never head of the ulama {cite:tdv-kadilkudat}.

## Growing powers

Under the early Abbasids the chief's authority rose by degrees: in 184/800 the qadi al-qudat Abu'l-Bakhtari dismissed the qadi of Egypt on the caliph's instruction, and by the later ninth century appointing provincial judges had become part of his writ, carried out either in person or through na'ibs {cite:tdv-kadilkudat}. The jurists debated the title's propriety — 'qadi of qadis' could sound like a divine claim — but accepted it in the sense of 'the most learned judge of his time' {cite:tdv-kadilkudat}.

## East and West

The Fatimids built the office in Egypt on the same name; their first holders, the Nu'man family — al-Qadi al-Nu'man and his son — ran the judicature and da'wa together {cite:tdv-kadilkudat}. In al-Andalus and the Maghreb the equivalent post was older and differently named: the qadi al-jama'a, chief judge of the capital, held in all the western Islamic states {cite:tdv-kadilkudat}.

## The Mamluk four

Under the Mamluks each of the four schools had its own qadi al-qudat, the Shafi'i ranking first; in 678/1280 the Shafi'i chief received the right to appoint every qadi in Egypt {cite:tdv-kadilkudat}. His installation was a public rite — the deed of appointment read from the minbar of the capital's mosque while he stood listening — and the office ran in great judicial families like Ibn Jama'a, al-Subki and Ibn Daqiq al-Id {cite:tdv-kadilkudat}.

## Meeting and succession

The Mamluk chief judged at his house, then in a fixed madrasa under the Bahri sultans; in the Ottoman system the office had no exact match — the Rumeli and Anadolu kazaskers headed the ilmiye instead — while the western lands kept their own forms {cite:tdv-kadilkudat}. The institution is best read as the top of a career ladder: the qadi al-qudat was a qadi who had risen to administer all the qadis {cite:tdv-kadilkudat}.

The entry follows the office's constitutional growth, less its daily work — the petitioners at his majlis, the registers he countersigned, the tension between his patronage and a sultan's pleasure — for which the biographies of the holders are the richest source the article itself names {cite:tdv-kadilkudat}.

## In more detail
The office begins with Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ibrahim (d. 182 AH), appointed the first Qadi al-Qudat by Harun al-Rashid in 170 AH — before him every city's qadi answered independently to caliph or governor. From Baghdad the new office appointed, audited and dismissed provincial qadis, which is what institutionalized the Hanafi school as the Abbasid judiciary. Under fragmentation, rival chiefs emerged — Cairo's Fatimid Qadi al-Qudat, Cordoba's Maliki Qadi al-Jama'a — until Baybars in 663 AH appointed four simultaneous chiefs in Cairo, one per Sunni madhhab, so each litigant could sue in his own school (al-Maqrizi, al-Suluk 1/539).
