## Summary

The bazzaz is the cloth merchant of the market and the bezestan: he buys the bolts of linen, cotton, silk and wool, measures by the cubit and sells the lengths {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 471-485, pp. 222-223}. Because cloth was the great portable wealth of the city, the hisba manuals put the whole law of sale at his counter, and both al-Shayzari and Ibn al-Ukhuwwa open their cloth chapters with the warning that a merchant who does not know the rules will fall into usury whether he means to or not {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 21, pp. 80-81} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVIII, pp. 40-41}.

## Knowing the law of sale

Al-Shayzari begins with 'Umar b. al-Khattab's saying that no one should trade in our market except he who knows the laws of his religion, and adds that in his own day he had seen most of the cloth merchants in the markets acting unlawfully in their transactions {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 21, p. 80}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa gives the same first rule to the drapers: they must know the laws of barter, otherwise they may be guilty of exacting riba {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVIII, p. 40}.

## The forbidden tricks

The manuals then list the market frauds one by one: the najash, collusion, when a man offers a high price for a commodity he does not want only to mislead others; sale against a brother's sale, offering a better price to a buyer who has already contracted with another; offering a commodity for sale against a brother's offer; and selling for payment at an unspecified time, as when a piece of stuff is sold to be paid for when the pilgrims come from Mecca {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 21, pp. 80-81} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVIII, pp. 40-41}. It is forbidden to buy an article and resell it before receiving it, to offer a garment on condition that the purchaser sells his own, and to sell by mutual touch, by throwing the garments at one another, or by the fall of a pebble {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 21, pp. 81-82} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVIII, p. 41}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa adds that a townsman may not sell on behalf of a man from outside, for people must be allowed to provision themselves from each other without an intermediary {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVIII, p. 41}.

## Truth in the price

In the resale at a stated profit the merchant must be truthful about the original price: al-Shayzari's example is the man who buys a piece of cloth for ten dirhams, has it bleached for one dirham, embroidered for one and repaired for one, and may then say it cost him thirteen but not that he bought it for thirteen; and if he did the work himself he must say he bought it for ten and worked on it to the value of three {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 21, p. 82}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa names the drapers' trick played with the money-changers: lending a man a dinar and selling him a cloak for two, so that three deferred dinars are owing, which is a disguised usury {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXVI, p. 46}.

## Scales, cubits and the criers

The muhtasib must examine the cloth merchants' scales and their cubits and prevent them from forming partnerships with the bazaar criers and brokers, and he must make sure they deal fairly with the customers and the importers of merchandise {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 21, pp. 82-83}.

## The bezestan of Istanbul

Evliya passes the guilds of the old bezestan one after another: the criers of the interior, a hundred men with badges of service who never leave it, and the criers of the exterior three hundred; the cloth-merchants (Chokajian) a hundred and seven men with a hundred shops, who pass with wagons loaded with cloth from London, Florence, Ancona and Marseilles, measuring it as they go {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 469-471, p. 222}. With them march the atlas, rich-stall, velvet, cushion, watered-silk and caftan merchants — the caftan merchants a hundred and five men with fifty shops who sew the robes of honour distributed at the imperial audiences — then the makers and merchants of cotton stuffs, mosquito-nets and linen {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 472-482, pp. 222-223}. The Bezzazan linen-merchants are a thousand men with seven hundred shops, passing with linen of different colours such as Ahmedabadi and Mahmudi, and after them the carpet, abba, felt, ihram, shalloon and frippery merchants, and the sheikhs and nakibs of the old bezestan with the eightfold music {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 485-495, pp. 223-224}.

## Today

The cloth merchant's shop survives wherever fabric is sold by the length, and the muhtasib's questions — an honest price, a true measure, no feigned bidding — are the same ones market law still asks of him {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 21, pp. 80-83} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVIII, pp. 40-42}.
