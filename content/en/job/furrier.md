## Summary

The farra' made furs and fur clothing — tanning, stitching and selling them in the furriers' suq {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXV, p. 93}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa gives the trade a short chapter of rules against old-for-new and house-to-house selling {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXV, p. 93}, while Evliya Çelebi counts the Istanbul fur-merchants at a thousand men in five hundred shops and gives them one of the loudest displays in the whole procession {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 373, pp. 204-205}.

## Honest fur

No furs of sheepskins or any other kind may be sold unless they are well tanned and properly stitched and seamed, Ibn al-Ukhuwwa writes {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXV, p. 93}. No old goods must be mixed with new, and no patched articles may be sold {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXV, p. 93}. The goods must not be carried amongst the houses for sale, which would give certain persons an advantage; they must be taken to the furriers' suq and sold publicly, so that the strong and the weak may acquire them {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXV, p. 93}.

## The guild of the kürkçü

In the procession before the sultan, Evliya counts the fur-merchants (Kurkjian) at a thousand men with five hundred shops {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 373, p. 204}. Their patron is Idris, the same as that of the tailors, because they are also busy with the needle {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 373, p. 204}. The first inventor of the fur-dress, Evliya reports, was Husheng, who wore the hides of beasts killed in hunting so that he might roam through the woods in animals' guise {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 373, p. 204}. In the Prophet's time the chief of the fur-makers was Amri Ben A'mri, who was girded by Selman Pak {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 373, p. 204}.

## Fur against leather

There was a wonderful contest between the fur-makers and the tanners about the precedency of rank, Evliya writes; at length the emperor decided in favour of the fur-makers, because all the first men of the state dress in fur {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 373, p. 204}. The furriers adorned their shops with furs of all kinds, of the value of many hundred thousand piastres {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 373, p. 204}.

## The wild men of the procession

The Greek fur-makers of the market-place of Mahmud Pasha formed a separate procession, with caps of bear-skin and breeches of fur {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 373, p. 204}. Some were dressed from head to foot in lions', leopards' and wolves' skins, with kalpaks of sable on their heads; some were dressed in skins as wild men and savages, each tied with six or sevenfold strong chains and led by six or seven people, assailing their keepers and spreading a noise and confusion beyond description {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 373, pp. 204-205}. Others, clad in lion-, leopard- and bear-skins, represented those animals walking on all-fours and dragged with chains, beaten by their guards each time they grew mutinous, while others assailed them with dogs and hounds in the show of a hunting party with halberds in their hands {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 373, p. 205}.

## Sable from Russia

The sable-merchants (Samurjian) were a thousand men, all Greeks from the towns of Servia, Fiorina, Lachasta and Golikesri, rich merchants who brought fur from Russia every year and fitted out their shops with all kinds of fur and with fish-bones {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 374, p. 205}. The sable kalpak-makers (Samur Kalpakjian) were a hundred and five men with eighty shops; the sable kalpak is the distinctive head-dress of the family of the Tatar princes of the Jingiz family {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 375, p. 205}.
