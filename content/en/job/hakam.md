## Summary

The hakam (also muhakkam) is the arbiter whom the parties to a dispute appoint by their own agreement, taking him from outside the official bench {cite:tdv-hakem}. The practice is older than Islam: in a Hijaz with no central authority, disputes between persons and tribes ended not in courts but before arbiters chosen for foresight, probity and standing {cite:tdv-hakem}.

## The tribal hakams

Every tribe kept its famous hakams — Aktham ibn Sayfi, Hajib ibn Zurara, Aqra' ibn Habis of Tamim; Amir ibn Zarib of Qays; Abd al-Muttalib, Abu Talib, Abu Sufyan and the future caliph Umar of Quraysh — and the sources also name women hakams like Suhn bint Luqman and Hind bint Hus {cite:tdv-hakem}. The Tamim arbiters held an accepted judging authority over all comers at the Ukaz fair for a long period {cite:tdv-hakem}.

## The Black Stone

The most famous arbitration in the tradition is the Prophet's own: at thirty-five, before his mission, he settled the clans' quarrel over who should set the Black Stone back in the rebuilt Ka'ba — he laid it on a cloth and had every clan lift the cloth together, then set it himself {cite:tdv-hakem}.

## Hakam and qadi

The jurists kept the hakam inside the legal order as a complement to the official court, and set out the difference precisely: the hakam has no general public writ, acts only over the dispute referred to him, is chosen by the parties, may be dismissed by them before the ruling, decides only between the parties, and cannot imprison or fine {cite:tdv-hakem} {cite:tdv-tahkim}. A qadi, by contrast, judges for the appointing authority over all within his district, cannot be removed by the litigants, and may enforce his rulings {cite:tdv-tahkim}.

## The fiqh of arbitration

Because he exercises a kind of judging, the jurists required of the hakam what they required of the qadi: full legal capacity, probity and knowledge of fiqh {cite:tdv-hakem}. Arbitration counted as a form of settlement (sulh), so it could cover only what the parties could themselves forgo — sales, surety, maintenance — and not public rights or fixed penalties, though a Hanbali view let arbiters hear whatever the judge could {cite:tdv-tahkim}. His award bound the parties once pronounced, and if the court had confirmed him he sat as its deputy {cite:tdv-tahkim}.
