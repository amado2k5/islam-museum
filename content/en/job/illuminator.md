## The craft

The illuminator — müzehhib, practitioner of tezhip ("gilding") — decorated manuscript pages, calligraphy panels and albums with burnished gold leaf and colour, one of the principal Islamic book arts {cite:tdv-tezhip}.

## Motifs and design

Müzehhibs stylised nature into small, simple units: the hatâyî group (leaf, penç, goncagül, hatâyî), semi-stylised garden flowers (carnation, tulip, rose, cyclamen, violet — the style named after Kara Memi), the münhanî and rûmî scrolls, the Chinese cloud and the çintemani {cite:tdv-tezhip}. Animal motifs — simurg, dragon, qilin, deer, lion — were kept out of religious manuscripts and reserved for miniatures, halkârî and lacquer (ruganî) work {cite:tdv-tezhip}. Designs fall into three classes: the bounded panel (pano), the repeating ulama and the geometric pattern generated from a centre by compass and ruler {cite:tdv-tezhip}.

## Where illumination appears

Tezhip covers every zone of the book: the zahriye (medallion or mekik pages that may carry the owner's temellük inscription — the müzehhib's showpiece, paired pages mirroring each other), the serlevha and unvan pages, chapter and sura-head panels (naming the sura, its place of revelation and verse count), the durak verse-stop rosettes (hamse every five, aşere every ten), ornament between the lines (satır arası), the koltuk triangles under the first long line, the kenar suyu page borders and the marginal "mushaf roses" marking hizb, juz', sajda and other divisions {cite:tdv-tezhip}. In imperial rescripts the lines stayed bare but the tuğra and its surroundings were gilded {cite:tdv-tezhip}.

## Origins and schools

The art arose among the Uyghur Turks of Central Asia, travelled with the Seljuks through Iran to Anatolia, and flourished in distinct styles {cite:tdv-tezhip}. The Timurid period was a peak: artist-rulers founded libraries and nakkaşhânes at Herat, home of the müzehhib-painter Hâce Ali Tebrîzî; a 1485 Herat mushaf in Topkapı carries twenty-eight different sura-head illuminations {cite:tdv-tezhip}. In Seljuk Konya Muhlis b. Abdullah el-Hindî illuminated a Mesnevi in 1278, and the Karamanid Ya‘kūb b. Gāzî el-Konevî signed a 1314 mushaf in gold on its final page — an early müzehhib signature {cite:tdv-tezhip}.

## Ottoman styles

Under Mehmed II came the Baba Nakkaş style of large hatâyî flowers and scattered cloud fragments; then the Saz yolu manner created by Şahkulu, pupil of Âgā Mîrek, in the palace nakkaşhâne; then the Kara Memi style — Kara Memi, sernakkaş under Süleyman, introduced the garden-flower repertoire that bears his name {cite:tdv-tezhip}. In the eighteenth century Ali Üsküdârî founded a ruganî (lacquer) style, and Hezargradî Ahmed Atâ the "pesend" floral manner — Atâ, the palace chief binder under Mahmud II, earned the pen-name "Hattî" for first gilding Yesârîzâde's hilye template; the style continued with Muhsin Demironat into the twentieth century {cite:tdv-tezhip}.

## Standing

The wish to honour the mushaf drove the art: where the earliest copies admitted nothing beyond the revealed text, sura-head illumination and verse, juz' and sajda markers came to be welcomed {cite:tdv-tezhip}. Müzehhibs worked in court nakkaşhânes — Ilkhanid Baghdad, Mamluk Cairo (İbrâhim el-Âmidî's mushaf under al-Malik al-Ashraf Sha'ban is counted a masterpiece), Safavid Shiraz, Tabriz, Qazvin and Isfahan — and many were also painters (musavvir) {cite:tdv-tezhip}.
