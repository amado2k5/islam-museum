## Summary

The sâdin is the keeper of the Ka'ba's door and keys, opening it to visitors at the stated times — the office the sources call hicâbe {cite:tdv-hicabe}. The man who held it was called hâcib or sâdin, and bore the nisba hacebî {cite:tdv-hicabe}. Since Shayba ibn Uthman, the charge has remained in his descendants, the Banu Shayba, down to today {cite:tdv-hicabe}.

## The office

Hicâbe, literally 'veiling' — standing in the way of entry — names the keeping of the Ka'ba: its maintenance, the guarding of its door and keys, and the opening of the door to visitors at set times, together with the care of the Maqam Ibrahim, of precious gifts and of the inner and outer covers {cite:tdv-hicabe}. It is used in the sources alongside sidâne — 'serving the Ka'ba' — and the two are distinguished: sidâne covers all the services of the House, hicâbe those of the door alone {cite:tdv-hicabe}.

## The lineage

Before Islam the custodianship passed, in the reports, from the people of Ismail to the Jurhum and then to the Huza'a, whose chief Hulayl ibn Hubshiyya held it {cite:tdv-hicabe}. Qusayy, who married Hulayl's daughter, succeeded his father-in-law as ruler of Mecca and gathered the honoured services — sikâye, hicâbe, imâre, rifâde — into his own hands; by his will the hicâbe, the Dar al-Nadwa and the standard went to his elder son Abd al-Dar, and the office ran in Abd al-Dar's line father to son: Uthman ibn Abd al-Dar, Abd al-Uzza ibn Uthman, Abu Talha (Abdullah) ibn Abd al-Uzza, Talha ibn Abi Talha, and Uthman ibn Talha {cite:tdv-hicabe} {cite:tdv-kabe §1}.

## The key at the conquest

At the conquest of Mecca the hicâbe was in Uthman ibn Talha's keeping, and the key of the Ka'ba lay with his mother {cite:tdv-hicabe}. After his tawaf the Prophet had Uthman called and the door opened; the House was cleared of the pictures and idols within, and he went in with Bilal and Usama and prayed {cite:tdv-hicabe}. When he came out, Abbas and Ali asked for the hicâbe together with the sikâye, but the Prophet instead called Uthman ibn Talha and his uncle's son Shayba ibn Uthman and delivered the key to the two of them, saying this was a day of goodness and loyalty — the trust was theirs until the Day of Resurrection, and only the unjust would take it from them {cite:tdv-hicabe}. He had hidden the key in his garment as he handed it over, bidding them do likewise; and it is said the key was given them after the revelation of the verse commanding that trusts be rendered to their people {cite:tdv-hicabe}.

## The Banu Shayba

Uthman ibn Talha moved to Medina after the conquest and stayed there until the Prophet's death; in those years Shayba ibn Uthman, his sons and his brother Wahb carried out the office, and Uthman joined them on his return {cite:tdv-hicabe}. On Uthman's death the custodianship passed to Shayba ibn Uthman and his grandsons — the Banu Shayba — and the historians from Ibn Qutayba and al-Baladhuri to Ibn al-Athir, al-Qalqashandi and Eyyub Sabri Pasha each record it held by the Banu Shayba in his own time; the office, the entry says, remains in the Shayba family today {cite:tdv-hicabe}.

## The service beyond the door

The Ka'ba's services began, the sources say, with Ibrahim and Ismail; Umar was the first to assign them a budget, Mu'awiya regularised the officers, and in later ages eunuch aghas were appointed, whose numbers grew under the Ottomans until paid officials and servants took their place {cite:tdv-kabe §1}. The perfuming of the Ka'ba was itself a pre-Islamic service that the Abd al-Dar had performed; Mu'awiya sent incense and burners in the pilgrimage seasons and the months of Rajab, and after the Hijaz entered Ottoman rule a share of the haremeyn allocations was set aside for it every year {cite:tdv-kabe §1}. The washing of the interior — begun, the sources say, on the day of the conquest — became a yearly or twice-yearly rite at which the zemzem-servers bring in the water and the guests, led by the amir, wash and dry the marble {cite:tdv-kabe §1}.

## Not yet covered

The two entries give the office, its lineage and its ritual services; the Banu Shayba's stipends and registers, the day-to-day division of work among the family's keepers, and the earlier sidâne under the Mamluk and Khedivial courts remain for other sources {cite:tdv-hicabe} {cite:tdv-kabe}.
