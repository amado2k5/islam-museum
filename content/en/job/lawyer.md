## Summary

The wakīl — from w-k-l, "to trust, to entrust a matter to a reliable person" — is the one his principal commissions to perform a legal act on his behalf; the act is the müvekkelün-bih, the principal the müvekkil, and the commissioning the tevkīl {cite:tdv-vekalet}. The Hanafis define agency as "entrusting the authority of legal transaction and its protection to the agent", the Shafi'is as "delegating to another, during one's life, a delegable act within one's own competence", the Hanbalis as "appointing a person capable of the transaction as nāib in a delegable matter", and the Malikis framed theirs so as to keep out the appointment of governors, kadıs, imams and executors, which are commands and devotions, not agency {cite:tdv-vekalet}. One of its forms is litigation agency, in which the agent represents his principal before the kadi {cite:tdv-vekalet}.

## The contract's elements and conditions

The Hanafis make the contract rest on the sīgha — the principal's offer and the agent's acceptance — while the majority count the parties and the subject-matter as pillars too {cite:tdv-vekalet}. The offer comes from the principal; acceptance need not be express — the agent's undertaking the work without refusing counts — though the Shafi'is required express acceptance in paid agency {cite:tdv-vekalet}. A principal may appoint an agent only for what he could do himself: the wholly incapable — the young child or the insane — cannot make valid dispositions at all; the discerning minor may appoint agents for what is purely to his benefit without his guardian's leave, but for what may benefit or harm, like sale and hire, agency depends on the guardian's permission {cite:tdv-vekalet}. The mandate must lie within the principal's authority and be known well enough for its purpose: the majority called an unrestricted "for everything" agency an excessive ignorance that blocks the contract; Ibn Abi Layla disagreed, Imam Muhammad held such an agency gave every power of disposal, and Abu Hanifa qualified it {cite:tdv-vekalet}.

## Agency in litigation

The jurists agree that agency is allowed in civil suits; the criminal field is disputed: the majority refused agency for proving hadd offences such as adultery and drinking, since the interest harmed belongs to God and the community rather than the individual, while the Hanbalis allowed it on the strength of the Unays hadith {cite:tdv-vekalet}. In litigation agency a condition tying the fee to winning the case is invalid, since the agent's obligation is one of due care, not of result {cite:tdv-vekalet}.

## The agent's fee and duties

Agency may be paid: the Mecelle (art. 1467) entitles the agent to a fee when it was stipulated at the contract or when he customarily works for one, and where none was fixed he earns the customary rate {cite:tdv-vekalet}. The Malikis treat service-form agency as binding on both parties and fee-by-cuʿāla agency as governed by cuʿāla rules: non-binding until the work begins, then binding on the principal {cite:tdv-vekalet}. The agent must carry out the mandate in the principal's interest — read first from his instructions — and what he does beyond them does not bind the principal; his obligation is personal, so he may not appoint a substitute unless permitted {cite:tdv-vekalet}. He hands over the principal's goods in his hands without a second act, and he may exercise a right of retention over the principal's goods to recover what is owed him {cite:tdv-vekalet}. He recovers necessary expenses incurred in performing the agency, and any loss he suffers in the course of it without fault of his own falls on the principal {cite:tdv-vekalet}.

## The effect of the agent's act

The Shafi'is, Hanbalis and Malikis — taking the marriage agent and the envoy as their model — hold that the effect of the agent's transaction, its rights and its duties pass directly to the principal even when he contracted in his own name, like direct representation {cite:tdv-vekalet}. The Hanafis distinguish: in contracts open to indirect representation, like sale and hire, the agent contracts in his own name and the effects pass to the principal through delivery {cite:tdv-vekalet}. Agents appointed at different times for the same task act independently; those appointed by one contract, or bound to act jointly, may not act alone, and the Hanafis divide further: in weighty transactions — marriage, khulʿ for compensation, manumission for payment, kitaba — they must act together, while in what needs no joint judgement — divorce without compensation, delivery of a gift, return of a deposit, payment of a debt — one suffices {cite:tdv-vekalet}.

## The end of agency

Agency is non-binding: the principal may dismiss at will without cause, but where a third party's right has attached — the pledgee, for instance — dismissal needs that party's consent {cite:tdv-vekalet}. The agent may likewise resign; Abu Hanifa required the principal's presence, the Malikis bar a litigation agent who has attended three hearings from stepping down, and the Shafi'i al-Azra'i bars resignation while the principal's goods would be at risk until his return {cite:tdv-vekalet}. Agency ends with the principal's death by the majority, the Hanafis and Zahiris dissenting — Malik excepting the mufavvad agent who acts not knowing of the death — and with the agent's own death, since his obligation is personal; it also ends with loss of capacity, with the principal's bankruptcy where it concerns the goods under embargo, and with impossibility, as when the goods to be sold perish or the principal deals with them himself {cite:tdv-vekalet}.
