## Summary

The samman — the zayyat of the Egyptian suq — kept the market's fats: butter and clarified butter, oil, cheese, honey, pickles and vinegar {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}. His shop is one of the most-policed in the hisba books, for every vessel in it could hide water, gum or rancid stock {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 20, pp. 77-78; ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVI, pp. 87-88}. In Istanbul the trade stood under a state inspector of its own, the keeper of the butter magazine, whose tithe and tax farm ran to seven million aspers a year {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 162-163}.

## A chapter of their own

Al-Shayzari heads the chapter "Sellers of Clarified Butter", but his translator notes they dealt in far more than butter {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 20, p. 77}. The muhtasib must inspect their measures, ratls and scales, and forbid the first family of frauds: poor goods mixed with good after being bought apart at a lower price, old dates mixed with new, dried grapes with fresh {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 20, p. 77}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa calls them simply the small traders — the retailers of savouries and pickles — and puts the same rule first: each kind sold separately at its own rate {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVI, p. 87}.

## The cheats of the jar

The manuals know the shelf of a grocer's shop by heart. No sprinkling water on dates and dried grapes to make them weigh more; no pouring oil on the dried grapes so their colour shines purer; no wetting dates with sugar-cane juice thinned with hot water {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 20, p. 77}. Oil gone stale could be reconditioned with safflower — but put the oil on a fire and it gives off a choking smoke {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 20, p. 77}. Oil mixed with the oil in which cheese has stood in casks betrays itself over a lamp-flame: it is greasy, and it crackles and spits {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 20, p. 77}. Vinegar thinned with water fails two tests: pure vinegar poured on the ground bubbles and froths, and a sprig of water-moss dropped in soaks up the water and leaves the vinegar — the same moss, dipped in watered milk, separates the two {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 20, pp. 77-78}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa adds the sulphur test for vinegar, and warm bread, a burning wick and lemon-juice for adulterated oil {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVI, p. 87}.

## Cheese, pickles and honey

The small trader's stock went beyond jars. The cheese-friers are watched: only fresh sesame oil may be used, and toasted cheese must be drained of all hot water — the water used to purify it must be hot, says the manual, or it may cause leprosy {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVI, p. 87}. Pickled foods are inspected; anything dry and unripened goes back to the vinegar, anything deteriorated, rotted or worm-infested is thrown away, and fermented sauces are poured out outside the town {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVI, pp. 87-88}. The murri pickling brine must not be prepared by cooking over a fire — it causes leprosy — and bees' honey is adulterated with gum {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVI, p. 88}. Their goods are kept in earthenware against flies and vermin and dust, their cloths clean, whisks raised against flies by day; and the shops apart from the suqs get particular observation {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVI, p. 88}.

## The inspector of the butter magazine

In Evliya's Istanbul the trade rose to a state office. The sixth of the city's inspectors holds the butter magazine: he receives the tithe of all fresh and melted butter and all sorts of oils, his inspectorship is farmed annually for seven million aspers, and beneath him stand the overseers (dideban), the brokers (simsar) and the receivers of the duties {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 162-163}. Under him march two guilds in the procession: the butter-merchants, a thousand men who are great merchants, exposing in their shops all sorts of butter and oil — crying to fine boys who pass, "Stay, young gentlemen, I'll grease you, stay that I may not lose my butter" — and the oil-merchants, twelve hundred and eighty-five men in six hundred shops, who carry great bottles full of oil behind their mounted inspector {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 162-163}.

## The zayyat of Cairo

Centuries later, Lane's list of the Cairo trades still gives the shop its place: "the oilman ('zeiyat'), who sells butter, cheese, honey, &c., as well as oil" {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}. The stock the hisba books policed — oil, clarified butter, cheese, honey — is exactly what still hung together in the Egyptian oilman's shop in the nineteenth century.
