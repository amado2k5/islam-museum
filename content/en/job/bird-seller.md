## Summary

The kushbaz caught, bred and sold birds — doves for the takla-players, nightingales for great men's houses, sparrows and waterfowl for the markets — in a guild of two hundred men and fifty shops in Evliya's Istanbul, with five hundred bird-hunters and five hundred nightingale-merchants in its train {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 343-350, pp. 197-200}. Al-Shayzari sets the muhtasib over the bird-sellers, and Lane finds the pigeon-house standing by the corn in Egypt's villages {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 9, pp. 52-53; lane-modern-egyptians p. 21}.

## Cages and lime-rods

The sparrow-merchants and other bird-sellers, the Serjeyan, have no shops: their abode is on heaths and gardens, where they spread nets or lime-rods and take all possible kinds of birds, which they exhibit in cages at the public procession {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 349, p. 200}. The pelicans draw water with their beaks from the cups put under the cage; the Mankirkushi throws the penny-stone in its cage from one side to the other, and the bird-catchers play different tricks {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 349, p. 200}. The hunters and fowlers, five hundred with no shops, are reckoned assistants of the bowmen because they bring feathers for the wings of arrows, and they catch birds with birds {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 343-344, pp. 197-198}.

## The takla-players

The bird-merchants proper have no patrons, and Evliya repeats an old proverb that couples them with gamblers for scorn {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 347, p. 198}. Yet the young gentlemen of Constantinople will give fifty piastres for an egg to put under their own doves, and when the young birds soar into the air and descend again, these gentlemen run after them — the play called takla {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 347, p. 198}. The takla-players breed a great number of doves of all sorts — Pal, Shebr, Joweizi, Shami, Missri, Baghdadi, Demkesh and many more — and the most esteemed is the Baghdadi, because it never misses its way but returns to its nest however great the distance {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 347, p. 198}.

## Sa'di-zadeh's thousand doves

Evliya tells of Sa'di-zadeh of Bursa, who had spent ten thousand piastres on a flock of a thousand doves which he fed with pomegranate grains wrapped in musk {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 347, pp. 198-199}. A storm carried the whole flock off; he wandered seven years to Algiers, found them in a palace there, and proved them his own by calling them to their accustomed feed of musk-soaked pomegranate seed — whereupon they soared and reached Bursa before him {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 347, p. 199}.

## Nightingales for kings

The nightingale-merchants, five hundred men without shops, furnish great men, mollas, sons of vezirs and barbers' shops with nightingales {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 350, p. 200}. They have precious cages set with onyxes and pearls, some worth a thousand piastres or more, made only as presents to kings; in these cages the nightingales sing loud against each other, and in others talking parrots and chattering starlings are seen, some reciting prayers {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 350, p. 200}.

## The muhtasib's watch

Al-Shayzari bids the muhtasib keep an eye on the hunters of sparrows and other birds: most of them, he says, have no religion and do not pray, and they might mix birds which have died naturally with those which were slaughtered and sell them together {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 9, pp. 52-53}. The inspector must take no bribe nor accept a gift from any of them {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 9, p. 52}.

## Pigeon-houses of the villages

In many of Egypt's villages, Lane notes, large pigeon-houses of a square form stand by the corn {cite:lane-modern-egyptians p. 21}. And in the conjurer's room, two live pigeons with little round bells attached fly ringing about the house {cite:lane-modern-egyptians p. 540}.

## Not yet covered

The sources give the guilds' numbers, the tackle and the breeds, but not the prices of ordinary birds, the seasons of netting, the trade beyond Istanbul, or how the bird-merchants stood with the falconers, a separate guild {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 343-350, pp. 197-200; shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 9, pp. 52-53}.
