## Summary

The tribal shaykh is the man recognised as the tribe's head for his personal merit or wealth: he holds the tribe together in war and peace and sees to its needs, and his burdens are heavy though his privileges are few {cite:tdv-seyh}. He wields no absolute authority over the members: great matters are debated in the council of the families' elders, so he arbitrates more than he commands, and the office demands patience, generosity, hospitality and a courage that does not shrink from danger for the tribe's sake {cite:tdv-seyh} {cite:tdv-kabile}. The Arabs also called him sayyid or ra'is; among the Ottomans the boy (tribe) was run by its boy beyi and the aşiret by its mîr-aşiret or kethüdâ, the choice generally hereditary {cite:tdv-asiret}.

## The tribe and its order

The tribe is a body of people sharing one ancestor, joined by kin; the Arab order ran: several aşirets make a fasīle, fasīles a fahz, fahzes a batn, batns an imāre, and imāres together the tribe {cite:tdv-asiret}. Every tribe had its territory — the "menzil" or "buyūt al-kabīla" — which others could not cross or settle without leave, marked by valleys, hills and dunes; private land ownership was unknown and pasture and water were held in common {cite:tdv-kabile}. Tribes contracted "hilf" pacts for solidarity and defence, whose members gathered and swore until they became as one tribe: an attack on one counted as an attack on all, and one man's grant of protection bound all {cite:tdv-kabile}.

## The shaykh at work

His duties were to keep the tribe together in war and peace and to provide for its needs, acting as arbiter more than commander {cite:tdv-seyh}. Whoever broke the customs and stained the tribe's honour was punished by the "khalʿ": his ties with family and tribe were cut until another tribe, family or person took him under protection, his expulsion was proclaimed in the pilgrimage and fair seasons, and he was called khalīʿ, ṭarīd or laʿīn; some of the expelled poor — the suʿlūk — formed raiding bands {cite:tdv-kabile}.

## Asabiyya and its censure

The bond of descent produced "asabiyya": the offence against a tribe or one of its members summoned the whole body to repel the wrong or exact compensation, and on it stood the intertribal wars called the ayyām al-Arab {cite:tdv-kabile}. Islam condemned asabiyya as aid in a wrongful cause and forbade it, while accepting that people know their genealogies and tribes — knowledge required anyway for marriage, inheritance, blood-money and the ʿāqila {cite:tdv-kabile}. Yet tribalism never wholly died: the ridda movements, the claim that the caliphate belonged to Quraysh, the society's splits into northern and southern, Mudar and Yaman, Umayyad and Hashimi lines, and the Shu'ubiyya movement {cite:tdv-kabile}.

## The Turkish tribe and the Ottoman state

Among the Turks the tribe was the sole social organisation, named by size il (el), budun, ulus, taife, boy, aşiret, urug, cemaat, oymak and oba; each boy had its "boy beyi" and the heads of the oymaks came from his family {cite:tdv-kabile}. The Oghuz divided into twenty-four boys in the Bozok and Üçok branches, each boy striking its own damga on its herds and goods, and the Akkoyunlu, Karakoyunlu and Dulkadir polities were built on Turkmen tribes {cite:tdv-kabile}. Ottoman registers graded the nomad order as boy, aşiret, cemaat, oymak, mahalle and oba (the family); the boy beyi ran the boy — in the Arab tribes the shaykh — and the mîr-aşiret or kethüdâ ran the aşiret, generally hereditary within the boy beyi's authority {cite:tdv-asiret}. The need to control the tribes led the state to reorganise them as kethüdâlıks and to permit movement only on fixed routes, their uncontrolled wandering once central authority weakened becoming a ground for chastisement and settlement {cite:tdv-asiret}. The Ottoman state itself was a tribe turned state: the Kayı boy's struggles with the other boys stand at the start of Ottoman history {cite:tdv-kabile}.

## The title beyond the tribe

The title spread far: the heads of the scholarly disciplines were called shaykh al-mufassirin, al-muhaddithin, al-fuqaha, al-qurra, al-lughawiyyin, al-mu'arrikhin, al-udaba and al-atibba; the servants and teachers of the two sanctuaries held shaykh al-Haram and shaykh al-Haramayn from the Ayyubids; the chief Mamluk bey in Ottoman Egypt was the famous shaykh al-balad; the Arab tribal delegates were the shaykh al-Arab; guild heads were shaykh al-suq, al-tujjar or al-dabbaghin; and the head of the Ottoman religious hierarchy was the shaykh al-Islam {cite:tdv-seyh}. Ibn Battuta records that the sultan of Mogadishu was called shaykh when he visited in 1331 {cite:tdv-seyh}. Today in the Arab lands the title still marks heads of state, officials, tribal chiefs and notables {cite:tdv-seyh}.
