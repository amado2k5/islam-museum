## Summary

The fisherman — the sayyād of the Arabs, the balıkçı of the Turks — took his fish by net, line, harpoon, pot, basket and lookout tower, and Ottoman Istanbul knew him in a dozen guilds {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer}. Evliya begins with the Serai fishermen (Balıkjian), three hundred men under the inspector of the Imperial fishery, which was farmed each year for seven million aspers; their establishment stood outside the Flour-hall at the old fish-market on the sea-shore, and their patron was Jonas {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer}.

## Patrons and legend

The fishermen's patron in the Prophet's time, Evliya says, was Nassr-ullah Semmad, girded by Selman the Persian, so famous a fisherman that casting his net on desert sand he was sure of catching fish; Evliya himself, on pilgrimage at the Emerald Well, saw pilgrims bring aprons full of fish found among the sand — the remains, they told him, of what the Prophet bade Semmad take there {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer}.

## The dalian watchmen

Seven hundred men worked the wooden lookout platforms called dalian: the chief one at Begkos fished the swordfish, where a watcher on a high mast, sighting the fish, dropped a stone to frighten it into the spread net; the catch was clubbed and carried to Constantinople {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer}. Other dalians took the turbot at the Black Stones, the kurek fish at Terkos, and lines of them stood on both sides of the Bosphorus canal for mackerel, palamida, grey mullet and more; they gave a tenth to the fishery inspector {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer}.

## Nets, lines and spears

The great ığrıp net kept three hundred men with no shops — so heavy that divers went under to cut and lighten it, with two hundred men dragging each wing {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer}. A hundred and fifty karatia nets hung on poles stretching out from shore houses between the Serai point and Eyyub; ten Greek fishermen whose ancestors opened the Petri gate to Mehmed II paid no tithe and were bound to hunt dolphins, which served as medicine for the Emperor, among the Princes' Islands {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer}. Behind them Evliya counts a thousand common-net men, a hundred satshma throwers who cast by hand along the shore, a thousand line fishermen, the harpooners (Sapkanjian) who killed palamida and alakerdeh from boats, the pot-fishers of Kaghid Khanah, and the basket-fishers who took lobsters, sea-spiders and crabs in still water {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer}.

## Market rules

The fishmongers — three thousand men who only sold, from Balata and Fener to Top-khanah and Beshik-tash — were a trade of their own {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa's hisba manual orders the fish-fryers to wash their baskets and trays every day and sprinkle them with salt each night, never to mingle fresh fish with overnight stock — the mark of freshness being the red nose and gills — to fry only in saffron-seed or fresh sesame oil, and to throw corrupted fish on the dung-hills outside the town {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy}. The same manual counts fishermen among the humble crafts over whose admissibility as witnesses three juristic views were held, and tells the tale of a ruler who forbade fishing in a stream where people once had leave, and the fish disappeared {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy}.
