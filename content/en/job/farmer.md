## Summary

The fellah tills the land and waters it; the Turkish farmer is the çiftçi, and Egyptian Arabic names the whole peasantry the fellaheen — in Lane's words, "the agriculturists" {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. Andrew Watson's entry on agriculture describes an early Islamic farming revolution: a wave of new crops carried across the conquered lands, old irrigation techniques generalised, and a new summer season that ended the year's fallow {cite:tdv-ziraat}.

## The new crops

The conquests of the seventh and eighth centuries joined lands from the Iberian peninsula to the Indus valley, and through them crops known before only in parts of Sind or just reaching the Sasanian realm spread wherever climate and irrigation allowed {cite:tdv-ziraat}. Sorghum, Asian rice, sugarcane, cotton, the bitter orange, lemon, lime, grapefruit, banana, plantain, watermelon, spinach, artichoke, aubergine, indigo, coconut and mango followed in later centuries, giving consumers new foods and lifting the yield and revenue of the land {cite:tdv-ziraat}. Later still came coffee, probably carried to Yemen in the thirteenth century and widely grown there by the fifteenth, and then the American arrivals — maize, potato, sweet potato, red pepper, tomato, tobacco, pineapple and American cotton {cite:tdv-ziraat}.

## Water and the summer season

The new crops drew on a spreading technology of water: the human-powered shaduf and Archimedes screw, animal- and water-powered dulab wheels, dams and cisterns, riverside pools, artificial lakes, wells, weirs, surface channels and the underground kâriz or kanat {cite:tdv-ziraat}. Most of the conquered belt had a Mediterranean climate — cool wet winters, hot rainless summers — and before, nearly all crops had been winter ones, with half the ploughland lying fallow each year {cite:tdv-ziraat}. Summer crops like sorghum, sugarcane, cotton and rice, grown under summer irrigation, opened an almost wholly new farming season; more green and animal manure and complex rotations let several crops come off the same field in two years {cite:tdv-ziraat}.

## The fellah's day in Egypt

Lane, writing of Egypt in 1833-35, counts nearly all of the lower orders as fellaheen and sets out their diet: bread of millet or maize, milk, new cheese, eggs, small salted fish, cucumbers, melons and gourds, onions, beans, chick-peas, lupins, lentils and dates, with rice and meat rare and the cheap tobacco the one luxury {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. The women fetched water in large jars on the head, spun cotton, linen or woollen yarn, and kneaded cattle dung with chopped straw into the flat fuel-cakes called gelleh dried on walls and roofs {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. Children became useful at five or six tending the flocks, and worked beside their fathers in the fields until they married {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.

## Taxes, ikta and waqf

The peasant's hardest dealings were with the state: from the ninth century the Abbasids adopted tax-farming, and the eleventh-century Buyid and Seljuk ikta gave officers the right to collect and keep the revenue of great tracts — arrangements that rewarded taking the most from the land with the least invested in it {cite:tdv-ziraat}. From the tenth century waqf endowments passed fields to mosques, madrasas and khans whose managers were often inexperienced, so that "the dead hand of the waqf" touched a growing share of the farmland {cite:tdv-ziraat}. Lane records the same burden on the ground: the fellaheen suffered under the mamoors who collected the taxes, and counted their stripes proudly {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.

## Reform and the modern field

The Ottoman Tanzimat of 1839 tried to replace tax-farms and iktas with centrally collected fair taxes, and a cadastral survey to give peasants title; both foundered on thin staff and the interference of the strong, though 1888 brought the Ziraat Bankası with cheap credit, and 1892 the Halkalı agricultural school {cite:tdv-ziraat}. Midhat Pasha, governing Iraq 1869-72, redistributed land to peasants and built dams; Mehmed Ali's Egypt deepened Nile canals, built barrages and pushed cotton — Lane himself watched the same era's pressures on the fellah, whose sons could be taken as army recruits {cite:tdv-ziraat} {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.
