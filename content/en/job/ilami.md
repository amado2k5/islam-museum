## Summary

The ilami works in a public medium — radio, television, satellite channels and now digital platforms — as announcer, presenter, editor, correspondent or commentator; specialists call the collective 'the media people' (al-i'lamiyyun). The trade carries the voice of crier, preacher and journalist farther than speech ever reached: from a limited hearing to millions of listeners at once.

## From press to airwaves

The profession came after the press had matured the news industry and trained generations in editing and presentation {cite:tdv-matbuat}. Broadcasting in the Islamic world began with Cairo radio in 1934 and the announcer's voice entered homes; television in the 1950s–60s added the face; Arab satellite channels from the 1990s made the announcer, preacher, commentator and correspondent public voices {cite:tdv-matbuat}.

## Its kinds

The announcer (newsreader), the presenter (the all-round host), the correspondent (the field voice), the commentator (sports, political and religious), the editor (the journalist inside the broadcast), and the Qur'an reader, mawwal singer and muezzin of the airwaves — the radio preacher and televised reciter are artists who moved from the mihrab to the studio {cite:tdv-matbuat}.

## Qualities and responsibility

He is chosen as the munadi once was: for a fine voice, clear articulation, command of language and presence of mind; today adding technical knowledge, camera manners and broadcast ethics {cite:tdv-matbuat}. Journalists' and broadcasters' syndicates and media institutes gather the profession, and ethics codes are written for it, because a word sent to millions is a trust.

## In the Islamic space

The media person in Islamic lands joined journalist and preacher: radio and television da'wah programmes, Qur'anic and educational channels, correspondents covering public affairs — carrying on the newspaper's legacy of taking news to public opinion and shaping public taste {cite:tdv-matbuat}.
