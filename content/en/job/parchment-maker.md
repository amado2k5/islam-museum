## Summary

The parchment-maker dressed animal skins into leaves for writing — the riqq on which Qur'an codices and chancery documents were written before paper spread {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 43-44}. In Evliya's Istanbul the parchment-makers were three hundred men in eighty shops, all Jews, and behind them walked the parchment-painters with painted skins {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 351-352}.

## The skin becomes a page

The craft's product survives in the libraries: Arnold and Grohmann describe the Egyptian Library codex ascribed to the Imam Ja'far b. Muhammad al-Sadiq, containing the first half of the Qur'an on 209 leaves of gazelle parchment — Stern dated the specimen to about 720 A.D., while Moritz placed it in the second or third century of the Hijra {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 43-44}.

## Older than paper

Parchment and skin rolls were in use where papyrus did not reach, and the earliest Muslim books lean on skin traditions: two wooden boards were used even in the Prophet's lifetime to enclose the loose leaves on which the sections of the Qur'an were written, and the idea of wooden covers probably came from Abyssinia, where books had long been bound that way {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 43-44}.

## Parchment inside the binding

The craft fed the bookbinders too: Grohmann found fine yellow parchment leaves pasted inside the covers of early Egyptian manuscripts — one fragment bearing a Coptic text of the seventh century — and strips of parchment drawn through slits to form openwork ornament {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 33-35}. An Arabic parchment end-paper in the Rainer collection shows the material used as a decorated guard-leaf {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 51}.

## The Istanbul guild

Evliya counts the parchment-makers (Keiderjian) at three hundred men with eighty shops; they are all Jews, and their manufactory is at Khoja-pasha {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 351, p. 207 area}. The parchment-painters (Turshejian) follow them, adorning their shops and themselves with painted parchments {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 352}.

## The turn to paper

When paper spread through the Islamic world the riqq trade did not vanish: its leaves remained the surface for firmans, for noble codices, and for the painted parchments Evliya's second guild displayed {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 352; arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 43-44}.

## Not yet covered

The sources give the Istanbul census and the craft's archaeological traces, but not the liming, stretching and scraping sequence of the Ottoman parchment works, nor the trade's history between the gazelle-skin codices of the eighth century and the guild of the seventeenth {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 30-44; evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 351}.
