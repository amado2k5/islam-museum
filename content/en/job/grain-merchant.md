## Summary

The habbab dealt in grain — the wheat, barley, lentils and beans on which every household's bread depended — and for that reason the market inspector watched him more closely than almost any other seller {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 5, pp. 45-46}. Both surviving hisba manuals open his chapter with the same two commandments: no hoarding, and no mixing bad wheat with good or old with new {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI; shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 5, p. 45}.

## The Prophet at the grain heap

The founding scene of the grain trade's regulation belongs to the Prophet's own lifetime. Passing a trader who was selling corn, he put his hand into the heap, found the grain beneath wet, and asked the reason; the man answered that rain had fallen on it. "Why then did you not put that at the top of the grain?" he asked — the ruling from which the manuals take the duty to display every fault in the goods {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy general rules of trading, ¶169}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa pairs the story with Jarir, the convert who always pointed out the flaws in his goods to customers and answered those who warned he would never sell anything: "We promised the Prophet to deal honestly with every Muslim" {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy general rules of trading, ¶170}.

## The hisba rules

The grain-merchant must not hoard grain {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 5, p. 45}. He must not mix bad wheat with good nor old wheat with fresh — that is swindling the people {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, p. 85}. If washing the grain is necessary, it must be thoroughly dried afterwards, and each kind of wheat is to be sold separately {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 5, p. 45}.

## The war over the granaries

The political stakes of the trade appear in the Fatimid record that Buckley quotes from al-Maqrizi's Itti'az. During one of the recurrent grain shortages the muhtasib Sulayman ibn 'Azza gathered all the grain merchants in one place, closed every road but one so that no gaddah of grain could leave the city unseen, and ordered the selling of grain in Misr controlled by closing some hundred and fifty granaries {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley introduction, Iti'az 2:165}. The measure defeated itself: the granaries were opened again "so that the country may thrive with bread and wheat until there is another crop" {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley introduction, Iti'az 2:165}.

## False weights

Since grain was sold by weight and measure, the grain market absorbed the hisba of the balance. The muhtasib had to inspect the habba weights when their owners were not expecting it, for some cheats soaked grains of wheat and barley in oil, buried the ends of needles in them and dried them in the shade, so that the falsified weight showed no sign {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 4, p. 44}. The cubic measures themselves were doctored: gypsum poured into the bottom, oil-dregs smeared on the sides, fig-sap kneaded with oil to a cream and stuck inside — and every shop was required to keep three sizes of measure {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 4, p. 45}.

## The magazines of Istanbul

In Evliya's Istanbul the corn arrived from Kessendir, Koluz, Beshdereh and Dobruja and lay in the magazines of the kilar, under their own officers: an Inspector of corn (Boghdai-emini) with three hundred men who threw corn on the spectators as they passed in the procession, and the Inspector of the Kilar, whose charge included the corn magazine of Sultan Suleiman outside the gate {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 222-224, p. 167}.

## Not yet covered

The manuals regulate the merchant's scales and storage but say nothing of his purchase contracts, the corn-ships' freight rates, or the price seasons that made hoarding profitable; the Fatimid anecdote stands nearly alone as narrative evidence {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley introduction; ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI}.
