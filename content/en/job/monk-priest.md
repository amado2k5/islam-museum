## Summary

The monk and the priest are the religious vocation the Islamic world met in the Christian communities under and around it — and, in the sources' own words, in the Buddhist, Jewish, Brahman, Zoroastrian and other traditions beyond {cite:tdv-kesis} {cite:tdv-ruhban}. Arabic knows two figures: the râhib (plural ruhbân), the one who withdraws to worship and live ascetically, and the qissîs or kâhin — the keşiş and papaz of Turkish — who performs the rites in his congregation's name, teaches it and interprets the scripture {cite:tdv-ruhban} {cite:tdv-kesis}.

## The words

Ruhbân is the plural of râhib, from rahbe — the fear and deep religious anxiety that makes a man suffer — and rahbâniyye, the giving of oneself to worship under that intense religious dread; the râhib is the one who fears God and worships in seclusion {cite:tdv-ruhban}. Keşiş is a Persian word for the Christians' learned, ascetic and leading religious men, its Syriac form keshshîshâ meaning elder, shaykh, priest; Arabic answered it with qass and qissîs, and used râhib for the Christian ascetics who lived withdrawn in monasteries {cite:tdv-kesis}. The Western words — monk, monasticism — go back to the Greek monos, the solitary {cite:tdv-kesis}.

## Two ways of withdrawal

The sources distinguish two monastic lives {cite:tdv-kesis}. The first is the hermit's: those who withdraw alone into desert, mountain or forest and see no one — among them those who took to a pillar, a tree-top or a sealed cell — a form rare today and found more in the Hindu and Jain traditions, though the Christian literature's travelling hermits, like Saint Antony, belong to it {cite:tdv-kesis}. The second is the cenobite's: those who keep the common life in a monastery under a rule — the life the word 'monasticism' ordinarily means in Christianity, the sangha in Buddhism, the samnyāsin in Hinduism {cite:tdv-kesis}. The monk enters by a stated rite, takes a distinctive dress and sometimes a mark on the body — the Buddhist's shaved head or the Christian's tonsure — and binds himself to poverty, humility, celibacy and obedience to the rule and the guide; the monastery day, ordered by bells, holds prayer, meditation, reading and work, and the community lives on alms or on its own endowment {cite:tdv-kesis}.

## The church priest

Beside the withdrawn monk stands the other ruhbân: the rahip or papaz who serves in the churches, a religious officer who leads worship in the congregation's name, instructs it in matters of religion and interprets the sacred text — above all through the altar and the offering {cite:tdv-ruhban}. The priesthood is hereditary in some religions — Judaism's kohen, the khoury, the Zoroastrian dastur, where marriage was required — and elective in Christianity, where celibacy was the rule in the Latin West but not binding in the Eastern churches {cite:tdv-ruhban}. The Christian hierarchy grew out of the New Testament's episkopos (overseer), presbuteros (elder) and diakonos (servant): in the developed order the bishop stands at the head as administrator and supervisor, under him the senior priests or presbyters who conduct the rite, and below them the deacons or priestly assistants {cite:tdv-ruhban}.

## How the Islamic books saw them

Ruhban and rahbâniyye come four times in the Qur'an, always with reference to the Christian tradition, and the tafsirs read the monks of the praised verse as the worshipping, humble, tender-hearted Christian group — with the qissîsîn beside them — and record the interpretation that the verse spoke of the Abyssinian Najashi and his people who accepted Islam in the Prophet's time {cite:tdv-ruhban}. The commentators explain the rise of rahbâniyye as the Christians' own practice, not a divine ordinance: a group of Christians, fearing tribulation in religion under unjust kings, withdrew to the mountains to worship and purify their souls, living alone in caves and keeping away from women {cite:tdv-ruhban}. Yet the same sources carry the blame addressed to the unfaithful ruhban: those taken as lords when their rulings displaced God's, those who devoured men's wealth unjustly — easing the rules for gain or taking bribes — and the hadith the scholars cite in which the Prophet, hearing that some Muslims had begun a monk's life, answered that he himself both fasts and eats, worships and sleeps, and that whoever turns from his way is not of him {cite:tdv-ruhban}.

## In the lands of Islam

Christian monasticism is held to have begun in third-century Egypt with Saint Antony's withdrawal to the desert, and the cenobitic life with Pachomius about 320; from Egypt it spread to Sinai, Palestine and Syria — lands that remained important monastic centres after the Muslim conquest — and Mar Augin, a disciple of Pachomius, founded it in northern Mesopotamia, where the monastery on Mount Izla became its centre {cite:tdv-kesis}. In Anatolia the work of Basil of Caesarea and his sister Macrina spread the institution; Istanbul, and from the tenth century Mount Athos, became centres {cite:tdv-kesis}. In the West the Benedictine rules shaped the monastery from the sixth century, and the monks who copied, taught and preached were also the missionaries who carried Christianity to the remotest parts {cite:tdv-kesis}.
