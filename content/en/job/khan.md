## Summary

Khan was the ruler's title of the Turkic peoples, in use from pre-Islamic times {cite:tdv-han-hukumdar}. Its feminine was khanum; the senior title above it was the khagan {cite:tdv-han-hukumdar}.

## From shanyü to khan

Chinese sources give the Xiongnu rulers the titles shanyü or tanhu; from the third century these yielded to khagan and khan {cite:tdv-han-hukumdar}. In the nomadic state the khagan stood at the top and appointed his kinsmen as khans over the dependent oymaks {cite:tdv-han-hukumdar}.

## The khagan's work

The khagan's office was absolute and universal — in the Oghuz Khagan epic the ruler claims to be 'khagan of the four corners of the earth' — and its task was to hold the oymaks and tribes together, multiply them, order their relations inside the state, lead the army as commander-in-chief and keep the people in plenty {cite:tdv-han-hukumdar}. Göktürk and Uyghur rulers bore 'khagan' as their official title, carried by the Avars to Europe as 'kaganus'; the Göktürks also used 'khan', and the Khazars ran their state as a khaganate whose prestige led even the first Rus princes to copy the title {cite:tdv-han-hukumdar}.

## Into the Islamic world

Khan rose to mean emperor, shah and sultan: Temüjin took it when he founded the Mongol empire and was thence known as Chinggis Khan, and his sons were styled khagan, a title scribes were ordered to use in official correspondence {cite:tdv-han-hukumdar}. The Ilkhanids put 'khagan' on their coins with epithets like 'the great' and 'the just'; Ghazan preferred 'al-sultan al-a'zam' {cite:tdv-han-hukumdar}. In the Khwarazmshah army khan was a rank equal to emir; in Dede Korkut it stands closer to 'bey' {cite:tdv-han-hukumdar}.

## Ottoman and later usage

Murad I was the first Ottoman padishah to take khan as a sovereign title; Yildirim Bayezid put it in his tughra and Mehmed I on his coins {cite:tdv-han-hukumdar}. The Crimean Girays and the rulers of Khiva, Bukhara and Kokand bore it — their states are simply 'khanates' — while the Karakhitay rulers were called gürhan {cite:tdv-han-hukumdar}.

## Khakan

'Khakan' is the Arabicised form of khagan, coined by Arab authors for Turkic, Mongol and Chinese rulers, and spreading among Turks after Islamisation {cite:tdv-han-hukumdar}. From Mehmed II the Ottoman sultans used it inside the formula 'sultan of the two lands and khakan of the two seas' {cite:tdv-han-hukumdar}. In India 'khan' also survived as a title of nobility, and 'khan-i khanan' — khan of khans — named a high military office in the Indian Islamic states {cite:tdv-han-hukumdar}.
