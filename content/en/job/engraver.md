## The craft

The engraver — hakkâk, from Arabic hakk, "to rub, scrape, carve" — cut script and ornament into stone, metal, ivory and wood; the term today points especially to stone and metal work, above all the carving of seals {cite:tdv-hakkaklik}.

## Selection and training

Seal engravers entered the craft on the recommendation of respected, trustworthy men, because any forgery could put the state, institutions and persons in difficulty — and over the Ottoman centuries no seal forgery is recorded {cite:tdv-hakkaklik}. Apprentices had to be both reliable and gifted: they first learned calligraphy, by meşk with their master or a calligrapher he recommended, then carved set exercises on the material he chose {cite:tdv-hakkaklik}. Each master was known for his material and script — some carved sülüs, some ta'lik, and the most famous cut whatever script the customer asked; the three faces of a three-sided seal kept the same character {cite:tdv-hakkaklik}.

## The work

Seals were engraved in mirror image so they would read correctly when stamped — demanding work on top of an already difficult craft {cite:tdv-hakkaklik}. For softer metals — gold, silver, brass — the engraver used steel pens, gripping the blank in a wooden hand vice in one hand and cutting with the other; harder stones — agate, carnelian, turquoise, jade — took diamond-tipped pens {cite:tdv-hakkaklik}.

## The Istanbul guilds

Evliya Çelebi's census of the Murad IV era (1623–1640) lists three guilds: the hakkâkân — 30 shops, 105 men, pir Abdullah Yümnî — carving Ceylon agate, turquoise and jade; the mührkünân — 50 shops, 80 men, pir the caliph Uthman — whose masters Mahmud, Rızâ and Ferid Çelebis charged 100–500 kuruş a seal and cut the viziers'; and the mührkünân-ı sîm ve heyâkil — 15 shops, 40 men, pir Ukkâşe — cutting silver seals and talismans but unable to work Yemeni agate {cite:tdv-hakkaklik}. Their later collective home was the Hakkâklar Çarşısı beside Beyazıt Mosque, today's Sahaflar book market {cite:tdv-hakkaklik}.

## Records against forgery

Before handing over a seal, engravers stamped it into a kept register (defter): clients were recorded, and a lost seal's replacement was made deliberately unlike the old one {cite:tdv-hakkaklik}. Two engravers of the same name could not work in one district {cite:tdv-hakkaklik}. Dating and signing seals began with the Ottomans, though some used symbols and others omitted signatures out of respect for notables and women customers; almost nothing is known of the late masters — Dânâ, Mecdî, Sâmi, İzzet, Fennî, Fehmî and above all Yümnî — beyond the signatures squeezed onto their seals {cite:tdv-hakkaklik}.

## Standing and decline

Until the Second Constitution (1908) Ottoman officials sealed rather than signed documents, and petitioners needed seals; sultans patronised and practised the art — Selim I learned gem-cutting in Trabzon and cut a die in his father Bayezid's name, and Mahmud II carved seals, sold them discreetly and gave the proceeds to the poor {cite:tdv-hakkaklik}. When the seal obligation ended and the alphabet reform spread signatures, engravers moved into kalemkârlık within the goldsmiths' trade; the related hâtemkârî — carving straight (not reversed) then inlaying silver or gold into copper, coloured marble into white, ebony, mother-of-pearl or tortoiseshell into wood — shades into kakmacılık (inlay) work {cite:tdv-hakkaklik}.
