## Summary

Malik (king) is the ruling title of the Semitic languages since before Islam, derived from the root m-l-k, "to own"; its plural is mulūk {cite:tdv-melik}. The first Muslims declined the title after the Prophet's death and chose khalifa, amir al-mu'minin and, rarely, imam; many regarded the Umayyads — for taking heredity as their rule — as mulk rather than a caliphate {cite:tdv-melik}. The title returned with the Samanids in Khurasan and Transoxiana, and with the Buyids who joined it to the Sasanian shahanshah, then spread to the Ghaznavids, Qarakhanids, Seljuks, Zengids, Fatimids, Ayyubids and Mamluks {cite:tdv-melik}. In the twentieth century it was reborn as the title of the new kingdoms of the Hijaz, Iraq, Egypt, Yemen, Afghanistan, Jordan, Libya and Morocco {cite:tdv-melik}.

## The word before Islam

The word comes from the root meaning possession and has its counterparts across the Semitic languages: Hebrew, Aramaic, Akkadian and Assyrian {cite:tdv-melik}. It appears as a royal title in pre-Islamic inscriptions: on the Sirwah inscription of South Arabia for a king of the Sabean house, and on the Namara epitaph of North Arabia where Imru'l-Qays b. Hujr is styled "malik al-Arab" {cite:tdv-melik}.

## Why the first Muslims refused it

In the political reshaping after the Prophet's death the Muslims did not adopt the title malik; the Rashidun, and after them the Umayyad and Abbasid heads of state, called themselves khalifa, amir al-mu'minin and, rarely, imam {cite:tdv-melik}. Yet broad Muslim opinion looked on the Umayyads not as an Islamic caliphate but as "mulk", since they made heredity the basis of choosing the head of state {cite:tdv-melik}. With the Abbasid takeover, elements of the Sasanian tradition — above all the sacred kingship worked into the Nasihat al-muluk literature — entered the bureaucracy and political thought and revived the idea of kingship {cite:tdv-melik}.

## The title returns

The Samanids were the first Muslim dynasty to take the malik title as a mark of relative independence from Baghdad, and Nuh b. Nasr (943-954) struck "al-Malik al-Mu'ayyad" on his coins {cite:tdv-melik}. The Buyids adopted "shahanshah", king of kings, and Adud al-Dawla used it widely together with malik; in 429/1038 Jalal al-Dawla asked the caliph's permission for "shahanshah al-a'zam malik al-muluk", while the ruler of Khuzistan, Hezaresb, offered the caliph 100,000 dinars for the malik title and was refused — it could be granted, he was told, only to members of the Seljuk house {cite:tdv-melik}. The title then spread to the Ghaznavids, Qarakhanids, Seljuks and Zengids, while the Fatimids used it not for caliphs but for amirs and above all viziers, Ridwan b. Walakhshi taking "al-sayyid al-ajall al-malik al-afdal" in 530/1136 {cite:tdv-melik}. Among the Ayyubids Saladin bore "al-Malik al-Nasir" from his vizierate days, his successors took al-Aziz, al-Adil, al-Kamil, al-Afdal and al-Muzaffar, and the Mamluks followed with al-Malik al-Mu'izz Aybak, al-Muzaffar Qutuz and al-Qahir Baybars {cite:tdv-melik}.

## Title inflation and the provincial maliks

Buyids, Seljuks, Ayyubids and Mamluks did not keep the title for the sovereign alone but gave it to princes, viziers and local governors, so that many within one realm bore it and its value fell {cite:tdv-melik}. The great rulers therefore compounded it: Tughril Beg was styled "al-malik al-jalil, malik al-Islam, malik al-ghuz" and received "malik al-mashriq wa'l-maghrib" from the Abbasid caliph, while Cagri Beg had the khutba read for himself at Merv in 1038 as "malik al-muluk" {cite:tdv-melik}. In Anatolian Seljuk usage the compounded title even marked the heads of professions: malik al-kuttab for the head of the chancery, malik al-shu'ara for the chief of poets, malik al-hujjab for the chief chamberlain, malik al-nuwwab for the chief of the nāibs {cite:tdv-melik}. Women bore it rarely: Shajar al-Durr ruled Egypt as "malikat al-Islam", and Raziyya of Delhi, the only female ruler of Muslim India, chose it rather than the sultan used by the rest of her house {cite:tdv-melik}. A malik holding a province under a great sultan kept a court modelled on the capital — his own vizier, treasury, commanders and divan — had the khutba read for the caliph, the sultan and himself, struck coin with the sultan's leave, and might beat no more than three nevbet a day where the great sultan beat five {cite:tdv-melik}.

## The modern kingdoms

The title later lost its worth and nearly vanished; the Ottomans scarcely used it {cite:tdv-melik}. It returned in the twentieth century with new content after a century of relations with European monarchies: the Sharif of Mecca, Husayn, proclaimed himself malik of the Arab lands in 1916 and was recognised internationally as "malik al-Hijaz"; his son Faysal was declared king of Syria in 1920 and of Iraq in 1921; the Egyptian sultan Fu'ad took the title in 1922; the Afghan amir Amanullah Khan turned king; the Yemeni imam was recognised as king in form; Abd al-Aziz b. Sa'ud declared himself king of the Hijaz and sultan of Najd in 1926 and king of Saudi Arabia in 1932; the Jordanian amir Abdallah b. Husayn took it in 1946; Idris I proclaimed himself king of Libya in 1951; and the Moroccan sultan Muhammad V converted his title to malik in 1957 {cite:tdv-melik}.
