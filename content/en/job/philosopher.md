## Summary

The faylasūf — plural falāsifa, an Arabicised plural of the Greek philosophos — is the scholar whose first interest is philosophy rather than kalam; so Guillaume defines the word, citing al-Shahrastani that they all followed the Aristotelian path save in a few details borrowed from Plato and the earlier philosophers {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}. Al-Shahrastani's list of the Arabian philosophers begins with al-Kindi and Hunayn ibn Ishaq and ends with Avicenna, held by the majority the keenest in insight; Guillaume adds Ibn Rushd of Cordova, Aristotle's most learned commentator, whom al-Shahrastani would have named had he lived longer {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}.

## The Greek inheritance

Guillaume grants that the whole framework, scope and material of Arabic philosophy traces to the civilisations the Arabs conquered, with Greek philosophy predominating, carried by the Syrian Christians and the cultured pagan community of Harran {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}. The Muslims themselves made no secret of the debt: he quotes al-Jahiz of Basra that without the books of the ancients the Muslims' share in wisdom would be immeasurably smaller {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}.

## Al-Kindi, the philosopher of the Arabs

Al-Kindi — born about 850 at Kufa and educated at Basra and Baghdad — is, in Guillaume's phrase, 'the first and last philosopher the Arabs produced', the one pure-blooded Arab to distinguish himself by a mastery of philosophical problems {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}. Much of his work survives only in the Latin translations of Gerard of Cremona and others; his most consequential act was lending his name and correction to the so-called Theology of Aristotle, in truth a neo-Platonic treatise drawn from the Enneads of Plotinus, translated by Ibn Na'ima of Emessa and corrected by al-Kindi for Ahmad ibn al-Mu'tasim {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}. On the disputed question of his Mu'tazilism, Kaya's TDV biography reports the classifications of Walzer and de Boer without endorsing them, and discusses his defence of Islam {cite:tdv-kindi-kaya}.

## The line after him

The line ran through al-Farabi — the prolific commentator on Aristotle whom Ibn Khallikan called the greatest of the earlier Islamic philosophers — to Avicenna, whose posthumous fame rested more on medicine than philosophy though he could make a subject his own and expound it briefly to the world {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}. In the West the name far better known than al-Farabi's was Avicenna's, and Guillaume closes the sequence with the Spanish philosopher Ibn Rushd {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}.

## A calling under suspicion

The philosopher's standing among the scholars was contested from the start: the obscurantists, Guillaume writes, directed condemnations against all intellectual pursuits unknown to the Arabs of the Prophet's time; philosophy was called 'wisdom mixed with unbelief', and hostile book-titles circulated {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}. A tale was spread of a well-known philosopher recanting on his deathbed with the cry that God had spoken truth and Avicenna was a liar {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}. Guillaume judges that some of the foremost exponents were nominal Muslims or self-confessed heretics who paid for their opinions with their lives or their liberty — the museum reports this as his judgement, attributed, not adopted {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}.

## What the philosopher did

The craft of the falasifa as these sources show it was commentary, correction and synthesis: al-Kindi collating a translated text for a prince, al-Farabi writing treatises on the soul and the intelligence that became known to the Latins, the line handing down the problem of the agent intellect until Averroes and Aquinas {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}. The physics of these men were founded on Aristotle's four causes, and they sought the principle of being in forms and natures {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}.
