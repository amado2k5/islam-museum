## Summary

The shadow puppeteer stands alone behind the lit screen: one artist who is actor, director, choreographer and painter together — moving the figures with hand-skill, giving every person its voice from women and children to the empire's dialects, setting the rhythm and tempo of entries and exits, ordering the dances, and cutting and colouring the leather figures himself {cite:tdv-karagoz}. The Turks knew the show as hayâl-i zıl — "shadow fancy" — distinguishing it from the older kukla puppet shows, and the karagözcü's art spread from Anatolia to Algeria, Tunis, Egypt, Romania, Yugoslavia, Syria, Lebanon and, later, Greece {cite:tdv-karagoz}.

## Where the play came from

Two old explanations compete: some researchers took the play for an inheritance of Central Asia through the Turkish-Chinese contact, though no shadow play is actually known among the Central Asian Turks — the Uzbek term çadır hayal names a marionette, not a shadow — while others traced it west from India with the Gypsy migrations, though shadow play is scarce in the north of India they left and no trace appears in Turkey before the sixteenth century {cite:tdv-karagoz}. The popular legends make Karagöz and Hacivat living men — Evliya's version puts a Gypsy groom of Kırklareli and a certain Yorkça Halil under the Seljuks, while the karagözcüler's own tale sets Karagöz the smith and Hacivat the mason joking on Sultan Orhan's mosque site in Bursa until their banter delayed the work, cost them their heads, and moved Sheikh Küşteri to make and play their figures — but the article counts these as stories without documents {cite:tdv-karagoz}.

## From Mamluk Egypt to Istanbul

The written record puts the play's arrival in the sixteenth century: Ibn Iyas's history of Egypt reports a shadow-player performing before Selim I at the palace on Roda island, enacting the hanging of Sultan Tumanbay at Bâbüzüveyle — rope breaking twice — which so pleased the sultan that he summoned the artist to Istanbul to show it to his son Süleyman {cite:tdv-karagoz}. With Selim came some six hundred Egyptian artists; some players returned after three years, some stayed, and their activity is documented under Ahmed I — while in Egypt itself the art reaches back to the eleventh century, with twelfth-century Egyptian figures kept in German museums {cite:tdv-karagoz}. The Mamluk figures were single-coloured and opaque; the translucent, jointed leather figures are the Turkish artists' seventeenth-century contribution, and the name Karagöz itself appears first in that century {cite:tdv-karagoz}.

## The karagözcü at work

Karagöz is in truth one artist's solo performance: he voices the whole cast — Karagöz the plain-spoken man of the people, thick-voiced and often in trouble, and Hacivat the fine-spoken Istanbul çelebi who suits his manner to every man — plus wives who never appear, a Gypsy witch-mother, daughters-in-law, and the regional and foreign types from the Kastamonu woodcutter and Bolu cook to the Acem, two kinds of Arab, Albanian, Rumelian, Jew, Greek and Frank, each entering to its own music {cite:tdv-karagoz}.

## The four parts of the play

Every play runs in four independent parts which each karagözcü reshapes to taste: the mukaddime, opened by the göstermelik figure on the screen, where Hacivat recites a semâi and the perde gazeli and the pair quarrel; the muhâvere, a talk on some theme stretchable at will, with "ara muhâveresi" scenes to lengthen it; the fasıl, the main piece, bearing the old titles — Ferhad ile Şirin, Leylâ ile Mecnun, Tâhir ile Zühre, Kanlı Nigâr, Hamam, Cazular, Kanlı Kavak — and many later ones; and the bitiş {cite:tdv-karagoz}.

## Night-players of the guild procession

Evliya's procession of the guilds gives the shadow-men a place among the pehlivan-entertainers: the Shebbazan — "night-players who play at night, representing les ombres chinoises" — and beside them the Khial-tassvirjian, night-players with painted figures performing with the magic-lantern, and the Küklabaz puppet-players {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}.
