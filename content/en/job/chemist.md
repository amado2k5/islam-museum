## Summary

The chemist worked the science the Arabic called al-kimya — among the Greeks' two derivations, from the Egyptian kemi, "black", the "black earth" of Egypt reckoned the science's homeland, or from khymeia, "smelting" — which passed into Latin as alchimia and thence to every Western tongue {cite:tdv-kimya}. Muslim writers also called their tradition ilm al-san'a, ilm al-tadbir, ilm al-hajar, ilm al-mizan and al-hikma, meaning that it was at once a theoretical science and a technique, bound to definite operations and a definite measure or balance {cite:tdv-kimya}. The craft came into Islam as the heir of Alexandrian old chemistry {cite:tdv-kimya}, was founded systematically by Jabir ibn Hayyan at Kufa and carried to its height by al-Razi {cite:tdv-kimya}.

## A science and its doubters

In the classical classifications alchemy is generally named among the natural sciences: al-Khwarizmi counts sina'at al-kimya among them for its tie to the mineral world and details its terminology; Ibn Sina lists ilm al-kimya among the sub-branches of the natural sciences beside medicine, astrology, physiognomy, dream-interpretation and the talismanic arts {cite:tdv-kimya}. Ibn Khaldun refuses it the rank of a natural science altogether: the traditional doctrine works by spiritual powers upon the natural world, he argues, so ilm al-kimya is a kind of magic practice with neither religious nor scientific validity {cite:tdv-kimya}. The Ottoman classifier Tashkoprizade still places it with the sub-group of the natural sciences, and Katib Çelebi records the attempt to tie its tradition to a prophetic origin — the claim that it was taught by Moses to Karun {cite:tdv-kimya}. The entry warns that what modern Turkish calls simya is not the same thing: simya names the art of illusion, and the old chemistry is properly called elkimya {cite:tdv-kimya}.

## The doctrine

The old chemistry the Muslims received rested on a nature philosophy in which all metaphysical and physical domains are bound together, the heavenly bodies act on earthly events and so on the formation of the minerals, and the chemist's own spiritual transformation runs parallel to the metals' transformation — the real aim being the perfection of his soul as of his metal {cite:tdv-kimya}. The most original mineralogical theory holds that all metals are formed of sulphur and mercury, to be understood not in today's sense but as cosmological principles in definite proportions, the chemist claiming to reduce a metal to its first matter and re-balance it into gold or silver {cite:tdv-kimya}. Muslim chemists took three attitudes: for men like Abu'l-Qasim al-Iraqi the spiritual transformation was the essence; for al-Razi the chemical transformation of the metals; and for Jabir and his school the metal-work was a support to the inner work {cite:tdv-kimya}.

## Jabir at Kufa

The first Muslim to take an interest in the Alexandrian science was the Umayyad prince Khalid ibn Yazid, who had old-chemistry books translated from Greek and Coptic and studied the science under Marianos (Morienos), the pupil of the Alexandrian chemist Stephanos {cite:tdv-kimya}. The founder of the systematic tradition was Jabir ibn Hayyan, born about fifteen years after Khalid's death, who pursued his work at Kufa by his own means, discovered minerals and acids, and so made possible many new compounds and chemical methods {cite:tdv-kimya}. He sorts matter into spirits that fly off when heated — camphor, arsenic, mercury and sal ammoniac — the seven metals including the Chinese "white copper" alloy, and the compounds that pound to powder {cite:tdv-kimya}. The earliest known recipe for producing nitric acid is his, and he gives formulas for making steel, refining other metals, dyeing leather and cloth, waterproofing fabrics, protecting iron from rust, and mordanting with alum; he also treats calcination, crystallisation, dissolution, sublimation and reduction in detail — and though called a kimyacı, he gave himself not to making gold from base matter but to the methods of the science {cite:tdv-kimya}.

## Al-Razi's laboratory

Abu Bakr al-Razi carried medieval Islamic chemistry to its peak: beside his work in medicine he opened a new period in organic and inorganic chemistry, and though he kept Jabir's terminology his experimental methods place him as the true forerunner of modern chemistry {cite:tdv-kimya}. In the Kitab Sirr al-asrar he classifies the chemical substances and pictures about twenty instruments of his laboratory — vessels, flasks, lamps, braziers and furnaces, ladles, tongs, strainers, the alembic, funnels and mortars — used in chemistry, pharmacy and metallurgy to the end of the nineteenth century {cite:tdv-kimya}. The Kitab al-Asrar describes distillation, sublimation, refining, amalgamation, solution, washing, calcination and precipitation in detail — some performed for the first time by him; he distinguished sodium carbonate from potassium, gave recipes for hydrochloric and nitric acids, found sulphuric acid, caustic soda and glycerine, and first produced formic acid by distilling ants {cite:tdv-kimya}.

## Today

The entry judges the old chemistry a meaningful and important bridge between the ancient world and modern times: the laboratory techniques and the experimental accumulation of the Muslim chemists played their part in the passage to modern chemistry, whatever the aims of the old doctrine {cite:tdv-kimya}. The chemist's name remains, but the science it serves has changed beyond the craft's recognition {cite:tdv-kimya}.
