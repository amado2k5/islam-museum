## Summary

Emir — amir, 'the one who commands' — was the commander and provincial ruler of the early Islamic state {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}. The word itself is Islamic: it does not appear in the Qur'an except in the phrase 'ulu'l-amr', but the hadith literature uses it widely {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}.

## A name for leader and governor

At the Saqifa the sources call the community's head-to-be 'emir'; under the Rashidun, army commanders — emir al-jaysh, emir al-jund — and the governors who had conquered their provinces alike bore the name {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}. In the earliest sources 'amil' runs as its synonym {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}.

## The Umayyad emir's writ

The Umayyads tried to separate fiscal from administrative duties, but in practice the emir held both, and thought himself invested with the caliph's powers in his own province {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}. Easterners called him kethüda or treated him as their shah {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}. He organised the army and appointed the 'arifs who kept its registers, discipline and pay; he led the prayer, built mosques, managed judicial affairs, usually appointing the qadis himself, and kept order through his sahib al-shurta {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}. Around him stood a bodyguard, a chamberlain (hajib), and a sahib al-barid who reported province affairs to the centre {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}. He supervised the mint and struck silver in his own name — some emirs were famous for their fine dirhams — though the caliph sometimes fixed type, weight and mints {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}.

## Money and loyalty

The emir set how and when taxes were gathered, reviewed the payroll, funded fortresses, roads, canals and bridges, and sent the revenue account to Damascus {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}. If the caliph appointed a separate 'amil for kharaj, the emir's powers shrank sharply — under Hisham the Egyptian 'amil Ibn al-Habhab could even replace the emir {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}. He received both salary and administrative advances, and when he left his province he named a khalifa to stand for him {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}. Some emirs enriched themselves by trade, confiscations and gifts, and caliphs called them to account {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}.

## Under the Abbasids

The Abbasids kept the Umayyad machinery but centralised it: emirs came mostly from the bureaucracy, with occasional Abbasid princes, and were drawn from Persians and later Turks rather than only Arabs {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}. The qadi now answered to the caliph directly, the sahib al-barid reported on the emir, complaints went to the new sahib al-nazar fi'l-mazalim, and terms of office grew short {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}. Ma'mun's appointment of his brother as emir of Egypt while the prince stayed in Baghdad began the practice of absentee emirs governing through deputies {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}.

## From office to dynasty

Then came emirs appointed by the caliph who paid only a fixed tribute and ruled freely — the Aghlabids and Tahirids founded dynasties, and the Tulunids, Ikhshidids, Samanids and Hamdanids put their own names in the khutba and on the dinars {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}. Strong men seized territory and then sought the caliph's confirmation for legitimacy — the Saffarids and Ghaznavids — and the Buyids went furthest, taking Baghdad itself and appointing viziers and even managing the succession of the caliphate {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}. In the west, the Umayyads of al-Andalus kept the title emir until Abd al-Rahman III took the caliphate {cite:tdv-emir-idareci}.
