## Summary

The flower-seller traded in the most perishable luxury of the market — cut blossoms for gifts, tables, weddings and graves — and held a full place in the guild procession and the street cries of two cities {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 252-253, p. 177; lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, pp. 318-319}.

## Two guilds of flowers

Evliya gives the flower trade two entries. "The Flower merchants are eight hundred, with three hundred shops. Their patron is Baba Reten" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 252, p. 177}. Beside them march "the Flower merchants, who at the same time sell fruit", three hundred persons with eighty shops, whose patron is Khabil Irmagham — "he used to bring baskets of flowers and fruit as presents to the Prophet and to his family", was girded by Ins Ben Malek, and is buried at Taif {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 253, p. 177}.

## The shop quarters

The shops of these merchants — the Jejekjian, formerly the Shukufehjian — stand "in the part of the town called Takt-ul-kala'a, A'k-serai, Sultan Mohammed, and Aya Sofiyah, before the gate of the Jebeh-khanah", and there "at all seasons of the year are found dishes full of flowers and fruits, which are offered as presents to the vezirs and great men" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 253, p. 177}. Flowers in every season meant buying, forcing or importing out of season — a trade run on the calendar as much as the stem {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 253, p. 177}.

## The procession of the koshks

In the great procession the flower-merchants "make a great show, being an exquisitely armed troop": they carry on poles koshks imitating the koshk of the muhtasib and of the fruit market, "wherein fine boys reclining on golden cushions do service" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 253, p. 177}. Behind them ride the bazar-bashi and the kyaya of the muhtasib, who calls merchants before him and "upbraids them for not having kept just weight and measure" — at the Alai-koshk, Evliya adds, some are punished with the bastinado, some dipped in honey, some mounted on jackasses: "these are the punishments of those who do not keep just measure" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 253, p. 177}. Even the petal trade stood under the scales.

## Cries of the Cairo streets

Lane heard the flower-sellers before he saw them. "A very singular cry is used by the seller of roses: 'The rose was a thorn; from the sweat of the Prophet it blossomed' — this alludes to a miracle related of the Prophet" {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, pp. 318-319}. And "the fragrant flowers of the henna-tree are carried about for sale, and the seller cries, 'Odours of paradise! O flowers of the henna!'" {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 319}. The same chapter's scene of the guest-tray — sherbet, coffee, pickles, and "a bunch of flowers stuck in a candlestick" — shows where the day's cut stock ended up {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. V, p. 150}.
