## Summary

The zelliji is the craftsman of zellij, the glazed-tile mosaic of Morocco, who trims glazed tiles into geometric pieces with a sharp steel hammer and lays them into pattern on floors, dados and fountains {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 pp. 21-22}. In Fez alone the craft numbered several hundred practitioners when Gallotti wrote {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p. 21}.

## The ringing hammers of Fez

Gallotti, quoted by Newcomb, pictures a street in Fez where the ground is strewn with little chips of enamelled terra-cotta — the debris of zellij — and from inside the courtyards rises 'the ringing noise of the tile-setters who trim their tiles with steel hammers' {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 pp. 21-22}. He counted several hundred wonderful practitioners in Morocco, and judged their art, though probably descended from the coloured stone and smalt mosaics of the Byzantines and first Muslims, so distinct that it must be described on its own terms {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p. 21}.

## An old industry

The art of the ceramic mosaicist in Fez is a very old one: Leo Africanus notes a brilliant ceramic industry in the century before his own, and Alfred Bel cites the Rawd al-Qirtas chronicler, writing about 1325, that fourteenth-century builders paid great attention to enamelled tiles, marble and carved work {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 pp. 21-22}. The Marinid madrasas of the fourteenth century, with the mosque and house revetments and the museum fragments that survive, show that the industry reached a considerable development {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 pp. 21-22}.

## The kinds of zellij

Newcomb classifies Moroccan tile decoration into ceramic mosaic and painted tiles; much of the mosaic is made of small squares, hexagons and other regular shapes whose pattern comes from colour variation, while a second variety is a true mosaic in which the tiles are cut and laid like marble or glass tesserae {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 pp. 21-22}.

## Cutting the inscription

The most delicate technique is the black tile inscription: the letters are laid out on whole glazed tiles, and the surface around them is cut through the enamel back to the terra-cotta with chisels or sharp hammers, leaving the inscription in black against the fired clay — work that requires extreme skill lest the enamel of the letters themselves be chipped away {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 pp. 21-22}. The background may then be enamelled a different colour or left plain {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 pp. 21-22}.

## Beyond Morocco

Zellij belongs to a wider Maghrebi and Andalusian world: from the fourteenth century the mosques of Tlemcen wore faience revetments, and the lustre-faience panels and inscriptions of the Mosque of Méchouar show evidence of Andalusian manufacture {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 pp. 19-20}. In Tunisia and Morocco a living industry survived into the twentieth century, and Saladin held that Morocco conserved the Andalusian tradition in its utmost purity {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p. 20}.

## Not yet covered

Newcomb describes the product and its history but not the workshop: no master-apprentice chain, no kiln records, no wages; how a boy learned to cut a star from a glazed square remains untold {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 pp. 18-22}.
