## Summary

The archer — okçu, kemankeş, tîrendâz, solak; Arabic râmī, nabbāl, nushshâb — held the oldest weaponised skill to be given a religious warrant in Islam: the Prophet glossed the Qur'anic command to prepare 'force' with 'shooting' (remy) and counted archery among the lawful games and a father's duties to his child {cite:tdv-ok}. Ibn Qayyim made archery one of the four branches of furusiyya; under the Ottomans it became a licensed discipline with its own register, records, prayer and pir {cite:tdv-ok}.

## A skill with a warrant

Some archery sources began the bow and arrow with Adam, taught by Gabriel to drive off the crows that ate his seed — whence the Arabic nushshâb — and the Old Testament already calls Ismail a good archer, a point the Prophet used to urge his shooting Companions to rival their forefather {cite:tdv-ok}. He said an arrow could carry three to paradise — its maker, its shooter and the one who hands it over — counted shooting his dearest sport, counted abandoning a learnt skill ingratitude, and called out to Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas at Uhud: 'Shoot, Sa'd — may my father and mother be your ransom' {cite:tdv-ok}. Because of this, the entry notes, archery took on a religious character and a special standing {cite:tdv-ok}.

## The weapon and its lore

Bows were of one piece or composite — wood, horn, sinew and glue in set ratios — and the Arabs used single-wood, double-wood and the horn-and-sinew reinforced kind, the last preferred by townsmen; Ibn Qayyim praised the Turkish bow above all {cite:tdv-ok}. Arrow-making was slow work: the wood was kiln-dried, rested in a dry store and worked to keep it straight, and the fletching — swan, kestrel, crow or pigeon — made it fly true; Ottoman arrows ran by purpose to tirkeş war arrows, puta target arrows, menzil record arrows, meşk and fire arrows and the Tatar patterns {cite:tdv-ok}. The shooter wore the zihgir thumb-ring and a wrist guard; the manuals on the manners of shooting, the ranges and the archers are called kavsnâme {cite:tdv-ok}.

## Licence and record

Ottoman archery was a discipline: to become a kemankeş — to take the icazet and enter the archers' register — a man had to hit the marks and cast the pişrev arrow past 900 gez and the azmayiş past 800, a rite called ahz-ı kabza, 'taking the grip'; the licensed archer was then tâlib-i menzil or müstahıkk-ı menzil, a candidate for the record, and breaking a menzil and raising its stone pillar had their own rules {cite:tdv-ok}. When the kemankeşler came to the ok meydanı for a match or an examination they recited a Fatiha and three Ihlas, invoked Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas — pir of the kemankeşler — with the expert shooting Companions, and prayed for the three great masters Abu Hashim al-Bawardi, Tahir-i Balkhi and Ishaq al-Raffa, the author al-Tabari of the Kitab al-Wadih, and the kemankeşler of the past {cite:tdv-ok}. Their contests ran to many branches: hitting a flying arrow, splitting a nock, shooting all ways at full gallop, casting farthest, threading a thrown or moving ring, piercing steel plate or layered elephant hide {cite:tdv-ok}.

## Evliya's bowmen

Evliya walked the convent of the bowmen on the Ok-meidan, where innumerable arrows were fixed around a domed shrine — every passing bowman leaving three as a remembrance — and watched the bowmen pass bending their munteha, ailan, sheddan and chain bows, shooting at weapon-points, bottles, mirrors and lamps and at targets of box and juniper, and casting wingless arrows into the air to catch them falling; the bowmen acknowledged Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas as patron and counted shooting 'an institution of the Prophet' {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}. He puts Istanbul's arrow-makers at 300 men in 200 shops and the bowyers at 500 in 200; the army's arrow-heads were made at Edirne, and the Cebeci Ocağı supplied the bows and arrows for the kapıkulu {cite:tdv-ok}.

## From bow to musket

Archery stayed a battle-arm until the musket spread: some four hundred kemankeşler ringed the sultan on the field, the solaks stood among his guards, and the navy carried chests of arrows — an Ottoman fleet list of 1478 names eighteen chests of arrows and 822 bows beside the guns — until the janissaries' drill-ground, the tâlimhâne at Okmeydanı, kept the training alive as heritage {cite:tdv-ok}. In the lands of Eastern Islam the mounted warrior was necessarily a proficient archer: the ruling classes wore ornamental thumb-rings to show they were archers, the crafts supplying them were highly esteemed, and sons of Ottoman sultans were apprenticed to bowyers — Mehmed III himself fashioned thumb-rings as gifts of honour {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979}.
