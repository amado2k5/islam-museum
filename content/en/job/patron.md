## Summary

The hāmi was the protector-patron of stateless Arabia — the hafîr, 'the people's protector', or hâmi'l-humeyyâ — who took a person, family or caravan under his himaye for passage, trade or refuge {cite:tdv-himaye}. With no central authority in Jahiliyya Arabia, protection was the institution that let the fairs and caravan roads run: the patron guaranteed, and was paid, in standing or in the toll called hakku'l-mürûr {cite:tdv-himaye}.

## The work of protection

Himaye — from hamy, 'to protect, to keep from harm' — named a person's, family's, clan's or tribe's guarding one another against attack; neighbouring terms did the same work: civâr (neighbourly protection), hafâre (protection for a stated price, by covenant), and tehaffür for the act of seeking it, while the grantor was the hafîr or hafîrü'l-kavm {cite:tdv-himaye}. Its cousins were the aman — the life-and-property security given to the foreigner entering the land or surrendering to the army — and the zimmet, the protected status the non-Muslim bought with jizya {cite:tdv-himaye}.

## Patrons of the road and the fair

With the tribes in mutual distrust, the merchant wanting a fair entered the himaye of a chief or eşraf of the tribes along the road and paid the hakku'l-mürûr before crossing their lands; patron and protected had to match in honour and lineage, and the profit of guarding a rich caravan could spark rivalry — the dispute over the Hira king Nu'man ibn al-Mundhir's caravan bound for the Ukaz fair in 585 ended in the Day of al-Nakhla {cite:tdv-himaye}. The Meccan merchants had himaye treaties with the tribes on the northern and southern roads; lone travellers needed the aman too, and the tribes boasted of those they sheltered as a measure of strength and standing {cite:tdv-himaye}.

## Patronage as honour

Jahiliyya verse already shows it: Amr ibn Kulthum boasts in his mu'allaqa of beating the tribal chiefs who protect the distressed, and of inheriting from his forefather Zelbürre the honour by which they are protected — no stronger keeper of honour, no more faithful to covenant {cite:tdv-himaye}. Sometimes the notables of one or several tribes swore pacts to protect the wronged against the wrongdoers: the finest was the Hilf al-Fudul at Mecca, sworn before the Prophet's mission and counting him among its witnesses {cite:tdv-himaye}.

## The first patrons of Islam

The Prophet lived under Abu Talib's himaye until his uncle's death, and re-entered Mecca from the Ta'if journey only under the protection of his kinsman Mut'im ibn Adi {cite:tdv-himaye}. Abu Bakr, leaving Mecca under persecution for Abyssinia, met Ibn al-Dughunna, who returned with him and declared his protection to the Quraysh chiefs — a himaye that held until Abu Bakr built a mosque beside his house and prayed openly; when the mushriks pressed Ibn al-Dughunna to withdraw it, Abu Bakr handed the protection back and took refuge in God's {cite:tdv-himaye}.
