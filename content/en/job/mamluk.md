## Summary

The mamluk — from the root mülk, "what is owned" — was the paid soldier picked from among captives and slaves, specially trained and taken into the ruler's guard, until the mamluks became an aristocratic class that seized political power itself {cite:tdv-memluk}. Elsewhere the same figure was called gulâm, in North Africa abîd, and in al-Andalus the Slav mercenary guard was the sakâlibe, 'mamluks' in meaning {cite:tdv-memluk}.

## The rise of the soldier-slaves

The Prophet's and the Rashidun's army was Arab; conquest brought non-Arab converts — Iranians, Copts, then Turks and Berbers — into the ranks, though the command stayed Arab and the mawālī chafed at second-class standing {cite:tdv-memluk}. Ubaydullah b. Ziyad settled a two-thousand-man Turkish unit at Basra in 674, and Qutayba b. Muslim's twelve thousand included some seven thousand non-Arabs, mostly Turks {cite:tdv-memluk}. The Khurasani forces that felled the Umayyads gave the early Abbasid army its best units for two generations, but al-Mu'tasim turned the practice into a system: to balance Arab and Persian he brought Turks from beyond the frontier, and the mamluks soon numbered thirty thousand with Turkish commanders, quartered in the new city of Samarra on broad iqtâ's with brides brought from the steppe so they would not mix {cite:tdv-memluk}. Within a short time every province had its ruler's mamluks — and the same troops that kept order for caliphs and governors began fighting the centre for independence {cite:tdv-memluk}.

## From regiment to dynasty

Ahmad b. Tulun, son of a mamluk of al-Ma'mun's service, founded the first Muslim-Turkish state in Egypt in 868 on the strength of his fellow mamluks; Muhammad b. Tughj al-Ikhshid took Egypt in 935 with eight thousand; and the Fatimids, toppling that state in 969, raised Turkish-only mamluk units from al-Mustansir's reign {cite:tdv-memluk}. From Saladin's time Ayyubid sultans and meliks built their retinues on mamluks, and in 1250 the mamluks took power in Egypt for themselves — the sultanate that lasted until Selim I in 1517, its beys' influence surviving until Muhammad Ali's purge of 1811 {cite:tdv-memluk}. The Turks' own dynasties had kept no slave regiments at first; the gulâm institution they adopted from the eleventh century was the mamluk system's twin, the equivalent in aim and office of the Ottoman kapıkulu soldier {cite:tdv-memluk}.

## The market and the school

The ideal recruit was a steppe youth — courageous, strong, tall, a pagan receptive to pure Islamic teaching who could fight for ghazi and shahid — and high prices were paid; fathers in some regions sold sons to the dealers and haggled hard, and the dealer's best-known title was 'hoja' {cite:tdv-memluk}. The slave-dealers, exempted from the taxes other merchants paid, brought the boys first to the great marts — the dâr al-raqîq of Fustat and Baghdad — then on to Egypt at the treasury's charge {cite:tdv-memluk}. A sultan's purchase entered the tābaka barracks-school of the Cairo Citadel: Sunni doctrine first, then the military curriculum under a discipline so strict the boy might not converse even with an emir; on finishing he was manumitted, issued his equipment and enrolled in the khāssa guard {cite:tdv-memluk}. When a sultan died or fell, his untrained küttâbiye returned to the bayt al-māl and the new sultan bought them again with the qadis witnessing {cite:tdv-memluk}.

## Master and barrack-mate

The pivot of a mamluk's life was his last purchaser and manumitter — the mu'tik, mevlâ, üstat or seyyid — to whom he owed loyalty till death; Baybars as sultan still paid deep respect to his own mevlâ Ay Tegin as an example to the corps {cite:tdv-memluk}. His barrack-mates in slavery and freedom were the huşdaşiye, bound in full solidarity — the senior 'ağa' watching over the junior 'ini', and the latecomer cold-shouldered as 'ecnebi' {cite:tdv-memluk}. Every mamluk bore a Turkish name whether Turk-born or not, the dealer renaming him at first sale; the language of the corps was Turkish and the state they raised was called Devletü Türk, the state of the Turks {cite:tdv-memluk}.

## An aristocracy of one generation

The mamluks formed an aristocratic class in Egyptian society by personal merit and military success — but a one-generation aristocracy: the rank began with being a mamluk and ended at death, and a mamluk's son, not coming from slavery, could not bear the name {cite:tdv-memluk}. Nor was Egypt the only seat: mamluks founded a dynasty of their own name in India, where the Delhi slave dynasty held power for close on a century {cite:tdv-memluk}. The article notes that the mamluks prized their station as a badge of selection — they did not resent the slave origin but counted the title a privilege and a nobility {cite:tdv-memluk}.
