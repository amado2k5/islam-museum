## Summary

The sirmakesh drew gold into wire and plied it into the threads — sirma and kilabudan — that bound embroidery, robes and regalia {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 215, pp. 162-163}. In Istanbul the craft had an imperial factory with its own inspector, its own repository, its place at the mint and its legend: the goldsmith Constantine who made the young prince Suleiman draw gold into five hundred fathoms of wire {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 158, 162-163, 165, 223}.

## The fifth inspector

The fifth of Istanbul's great inspectors governs the gold-wire factory (Sirma): an agha, a kyaya, a clerk and chaushes, all appointed by imperial diploma, in "a great fabric" near the gate Partnak at the bottom of Tevekelli Cheshmeh {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 162}. The corporation of the gold-thread makers (Sirma-keshan) consists of four hundred men employed in the different occupations of the manufactory; they adorn their shops with sirma and kilabudan "made in the form of nets" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 215, p. 162}. Beside them, the merchants of sirma and kilabudan are a hundred and fifty men with eighty shops, whose patron — girded by Ins Ben Malek — is buried at Rei {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 216, p. 162}.

## A prince and the draw-plate

The guild's founding story is the finest in the procession. Sultans Selim I and Suleiman were both brought up at Trebizond as apprentices in the art of the goldsmiths, and Suleiman's master — "the Greek, Constantine" — was still alive in Evliya's day {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 158}. Once, angered by the prince, Constantine swore to give him a thousand strokes; the boy's mother offered a thousand ducats in vain. The master then ordered the prince to draw his gold through the steel plate (haddeli) into five hundred fathoms of wire, and wrapped the five hundred gold-wires twice round Suleiman's feet — "in order to acquit himself of his oath," Evliya says, "this story is well known" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 158}. In gratitude Suleiman built the goldsmiths the fountain called Sakachesmeh, endowed with a mosque, a bath and an assembly-room {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 158}.

## Wire in the state

The thread ran through the machinery of empire. Istanbul keeps a repository for gold-wire (Amanet sirmakesh) among its state magazines {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 223}. At the mint — where the men "go and come naked" — the Kehleji wire-drawers are so called because they cut the wire into small pieces, working beside the joiners, wire-makers, coiners and weighers {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 165}. Even the couriers who carry the letters of the Ottoman victors wear "on the head a glory of gold-wire" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 146}.

## The patron of gold-wire

The Sirma-keshan claim Khaled son of Nassr son of Abd-ullah as their patron: his father Nassr is patron of the goldsmiths, Khaled invented gold-wire, was girded by Selman, and is buried at Mecca {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 215, p. 162}. As with every patron in the procession, this is the guild's own tradition — a pedigree of craft honour, not a dated record.

## Thread in the suq

The wire found its way into the goods of the ordinary market. In Lane's Cairo the common long pipe-stick, covered for three-quarters of its length with silk, is confined at each extremity "by gold thread, often intertwined with coloured silks" {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. V, p. 186}. The same plying that dressed a courier's head ran round the silk wrapping of a smoker's pipe.
