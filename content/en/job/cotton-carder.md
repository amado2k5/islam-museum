## Summary

The naddaf carded the suq's cotton: women brought him the raw bolls, and he returned a clean, even fleece ready for the spindle or the quilt {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXIV, pp. 76-77}. Two hisba manuals — Ibn al-Ukhuwwa's and al-Shayzari's — give the carders a chapter of their own, and between them they preserve the whole craft: the repeated carding, the seed and husk that must come out, the two classic frauds, and the rule of honourable dealing with the women who were the trade's customers {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXIV, pp. 76-77; shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 25, pp. 88-89}.

## Card until the seeds are out

The first rule is mixing: no new cotton with old, no red with white {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXIV, p. 76; shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 25, p. 88}. The cotton must be carded repeatedly "so that the black husks and broken seeds shall be removed"; if seed is left it shows in the weight, and if left in a jubba or blanket that is then washed and crushed it will cut the article — "people's clothes will suffer damage" {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXIV, p. 76}. Al-Shayzari puts the same rule with the carding box: they must not mix the finest cotton at the bottom of the box nor the pure cotton at its walls with anything else, and the seeds left in a jubba or a qaba will be nibbled by mice {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 25, p. 88}.

## The frauds of the pile

Two cheats have their own paragraphs. Some card the bad red cotton, lay it at the bottom of the pile and cover it with clean white — "this does not appear in the spinning" {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXIV, p. 76; shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 25, p. 88}. And the carded cotton must not be stored in cold damp places, for the damp increases its weight — a weight that vanishes when it dries; "that is fraud," says al-Shayzari, "which they all practise" {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXIV, p. 76; shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 25, pp. 88-89}.

## A trade of women

The carder's customers were mostly the women who spun at home, and the manuals write the rule accordingly. No one may hold the trade unless he is known for his chastity and honour — in Ibn al-Ukhuwwa's phrase, proved in the muhtasib's court to be honourable and of good character — "for their dealings are with women" {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy chs. XXXIV-XXXV, pp. 76-77}. Women must not be allowed to sit at the shop-doors waiting for the carding to be completed, nor may the carders converse with them {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXIV, p. 76; shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 25, p. 89}.

## Flax beside cotton

The manuals put the flax-spinners in the next chapter, and the rules rhyme. The finest flax is Giza's Egyptian — the soft leafy variety best, the short rough kind that snaps without warning the worst {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 26, p. 89; ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXV, p. 77}. No mixing good with bad, no Nablus flax with Egyptian, no Lower-Egypt fibre with Upper-Egypt or the Kuri variety; and some mix unspun cotton into combed flax — all of it fraud {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 26, p. 89; ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXV, p. 77}. The same shop-door rule applies {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 26, p. 89}.

## The craft behind the cloth

Every woven thread began in hands like these. Before the spindle there was the carder's bench, and before the carder's bench the rules that kept a woman's basket of raw cotton from becoming a lighter, redder or damper one on the way back {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXIV, pp. 76-77; shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 25, pp. 88-89}.
