## Summary

The mujallid gathered the quires, glued them and cased the book in boards and decorated leather {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 30-33}. His craft stood on the leather industry of South Arabia and al-Andalus, and a fine binding for a Qur'an could earn a binder two dinars {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 31}. ## Leather and boards

The binder's art received a great impetus from the ancient leather industry of South Arabia: Zabid was noted for its red kid-leather, San'a had thirteen tanneries working in 381 AH (991-92), and Ta'if produced the celebrated striped Cordovan leather that rivalled the best Morocco, as well as excellent bindings {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 30-31}. In Egypt, where wood was scarce, the boards were made of papyrus pulp; for the great mosque Qur'ans covers of ornamented wood were used instead, like the cedar-wood cover inlaid with a mosaic of ivory, bone and coloured woods preserved in Berlin {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 33-34}.

## The bookbinder-geographer

The geographer al-Muqaddasi, who practised his handicraft on his journeys, was proud of the fine bindings he made in the Yemen and of the rich reward they brought him — sometimes as much as two dinars for a volume of the Qur'an {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 31}. In Yemen the quires were glued together and the volumes cased with wheat-starch; when he enquired for the asphodel paste he knew from Palestine, the druggists knew nothing of it {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 31}.

## The named binders

Ibn al-Nadim in his Fihrist (987-88) names the famous bookbinders of the Abbasid capital: Ibn Abi'l-Harith, who worked in the library of al-Ma'mun, Shifa' al-Miqra' al-Ujaydi, Abu 'Isa b. Shiran, Dimyana al-A'sar b. al-Hajjam, Ibrahim and his son Muhammad, and al-Husayn b. al-Saffar; none of their bindings have survived {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 32}. For precious books the coverings matched: in 1174 Saladin sent Sultan Nur al-Din five Qur'ans, one bound in blue satin enclosed in sheets of gold with golden clasps, and a jurist of Damascus had an embroidered satin covering costing 4,500 dirhams prepared for the copy believed to date to 'Uthman {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 32}.

## The guild and the cover

In Turkey the bookbinders formed a recognised guild of their own, said to have been established under Sultan Bayezid II (1481-1512) {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 98}. A characteristic mark of Persian binding appears at Baghdad in 1407: a piece of perforated leather cut into an intricate pattern and superimposed on a ground of blue and gold {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 98}. On the covers of one manuscript of 838 AH (1434), believed made for Shah Rukh, as many as 550,000 impressions were made by the binder's tool, a work of two or even three years {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 99}. In Evliya's procession the Mujellidan are four hundred men with one hundred shops, their patron Abdullah Taymi {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 451, p. 218}.

## Painted and lacquered covers

When painted covers began to replace tooled or stamped leather is uncertain, but examples appear as early as the sixteenth century, and they became very common in Persia towards the close of the seventeenth {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 99-100}. The pictures were first painted on leather, but that was probably abandoned when the paint cracked; painted covers of papier-mâché on a thick layer of gesso are much more common, the subject often answering to the text — a warrior or battle scene for a Shah-nama {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 100}. Haji Muhammad Taqi of Isfahan designed such a lacquer binding in 1270 AH (1854) {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 100}.
