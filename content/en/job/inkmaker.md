## The craft

The inkmaker prepared the liquid writing material on which calligraphers, copyists and chanceries depended. Arabic calls it midâd or hibr, Persian siyâhî or zekab, and Turkish mürekkep — "compound" — because it is built from several ingredients {cite:tdv-murekkep}.

## Materials and recipes

The classic Islamic ink is soot (is) ink: lampblack produced by burning linseed oil, beeswax, naphtha or kerosene under controlled conditions, bound in a solution of gum arabic {cite:tdv-murekkep}. The prized gum was the Sudanese "cellâbî" grade, which dissolves in cold water overnight; recipes added saffron, alum, vinegar, oak-gall juice, rose water and many other adjuvants, beside iron-gall (mazı) inks and red, gold and silver writing fluids {cite:tdv-murekkep}.

## Manufacture

The simplified formula is soot + gum solution + pure water — but the craft is the pounding. Strained gum was placed in a stone mortar, soot added little by little, and the mixture beaten with a stone pestle for days: the calligrapher Necmeddin Okyay calculated about 500,000 strokes per mortar (older sources said 80,000) until the soot dissolved completely in the gum {cite:tdv-murekkep}. Four parts gum to one part soot was the standard: more gum gave a shiny but sluggish, crack-prone ink, less gave ink that rubbed off on the hand {cite:tdv-murekkep}.

## Where the work happened

Ottoman soot-burners kept ishâne workshops near the Tekfur Sarayı in Istanbul's Eğrikapı quarter, and Süleymaniye Mosque still preserves its "soot chamber" (is odası), where lampblack from olive-oil lamps was channelled by engineered air currents to a room above the Golden Horn gate {cite:tdv-murekkep}. Freshly made ink was "matured" by motion — hung on frequently opened hammam doors or on camels' saddles; the sixteenth-century calligrapher Revânî Receb Halife reportedly walked with ink bottles tied to his legs {cite:tdv-murekkep}.

## Selling to scribes

A proper mürekkepçi asked which script the ink was for, since sülüs, ta'lik and nesih each want a different consistency; ink was strained through felt (çuha) and diluted eight-to-tenfold to order {cite:tdv-murekkep}. Watery ink that wrote grey could be evaporated down, and a scribe's quick fix was a few drops warmed in the hollow of the left hand, which thickened by body heat {cite:tdv-murekkep}.

## Reputation

Soot ink is a suspension, not a solution: the particles hang in water on the gum, so the writing never fades and sits on the surface of sized (âharlı) paper where it can be scraped or licked away — the origin of the Ottoman phrase "one who has licked ink" for the literate {cite:tdv-murekkep}. By the Gülzâr-ı Savâb recipe the soot was even baked inside dough to draw out its oil; and the oldest Islamic manuscripts still shine with this ink today {cite:tdv-murekkep}.
