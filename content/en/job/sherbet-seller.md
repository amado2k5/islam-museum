## Summary

The sharbetlee made and sold the sweet cold drinks of the Islamic city — sugar-water, lemonade, violet and mulberry sherbets, raisin and licorice infusions — from a shop shelf of porcelain cups or from a glass flagon carried through the streets {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. V, pp. 150-151; ch. XIV, pp. 318-322}. In Evliya's Istanbul the trade fills a whole run of guild entries: the Sherbetjian proper, the khoshab-merchants, the shop-less sellers of sherbet and julap, the sellers of warm sherbet and of salep {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 182-187, 190, pp. 154-155}.

## The sherbets themselves

Lane catalogues what the Egyptians drank: the commonest sherbet is merely sugar and water, very sweet; then lemonade; then the most esteemed kind, a green sherbet boiled from a hard conserve of pounded violet-flowers and sugar; then mulberry and sorrel sherbets; and in the streets, zebeeb — an infusion of raisins — the strong infusion of licorice-root, and the drink of the locust-tree fruit {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. V, pp. 150-151}. At table the sherbet came in covered glass cups called kollehs, gilt-flowered in the better sort, set on a round tray under a cover of embroidered silk or cloth of gold, with a wide napkin of oblong form hung on the bearer's arm, more for display than for wiping lips {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. V, pp. 150-151}.

## The guilds of Istanbul

Evliya's procession lists the drink trade in ranks. The merchants of khoshab — seven hundred men in five hundred shops — lay out vases, plates and cups of China, Martaban and bronze filled with the juice of the fruits of every province: apricots of Bokhara, plums of Manisa, pears of Azerbeijan, sour cherries of Rodosto, peaches of Constantinople, made into khoshab with amber and musk, crying "Rest to the soul and blood to the body" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 182, p. 154}. The Sherbetjian proper are five hundred men in three hundred shops, their stalls dressed with thousands of China and Fayence cups of sherbets of rhubarb, roses, lemons, lotus, tamarinds and grapes, which they hand to the spectators {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 183, p. 154}. Three hundred more sell sherbet and julap without shops {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 184, p. 154}.

## Warm drinks for winter

The trade did not stop for cold weather. The issi-sherbetjian boiled the sherbet in their houses, then walked the streets like the almond-cream sellers, holding in one hand the little vessels, bakraj, on the fire and in the other the cups, selling warm sherbet through the winter {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 187, p. 155}. Beside them worked the salep-merchants — two hundred men without shops — whose "fox's cully" grows on the high mountains from Brussa's Olympus to Ararat, ground to powder, cooked with sugar like jelly, and cried as "rest for the soul, health for the body" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 190, p. 155}. All these warm-drink sellers share, in the guild's own telling, one patron: Mokabbil, girded by Hamza, who shortened the long winter days by cooking warm sherbet, cream, jelly, salep and julap for him, and was cut to pieces at the battle of Ohod {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 191, p. 155}.

## The famous shops

Evliya names names. The most celebrated sherbet-shop of the city was that of the Bedouin sherbet-merchant opposite the monument of Taj Zadeh, near the fountain of Mahmud Pasha — "in Arabia, Persia and Turkey there is no sherbet-merchant to be compared to him" — where vezirs and the first men of the Empire sent for their sherbet {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 183, p. 154}. The best shops were those of Teriakis, Feshnesh, the Imam, Darjinli-haji and Karanlilli dull at the Scutari landing; in Takht-ul-kala'a the sherbet of Bulbul Ermeni was the most refreshing, and at the flour-hall that of Arnaud Kassim, which in autumn "invigorates men to such a degree that they begin to dance" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 183, p. 154}.

## The street sellers of Cairo

In Lane's Cairo the sherbet left the shop and walked. The sharbetlee commonly carried in his left hand the glass vessel of a sheesleh filled with zebeeb, and in his right a large tin or copper jug of the same with several glass cups; some bore a round tinned tray on the head {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 322}. The erk-soosee — seller of the licorice-root drink — carried a red earthen jar on his left side, held by a strap and chain and his left arm, the mouth stuffed with palm-fibre, and two or more brass or china cups which he knocked together {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 322}. Between the ranked porcelain of the guild shop and the earthen jar of the street, the same trade served a whole city's thirst {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 182-184, p. 154; lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 322}.
