## Summary

The geographer described the earth and its climes; the Greek geographia — "the writing that tells of the earth" — was arabised as cughrafiya, al-Khwarizmi titled his book Surat al-ard, "the image of the earth", and al-Mas'udi rendered the science as qat' al-ard, "the cutting open of the earth" {cite:tdv-cografya}. The medieval geographers did not know the science as a sharply bounded discipline: its branches lay scattered through astronomy, cosmography, philosophy, mathematical and descriptive geography, and the books of travels {cite:tdv-cografya}. The craft arose as a science under the Abbasids in the eighth century, formed its two classical schools of Iraq and Balkh, and passed to the Ottomans by translation {cite:tdv-cografya}.

## The making of the science

Before Islam the Arabs held some geographical knowledge of Arabia and its neighbours and daily observations of the planets, sky and stars, which they used in travel, calendar-keeping and weather-forecasting {cite:tdv-cografya}. After the founding of the Abbasid state geography appeared as a science: the process began in 771 or 773 when an Indian delegation brought Sanskrit astronomical works to the Abbasid court, among them the Aryabhatiya, Brahmagupta's Brahmasphutasiddhanta (the Sind-Hind) and the Khandakhadyaka — and with the Pahlavi translations that followed, Sasanian maps and land-survey records stood ready as sources for the first Islamic geographers, while the Greek inheritance came through the Almagest, the Geographia, the Tetrabiblos and Aristotle's Meteorologica {cite:tdv-cografya}.

## The two schools

In the classical period of the ninth and tenth centuries two schools formed: the descriptive-geography school of mid-ninth-century Iraq and the Balkh school of tenth-century Central Asia, both under Greek influence {cite:tdv-cografya}. Under al-Ma'mun's patronage at Baghdad the scholars verified the geographical data of the Greek books and measured the earth to find the true length of a degree of arc — Ptolemy's figure of 66⅔ Arab miles being their target — and drew a world map they called al-Surat al-Ma'muniyya, which has not survived {cite:tdv-cografya}. Al-Khwarizmi drew a world map on the basis of Ptolemy's Geographia, also lost, and his Kitab Surat al-ard gives descriptions conforming to the Greek climate zones; Ibn Serabiyun (SuhraB) followed him in the Kitab Aja'ib al-aqalim al-sab'a {cite:tdv-cografya}. Among the geographer's companions in the mathematical work stand al-Farghani, sent by al-Mutawakkil to Fustat to supervise the great Nilometer completed in 861, Habash al-Hasib, and al-Battani, whose zij gives the obliquity of the ecliptic at 23°35′, sixteen minutes below Ptolemy, and lists 273 places with latitudes and longitudes {cite:tdv-cografya}.

## Books of roads and wonders

The geographical books went by many names — al-Masalik wa'l-mamalik, Kitab al-Buldan, Suwar al-aqalim, the Nuzhat al-mushtaq — for the writers had no single name for their science {cite:tdv-cografya}. The Balkhi school and its successors described the lands region by region after a systematic opening on the whole earth; the Hudud al-'alam divides its sixty-one sections between a preface, systematic geography, and country-by-country description {cite:tdv-cografya}. Kramers opens his survey in the Legacy of Islam with the tenth-century map: by far the greater part of the inhabited world the Greeks called the oikoumene then stood under Islamic government and civilisation, its inhabitants feeling themselves citizens of one vast empire of which Mecca was the religious and Baghdad the cultural and political centre {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}.

## The Ottoman geographers

Ottoman geography began under the influence of the Arabic and Persian books and spent the fifteenth century coming to know them; the earliest dated Ottoman geographical work is a translation of the astronomical parts of Ibn al-Wardi's Kharidat al-aja'ib made shortly after the conquest of Edirne in 1362, counted the first Ottoman geography book {cite:tdv-cografya}. Knowledge of place reached the Ottoman Turks through the Samarkand school by way of mathematical astronomy — the influence of the Samarkand observatory on Ottoman scholars was great, and Ali Kuscu carried the school to Istanbul {cite:tdv-cografya}. The sixteenth century both translated the Eastern works and wrote of the day's discoveries — the admiral's two world maps told the Ottomans of the discovery of America — and in the seventeenth century Ottoman geography reached its peak: an Ottoman author produced the first and only systematic geography of the Ottoman countries, and Ibrahim Müteferrika printed geographical books with illustrations in Istanbul in 1142 {cite:tdv-cografya}. Evliya Çelebi travelled for some twenty-five years from the age of twenty through nearly all the Ottoman lands, and through his works Ottoman geography climbed to its height {cite:tdv-cografya}.

## Today

The geographer's name is carried by the modern discipline, and the science the medieval writers practised under many names — descriptive, mathematical, the masalik books and the travel accounts — now stands as one university subject {cite:tdv-cografya}.
