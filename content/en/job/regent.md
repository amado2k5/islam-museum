## Summary

The nāib — from nevb, "to represent, to stand in for" — is the person who carries an office's responsibility in its holder's place for a time; sources also use khalīfa and vekīl for the same sense {cite:tdv-naib}. When the Prophet left Medina he left Ibn Umm Maktum, Abu Ruhm Kulthum b. Husayn or Muhammad b. Maslama as deputies, and the Rashidun kept the custom {cite:tdv-naib}. The Islamic states then stretched deputyship across governorships, judgeships, professorships and imamates, until the Seljuk divan nāib held the second seat in every office and the Mamluk sultan's nāibs ruled Egypt and Syria in his name {cite:tdv-naib}. In the Ottoman lands the nāib was the medrese-trained legist who sat for the kadı in the kaza; a dedicated school, Muallimhane-i Nüvvâb, opened in 1855 to qualify them, and the office ended with the abolition of the şerʿî courts {cite:tdv-naib}.

## Deputyship in the first centuries

Umar appointed Uthman b. Abi'l-As over Bahrayn, and since he was in Iran his brother Mughira — or Hafs — carried the office as his nāib {cite:tdv-naib}. Hisham b. Abd al-Malik named Maslama b. Abd al-Malik over the Jazira, Armenia and Azerbaijan in 107 (725-726), and Maslama sent Harith b. Amr al-Ta'i in his place, being busy with the Byzantine campaign {cite:tdv-naib}. Bayik Bey, appointed to Egypt to be removed from the centre, sent his stepson Ahmad b. Tulun as his nāib — and the Abbasid caliphs at times appointed a vizier's nāib in place of a vizier {cite:tdv-naib}.

## The deputy judge, professor and imam

When the qadi al-qudat office arose under Harun al-Rashid (786-809), judges sent to wide districts won the right to appoint their own nāibs {cite:tdv-naib}. A professor unable to teach also had a nāib: when al-Ghazali left the Baghdad Nizamiyya for Syria his brother Ahmad al-Ghazali took his chair {cite:tdv-naib}. Ibn Taymiyya criticised scholars who held high-salaried posts and had the work done by nāibs for little pay {cite:tdv-naib}. Imams too engaged nāibs to serve in their place {cite:tdv-naib}.

## The divan nāib and the sultan's nāib

Under the Ghaznavids Mahmud left Ahmad b. Yinal Tegin as his nāib in the capital after the Indian campaigns, and Sultan Mas'ud kept him in post {cite:tdv-naib}. In the Great Seljuk state the nāib held the second seat after the divan chief in every divan and its provincial branches, and a divan nāib who won the ruler's favour could overshadow the other dignitaries, even the vizier; the müşrif at the head of the Divan-i İşraf sent trusted nāibs to every province and city to keep the sultan's estates and revenues under watch {cite:tdv-naib}. Nizam al-Mulk advised paying nāibs a treasury salary so they would neither burden the people nor turn to unlawful gain {cite:tdv-naib}. Under the Ayyubids one member of the Divan al-mal was a nāib controlling business in the sultan's name, while the nāibs of Egypt and Syria wielded the sultan's own powers — they appointed and dismissed for him and consulted him only on the gravest matters — and in the Mamluk period the Syrian nāibs bore the title malik al-umara {cite:tdv-naib}. In India the term took the form nevab, the nawab {cite:tdv-naib}.

## The Ottoman kadı's nāib

In the Ottoman lands the nāib was an ilmiye man medrese-trained in fiqh to a level able to settle disputes in his own kaza; the kadı chose him and the kazasker of Anatolia or Rumelia confirmed him, and in Istanbul the kadı of Istanbul appointed {cite:tdv-naib}. Eighteenth-century Istanbul counted about twenty-five nāibs: at their head the Bab Mahkemesi nāib, the Istanbul kadı's chief deputy, then the court nāibs of the five courts of Mahmud Paşa, Ahi Çelebi, Balat and Davud Paşa, seven at Galata, seven at Eyüp and six at Üsküdar, plus itinerant nāibs — the ayak, kapan, çardak, yağ, keşif and avarız nāibs — who inspected the markets and settled guild disputes {cite:tdv-naib}. Kadıs posted to the Arab cities customarily picked their nāibs from the local Arab ulema, probably for command of the spoken dialects: Fazlullah of the Muhibbi family served as nāib to the Egypt kadı Esiri Mehmed Efendi in 1059 (1649) {cite:tdv-naib}.

## Pay and corruption

Nāibs were generally appointed sine die with periodic reshuffles, and exceptionally one might hold the same niyabet for twenty-five or thirty years {cite:tdv-naib}. His kadı could not remove him arbitrarily, nor did the kadı's removal or death end his term, but he was dismissed for inadequacy, ignorance, corruption or collusion with the ehl-i örf; the şeyhülislam Hamid Efendi ruled removal obligatory for a nāib who oppressed the people with the ehl-i örf, extorted money and divided inheritances wrongly for lack of the science of shares {cite:tdv-naib}. Court fees — for registers, hüccets, correspondence, marriage, divorce and manumission — were their livelihood, the nāib's share running at about a fifth or a sixth of the kadı's according to the kanunnames {cite:tdv-naib}. Excess in collecting fees drew repeated complaints and prohibition in the adaletnames, until the Second Constitutional reforms put the nāibs on fixed salaries instead {cite:tdv-naib}. Corruption grew worse when niyabets were farmed out by iltizam, and a nāib's misconduct hurt the kadı and kazasker he served: the people of Eskişehir complained of the arpalık nāib of the Mecca kadı Şeyh Mollazade and the arpalık was taken from him {cite:tdv-naib}.

## The school for nāibs and the end of the office

The Muallimhane-i Nüvvâb opened in 1855 under the şeyhülislam Meşrepzade Arif Efendi to train kadıs and nāibs for the Ottoman courts, becoming the Mekteb-i Nüvvâb in 1883, the Mekteb-i Kudât in 1908 and the Medresetü'l-kudât a year later {cite:tdv-naib}. With the abolition of the şerʿî courts under the Republic the institution of the nāib came to an end {cite:tdv-naib}.
