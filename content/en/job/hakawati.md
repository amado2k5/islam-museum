## Summary

The hakawati tells the tales: in the eastern Islamic literatures his name shifts with tongue and setting — kissahan (tale-teller), kıssa-gû, nakkāl, rûzehan, perdedârî, meddâh, şahnâmehan — all carrying the same or a neighbouring sense {cite:tdv-kissahan}. Among the Jahiliyya Arabs storytelling was already a special trade recounting the ayyam of the tribes, every tribe's poet, orator and soothsayer standing as its kāss {cite:tdv-kissahan}. After the killing of Uthman the religious tale-telling of the kussâs grew up in the mosques, drew the hadith scholars' objections, and over the centuries branched toward popular entertainment {cite:tdv-kussas} {cite:tdv-kissahan}.

## The storyteller's ancestors

In pre-Islamic Arab society the kisas that taught good conduct, warned against wrong and passed the time pleasantly were a special occupation; their subjects were mostly the intertribal wars called the ayyam al-Arab, and the whole body of reports was called akhbar {cite:tdv-kissahan}. Among the Turks the baksı or ozan of the nomad obas sang heroic tales to the kopuz — after Islam the same singers took the name âşık and yielded ground, especially in settled Anatolia, to the meddah {cite:tdv-meddah} {cite:tdv-kissahan}. In Iran the tellers of the old legendary tales, above all the Shahnameh stories they recited from memory, were called şahnâmehan; those who told in meddah style were nakkāl, the Karbala laments belonged to the rûzehan, and the sorrows of the ahl al-bayt told before a curtain belonged to the perdedârî {cite:tdv-kissahan}.

## From mosque to market

The Prophet gave wa'z and counsel a special place in his mission and sometimes told exemplary kisas in his sermons; the custom of telling kisas in preaching spread after the killing of Uthman, when the splitting community produced political factions whose agents spread fabricated reports and stories to win followers {cite:tdv-kussas}. The scholars, the muhaddiths above all, opposed the kussâs and set conditions: no kisas without the emir's permission, and stated qualities in the qāss himself; Ibn al-Jawzi's Kitab al-Qussas wa'l-mudhakkirin stands as the standing witness of the debate {cite:tdv-kussas} {cite:tdv-kissahan}. The first kissahans who read na'ts, münacats and praise-poems for rulers at gatherings were called muarrif; the palace tellers who recounted the Prophet's life, the Companions' wars and the conquests were called siyerci — and as these drifted toward secular matter they, too, came under the kissahan name {cite:tdv-kissahan}.

## The meddah

Meddâh comes from the Arabic root medh, "the one who praises"; the Ottomans used it in place of kıssahan and şehnamehan {cite:tdv-meddah}. The craft's own genealogy reaches to Hassan ibn Thabit, widely held the first meddah for praising the Prophet in verse, and to Suhayb al-Rumi — Evliya says his real name was Abdülvehhâb Gâzî, that he read the Anternâme even in the Jahiliyya and recited the campaigns of Uhud, Mecca and Hunayn before the Prophet, who rewarded him, and that his tomb is at Sivas; Kâşifî counts the meddahs of the Prophet's line among the highest-ranking communities {cite:tdv-meddah}.

## The teller at work

The meddah performed before the crowd with imitations: as the kissahans drifted from religious matter they worked the voices and movements of the animals and characters of their tales, and some admitted the freer anecdotes and jokes, while others kept to praising the Prophet, his family, the holy men and the rulers — and were still called meddah {cite:tdv-kissahan}. The kissahan's stage was wide: the name itself, kıssa plus Persian khân (reader/teller), marked the teller of tales among the people, in the konaks and in the palaces {cite:tdv-kissahan}.
