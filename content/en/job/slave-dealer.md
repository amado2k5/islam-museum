## Summary

The slave-dealer traded men, women and children in the slave markets of the Islamic city — jallāb and nakhkhās in the Arabic of the brokers, esirci in Ottoman usage, where "esirci, esir tüccarı, esir pazarı, esirciler şeyhi" name the trader, the merchant, the market and the guild head {cite:tdv-esir} {cite:tdv-simsar}. This was a real and regulated trade of the pre-modern city, described here as the sources describe it: an institution ended, recorded for the record {cite:tdv-esir}.

## What was sold

The captives who fed the markets were the product of war and raiding in a world where every people enslaved: the ancients killed captives or worked them, the Romans auctioned war prisoners by the hundred thousand, and the Arabs before Islam ransomed, exchanged or kept captives as slaves {cite:tdv-esir}. The Quran contains no verse commanding enslavement, and the jurists' treating captives as eligible for it rested on the international custom of the age; the Prophet applied slave status in a narrowly limited way to women and children and enslaved no adult man {cite:tdv-esir}.

## Rules of the trade

Al-Shayzari's chapter on slave and animal traders makes integrity the first condition: the dealer answers for other people's male and female slaves and is sometimes alone with them in his house, so he must be trustworthy, reliable and known for respectability {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley}. He may not sell a slave unless he knows the seller or can find someone who does, and he writes the seller's name and description in his register in case the person sold is free or stolen; a mother may not be parted from her child under seven, and no slave may be sold to a dhimmi {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley}. A buyer may look at a woman's face and palms, and at what lies above a man's navel and below his knees, before the sale; only after the contract may a man see the whole body of a female slave {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley}.

## The market and its keepers

In the Abbasid and Mamluk city the slave market could be a charge apart from the hisba: al-Sarakhsi under al-Mu'tadid held "the hisba, the inheritances and the slave market", and a Buyid amir was likewise entrusted with hisba and the slave markets, while the Mamluk nazir al-aswaq held jurisdiction over the horse and slave markets {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley}. Under the Ottomans the trade kept its own broker, the esirci dallâlı, counted by Halaçoğlu among the specialised dellals of the bedestans — and their abuses were known, for the esirci dallals were the ones who most often cheated sellers {cite:tdv-dellal}.

## The dealer in Cairo

In Lane's Cairo a slave-dealer told him that young Egyptian girls were sometimes sold into slavery from other countries by a parent or relative and handed to him for sale — taught to expect rich dresses and luxuries and instructed to say they had been carried off at three or four and could speak only Arabic {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. Lane records the trade as a working Cairo institution of his day, dealers with shops and households, some risen and some ruined {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.

## End

Slavery stood on the international custom the jurists describe; the law of the book is, in the entry's own conclusion, that the Islamic ruling on captives is release for nothing or for ransom, and with the abolition of slavery the trade has ended — there are no lawful slave markets, and the job is extinct {cite:tdv-esir}.

The sources describe the trade's rules, markets and brokers but not the dealers' own numbers, guild registers, prices by period and port, nor the lives of the traded themselves beyond the legal frame {cite:tdv-esir} {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley}.
