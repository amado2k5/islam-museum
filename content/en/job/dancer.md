## Summary

The dancer worked the feast and the open square: in Egypt the women of the Ghawazee tribe who danced unveiled in the public streets, and in Ottoman Istanbul the çengi and köçek — the women dancers and the boys dancing in female manner — who shared the meydan with the players of the orta oyunu {cite:lane-modern-egyptians} {cite:tdv-orta-oyunu}. The trade ran in family and company: the Ghawazee were a tribe whose musicians were mostly of their own people, and Evliya counts the Istanbul dancers among twelve named companies of boys, all called çengi {cite:lane-modern-egyptians} {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}.

## The Ghawazee

Egypt's public dancing-girls were most famously of a distinct tribe called Ghawazee — a female a Ghâziya, a man a Ghâzee, though the plural usually means the women {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. They danced unveiled in the streets, their manner a very rapid vibrating motion of the hips, quickening with the brass castanets as the performance went on; the dress was that of a middle-class woman in the hareem — yelek or ʿanteree and shintiyân — with kohl on the eyes and henna on the hands and feet {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. They were accompanied by musicians mostly of the same tribe on the kemengeh or the rabâb with the târ, or the darabukkeh with the summârah or semr, the târ usually in an old woman's hands {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.

## Work and ban

The Ghawazee performed in the court of a house or in the street before the door at hareem festivities — a marriage, a birth — though never admitted inside a respectable hareem, and sometimes hired to entertain a party of men {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. They called themselves Baramikeh, claiming descent from the Barmakid family of Hârûn er-Rasheed's court — Lane doubts the right — and in many ancient Egyptian tombs women are shown dancing at private entertainments in a similar manner, so the article reasons the way of dancing is probably of very remote antiquity {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. Lane's notes record the government's prohibition of public dancing and prostitution in Cairo in June 1834, which drove the tribe's trade to Upper Egypt, where the dancers kept their profession on the Nile {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.

## The çengi and köçek

In the Ottoman performance world the dancers stood inside the orta oyunu itself: its dance element belonged to the çengi — the woman dancer — the köçek, young male dancers imitating women, and the curcunabazlar, coarsely and comically dressed types who came out and mimicked the dancers clumsily to make the people laugh {cite:tdv-orta-oyunu}. Karagöz drew the same arts into the shadow screen, the article notes — çengi and köçek dances among them {cite:tdv-karagoz}.

## Evliya's twelve companies

At Constantinople's feasts of imperial circumcision, nuptials or victory, two or three hundred singers, dancers, comics and mimics crowded together and played day and night; at some private weddings they gained a thousand piastres in a night, collecting the money in the half-drum after each dance {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}. Evliya divides them into twelve companies — kols — of boys: the three thousand Parpid of Balata, mostly Gypsies; the dancing boys of Ahmed's company; Osman's four hundred "excellent mimics" with no Gypsy among them; Armenian, Greek and Jewish companies of the Seven Towers and Galata; and the twelfth of Semürkash, two hundred Jewish boys who were tumblers, fire-eaters and cup-players asking more than a hundred piastres a night {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}. "These twelve companies of boys, who are called Chengi," he writes, vie with each other in the most comical scenes, dressed in gold stuff as they pass under the Alai kiosk {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}.

## Standing

The dancer's standing was low and contested: the Cairo government banned public dancing in 1834, the respectable hareem refused the Ghawazee admission even while hiring them at the door, and the köçek's art shared the orta oyunu's decline before the Western stage {cite:lane-modern-egyptians} {cite:tdv-orta-oyunu}. Evliya's companies nonetheless held their place in the city's feast economy — the crowd, he says, could barely contain itself as they passed in gold before the Alai kiosk {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}.
