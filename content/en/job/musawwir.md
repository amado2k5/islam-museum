## Summary

The musawwir or naqqash painted the miniatures of manuscripts and decorated their pages in colour and gold {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 1-14, 97-98}. The painter's standing in the Muslim world rose and fell with the book: where the calligrapher was honoured, the painter worked in his shadow, yet many painters proudly added the appellation mudhahhib, worker in gold, to their names {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 97}. In Evliya's Istanbul the painters (Nakkashan) march a thousand strong, while the portrait-painters are twenty men with four shops and no legal patron {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 448, 457, pp. 217-219}.

## The early painters

The history of miniature painting in Islam begins with the Manichaean miniatures, precious relics of which were brought back by the German expeditions, and the fragments of early Arab miniatures that survived the catastrophes of the period {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 1-6}. In early manuscripts the miniaturist had already seized upon religious subjects {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 1-6}.

## The workshop of specialists

Towards the end of his reign, in 1522, Shah Isma'il conferred on Bihzad the post of director of the royal library, with control over all the copyists, painters, gilders, margin-drawers, gold mixers, gold-beaters and washers of lapis lazuli {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 75}. In the elaborate pictures of the Mughal school, upon which more than one painter used to work, one artist sketched the design and left the filling-in of the details — especially the painting of the faces — to a colleague {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 98}.

## Bihzad and his pupils

Bihzad's first patron was Mir Ali Shir, the minister and confidant of Sultan Husayn Mirza Bayqara, who ruled in Herat from 1468 to 1505; when the Uzbeg Muhammad Shaybani conquered Herat in 1507, Bihzad probably passed, like all the other employees of the palace, into the hands of the new master {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 74-75}. An account of 1521 records his pupil Darwish Muhammad Naqqash, first a maker of oil-colours, who attached himself to Bihzad, acquired a taste for drawing and painting, and was entrusted by the master with his own work {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 pp. 75-76}.

## The guild and the forbidden image

In Evliya's procession the painters (Nakkashan) fill the upper story of the lion-house with cells and a hundred shops besides, a thousand men in all; their litters carry works of the most famous painters, Shahkuli, Delijan, Behzad among them {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 448, pp. 217-218}. The portrait-painters (Nakkashan Musaviran) are twenty men with four shops: they have no legal patron, Evliya says, because portrait-painting is prohibited by their law — yet they paint the battles of the heroes of Islam on standards and adorn their shops with figures of the heroes of the Shah-nameh {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 457, pp. 218-219}. Beside them march the painter-fortunetellers, whose pictures served the passers-by for soothsaying in comical verses {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 458, p. 219}.

## The painter's rank

Of the craftsmen who took part in the preparation of a manuscript, the calligrapher was held in highest esteem; next in dignity came the decorators of the written page, and among these the mudhahhib, the worker in gold, appears to have been highly esteemed {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 97}. Many painters, when they sign their names to a picture, add mudhahhib after it, an appellation clearly intended to imply a claim to respect, and the historian often makes special note of the fact that a painter was at the same time a worker in gold {cite:arnold-grohmann-islamic-book-1929 p. 97}.
