## Summary

The jabban dealt in cheese and the rest of the dairy shelf — curds, cream, butter — between the cask, the frying-pan and the shoulder-bag {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVI, pp. 87-88; evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 140-144, pp. 142-143}. The hisba books set the rules for how cheese may be kept and fried; Evliya's procession shows the dairy guilds of Istanbul and the guild's own story of where cheese began {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 20, pp. 77-79; evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 140, p. 142}.

## The cask rules

Al-Shayzari's chapter on the sellers of clarified butter — who also handled cheese — lays down what may not be sold: "they are not permitted to sell any unsold cheese in casks, nor fat and oils which have gone rancid, putrefied or become maggot-infested because of the harm they cause to people" {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 20, p. 79}. The same passage hands the inspector a test aimed at the dairy shelf: oil in which cheeses have stood in casks "is greasy and crackles and spits when held over the flame of a lamp" {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 20, p. 78}. The cask that kept the cheese could betray the oil.

## Fried cheese, fresh oil

Ibn al-Ukhuwwa's chapter on the small traders watches the cheese-friers specifically: "only fresh sesame-oil may be used for cooking it", and toasted cheese "must be drained of all" fat {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXVI, p. 88}. It is the same hisba logic as the milk rules — the cheapest fraud is always the old oil, the old stock, the water added back.

## Abraham's white cow

In Evliya the cheesemongers — the Pemrjian, whose numbers he does not know — claim the oldest patronage of any dairy trade: "their patron is Abraham, who when he staid at Aleppo had a white cow, which was called Thuresh-sheheb. He then made all sorts of cheeses and other productions of the dairy, such as butter, curds, cream, and cheese, with which he treated his guests" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 140, p. 142}. From the name of that white cow, the guild says, the town is called Haleb-esh-shehba — haleb signifying to milk, shehba white; the place where Abraham milked her and gave the milk to the poor "is shown within the mosque of the interior castle underneath the minber", a stone vessel that kept filling itself by his blessing {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 140, p. 142}. In the Prophet's time the patron was Zeid Kaissari, girded by Ins Ben Malek {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 140, p. 142}.

## Cream, curds and the Albanian bags

The neighbouring guilds fill the dairy shelf. The cream-merchants, the Kaimakji, are one hundred men with forty shops, sharing the patrons Abraham and Zeid Kaissari; "they spread over their kaimak odoriferous herbs, and pass by distributing it amongst the spectators" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 141, pp. 142-143}. The curd-merchants keep their establishment below the mosque of Sultan Ahmed, with a hundred private establishments, the greatest part at Eyyub, where on the third day of the Bairam many thousand men gather to eat kaimak and yoghurt {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 143, p. 143}. And the merchants of Telmeh Penir — "for the greatest part Albanians" — fill white bags with the cheese and carry them on their shoulders, crying "Telmeh-penir": "it is so fresh and sweet a cheese, that if mingled with honey and laid on bread a man may eat of it, God knows, till he dies" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 144, p. 143}. All these corporations, Evliya adds, are reckoned yamaks — assistants — to the butchers, "because their business originates with cattle" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 144, p. 143}.
