## Summary

The fakihji sold fruit from shop and market stall — the trade al-Shayzari polices with the greengrocers: washed produce, cut roots, no worm-eaten melon {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 40, pp. 133-134}. In Evliya's Istanbul the fruit-merchants were three thousand men in a thousand and seven shops, and their procession display — fruit-trees on poles, fountain koshks, ships of fruit towed by a thousand men — was among the loudest of the whole parade {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 249, pp. 175-176}.

## Clean produce

The muhtasib should order the greengrocers and sellers of vegetables to sell their produce washed of manure and free of grass and weeds {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 40, p. 133}. He should instruct them to cut the roots off lettuce and radishes, and forbid them from washing fresh onions and garlic, because the water increases their pungency and offensive smell {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 40, p. 133}. If any vegetables remain overnight in their shops, they must not mix them with the fresh ones picked during the day {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 40, pp. 133-134}.

## No worm-eaten fruit

The muhtasib should forbid them from selling any melon, cucumber or fresh figs which are worm-eaten, and anything which is so over-ripe that its skin has split {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 40, p. 134}.

## The fruit-merchants' guild

In the procession before the sultan, the fruit-merchants (Yemishjian) were three thousand men with a thousand and seven shops; their patron is the same as the grocers', Azi Ibn-en-nebadi {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 249, p. 175}. They passed on wagons adorned with all kinds of fruits {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 249, p. 175}.

## Trees, fountains and ships of fruit

The fruit-merchants made artificial trees of apples and apricots, each carried by eight or ten men on poles; others made koshks with fountains playing, the four sides festooned with fruit, their boys seated inside bargaining with the spectators and throwing fruits to them {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 249, pp. 175-176}. Some dressed in robes made of chestnuts, holding rosaries of oranges and apples, reciting verses on the fruits {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 249, p. 176}. They also built artificial ships full of fruits, each towed by a thousand men, the nails, masts, head and stern ornamented with the kernels of fruits; merchants flocked in crowds to enter the fruit-ships and fill their baskets {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 249, p. 176}. With the greatest noise and quarrelling arising from these simulated sales they passed the Alai-koshk — a faithful representation, Evliya writes, of what occurs at the port on the arrival of every fruit-ship, where such noise arises and many heads are broken without the injured persons being allowed to ask for legal satisfaction; the emperor delighted and laughed at their quarrels and riot {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 249, p. 176}.

## Melons by the landing-place

The merchants of water-melons (Menawian) were three thousand men with but three hundred shops, for the greater part sold at the landing-places where melons and water-melons were heaped up in mountains; their trade lasted but six months, and they passed on wagons amongst heaps of melons crying 'Three occas for one asper' {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 251, p. 177}. The gardeners inside the town who supplied the fruit were three thousand, and the gardens one thousand, producing the famous peaches that weighed an hundred drachmas and the pomegranates sold two for a ducat as presents for the emperor {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 250, pp. 176-177}. At Top-khanah and Findikli the fruit-shops were famous for their elegance, shaded by large trees {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 61}.

## The fruiterer of Cairo

In Lane's description of a Cairo market street, the fruiterer and the dried-fruit seller stand among the shops of the 'attar, the tobacconist, the oilman with butter, cheese and honey, the greengrocer, the butcher and the farrani {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}.
