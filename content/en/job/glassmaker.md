## Summary

The glassmaker (zajjāj, Ottoman camcı/camger) melts silica sand with potash or soda and lime at 1500-1600°C and shapes the molten gather by blowing, casting or cutting {cite:tdv-cam}. Islamic glassmaking descends from Sasanian and Byzantine traditions, then produced its most impressive works in the gilded and enamelled mosque lamps of the Mamluk period {cite:tdv-cam}.

## Material and technique

Glass is made by melting silica sand with potash or soda, lime and sometimes other additives: fully molten at 1500-1600°C it flows, at 1100-1200°C it shapes easily, and fully cooled it becomes hard and breakable {cite:tdv-cam}. Its natural counterparts are rock crystal and obsidian, and the finest colourless kind of crystal, "necef", inspired and was imitated by glassmakers across the ages {cite:tdv-cam}. The blowing technique — a gather of hot glass taken on the end of the blowpipe, turned and blown — was discovered in Syria in the mid-first century BC and gradually took over the whole industry {cite:tdv-cam}.

## Islamic glass

Early Islamic glass bears Sasanian influence; over time more original forms and decorations emerged, clear in Abbasid and Fatimid glass {cite:tdv-cam}. In the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries Mamluk sultans ordered gilded and enamelled mosque lamps from Aleppo, Damascus and Raqqa — among the most impressive examples in world glassmaking {cite:tdv-cam}. Anatolian Seljuk pieces (a dish bearing the name of Kaykhusraw II) recall the Mamluk types {cite:tdv-cam}. Crusaders carried Islamic glass west, where it won great admiration; after Timur's invasion around 1400 Islamic glassmaking declined while Venetian glass advanced {cite:tdv-cam}.

## The Ottoman Beykoz workshops

The Ottomans' bright age of glass came with Bohemia's rise in the nineteenth century: workshops were set up around Beykoz in Istanbul producing glass of many kinds, the first founded by the Mevlevi dervish Mehmed Dede, who had learned the opal technique in Venice and returned under Selim III (1789-1807) {cite:tdv-cam}. The Takvîm-i Vekāyi of 1263/1847 mentions a crystal factory founded by the Bursa governor Mustafa Nûri Paşa at İncirköy on Sultan Mustafa waqf land; at his request its management passed to the Darphâne-i Âmire under the mint director Tâhir Efendi {cite:tdv-cam}. Beykoz's most common forms were the gülâbdan, ibrik, vase, lâledan, daldırma, bird, sugar-bowl, cup and plate; Beykoz-work prayer beads were also made {cite:tdv-cam}.

## Beykoz types and çeşmibülbül

The best-known colourless glass is the cut "maydanozlu" type, gilt-decorated with parsley-leaf motifs; the coloured kinds run cobalt blue, violet and dark blue {cite:tdv-cam}. Opal glass is made by adding calcined bone ash and tin oxide to the batch, the degree of opacity set by the amount and the length of the process {cite:tdv-cam}. Çeşmibülbül is made by dipping the gather (the glass mass on the pipe) into a mould lined with coloured glass rods, blowing so the rods stick to the hot glass, then dipping and turning and blowing again {cite:tdv-cam}.

## Revzen stained glass

Revzen-i menkūş — today's stained glass — is the decorated fixed window glass seen on the inner faces of mosque walls {cite:tdv-cam}. In the Ottoman method the window's shape was drawn on a board laid flat, the coloured glasses cut slightly larger so plaster could grip their edges, the areas that would become plaster lattice filled with clay, and liquid plaster poured to form the lattice holding the glass edges {cite:tdv-cam}. The Süleymaniye mosque and imaret construction registers name glass masters (camgerân) among the artisans working in 1557 {cite:tdv-cam}.
