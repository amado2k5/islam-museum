## Summary

The khudari sold vegetables — from a market shop, or from the hawker's back through the quarters. The sources honour him with the producers: in Evliya's Istanbul procession the vegetable-sellers marched immediately after the divines, because bread, the column of faith and the support of life, is the result of their labours {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 60, pp. 121-122}.

## The five hundred Sebzehvatj

The vendors of vegetables (Sebzehvatj) were five hundred men and shops; they sold in their shops all kinds of vegetables, and in passing threw on the spectators fresh cucumbers, carrots, parsley and the like {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 60, p. 121}. They shared the patron of the gardeners, Baba Reten, and marched with an incredible noise together with the peasants, the gardeners and the grafters {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 60, pp. 121-122}.

## The Terekehjibashi

Their head was the Terekehjibashi, appointed from the Bostanjibashi's department as chief of the bakers and the greengrocers together; a clerk provided with an imperial diploma was attached to him {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 180, p. 154}. In the public procession the chief walked with the head of the bakers while his deputy (kyaya) walked with the greengrocers, who ornamented their shops with wreaths of parsley, salad, celery, cabbages, cucumbers and aubergines and threw them amongst the crowd {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 180, p. 154}.

## Spinach and the Scala

The spinach-merchants (Ispanakjian) formed a sister guild of five hundred men with four hundred shops; their great establishment was at the Scala, the landing-place where the public repository of vegetables stood, and their chief was likewise Baba Reten the gardener {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 180, p. 154}.

## The Cairo khudari

In Lane's Cairo the greengrocer (khudari) stands in the market-shops list between the oilman and the butcher {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 316}. Bread, vegetables and a variety of eatables are carried about for sale, and the cries of some of the hawkers are curious: the seller of tirmis (lupins) often cries "Aid! O Imbabee!" — understood both as an invocation to the sheykh El-Imbabee, the celebrated Muslim saint buried at Imbabeh on the west bank of the Nile, where the best tirmis grows, and as implying that its excellence comes through the saint's aid {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 317}. Other cries of the same seller: "The tirmis of Imbabeh surpasses the almond", and — heard mostly in the country towns — "O how sweet the little offspring of the river", alluding to the tirmis's soaking for two or three days to deprive it of its natural bitterness {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, pp. 317-318}.

## Not yet covered

The sources give the guild census, the procession order and the street cries, but no market rules for vegetables — weighing, wilting, watering to freshen — and nothing on the village growers who supplied the stalls {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 60; lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV}.
