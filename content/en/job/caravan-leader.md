## Summary

The caravan leader — the kervancıbaşı of the Ottoman roads — marched a train of pack animals carrying trade goods and travellers between distant places; the word kervan itself is Persian kâr-bân, "one who manages the work", and the Arabs had said 'îr and seyyâre before kāfile prevailed {cite:tdv-kervan}. His craft was the road: the wells, the halts, the watch and the square of goods {cite:tdv-kervan}.

## The oldest trade trains

The first known regular caravan business ran in the age of the Assyrian trade colonies, about 1900-1750 BCE: donkey trains moved between Assur and Kanesh under the protection of the city-states on the route, paying their passage dues, carrying tin, heavy cloth and luxuries out and gold, silver, copper, wool and half-worked goods back {cite:tdv-kervan}. The camel spread in caravan work around 1200 BCE and nearly halved the cost of transport {cite:tdv-kervan}. The Ishmaelite camel-drivers who found Joseph carried balm, gum and myrrh from Gilead to Egypt, and the queen of Sheba's gifts travelled in a great caravan {cite:tdv-kervan}.

## The leader's art

A caravan divided into heavy and light: in a heavy train each camel bore five or six kantars, about 250 kilograms, in a light one about three; a thousand camels made a middling caravan, and Saharan trains of twenty thousand are recorded {cite:tdv-kervan}. Camels were strung nose to tail in groups of seven to twenty by thin cords made to part quickly under attack, with a bell hung on the last — its silence warned that a cord had broken {cite:tdv-kervan}. In the open desert guides called tekşif, masters at finding water, were hired; some countries chose blind men for the sharpness of their senses {cite:tdv-kervan}.

## Guard and breastwork

Caravans had to reckon with nature and brigands: they carried armed guards, sometimes professional soldiers, stayed awake through night marches against raids, and kept watchmen who warned one another at intervals by drum {cite:tdv-kervan}. By day a bayraktar rode behind the caravan leader bearing a flag he waved to summon the musketeers when danger came; at halts the bales were unloaded and stacked into a four-cornered breastwork for defence {cite:tdv-kervan}. The safety of caravans lay on the states they crossed, and harm to a train could start a war — the blocking of the Kanesh-Assur road by the king of Mama brought war with Kanesh, and the plundering of a 450-man Mongol caravan at Otrar in 1218 became the nominal cause of the fall of the Khwarazmshahs {cite:tdv-kervan}.

## Quraysh and the hajj road

The Quraysh chiefs, honoured as guardians of the sanctuary, arranged trading privileges with the Byzantine, Sasanian, Abyssinian and Yemeni courts in 467; their merchants went to Yemen and Abyssinia in winter and to Palestine, Syria, Egypt and Iraq in summer {cite:tdv-kervan}. Hajj caravans sometimes travelled with trade caravans; their numbers ran from five or six thousand to sixty or seventy thousand, and when the Cairo caravan joined those of North Africa and the trade trains the count could reach a hundred thousand {cite:tdv-kervan}.

## Ottoman kervancıs and the end

The Seljuks built ribats and caravanserais for the road trade, charged foreign merchants little customs and compensated goods lost to theft or disaster; the Ottomans ran their transport and post on a wide caravan network — the Bursa-Tabriz road was the chief late-fifteenth-century route, with hired "makkârî" trains between towns and regular runs like the Belgrade caravan {cite:tdv-kervan}. In Murad IV's Istanbul three thousand people earned their living by the caravan trade {cite:tdv-kervan}. The turn came when the English and Dutch companies took the sea lanes in the mid-seventeenth century, and the Suez Canal of 1869 with the colonial railways finished it; yet small trade and hajj caravans kept to their rounds into the twentieth century, and twenty-to-twenty-five-camel trains still carry salt slabs from the Taoudenni mines to Timbuktu {cite:tdv-kervan}.
