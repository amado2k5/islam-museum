## Summary

The Sufi shaykh — the murshid, the pir — is the guide who trains murids on the order's path; in tasawwuf the word stands with walî, pîr and murshid for the one who shows seekers the way {cite:tdv-seyh-tasavvuf}. The title took on this sense from the middle of the fourth century AH, when al-Sarraj, al-Kalabadhi and al-Sulami wrote of the early masters as 'mashayikh' {cite:tdv-seyh-tasavvuf}.

## What makes a shaykh

The Sufi authors required the shaykh to be exemplary, road-tested, courteous, generous and indifferent to the murid's property, correcting by gentleness before force and practising before preaching; Najm al-Din Daya listed twenty qualities, beginning with sound shari'a knowledge and Sunni belief {cite:tdv-seyh-tasavvuf}. They named three kinds: the ta'lim shaykh who instructs in the law and the inner life, the sohbet shaykh who teaches mostly through presence and gaze, and the terbiye shaykh who raises the murid as a parent raises a child {cite:tdv-seyh-tasavvuf}.

## The work of guidance

The shaykh trains by litany, retreat, discipline and the interpretation of dreams; his purposeful gaze on the murid is called tawajjuh, and some orders — the Naqshbandis especially — value his sohbet above all {cite:tdv-seyh-tasavvuf}. The murid owes him near-total surrender — 'dying into the shaykh' as the first step to dying into God — and a murid who leaves his shaykh mid-path was called a renegade {cite:tdv-seyh-tasavvuf}.

## Ijaza and succession

When the murid has finished the suluk the shaykh grants the ijaza of guidance and invests him with the khilafat, irshad or icazet cloak; only then does he become the shaykh's khalifa, licensed to guide others in the order {cite:tdv-seyh-tasavvuf}. The blessing that flows through him is held to descend through the silsile — 'feyz-i isnadi' — so the office is a link in a chain that leads back to the Prophet {cite:tdv-seyh-tasavvuf}.

## Many names for one office

The regions renamed the office in their own tongues: the Naqshbandi order says hoca, the Yasawi line ata, the Turks of Turkestan and the Volga ishan, Anatolian orders baba, and the Bektashis have used dede-baba since the sixteenth century {cite:tdv-seyh-tasavvuf}. The literature also warned against pretenders — 'mutashayyih', the cloak-shaykh with no substance — whom would-be murids were told to avoid {cite:tdv-seyh-tasavvuf}.

## Not yet covered
