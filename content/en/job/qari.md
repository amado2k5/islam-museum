## Summary

The qari (plural qurra) is the professional reciter, preserver and teacher of the Qur'an's ways of being read {cite:tdv-kiraat}. The vocation began with the Companions whom the Prophet sent out as Qur'an teachers — Mus'ab ibn Umayr to Medina, and others who died teaching at Bi'r Ma'una — and it grew into the scholarly elite of the garrison towns, from whom the seven and ten readings were named {cite:tdv-kiraat} {cite:tdv-darulkurra}.

## A craft of mouths and chains

The science of qira'a keeps a precise professional vocabulary: the qari is the reciter, the muqri the one who transmits the readings mouth to mouth (mushafaha) on an unbroken isnad, the rawi the transmitter of a reading, the riwaya the reading as ascribed to him, and the tariq the sub-path taken from a rawi {cite:tdv-kiraat}. Students were graded: the mubtadi learnt by the ifrad method, the mutawassit knew four or five readings that way, and the muntahi had mastered the famous readings — Ibn al-Jazari's manuals set the canon of this hierarchy {cite:tdv-kiraat}.

## The great reading centres

In the second century AH each centre had its masters: Nafi' at Medina, Ibn Kathir and Humayd at Mecca, Asim, Hamza, al-Kisa'i and al-A'mash at Kufa, Abu Amr at Basra and Ibn Amir at Damascus — the seven of the kiraat-i seb'a, to whom Abu Ja'far, Ya'qub and Khalaf were added for the ten {cite:tdv-darulkurra}. The Companions' copies and the writing of the canonical readings grew out of this same circle of qurra who had preserved the different recitations {cite:tdv-kiraat}.

## Circles, kuttabs and dar al-qurra

Higher teaching ran in the great mosques: at the Umayyad Mosque Abu al-Darda — called the 'qari of al-Sham' — drew more than 1,500 students, and Ibn Jubayr found all-day lessons there, the 'sab'' circle after dawn and the 'Kawthariyya' sessions after the afternoon prayer for those struggling to memorise {cite:tdv-darulkurra}. Children's letters were learnt in the kuttab, where the Qur'an teacher was the 'mu'allim' or 'muqri' distinct from the writing-master 'mukattib' {cite:tdv-darulkurra}. The first independent house of recitation, Damascus's Rishaiyya dar al-qurra, was founded by Risha ibn Nazif around the turn of the fifth century AH; later Damascus kept several more named for their qurra masters {cite:tdv-darulkurra}.

## Service and office

The reciter's skill carried public offices: the circle's head was the 'shaykh al-qiraa' holding the 'mashikhat al-qiraa', the city mosque's teaching was the 'mashikhat al-masjid', and the army camp had its 'mashikhat al-jund' whose teachers were called 'qari al-jund' {cite:tdv-darulkurra}. The Ottomans gave the name darülkurra to the recitation wing in the imperial and vizierial mosques, and the profession's summit was the reisülkurra, the chief of the licensed reciters {cite:tdv-darulkurra}.
