## Summary

The halwani is the confectioner who cooks sweets of honey, sugar, flour and nuts {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 16, pp. 63-64}. Al-Shayzari opens his chapter on the confectioners by admitting that the kinds of confectionery and the measures of their ingredients are too many to master, and leaves their detail to the 'arif who oversees the trade {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 16, pp. 63-64}. Centuries later Evliya Çelebi counts several guilds of sweet-makers in Istanbul, from the shopkeeping halva-makers to the men who carried trays of sweets on their heads through the streets {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 196-199, pp. 158-159}.

## The craft and its rules

The confection must be well cooked, neither raw nor burnt, and the maker should keep a fly-whisk in his hand at all times to drive the flies away {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 16, pp. 63-64}. Because the recipes are so many — cornstarch, almonds, poppy seeds and more in varying measure — knowledge of the right proportions belongs to the 'arif's jurisdiction {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 16, pp. 63-64}.

## The named sweets

Among the confections al-Shayzari names are the natif of honey, sugar, pistachios and hazelnuts, the poppy-seed and yellow kinds of natif, basandid, zulabiyya, the soft and juicy khabis, the sabuniyya formed into cakes resembling soap and baked on a tray, and the kishkananij, a semolina sweet with sugar, almonds, camphor and rose water that is baked in the tannur {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 16, pp. 63-64}.

## The frauds of the sweet-shop

The muhtasib must watch the ways they adulterate the confection, for they are many: honey is mixed with grape pulp, betrayed by its smell of grapes when cooked; cane-juice syrup, called qattara, is stirred into syrup and settles at the bottom of the vessel; and sweets are thickened with flour, cornstarch, rice flour, lentil flour and the husks of sesame seeds, all of which make the confection float on water {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 16, pp. 63-64}. Zulabiyya may be cooked in dissolved cane crystals instead of honey, nubiyya bulked with flour, and an adulterated kishkananij falls off the side of the oven — every fraud, he concludes, shows in the sweet's appearance and taste {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 16, pp. 63-64}.

## The confectioners of Istanbul

Evliya numbers four guilds of sweet-makers: the town confectioners (Halvajian-i Shehir), four hundred men in a hundred and seventy shops, who fit up their litters with white halva, moon halva, date halva, almond halva and more, distributing sweetmeats as they pass {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 196-197, pp. 158-159}. Five hundred more, the tablakaran, have no shops at all but carry plates of halvas and pastes of roses, sesame, nuts and pistachios on their heads, crying them with sweet alluring words {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 197, p. 158}.

## Makers of electuaries and sugar work

Earlier in the procession march the confectioners of electuary (Ma'junjian), whose patron is Obeid Attar, girded by Selman, and who pass grinding spices in bronze mortars while fair youths carry silver cups {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 55, pp. 117-118}. The akideh-makers keep seventy shops, the most famous at Aya Sofia and the Flour-hall, whose confections do not spoil in five years {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 198, p. 158}; and the halva-makers of Galata, mostly Greeks from Chios or Franks versed in medicine, exhibit trees of sugar with fruits upon them, behind whom walk the chief confectioners of the Serai and of the town {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 199, p. 159}.

## Today

The halwani's craft is still living: the sweet-shops of the same cities still sell halva, kunafa and candied nuts, though much production moved to factories and the tray-carrier on his rounds has largely disappeared {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 196-199, pp. 158-159}.
