## Summary

The wāqif was the founder of a waqf — the man or woman who, by a legal act the jurists sum as 'the perpetual dedication of a property by its owner to a religious, social or charitable purpose', endowed property that would outlive him {cite:tdv-vakif}. It is a job of a single deed, and among the most consequential in the museum: mosques, madrasas, soup-kitchens, fountains, hospitals — and wrestlers' tekkes and a cirit field — all stood on founders' endowments {cite:tdv-vakif} {cite:tdv-gures} {cite:tdv-cirit}.

## Who could found a waqf

The jurists were unanimous that the vâkıf must be of full capacity — sane, adult, prudent, free and not interdicted — and they required Islam of neither founder nor beneficiary; a waqf made in mortal illness fell under the law of wills {cite:tdv-vakif}. The act itself was unilateral: the founder's declaration alone created it, with no acceptance needed for a public waqf; the words 'vakıf', 'habs' and 'tesbîl' were explicit declaration in Hanafi doctrine, while 'sadaqa' — a word that could mean ordinary alms — counted as figurative {cite:tdv-vakif}. The declaration had to be definite and final, not bound to a term or a dissolving condition, though the Malikis accepted even time-limited waqfs; once registered by a judge's ruling, made by will at death, or put to actual use — prayer held in a dedicated mosque, a burial made in a dedicated cemetery — the waqf became binding on all schools {cite:tdv-vakif}.

## The first founders

The Prophet set the example: he turned parts of Fadak, of his Khaybar shares and of some Medina plots into sadaqa for the Muslims, and left behind little besides his riding-beast, his weapons and the lands he had endowed {cite:tdv-vakif}. The Companions followed: Umar sealed his valuable Khaybar land on the Prophet's counsel 'hold the principal, give the yield' — never to be sold, gifted or inherited — Uthman bought the Well of Ruma and dedicated it to all Muslims, Ali endowed a plot and a spring at Yanbu', Khalid his arms and horses, and Jabir ibn Abdallah said he knew no property-holder among the Muhajirun and Ansar who had not made a waqf {cite:tdv-vakif}. From there the practice grew until the jurists' term — habs and hubus in the Shafi'i, Maliki and Zahiri sources, vakıf in the Hanafi and Ottoman — named one of the chief institutions of Islamic civilisation {cite:tdv-vakif}.

## What the founder could endow

The endowed thing had to be private property, lawful to use, and defined enough to avoid dispute; for most schools it had also to be 'ayn' — a fixed thing, since a waqf was perpetual — while the Maliki school accepted endowments of use-rights {cite:tdv-vakif}. The Hanafis required real property and then let custom carry it further: the buildings' appurtenant movables, Abu Yusuf's weapons and horses of war, Imam Muhammad's mushafs, books, funeral gear, axes and cooking-pots where custom made them needful — and in Ottoman practice seed-wheat for poor farmers, milk-animals for the poor, and trousseaux for poor brides {cite:tdv-vakif}. Cash waqfs — lending the endowment capital at the sharia-sanctioned return — ran through the Ottoman fifteenth and sixteenth centuries into a famous controversy over their closeness to riba, until Ebussuud's 1548 fetva made them official {cite:tdv-vakif}.

## The founder's hand after death

The deed was the founder's voice after the grave: it named the purpose, the trustee (mutawalli) and the supervisor, set the workers' wages and the beneficiaries' shares, and the jurists treated its conditions as binding law — 'the founders' conditions are as the Lawgiver's text', in the jurists' rule the entry records in its history section {cite:tdv-vakif}. A founder might endow for his own family first and the poor after them, for a tekke whose şeyhlik he settled on his sons and even daughters, for the külliye's staff, the mektep and medrese students, the dervishes, the region's poor and every passing traveller fed free at the imaret — and he could charge the record to keep the villagers un-harried and to pray that his waqf be preserved from harm {cite:tdv-vakif}.
