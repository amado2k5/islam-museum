## Summary

The cartographer drew the 'images' of the earth in geographical books and on royal plates {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 pp. 84-87}. Kramers traces the craft from al-Khwarizmi's lost map of about 830 and al-Ma'mun's 'image of the earth' executed by seventy scholars, through the tenth-century Balkhi school whose texts were commentaries on their maps, to al-Idrisi's seventy maps for Roger II of Sicily {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 pp. 84-90}.

## Al-Ma'mun's image of the earth

The scientific study of geography in Islam began under Greek influence: the Muslims became acquainted with Ptolemy's geographical work, and al-Khwarizmi made an adaptation of it about 830, whose map is lost {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 84}. The Caliph al-Ma'mun ordered the measurement of a geographical degree in the Syrian desert and caused an 'image of the earth' to be executed by seventy scholars, amongst whom was al-Khwarizmi {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 84}. The book divided the inhabited world into seven climates, a theory probably of Persian-Babylonian origin that held a predominant place in much of the Muslims' geographical literature {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 pp. 84-85}.

## The map-makers' school

In the tenth century a literary geographical school developed whose works were accompanied by a number of maps, the text meant to be their description {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 86}. The first map is a circular map of the world with Mecca at its centre, girdled by the encircling ocean from which two gulfs — the Sea of Rum and the Sea of Fars — enter the continent and nearly meet at the isthmus of Suez {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 86}. Abu Zayd al-Balkhi (d. 934), a scholar at the Samanid court, is said to have composed the first treatise of this kind; his system was elaborated in the books of al-Istakhri (c. 950), Ibn Hawqal (c. 975) and al-Maqdisi (c. 985) {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 pp. 86-87}.

## A style without figures

The Balkhi-school maps show a more exact notion of geographical reality than the maps circulating in Europe at the same time, yet they never carry pictures of men and animals, owing no doubt to the prohibition against the pictorial representation of living beings {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 87}. Already in the tenth century a tendency appears to represent coast-lines and rivers under conventionalised forms: many Istakhri maps show the Mediterranean in a circular or elliptical form {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 87}.

## Al-Idrisi's seventy maps

The most brilliant cartographer of the next age was al-Idrisi, working at the court of the Norman King Roger II of Sicily (1101-54), the meeting-point of the two cultural areas {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 89}. According to al-Idrisi's preface, Roger had sent in all directions for information and, like al-Ma'mun, ordered the construction of a big world-map {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 pp. 89-90}. In the best-known edition the work contains seventy maps, each a tenth part of one of the seven climates; fitted together they make an oblong quadrangle, and the Mediterranean coast-line answers much better to reality than on any previous Islamic map {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 90}. The first translation known of al-Idrisi was published in Rome in 1619, in an incomplete abridgement whose translator did not even know the author's name {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 pp. 90-91}.

## The map's travels

Literary Islamic geography left little direct impression on Europe in the Middle Ages, but one clear trace survives: the world-map in Marino Sanuto's Opus Terrae Sanctae, completed in 1321 and dedicated to the Pope, is round with Jerusalem at its centre and shows the two big seas derived from the ocean — the work of a reviver of the crusading spirit drawing on the lore of the people he wanted to fight {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931 p. 92}.
