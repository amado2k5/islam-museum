## Summary

The saghriji tanned and finished shagreen — the hard granular leather that grips a sword-hilt better than any smooth skin — and Istanbul's guild won the right to be counted assistants to the sword-makers {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 260, p. 180}. The arms themselves confirm the claim: shagreen-covered scabbards from Bukhara to the Vienna booty {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 pp. 23, 222}.

## A hundred and fifty men, fifty shops

The shagreen-makers (Saghrijian) are an hundred and fifty men, with fifty shops. Their patron was the grandsire of Ah-averan-dedeh, the sheikh Serraj-ud-din of Pahsa, who was directed and girded by Selman Pak; his tomb is near Ghaza, in the ruins of Ascalon {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 260, p. 180}.

## The contest with the tanners

The shagreen-makers had a great contest with the tanners, which was decided in their favour: they should be considered assistants to the sword-makers {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 260, p. 180}. The adjudication placed a leather trade inside the arms-makers' guild family — and so they march: after them follow the Kilij-bashi, the head of the sword-makers, and the head of the cuirass-makers, attended by the eightfold Turkish music {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 260, p. 180}.

## Shagreen of different colours

They adorn their shops on litters with shagreen of different colours, and their manufactory is established inside the Flour-hall {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 260, p. 180}. Shagreen is the granular skin of shark and ray — or, in the saddlery sense, the croup leather worked with seeds pressed into its surface — dyed, polished, and laid over a wooden core: hard-wearing and non-slip, exactly what a hilt or scabbard needs in the hand.

## The leather on the hilt

Surviving arms show the product at work. In Bukhara the sheaths and scabbards were made with a wooden core covered with shagreen under silver mounts {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 p. 23}. Among the Ottoman booty at Cracow are sabres and pallaches sumptuously mounted in gilded and chiselled silver, inlaid with jade and turquoise, with sheaths clad in shagreen or velvet — blades of Persian make mounted to suit Ottoman taste {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 p. 222}. The guild's claim to serve the sword-makers was not ceremonial: the shagreen went on the weapon itself.

## Not yet covered

The sources give the guild's numbers, patron, lawsuit and product, but not the dressing process in the workshop — how the grain was raised or the skins dyed — nor the tanners' side of the great contest, nor the trade outside Istanbul {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 260, p. 180; elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 pp. 23, 222}.
