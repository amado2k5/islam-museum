## Summary

The khabbaz baked the town's bread, and the farran kept the public oven where households sent their dough {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}. No food trade stood so close to the muhtasib's eye: al-Shayzari has him register the bakers and their shops, spread the oven keepers through the quarters, police the kneading, and forbid the adulteration of the loaf {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 6-7, pp. 46-49}. In Evliya's Istanbul procession the bakers passed on wagons with nine hundred and ninety-nine shops behind them, acknowledging Adam as their first patron {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 66, pp. 120-121}.

## The baker under hisba

The bakers were told to raise the ceilings of their shops, open the doors and make wide holes in the oven roofs so that smoke escapes and people are not harmed, and when the tannur had been lit the baker first wiped it inside with a clean rag before baking {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 6, p. 46}. The muhtasib wrote their names and shop locations in his register and ordered them to clean and cover the water containers and to wash the kneading troughs {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 6, p. 46}.

## Clean kneading

The dough kneader must not knead with his feet, knees or elbows, for that shows contempt for the food and lets sweat fall into the dough; he kneads in a sleeveless robe or woollen gown, veiled, a white band tied on his forehead, his arm hair shaved, with a fly-whisk kept over him if he works by day {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 6, pp. 46-47}.

## An honest loaf

The manual names the frauds: vetch and beans redden the crust, chick-pea and rice flour make the bread heavy and under-cooked, coarsely ground grain, barley flour and darnel pass for wheat, and borax, forbidden though it improves the crust {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 6, p. 47}. Bread must be baked only after it has risen, the crust sprinkled with white cumin, black cumin, sesame and mastic seeds, and the loaf left in the oven until well baked but not burnt {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 6, p. 47}. For the common good the muhtasib stipulates how much each shop bakes daily, so the town is not disturbed by want of bread {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 6, p. 47}.

## The oven keeper

Al-Shayzari disperses the oven keepers among the roads, quarters and outskirts because of their importance to the people {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 7, pp. 48-49}. The keeper cleans the oven tiles of cinders and sand, keeps clean water to swill the oven, washes it each day, marks the people's bowls of dough so they are not confused, keeps two bakers, one for bread and one for fish so that no fish oil reaches the loaves, and takes no more dough than the agreed fee {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 7, pp. 48-49}. In Lane's Cairo this is exactly the trade: the farran is the baker "to whom bread, meat, &c., are sent to be baked" {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}.

## Bread and power

Buckley's introduction explains why the craft drew such attention: where trades were normally gathered into their own market areas, the bakers were the exception, distributed through the town so every inhabitant could reach bread; the muhtasib ensured they had flour from the millers and baked a stipulated amount {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley introduction, pp. 17-18}. He notes that under the Fatimids control of bakers and grain merchants was one of the muhtasib's main functions, and records that in 1024, when the muhtasib Baqi fixed bread prices, the millers and bakers locked their mills and shops and Baqi was dismissed the same day; on another occasion bakers were publicly pilloried for raising prices {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley introduction, pp. 17-18}. Trades that light fires, such as bakers and cooks, were also segregated from perfumers and cloth merchants {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 2, p. 36}.

## The Istanbul guild

Evliya's procession puts the Bakers, "those columns of faith", under their chief: their first patron is Adam, to whom Gabriel brought corn and taught grinding and baking, and their patron in the Prophet's time Omar b. Umran Berberi, girded by Selman in Ali's presence and buried near Medina at Bokai {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 66, pp. 120-121}. They fielded nine hundred and ninety-nine shops, passing on wagons where some kneaded, some baked and some threw small loaves to the crowd, and they baked for the occasion immense loaves the size of a hamam cupola, weighing fifty quintals, cooked in pits because no oven could hold them {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 66, p. 121}. The Janissaries kept their own corps of three hundred bakers in a great bakehouse with its colonel, bread clerk, repairers, superintendents and head baker, who distributed black fodula loaves to the poor {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 67, p. 121}. Their train of assistants included the salt-makers, the cracknel-bakers, the pastry-cooks, the millers, the flour-merchants, the corn-sifters, the sieve-makers and the bag-makers {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 67-86, pp. 121-125}.

## Today

Bread is still baked in the cities described, but the farran's public oven for household dough has largely disappeared where kitchens carry their own ovens, and large mechanised bakeries do the work of whole quarters of ovens {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318} {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 7, pp. 48-49}.
