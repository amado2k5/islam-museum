## Summary

The alim — plural 'ulama — is the man formed in the religious sciences and licensed by his shaykhs, who then carries learning into teaching, fatwa, the bench and the pulpit {cite:tdv-icazet-egitim} {cite:tdv-fetva}. Lane's Cairo shows the class at work in the 1830s: a council of the 'ulama sat beside the kadi in the great trials {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. The Azhar student who had sufficiently advanced took a licence to teach from the sheykh of the mosque and became a professor; others became kadis, muftis, imams or schoolmasters, and some stayed all their lives in the Azhar aspiring to the higher 'ulama {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.

## The making of an alim

Guillaume records the learning journey as the hallmark of the calling: before and after the universities existed, students set out on journeys of a thousand miles or more — from Spain to Mecca, from Morocco to Baghdad — leaving home practically penniless to sit at the feet of a chosen master {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}. At al-Azhar the students from all parts of the Muslim world are grouped in separate communal quarters and partially supported by pious benefactions and grants {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}.

## The ijaza

The scholar's warrant is the ijaza: the TDV education entry gives its elements and conditions and distinguishes the ijaza of ifta' — the licence to give fatwa — from the ijaza of teaching {cite:tdv-icazet-egitim}. Guillaume likens the ijaza, granted by the Muslim professor in his own name and authority, to the medieval licentia docendi, the earliest form of the academic degree {cite:legacy-of-islam-1931}. At Lane's Azhar the same idea appeared as the licence of the sheykh of the mosque by which a competent man became a professor {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.

## The 'ulama in the city

In Lane's Cairo the 'ulama had public weight: when a grave case was tried, the kadi and the council of the 'ulama heard it together, and Lane watched them stroke their beards as the sentence was demanded {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. The higher 'ulama issued fatwas — the fatwa entry lays out the grades of muftis in each law-school and the form of the answer {cite:tdv-fetva}. In the old days a sheykh of the Azhar passed through the street in his ample dress and great muffeh turban while passengers pressed for a short ejaculatory prayer, the butcher refused his coin and a Frank had to dismount; Lane writes that the class's condition had by his day fallen to a scanty subsistence save for extraordinary talent {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.

## The bounds the books draw

The same sources mark the class's limits in their period. Lane writes that the Muslim 'ulama were much fettered by their religion in some paths of learning, and that superstition sometimes settled a point controverted for centuries {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. After the confiscation of the Azhar's lands under Muhammad Ali, the number of students without an endowed riwaq decreased very much, he reports {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. The museum records these as the observers' judgements, not its own.

## The learned calling's branches

The 'ulama of these sources shade into the museum's named offices: the mufti whose ijaza covers fatwa {cite:tdv-fetva}, the müderris whose chair the Nizamiyya carried {cite:tdv-icazet-egitim}, the kadi, the imam and the schoolmaster into whom Lane's Azhar students dispersed {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. What makes the alim a single figure is the formation: the chain of teachers, the named books transmitted, and the shaykhs' licence at the end {cite:tdv-icazet-egitim}.
