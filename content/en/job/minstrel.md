## Summary

The âşık is the saz poet: he plays and sings his own or others' poems and tells the folk tales, wandering from land to land with the instrument in his hand {cite:tdv-asik}. The name entered Turkish literature in the fifteenth century; the way of life and the poetry are older, rooted in the national tradition the old Turks carried on after Islam — the line of the baksı and ozan singers of heroic tales to the kopuz, who after Islam took the name âşık and in settled Anatolia yielded ground to the meddah {cite:tdv-asik} {cite:tdv-meddah}.

## A name that changed meaning

At first âşık was a title the tekke poets used to mark themselves off from the secular saz poets — "the lover of God," as it reads in many poems bearing the mahlas Âşık Yûnus, whose greatest Anatolian representative is Yunus Emre {cite:tdv-asik}. From the seventeenth century the saz poets who sang non-religious verse came to be called âşık themselves, and the term kept that sense until recent times, when the wandering poet with his own order, dress and customs began to fade {cite:tdv-asik}.

## The bâde and the call

The tradition explains how a poet is made: the bâdeli âşık drinks the cup in a dream at the hands of a pir — Hızır Nebî, İlyas Nebî or Kutub Nebî in the legends — who offers the bâde three times, "for his own love," "for the pirs' love," "for the beloved's love," then shows a beloved's face and vanishes; the sleeper wakes weeping, beating himself until blood comes, and sets out after the beloved — so the people know he has drunk the bâde and become an âşık, and the pir's dream-given ledünnî secrets bring him religious and mystical knowledge besides {cite:tdv-asik}. One road made the hero-âşık who, like Köroğlu, drank "the braves' cup"; another the plain âşık who, like Ercişli Emrah, spent his years wandering behind the beloved {cite:tdv-asik}.

## Apprenticeship

Dream and gift are not enough: the finished âşık must hold music, poetry and tale-telling together, and that needs a long çıraklık — years spent at a master âşık's side through saz sessions and tale gatherings, learning the older âşıks' works, the master's own poems and tales, fine saz playing, improvisation on a demanded rhyme, and the rules of pleasing each audience {cite:tdv-asik}. When the apprenticeship is done the master grants the mahlas, and the craft stands registered; they called speaking verse without the saz "from the tongue," singing it to the saz "from the string," and counted improvised composition on any set rhyme their chief boast over the classical poets {cite:tdv-asik}.

## The fasıl and the four kinds

The âşık fasıl was the craft's public proof: the saz is tuned, the Prophet, the school and order elders, imams and pirs are praised, and the twelve-part programme runs through taksim, peşrev, divan, semâi, kalenderî, müstezad, methiye, satranç, koşma, hece semâi, mâni and destan — conducted by a master called the reis-i âşıkan, with coins collected to reward the solver of muamma riddles {cite:tdv-asik}. The article sorts the âşıks by where they grew: the town âşıks touched by divan literature and welcome in konaks and palaces, many raised among the Janissaries; the âşıks of nomad and half-nomad tribes, above all the Turkmen of southern Anatolia, serving the aşiret beys; the village âşıks of weddings, oda gatherings and the beys' assemblies; and the order âşıks — Alevi-Kızılbaş and Bektashi poets and those of other tarikats who voice their order's character in verse {cite:tdv-asik}.

## Decline

The âşık held his place in Ottoman life to the empire's last centuries, sometimes growing further, but his importance waned from the late reign of Sultan Abdülaziz onward; though âşıks are still met in some regions, the article judges they no longer hold an important place in Turkish social life {cite:tdv-asik}.
