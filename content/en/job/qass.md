## Summary

The qass — plural qussas — was the storyteller-preacher: the person who used many tales (qisas) in his exhortation was called a kās or kassâs {cite:tdv-vaaz}. He addressed the common people in mosque and market, telling religious stories and cautionary examples to soften hearts and move hearers to good {cite:tdv-vaaz}.

## The first storytellers

The craft emerged under the caliphate of Umar, when the qass and the qari appeared as popular educators or preachers {cite:tdv-vaaz}. Ubayd ibn Umayr al-Laythi of the Successors is recorded as the first qass; al-Harith ibn Mu'awiya al-Kindi asked Umar's permission to tell stories; and in the mosque of Basra the first to narrate was al-Aswad ibn Sari' {cite:tdv-vaaz}. In the early period the term kās denoted those who gave religious counsel to the people, and most of them were scholars and trustworthy transmitters {cite:tdv-vaaz}.

## From counsel to commerce

From the end of the Rashidun period the storytellers' art was taken up by political currents as a vehicle of propaganda, and different groups of preachers and storytellers arose under the pull of ascetic movements, heresy, letters and amusement {cite:tdv-vaaz}. As the Companions and Successors died out in the eighth century and the lands of Islam widened, weak and fabricated reports — Isra'iliyyat above all — spread through their tales, and by the late third Islamic century the craft had largely turned into a charlatanry pursued for gain {cite:tdv-vaaz}.

## The critics

The storytellers' liberties drew the scholars' books: Abu'l-Faraj Ibn al-Jawzi wrote 'The Qussas and the Reminders' (al-Qussas wa'l-mudhakkirun), treating such speech as an art divided into tale, reminder and exhortation, and al-Suyuti wrote 'Warning the Elite against the Lies of the Qussas' (Tahdhir al-khawass min akadhib al-qussas) {cite:tdv-vaaz}. Books were written to introduce the storytellers and warn against their harm — works the entry counts within the literature of wa'z {cite:tdv-vaaz}.

## The name that replaced him

From the fourth Islamic century the titles wa'iz (exhorter) and muzakkir (reminder) gained ground in place of kās, and Abu'l-Layth al-Samarqandi used muzakkir for one who gives religious counsel {cite:tdv-vaaz}. Al-Ghazali judged that terms had drifted from their first meanings — that zikr and tezkir, once an honourable science, had in later times turned into storyteller-craft {cite:tdv-vaaz}.

## What the preacher owes the tale

The wa'z article's counsel to preachers applies the lesson of the qussas' fall: the exhorter should master his sources, know his audience's temper, keep false tales and legends out of his sermons, warn without terrifying and encourage without promising cheaply {cite:tdv-vaaz}.
