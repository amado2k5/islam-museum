## Summary

The khayyat is the tailor who cuts cloth and sews garments to order {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 24, pp. 86-87}. His craft sat inside the market order of the Islamic city: the muhtasib policed the quality of the sewing and the honesty of the workroom {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 24, pp. 86-87}, the guilds of Cairo admitted their new members with a girding ceremony {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII, pp. 609-610}, and in Istanbul Evliya Çelebi counted three thousand tailors' shops and gave the craft the prophet Idris for its first patron {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 351, pp. 200-201}.

## The craft and its rules

Al-Shayzari's hisba manual sets out what good tailoring is: the cloth must be cut well, the neck opening made properly, the gussets wide, the sleeves and cuffs of equal length and the hems sewn evenly {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 24, pp. 86-87}. The stitches should be tight rather than loose and large, the needle fine, and the thread in it short, because a long thread frays and weakens as the tailor pulls it through the cloth {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 24, pp. 86-87}.

## The customer's cloth

Because the tailor worked on other people's material, the manual guards it: he must not cut a valuable piece of cloth until he has measured it, and if it is silk or brocade he must weigh it before taking it and return the sewn garment at the same weight {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 24, pp. 86-87}. One recorded fraud was to put sand and glue in the lining of a silk garment and so steal a corresponding weight of the customer's cloth {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 24, pp. 86-87}. Work should not keep its owner waiting more than a week unless a longer time was agreed, and the muhtasib must watch the tailors because they hold people's property {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 24, pp. 86-87}.

## Neighbours of the needle

The chapter places the tailor among the trades that pass cloth between them: repairers had to swear not to mend a torn garment for the dyers and cloth-beaters unless its owner was present, and embroiderers and ornamental stitchers were forbidden to move embroidery from one garment to another at the bleachers' or cloth-beaters' request {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 24, pp. 86-87}. The makers of the qalansuwa caps were ordered to use new cloth, silk thread and dyed linen, not old dyed pieces {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 24, pp. 86-87}.

## The guild and admission into the trade

In Lane's Cairo the kheiyat stood among the named trades of the market streets {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}. When a young man had mastered his trade, a ceremony of admission was performed among carpenters, turners, barbers, tailors, book-binders and a few others: the father applied to the Sheykh of the trade, the Sheykh's officer, the Nakeeb, invited the masters with a sprig of green and a cup of coffee, led the candidate before the Sheykh and stated his qualifications, and girded him with a shawl while the company recited the Fat'hah {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII, pp. 609-610}.

## Istanbul's tailors

Evliya numbers the Derzian, the tailors, among the great guilds of the procession: two imperial establishments, the first by the menagerie built by Mehmed II and the second opposite the Alai-koshk built by Sultan Suleiman, each with five hundred men, and some three thousand shops and five thousand men across the capital's four jurisdictions {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 351, pp. 200-201}. Their first patron, in his account, is the prophet Idris, and their patron in the Prophet's time David Taheri, girded by Selman; besides the two imperial chiefs a third heads all the tailors in and out of town {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 351, pp. 200-201}. The guild marched with shops on litters dressed in precious garments, its boys in armour, because tailors were necessary to the camp {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 351, pp. 200-201}. The tailors of dolimans had their manufactory near the Bezestan under the Imperial treasurer, and the kapama gown tailors numbered five hundred men with three hundred shops {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 352-353, p. 201}.

## Today

The khayyat's trade is still living: made-to-measure sewing survived the factory, and tailors still work the suqs and shopping streets of the cities described above, though much ordinary garment-making moved to workshops and factories {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318} {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 351, pp. 200-201}.
