## Summary

The hallaq or muzayyin shaved the head, trimmed the beard and groomed his customers, and in Egypt he also performed circumcision {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII, pp. 605-609}. The hisba rules ask for a light touch and a wet, sharp razor {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 35, pp. 105-106}, and Evliya's Istanbul procession knows three barber guilds — the shop barbers, the circumcision barbers and the barbers on foot — under patrons Abraham and Selman {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 436-438, pp. 214-216}.

## The razor and the rules

Al-Shayzari writes that the barber, "that is, the bathhouse attendant", should have a light touch, be nimble and proficient in shaving, and keep his razor wet and sharp {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 35, pp. 105-106}. He should not stand directly in front of the head and the places where the hair is, nor eat onion, garlic or leek lest his breath offend the customer; he cuts the hair of the forehead and temples as required, must not cut a young boy's hair without his guardian's permission, nor the first growths of a beard, nor the full beard of an effeminate man {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 35, pp. 105-106}.

## The barber at circumcision

In Lane's Cairo the barber was the operator of the circumcision: in the procession his servant carried the heml, his sign, and at the feast the barber stood on one side of the boy while the servant held the heml on the other, a cap on its top gathering the guests' nukoot for him {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII, pp. 605-609}. When the guests had gone he conducted the boy with a male relation or two to a private apartment and performed the operation, sometimes the next day, and about a week after he took the boy to the bath {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII, p. 609}.

## Admission into the trade

The girding ceremony by which a young tradesman joined his craft was customary, Lane says, among carpenters, turners, barbers, tailors, book-binders and a few others: the father applied to the Sheykh of the trade, whose officer, the Nakeeb, invited the masters with a sprig of green; after coffee and dinner the Nakeeb stated the candidate's qualifications and girded him with a shawl over his outer coat while the company recited the Fat'hah {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII, pp. 609-610}.

## The barber's patrons in guild lore

Evliya relates that from Adam to Abraham the prophets let their hair grow and nothing was heard of a barber; Abraham, having built the Ka'bah and performed the pilgrimage rites, was ordered to shave his hair at Mina, and so became the patron of the barbers {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 436, p. 214}. Their second patron is Selman Pak, who, on the day the Prophet's principal opponents embraced Islam, was ordered by him to shave his head, was girded by Ali, and is buried on the Tigris near Baghdad, where barbers and crowds assemble once a year {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 436, pp. 215-216}.

## The Istanbul guilds

The barbers passed on litters with silk aprons before them, their shops on the wagons adorned with looking-glasses, brass basins and thousands of German razors {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 436, p. 216}. The circumcision barbers were four hundred men with three hundred shops, their patron Abulhawakhi whose office was circumcising converts, and they passed circumcising boys to the sound of drums {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 437, p. 216}. The barbers on foot, those without shops, numbered two thousand and acknowledged Selman Pak {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 438, p. 216}. Their neighbouring crafts were the grinders who whetted razors on great wheels and the razor-handle-makers {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 439-440, p. 216}.

## Today

Barbering is a living trade in every city described; the circumcision operation and the public procession have gone, but the shop with its mirror, basin and razor remains recognisable from the sources {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII, pp. 605-609} {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 436, p. 216}.
