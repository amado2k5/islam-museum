## Summary

The musician makes the sounds: the mutrib who plays to move the hearer, the sâzende who carries the instruments inside the Ottoman fasıl ensemble, the mehter whose drums and pipes belong to the army, and the alâteeyeh whom Lane found plying the trade in Cairo {cite:tdv-musiki} {cite:tdv-hanende} {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. The craft stands in an old argument: the fiqh books debated the ruling on gınâ and the instruments named melâhî, meâzif and mezâmîr for a thousand years, and yet the player never lacked employment — at the Abbasid court, in the palace guild of Istanbul, at the weddings and circumcisions of Egypt {cite:tdv-musiki} {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2} {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.

## Names and debates

Classical fiqh has no single word covering today's "music": nearly every artful performance of a fine voice falls under gınâ — raising the voice, singing a poem in a moving manner — and listening to it under samâʿ, while the instruments are named melâhî in general, with stringed instruments called evtâr, percussion kûb and wind instruments mizmâr {cite:tdv-musiki}. The theorists took up the art early: under the Umayyads and Abbasids Zelzel the lutanist, Yûnus al-Kâtib and Halîl ibn Ahmad stand as the first mûsikişinas, followed by Ibrâhim and Ishâq al-Mawsilî, Ibn Hurdâzbih and Ibn al-Munajjim {cite:tdv-musiki}. Al-Fârâbî, himself a good performer, wrote his systematic treatise for those who practice the art and wish to strengthen practice with theory — the work holds that performance comes before theory {cite:tdv-musiki}.

## The palace band

In Ottoman Istanbul the mehter musicians formed a corporation of three hundred men who acknowledged Jemshid as their patron; their building stood near the Iron Gate and the palace gardens, and there they played three fasıls every afternoon and three fasıls three hours before dawn to wake the divan's men — "the Ottoman rule in both winter and summer," Evliya says, well esteemed and well paid {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}. A second chapel of forty men played at the Seven Towers, a custom Evliya ascribes to Mehmed II, and the same Turkish music sounded at the frontier-posted suburbs from Eyyub to Scutari; the musicians of the town jurisdictions numbered some four thousand, salaried by no one but playing the weddings, circumcisions and ambassadorial audiences, bringing the chief of the Imperial chapel a present for his leave {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}.

## The guild of the procession

When the guilds marched in the great procession, the musicians pleaded their precedence against the chief architect's men before the sultan and won it: "wherever the Prophet's standard goes, it is followed by the Turkish drum," their chief argued, and the mehter claimed the army's courage and the emperor's magnificence as their work {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}. Evliya then enumerates the players by instrument: five hundred beaters of the kettle-drum Mehterân-ı Kûsjiyân with their Chinese-patroned khâkhâni drums, the players of half-drum, violin, flute, trumpet, tanbûr, kanûn, lute and the newer şeshtâr and kâpûz, all distributed into six bands playing alternately in the procession {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}.

## The Cairene band

Lane's Cairo knew the professional as the alâtee — strictly "a player upon an instrument," though the alâteeyeh were generally vocal performers too — hired at most grand entertainments for about two or three shillings each a night, contributed by the guests, and often supplied with brandy until some could no longer sing nor strike a chord {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. Egyptians of all classes, Lane writes, delight in the performance of their vocal and instrumental musicians and applaud with cries of "Allah!" and "God preserve thy voice!"; yet the profession was held scarcely less disreputable than the public dancers', and the study of music was judged unworthy of a man of sense even as it was used, by the darweeshes especially, in religious ceremony {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.
