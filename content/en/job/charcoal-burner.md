## Summary

The fahham made charcoal and sold it — the fuel of bath-fires, kitchens and braziers {cite:lane-modern-egyptians pp. 139-140}. Evliya counts the coalmen of Istanbul at eight hundred men in four hundred shops, sorted into four kinds, and gives the guild two patrons: Adam, the first who lighted a fire, and Harun of Khaybar, the coal-carrier of the Prophet's Medina {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 281, pp. 184-185}.

## Four sorts of coalman

Evliya divides the eight hundred coalmen (Komurjian) into kinds: a great number of colliers come from the mountains with faces black as coal, yet they gain white money; the second sort are the men of the coal-ships, Greek infidels condemned, he says, to be black-faced in this world and the next; the third are the keepers of the coal magazines, partly Muslims and partly Armenians; and the last are the men who sell coals in retail, crying them in the streets {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 281, p. 184}. Their coal-bags are carried on Lesbian horses or Cyprian mules, and they pass weighing and crying coals {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 281, p. 184}.

## Adam and Harun of Khaybar

The coalmen's first patron, as the first who lighted a fire, is Adam; Nimrod too is famous in the history of fire for lighting the pile on which Abraham was to be burned, changed into a bed of roses {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 281, p. 184}. The patron of the Prophet's time is Harun of Khaybar, girded by Selman: he carried coals from Khaybar and from Mount Ohod to Medina and in this trade gained his subsistence {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 281, p. 184}. The people of Khaybar put him to death for having turned Muslim; his tomb is at Wadi-ul-kira {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 281, p. 185}.

## Fuel under hisba

Al-Shayzari orders that bundles of firewood, sacks of straw, water-containers, baskets of dung and sand not be taken into the market, because of the damage they cause to people's clothes {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley pp. 37-38}. When those who bring firewood and straw stop with it in the open squares, the muhtasib orders them to take the loads off their animals, for the loads injure the beasts while they stand {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley p. 38}. Donkey-drivers and those who carry firewood on their animals must tie bells or clappers of iron or brass around the animals' necks so they make a noise when passing through market or quarter, and blind people, children and the inattentive are put on their guard {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley pp. 134-135}.

## The beast under the load

The carriers must not burden their animals with more than they can bear, drive them too fiercely when carrying loads, beat them too severely, or leave them standing in a courtyard with their loads on their backs — al-Shayzari grounds all of it in the law's prohibition of harming animals {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley p. 135}.

## Charcoal in the house

In Lane's Cairo, a brazier or chafing-dish called the mankal, of tinned copper full of burning charcoal, is placed on the floor in cold weather, and perfume is sometimes burnt in it {cite:lane-modern-egyptians p. 139}. The coffee-pot itself is sometimes brought in a vessel suspended by three chains and containing burning charcoal {cite:lane-modern-egyptians p. 139}.

## Not yet covered

The sources give the guild's census, the fourfold division of the trade and the porters' rules, but not the colliers' mountain technique, the charcoal yards of the wooded coasts, or prices by the bag {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 281; shayzari-nihaya-buckley pp. 37-38}.
