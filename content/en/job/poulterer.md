## Summary

The dajjaj bred and sold poultry — the cocks and hens of the market stall and, in Egypt, the chicks of the famous hatching-ovens. Evliya gives the guild four hundred men in a hundred and five shops and a patron who was said to have invented hatching chickens in ovens {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 348, pp. 199-200}.

## Karun and the oven

The poulterers' (Tavukjian) patron is Karun, the famous alchemist — whom Hammer renders Charon — who fed a great number of poultry as a real capital; when the earth swallowed him, his poultry and the natron remained as his heritage to the land of Egypt {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 348, p. 199}. It was he, the guild said, who invented hatching chickens in ovens — he who has not seen it has seen nothing in this world {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 348, pp. 199-200}.

## Feathers for the bowmen

The poulterers marched in the suite of the bowmen and arrow-makers, because these sometimes stand in need of poultry feathers; they passed with different kinds of cocks and hens in cages — some with horns, some with two beaks, some forked — the cocks all crowing {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 348, p. 200}.

## The Tauk-emini

The poultry had its own imperial officer, the Tauk-emini: an Agha with two hundred men, to whom a number of villages near Rodosto, Malakra and Khireboli are assigned for breeding poultry, and who supplies the imperial kitchen every year with twelve hundred chickens {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 227, p. 167}.

## The chicken-ovens of Egypt

Lane describes the maamal, the Egyptian hatching-oven, at work. The neighbourhood supplies the eggs; the attendant examines them and afterwards usually gives one chicken for every two eggs received {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 309}. In general only half the ovens are used for the first ten days, fires being lighted only in the fire-cells above them; on the eleventh day these fires are put out, others are lighted in the remaining fire-cells with fresh eggs placed below, and some of the first eggs are moved up to the floor of the cells where the fires have gone out {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, pp. 309-310}. The general heat is kept at 100 to 108 degrees Fahrenheit, and the manager, accustomed to the art from his youth, knows the exact temperature without any instrument like a thermometer to guide him {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 310}.

## Twenty-first day

On the twentieth day some of the first eggs hatch, most on the twenty-first — the same period as natural incubation; the weaker chickens are placed in the passage, the rest in the innermost anterior apartment a day or two before being given to their owners, and the freed ovens receive a third supply, then a fourth {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 310}. Lane found the oven-hatched fowls in no way inferior in flavour to those hatched naturally, and an official Egyptian newspaper of 1246 AH (1831) printed the census of the hatching establishments in Lower and Upper Egypt — the eggs used numbering in the millions {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, pp. 310-311}.
