## Summary
The teacher gives lessons to pupils in a mosque, a madrasa or a kuttab. The TDV encyclopaedia defines the müderris as the high-ranking teacher who teaches in a madrasa, and the word became official after Nizam al-Mulk founded the Nizamiyya madrasas. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467} The sources distinguish three forms: the teacher of the kuttab, who teaches children; the teacher who gives a public lesson in a mosque; and the teacher in a madrasa from whom students of learning take their lessons. {cite:tdv-mektep p.5-6} {cite:tdv-dersiam p.185-186}

## Origins
The encyclopaedia holds that teaching children came before the madrasas. Kuttab schools are attested in the Prophet's time, and the Prophet released those Badr captives who could read and write on condition that each of them teach ten of the children of the Muslims. {cite:tdv-mektep p.5-6} Among the innovations credited to [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar ibn al-Khattab]] was the founding of schools and the payment of salaries to teachers. {cite:tdv-mektep p.5-6}

The encyclopaedia names [[person/hajjaj-ibn-yusuf-al-thaqafi|Hajjaj ibn Yusuf al-Thaqafi]] among the teachers: it reports that he worked as a teacher in Taif in his youth. {cite:tdv-mektep p.5-6} It also names Dahhak ibn Muzahim, who built a large school in Kufa that the entry says about 3000 children attended, and where he taught without pay; the entry dates his death to 105 AH (723 CE). {cite:tdv-mektep p.5-6}

Not every kuttab had its own building. The teacher sat with the children in a circle in the neighbourhood mosque, and separate schools were built beside mosques at an early date. A pupil who had learnt to read and had finished the Qur'an went on to higher lessons in the mosques. {cite:tdv-mektep p.5-6}

## The lesson in the mosque
The phrase «public lesson» (ders-i âm) means a lesson open to the people. It appears in sources of the sixteenth century, and it became the title of a müderris licensed to give public lessons in mosques, probably in the seventeenth century. {cite:tdv-dersiam p.185-186}

The dersiâm gave his lessons in the mosques between the morning and noon prayers, and people of every class attended them. His best-known posts were the great mosques of Istanbul, such as the Fatih, the Suleymaniye and the Bayezid. {cite:tdv-dersiam p.185-186} In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the lesson service (dersiye) in mosques and madrasas was usually carried out by dersiâms. {cite:tdv-dersiam p.185-186}

The title went only to someone who had graduated from a madrasa, received an ijaza, and then passed a further examination. In Istanbul in 1286 AH (1869 CE) the entry cites a list drawn up for the Shaykh al-Islam, which records that eighty of the 166 madrasas then active had 180 dersiâms between them. {cite:tdv-dersiam p.185-186} In the first kuttab the teacher taught in a circle in the neighbourhood mosque. The formal title for the public lesson was dersiâm, and the sources read here describe no other mosque teacher, so this record does not show a regular teacher in every mosque.

## The madrasa professor
In the Seljuk period a müderris was appointed by a patent (menşur) issued from the Diwan of the Vizierate and bearing the signature of [[person/nizam-al-mulk|Nizam al-Mulk]]; later appointments were made by order of the sultan and the caliph. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467} Scholarly merit counted in an appointment, and so did the type of ijaza the teacher had received. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467} Some appointment texts praise learning, excellence and piety; one such text is the patent of Sultan Qalawun appointing a Maliki scholar as müderris of the Mansuriyya madrasa. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467}

The post began with a ceremony: a robe of honour was put on the müderris, a procession was held for him, and he gave his first lesson from the lectern before the jurists and the viziers. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467} It is said that Abu Yusuf made a black turban and the taylasan a requirement, so that the learned could be told apart from the common people; later this dress became obligatory for müderris and jurists. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467}

A müderris was known by his city, his madrasa, his school of law or his subject: «müderris of Herat», «müderris of the Nizamiyya», «müderris of the Hanafis», «müderris of hadith». {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467} A man appointed to more than one madrasa taught in person at one and paid a deputy at the rest. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467}

Müderris generally kept their posts for life. One known dismissal under [[person/alp-arslan|Alp Arslan]] concerned the Nizamiyya in Baghdad: [[person/abu-ishaq-al-shirazi|Abu Ishaq al-Shirazi]] refused to teach there, on the ground that its land had been seized, and Ibn al-Sabbagh was appointed in his place; Ibn al-Sabbagh was removed when al-Shirazi was persuaded. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467} A müderris could also be dismissed for lack of scholarly ability, absence from the madrasa, or breach of the endowment's conditions. {cite:tdv-muderris p.467-468}

A müderris's pay followed the income of his madrasa's endowment. At the Salahiyya madrasa founded by Saladin, the teacher was paid forty dinars a month, with ten more for supervising the madrasa endowments, and his daily bread and water were provided. At the madrasa of Emir Jamal al-Din the pay was three hundred dirhams a month, and at the Nasiriyya two hundred; the sums changed with the time and the state of the finances. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467}

## The teacher of children
The entry calls the children's school «kuttab» in the Abbasid period. The Karakhanids and the Seljuks called it a «sıbyan school» (mekteb), and the Ottomans gave it names including «house of teaching» (dar al-talim), «neighbourhood school» and «primary school». Its teacher was called the «muallim», and his assistant the «kalfa» (khalifa). {cite:tdv-mektep p.6-7}

The curriculum centred on the Qur'an: reading, writing, grammar, the Qur'an, hadith, poetry and arithmetic, and its details varied from place to place. {cite:tdv-mektep p.5-6} {cite:tdv-mektep p.6-7} Teachers attended to the abilities of their pupils and rewarded successful ones by parading them through the streets and throwing almonds on them. {cite:tdv-mektep p.5-6}

The teacher of a kuttab was, for a period, held in low esteem. The entry attributes this to the shallowness of some teachers' learning, to some men entering the work to escape war, and to the childish conduct of some, and it reports Adam Mez's view that reports of Greek origin shaped this picture. {cite:tdv-mektep p.5-6}

In the Ottoman period the endowments for teaching required the Qur'an: Mehmed II made the teaching of the Qur'an a condition of the endowment of the first house of teaching he founded in Istanbul, and the endowment of Bayezid II ordered that the teacher and his assistant teach children the Qur'an and some basic religious knowledge. {cite:tdv-mektep p.6-7} In 1824 a firman of [[person/mahmud-ii-uthmani|Mahmud II]] required that children be taught the religious essentials first. {cite:tdv-mektep p.6-7}

Teachers of children were in most cases madrasa graduates who also served as imam, preacher or muezzin in a mosque; women taught the girls, and they were elderly women who knew the Qur'an. {cite:tdv-mektep p.6-7} In 1868 a school to train teachers for the sıbyan schools opened in Istanbul, and the regulation of the following year stated that only graduates of that school could be appointed teachers in the sıbyan schools. {cite:tdv-mektep p.6-7} The pay of kuttab teachers was not fixed; it was left to the means of the child's guardian. {cite:tdv-mektep p.5-6}

## Training, licence and appointment
In the Ottoman period a teacher was chosen from madrasa graduates who held an ijaza. A candidate registered with the kazasker of Anatolia or of Rumelia and waited on a list, then climbed the madrasa grades from twenty to fifty akçe, and when his turn came a scholarly board, with the kazasker present, examined him. {cite:tdv-muderris p.467-468} The waiting list was called the «matlab», in which the names of graduates were recorded until their turn came. {cite:tdv-muderris p.467-468}

Sons of the learned had the privilege of the «mevali-zade law» and rose to a higher grade than others. {cite:tdv-muderris p.467-468} Until the end of the sixteenth century appointments were made on the proposal of the kazasker; afterwards the highest grades were appointed on the proposal of the Shaykh al-Islam and the grand vizier's presentation to the sultan, and an appointment was confirmed by a berat bearing the tughra. {cite:tdv-muderris p.467-468}

The entry describes examinations. In 1528 a candidate examination was held for a post in one of the Fatih madrasas. Three teachers presented themselves, wrote treatises answering the questions, and their work was judged and debated before the kazaskers of Rumelia and Anatolia in the Hagia Sophia mosque. {cite:tdv-muderris p.467-468} At the end of the eighteenth century the sultan ordered the Shaykh al-Islam to hold an examination for müderris; 199 people took part, and sixty-four were chosen a week later, among them sons of the learned. {cite:tdv-muderris p.467-468}

The endowment deeds ask that the müderris be «people of learning, possessing virtue and dignity», and some of them specify the lessons and the school of law. {cite:tdv-muderris p.467-468}

## Ottoman ranks
The grades of müderris climbed from twenty to fifty akçe, then the grades of the Sahn, of the Suleymaniye and of the Dar al-Hadith. The custom was for a müderris who reached a certain grade to move into judgeship: from twenty to fifty akçe to a judgeship worth 150 to 300 akçe; from fifty to sixty akçe to the judgeship of a large city at 500 akçe; from sixty to a hundred akçe to the judgeship of Istanbul or the kazaskership, and from there to the shaykh al-Islam. {cite:tdv-muderris p.467-468}

The principle behind this overlap was set by the law of Mehmed II, which placed teaching, judging and giving fatwas on equal ranks and made the entry to the judgeship depend on study in the Sahn madrasas. {cite:tdv-kadi-osmanli-ortayli p.69-73}

In the sixteenth century the number of müderris grew, so the grades and ranks increased (the Sahn, the rank of thirty, the rank of forty), terms of service were shortened, and dismissal could follow from weak learning, absence from the madrasa or breach of the endowment's conditions. {cite:tdv-muderris p.467-468}

A müderris's duties were to teach his pupils and attend to them, to record what each pupil studied, and to keep order in the madrasa; a judge inspected him if a complaint was made about the lessons or about wrongdoing. {cite:tdv-muderris p.467-468} The state sometimes gave müderris investigations, inspections, judicial and arbitration tasks, and expert opinions, and sometimes made them deputise for a judge until a new one arrived. {cite:tdv-muderris p.467-468}

## The chain of teaching
The museum records the bond between a pupil and his teacher as a «studied under» (student_of) edge between two persons. Such an edge is not accepted on its own; it rests on what a readable source states. Examples from the sources read for this record:

- [[person/ahmed-pasha-bursali|Ahmed Pasha of Bursa]] was a pupil of [[person/melihi|Melihi]], and it was Melihi whom he presented to the sultan Mehmed II. After Mehmed's accession Ahmed Pasha became the sultan's companion and his teacher. {cite:tdv-ahmed-pasa-bursali p.111-112}
- [[person/imam-al-haramayn-al-juwayni|Imam al-Haramayn al-Juwayni]] taught in a madrasa he had inherited from his father, and at the same time took lessons from Abu al-Qasim al-Isfara'ini in another madrasa. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467}
- [[person/al-shafii|Imam al-Shafi'i]] had begun his learning in a kuttab, as the encyclopaedia records. {cite:tdv-mektep p.5-6}

The entry says that the choice of a teacher looked to the type of ijaza he had received and to the quality of the teachers from whom he had received it. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467}

## Pay and custom
It is said that about four hundred of the pupils of [[person/imam-al-haramayn-al-juwayni|al-Juwayni]] broke their pens and inkwells at his death, and that lessons were suspended for a full year. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467} Nizam al-Mulk did not think a new müderris should be appointed after the death of Abu Ishaq al-Shirazi, and he said that the Nizamiyya should remain closed for a year. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467}

The sources say that the teacher speaks to the pupil according to his readiness, uses language that is understood, takes care to hold the pupil's interest, and neither he nor his assistant (muid) should be harsh to pupils. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467} Some teachers were slow to accept the post, and some changed their school of law to obtain an appointment in a madrasa of another school. {cite:tdv-muderris p.465-467}

In the kuttab, [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]] is reported to have said that a teacher who beat a child for discipline and caused his death owed blood money. {cite:tdv-mektep p.5-6} The entry also gives a report from the Prophet that counts among those whom God will not regard on the Day of Judgement the kuttab teacher who burdens an orphan with what he cannot bear; the report is attributed to its transmitters and is not treated here as an event. {cite:tdv-mektep p.5-6}

## What became of the office
The madrasa system on which the müderris depended came to an end in the Ottoman state, and the title stayed with teachers in the higher schools. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the term was used for the teachers of the Western-style schools and the university: «müderris» was the equivalent of professor (profesör), and «müderris assistant» (müderris muavini) the equivalent of docent (doçent). The usage was officially withdrawn after the university reform of 1933. {cite:tdv-muderris p.467-468} In the early Republic the dersiâm title was kept in the organisation of the Diyanet, placed third in the provinces after the mufti and the assistant mufti, and paid from the budget. {cite:tdv-dersiam p.185-186}

The status today is therefore «transformed»: the act of teaching continues, and the madrasa system and its old titles have ended. This record does not cover teaching in universities and modern schools in the twentieth century, which needs sources not yet read.

## Not yet covered
The TDV encyclopaedia has no separate article on the teacher as such: the search for «MUALLİM» gave personal names and no article on the subject, and the search for «MÜDERRİSLİK» gave Ottoman grade names that refer to the article MEDRESE, which was not read. The roles of the teacher in al-Andalus, the Maghrib, Greater Syria, Egypt and Persia are therefore not covered here; nor are the tools and the writings of teachers, the gatherings and study circles, the journeys of students, or the image.
