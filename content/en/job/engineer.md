## Summary

The engineer's work in the Islamic world belonged to hiyel, the science of mechanical technology — in al-Farabi's broad definition the practical branch of every mathematical science, in al-Khwarizmi's narrow one the making of levers, mangonels, hydraulic machines, water screws and automata {cite:tdv-hiyel}. The Banu Musa wrote the oldest known original book on machines in the ninth century {cite:tdv-hiyel}, and al-Jazari wrote the greatest book of the classical mechanical craft at the Artukid court in 1206 {cite:tdv-hiyel}. In the modern age the name returned as a school: the Mühendishâne-i Berrî-i Hümâyun opened at Hasköy in 1795 to graduate army engineers {cite:tdv-muhendishane-i-berri-i-humayun}.

## What hiyel meant

Hiyel, the plural of hîle — cleverness, contrivance, device — is used in the history of the sciences for "machine knowledge" or mechanical technology {cite:tdv-hiyel}. Al-Farabi in the Ihsa al-ulum makes it the practical branch of the riyâzî sciences: the discipline that shows the fit between mathematical data and the facts of physics, and the instruments that work by that fit — so that the operations of algebra belong to arithmetic, construction engineering to geometry, observation and its instruments to astronomy, lenses and mirrors to optics, and hydraulic vessels and levers to the science of weights {cite:tdv-hiyel}. Al-Khwarizmi's Mafatih al-ulum defines it far more narrowly — levers for lifting weights, war engines such as the mangonel, hydraulic machines, water screws and automata — matching the contents of the books written under the name hiyel {cite:tdv-hiyel}.

## The books of machines

The Muslim mechanicians learned from the Hellenistic books and soon passed them: Philo's Pneumatica survives only in Arabic, Hero's Mechanica came through the translation of Kusta ibn Luqa, and a treatise on water clocks ascribed to Archimedes also had its effect {cite:tdv-hiyel}. The oldest known original composition is the Kitab al-Hiyal of the Banu Musa — the brothers Muhammad, Ahmad and Hasan — written in the ninth century; it describes about a hundred mechanical devices, more than eighty of them automatic vessels, the rest automatic fountains, lamps whose oil and wick come on of themselves, gas masks for workers in foul wells, bellows and a pump {cite:tdv-hiyel}. About twenty vessels came from Philo and Hero; the rest are original, and the book's most striking element is its automatic control mechanisms, turning the least changes of air and water pressure into work — far beyond the Greek texts it used {cite:tdv-hiyel}.

## The clockmakers and al-Jazari

The eleventh century gave Ibn Khalaf al-Muradi's Kitab al-Asrar in al-Andalus, most of its devices water clocks and automata driven by water wheels with a gear-train so complex that Europe sees nothing like it until the fourteenth century {cite:tdv-hiyel}. Fakhr al-Din Ibn al-Sa'ati completed his book on his father's great Damascus water clock — 4.23 by 2.78 metres — in 600/1203; when the clock could not be mended after his father's death, the son set it working again and wrote the book on it {cite:tdv-hiyel}. Two years later Ismail ibn al-Razzaz al-Jazari finished the Kitab fi ma'rifat al-hiyal al-handasiyya, the most important book written on mechanical technology in the Islamic classical age: in his introduction al-Jazari notes that most of his predecessors could not turn their knowledge into practice, and that nothing can be said of the truth of technical knowledge that is not put to work — so he compiled the earlier matter, systematised it, and carried his own mastery further under the patronage of the Artukid sultan Nasir al-Din Mahmud at Hisn Kayfa {cite:tdv-hiyel}. The six sections cover water clocks, automatic vessels, human- and animal-shaped machines, fountains working in sequence, automatic musical instruments, pumps, service machines and cipher locks {cite:tdv-hiyel}.

## The school of the mühendis

The modern profession arrived with the Mühendishâne-i Berrî-i Hümâyun, opened at Hasköy in 1210 (1795) under Selim III as the empire's second engineering school after the naval Mühendishâne of 1775, modelled on the military and engineering academies observed in France and Vienna {cite:tdv-muhendishane-i-berri-i-humayun}. No foreign teachers were employed: the mathematics-and-geometry-weighted lessons were carried by Turkish teachers under the geometry-and-algebra master Abdurrahman Efendi — Hüseyin Rıfkı Tamânî, İbrâhim Kâmî and the rest, five teachers by 1801 — and a printing press was opened in the school's ground floor in 1797 to reproduce the log tables and geometric figures of the textbooks {cite:tdv-muhendishane-i-berri-i-humayun}. The four classes ran from hat, spelling, arithmetic, geometry and technical drawing up to geography, surveying and map-drawing, mining, the building of fortifications, conic sections, differential and integral calculus, mechanics and astronomy {cite:tdv-muhendishane-i-berri-i-humayun}.

## The engineer in the state

The school met the new army's need: after the abolition of the Janissary corps the engineers taught the modern science of war — frontier fortresses built and strengthened by modern fortification, maps drawn, the growing capital's cadastre, water courses and the new roads {cite:tdv-muhendishane-i-berri-i-humayun}. In the new army the "mansûre mühendisi" was employed with the rank of binbaşı as chief engineer, kolağası and yüzbaşı as engineer adjutants, two engineers to every battalion, wearing the crescent-starred compass epaulette; and in 1841 the bombardier and miner companies were formed into regiments named the mühendis alayı {cite:tdv-muhendishane-i-berri-i-humayun}.

## Today

The school the sultans founded is the ancestor of the modern Turkish engineering faculties; the name mühendis it fixed on the graduate of the state school is today's word for the engineer {cite:tdv-muhendishane-i-berri-i-humayun}.
