## Summary

The râvî — plural ruvât — is the one who learns what he hears and transmits it with the received formulas of reporting {cite:tdv-ravi}. The office has two great fields in the Islamic tradition: in the poetry world every ancient Arab poet had his own râvîs who memorised and carried his verse in a master-apprentice method, and in the hadith sciences the râvî is the bearer of the report whose reliability fixes its standing {cite:tdv-siir} {cite:tdv-ravi}.

## The poet's memory

In the transmission of ancient Arabic poetry it was the rule that each poet's verse was memorised and handed on by his own râvî or râvîs in a master-apprentice arrangement; râvilik stood as a tradition-bearing school, and the râvîs who carried only one poet's work were a recognised class beside those who transmitted from several poets {cite:tdv-siir}. The ŞAİR entry adds that many later poets themselves grew up through this râvî experience — memorising and reciting the ancients' verse was the poet's apprenticeship {cite:tdv-sair}.

## The first transmitters of the hadith

From Islam's first days râvî meant the person who passed on what he had heard and learned from the Prophet; the Companions took it as a religious duty to teach others what they had heard {cite:tdv-ravi}. In the second half of the first century the office grew critical as reports multiplied: the Companions Abdullah ibn Amr ibn al-As, Ibn Abbas, Ibn Umar, Abu Sa'id al-Khudri, Jabir ibn Abdullah and Anas ibn Malik laboured to preserve the reports against fabrication, and their students — Abu al-Aliya al-Riyahi, Sa'id ibn al-Musayyib, Ibrahim al-Nakha'i, al-Sha'bi and Muhammad ibn Sirin among them — began asking for the isnad and investigating the transmitters {cite:tdv-ravi}.

## The generations of the râvîs

The traditionists developed the method of studying the isnad's transmitters by generations, fixing the first three as the Companions, the Followers and the followers of the Followers — the Companions' generation running from the beginning of revelation to 110 AH, the Followers' to 180 AH, and the first systematic hadith books, Malik's Muwatta' and Abd al-Razzaq's Musannaf, appearing in the third generation {cite:tdv-ravi}. The six imams of the canonical collections stand among the fourth and fifth generations; al-Dhahabi arranged 2,424 hadith memorisers and transmitters down to his own age into twenty-eight ranks, and Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani divided the first three centuries into twelve {cite:tdv-ravi}.

## The two tests of a transmitter

The râvî is the decisive element in judging a report: if one transmitter in the chain fails, the hadith falls whatever its other merits {cite:tdv-ravi}. Two qualities are demanded. Adala — uprightness — requires that he be Muslim, adult, sane, God-fearing and free of bad conduct. Dabt — precision — requires that he be neither forgetful nor careless: if he transmits from memory he must have memorised perfectly; if from a book it must be carefully written, checked and preserved; if he transmits the sense he must know the meanings of the words he changes and alter no meaning {cite:tdv-ravi}. The râvî who fulfils both is thiqa, trustworthy, and his report may be acted upon; one with disqualifying faults is weighed against the ten points of criticism and impugned accordingly {cite:tdv-ravi}.

## How the craft was practised

A report counted only when taken by one of the eight recognised ways of receiving (tahammul) fixed by the hadith scholars, and students travelled to living râvîs and faced every hardship to learn a report at first hand {cite:tdv-ravi}. Around the office grew its own vocabulary — fih al-râvî, adalat al-râvî, tabaqat al-ruwat, buldan al-ruwat — and the râvî of the qira'at, who carried the Qur'an-readings from the imams, is a recognised branch of the same title {cite:tdv-ravi}.
