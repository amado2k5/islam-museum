## Summary

The samkari tinned copper vessels — coating the inside of pots, ewers, trays and dishes with tin so that food and drink could be served from them safely {cite:lane-modern-egyptians pp. 139-144}. The hisba manuals obliged the coppersmiths to declare every soldered repair on a tinned piece {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXVIII, p. 48}, and in Evliya's Istanbul the tinners marched five hundred strong behind a patron whose story reached back to the caliph Omar {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 302, pp. 187-188}.

## Why copper needs tin

Copper vessels were the tableware of the Cairo house: Lane lists the tinned-copper mankal or brazier full of burning charcoal, the tisht and ibrik — basin and ewer — of tinned copper or brass, the round seneeyeh tray of tinned copper two to three feet across serving as the table, and several dishes of tinned copper holding the meal {cite:lane-modern-egyptians pp. 139-143}. Even the folding fanoos lantern, made of waxed cloth strained over rings of wire, had a top and bottom of tinned copper {cite:lane-modern-egyptians pp. 143-144}.

## The muhtasib's rules

Ibn al-Ukhuwwa compels the coppersmiths to inform the customer when they sell an article of copper containing solder; if it is tinned they must write "old, soldered" on it with a thick pen, so that the purchaser may clearly realise it {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXVIII, p. 48}. Al-Shayzari forbids mixing copper with the habag drawn off by the goldsmiths and silver-smelters, for it hardens the copper so that a drinking vessel or mortar moulded from it breaks easily like glass {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 32, pp. 98-99}. Broken old copper containers must not be smelted together with unused copper; each is smelted and worked separately {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 32, p. 98}.

## Tinning on a schedule

Tin wore off with use and heat, so the hisba put the craft on a timetable: Ibn al-Ukhuwwa rules that the maker of barley-water must wash his vessels every day and renew their tinning every three months {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXIV}. Workers must not add overmuch lead to the copper for smelting, for a mortar or bowl made of it breaks easily like glass if it falls; cast ware must be made solid, and the bowls and trays carry settled weights {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXVIII, p. 48}.

## The guild in procession

Evliya counts the tinmen (Kalaijian) at five hundred men with a hundred and six shops {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 302, p. 187}. Their patron is Sari, girded by Ali, commanding general under Omar against Nahavand: on the day of the decisive battle, when Sari was nearly defeated, Omar — praying at Medina — cried out through second sight, "O Sari, take the post of the mountain!", and the sound was heard by the Muslim troops at Nahavand, who planted the banners of victory on the mountain {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 302, p. 187}. Sari's tomb is at Cairo, descended to by nine steps in the courtyard of the mosque of Tawashi Suleiman in the interior castle {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 302, p. 188}.

## Hammers on the trunk

In the procession the tinmen passed with all kinds of hammers for turning, cleansing and stretching; they put a large trunk (tomruk) in the middle of the road, on which fourteen to sixteen men hammering with all their force showed the excellence of their art {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 302, p. 188}. The seniors of the guild had beards of different colours from the damp of the brass, some green, some sulphur-coloured {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 302, p. 188}. After them walked the head of the kettle-makers, the head of the polishers, and the boys of the mines of Kastenaim and Tashkoprili behind the eightfold Turkish music {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 302, p. 188}. The tin-melters (Kalaiji) are a separate hundred men, all Jews {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 220}.

## Not yet covered

The sources fix the guild's numbers, its patron, its fraud rules and the three-month tinning cycle, but not the price of a tinning, the tin supply of Istanbul — much of it European — or the tinner's apprenticeship {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXIV, XXXVIII; evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 302}.
