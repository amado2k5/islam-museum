## Summary

The imam leads the congregation in the prayer; the word also names the head of state, and this entry is the office of prayer leadership — the man who stands before the rows {cite:tdv-imam}. The office began as a deputyship: the Prophet appointed a substitute imam whenever he left Medina, and when his last illness kept him from the mosque he put Abu Bakr in his place {cite:tdv-imam}.

## The first imams

Before the hijra the Prophet is reported to have led his followers in prayer in the Ka'ba's precinct and then in outlying houses of Mecca, and after the first Aqaba pledge Mus'ab ibn Umayr and As'ad ibn Zurara established regular and Friday prayers in Medina {cite:tdv-imam}. In choosing imams for the newly converted tribes the Prophet looked first to knowledge of the Qur'an: the boy Amr ibn Salima of the Jarm was made his tribe's imam at seven or eight because he knew it best, and kept the office until death {cite:tdv-imam}. Women too led prayers in the earliest period — Umm Waraqa was appointed over her household and had an elderly muezzin {cite:tdv-imam}.

## Governors and deputies

Leading the prayer was long part of rule: governors held "the governorship of the prayer" (wilayat al-salat), led it themselves or named an imam, and when absent left the duty to deputies {cite:tdv-imam}. The Rashidun caliphs led the prayer in person; after Umar and Ali were struck down while leading it, Mu'awiya kept armed guards about him at prayer, and the Abbasid caliphs gradually left the five daily prayers — and after 329/940 the Friday prayer — to appointed officials {cite:tdv-imam}. Funeral prayers for the great were likewise led by the governors, and hajj emirs doubled as imams {cite:tdv-imam}.

## Pay from treasury and waqf

Regular salaries for imams are reported under the Abbasids — pay registers of Muqtadir's time, allotments by the Buyid Adud al-Dawla — and in Umayyad Cordoba the imams' stipends were administered from the beytülmal housed in the great mosque {cite:tdv-imam}. Thereafter the waqf carried the office: Cairo's 830 mosques drew 9,220 dirhams a month from waqfs in 403/1012, under Qalawun each imam had forty dirhams a month plus food, and the Sultan Hasan complex paid the four schools' imams sixty to a hundred dirhams with a Ramadan supplement {cite:tdv-imam}.

## The Ottoman neighbourhood imam

Ottoman imams, appointed by the sultan's berat, counted among the askeri exempt from reaya dues, and were normally proposed by the mosque's waqf trustee with the sheyhülislam's assent {cite:tdv-imam}. The mahalle imamı was far more than a prayer leader: until the Tanzimat he was the state's responsible man in the quarter — registering residents, births, deaths and marriages, standing surety for newcomers, overseeing the quarter's morals, its cleanliness, the avârız relief fund, and even the marriage contracts of non-Muslims, a practice the patriarchs protested {cite:tdv-imam}. Village and quarter posts often passed from father to son, and where several candidates stood, an examination decided {cite:tdv-imam}.

## Examinations and the state

The Tevcihat-i Cihat regulation of 1888 required candidates for imamate, preaching and muezzin posts to be examined — on fiqh and correct Qur'an recitation — before the Mahkeme Teftiş Heyeti in Istanbul and local boards in the provinces, and to produce certificates of good conduct {cite:tdv-imam}. Army imams did better than mosque ones: a battalion imam drew 160 kuruş a month in 1838 against a regimental chief imam's 500, while city imams made do on forty to a hundred {cite:tdv-imam}.
