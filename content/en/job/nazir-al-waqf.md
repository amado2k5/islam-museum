## Summary
The nazir of a waqf was the person who watched that the endowment was run on the founder's conditions. His work was called nezaret. The person who managed the waqf was the mutawalli, and that work was called tevliyet. The trustee was usually the founder, and after him his children. Sultans could not give their own waqfs enough attention, so the supervision of those waqfs was stipulated for the grand vizier, the shaykh al-Islam and the aghas of the gate. Out of that scattering a single administration later grew: the imperial ministry of waqfs, which Mahmud II founded in 1826. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

## The two charges
The entry separates two powers over every waqf. Tevliyet is management, done by the mutawalli. Nezaret is supervision, done by the nazir. Until the ministry was founded, working waqfs fell into three classes, which the entry takes from Elmalılı Muhammed Hamdi: ancient waqfs inherited from earlier Muslim states, irsadi waqfs created by granting state land, and sound binding waqfs that their founders set aside from their own property. Each waqf was a separate legal person, run by its deed, or by old custom where it had no deed, which is to say on the rules the founder had set. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

## Before one ministry
Beside the grand vizier, the shaykh al-Islam and the aghas of the gate, a nezaret was set up late in the tenth Hijri century to gather the waqfs assigned to the institutions of Mecca and Medina. That was the Evkaf-ı Haremeyn Nezareti, in 995 AH (1586). The waqfs of the sultans, the royal women, the pashas and the aghas of the Abode of Felicity were later joined to it. It was then run through four offices: inspection, accounting, tax farms, and the secretariat of the Abode of Felicity. Nezarets were added for the judges of Istanbul, Galata, Üsküdar and Eyüp, and for the kapudan pasha, the janissary agha, the sekban başı and the bostancı başıs. The entry says the number of waqf nezarets in Istanbul reached twelve. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

The entry says this scatter of offices produced many abuses. Abdülhamid I therefore set up, in 1188 AH (1774), an organisation for the supervision of the waqfs he founded under the name Hamidiyye. It worked in an administrative building he built inside those waqfs, and it had three offices: a deputy trusteeship, a waqf secretariat, and a day-book secretariat. When it was seen to succeed, the nezarets of the imperial harem and of the bostancı başı were attached to it. The name became Evkaf-ı Hamidiyye ve Mülhakatı, and from 1777 its management was given to the nazirs of the imperial mint. The Laleli waqfs and their annexes, for which Mustafa III had already built a separate department, were joined to the Hamidiyye waqfs in 1788. The entry presents this as the start of a central practice by the sultans for their own waqfs, and as the base of the later imperial ministry. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

In 1809 Mahmud II joined the waqfs he had founded to the Hamidiyye waqfs under the name Evkaf-ı Hamidiyye ve Mahmudiyye, and in 1814 he left the administration to the nazir of the imperial mint. That produced the deputy trusteeship of the three waqf groups, Laleli, Hamidiyye and Mahmudiyye. Further waqfs were added, and in 1826, when the janissary corps was abolished, the waqfs run by the janissary and sekban başı aghas were added too. The mint ministry could no longer run the whole. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

## The founding
The waqfs were taken out of the mint and gathered in an independent ministry, the Evkaf-ı Hümayun Nezareti. Yusuf Efendi was appointed its nazir. He had been nazir of the imperial mint and deputy trustee. İbnülemin Mahmud Kemal and Hüseyin Hüsameddin date the founding to 12 Rabi al-awwal 1242 AH (14 October 1826). The entry notes that the archive of the General Directorate of Foundations keeps a document of that same date signed by Yusuf Efendi as nazir of the imperial waqfs, which places the founding a few days earlier. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

The entry lists the reasons for the founding as the state papers and the studies it cites give them: to gather a scattered administration in one hand, to stop abuses in the waqf sector, to rebuild the state on a Western-style central pattern, to use the waqf's resources in other sectors, to reduce the power of religious circles, and to please Western friends. It then says that what was put first was ending disorder and arbitrariness in the sector, repairing the administration for the sake of society and the institution, and moving money between waqfs through the waqf treasury so that a waqf whose income did not meet its costs could be supported. A imperial rescript on joining the waqfs under the shaykh al-Islam gives the reason as setting the administration on a sound course. A later rescript, on joining every waqf except those of the Haremeyn and the shaykh al-Islam's office, gives the reason as building up the charitable works and carrying out their services. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

The entry also sets out another reading of the centralisation. It attributes to Westerners the view that holding real estate in the Ottoman lands would make it easier to press the central government, and the view that the state's survival, and its being counted among Western states, depended on taking the reforms they recommended, which in their view required a strong central government. It says the land code was imposed by political pressure from outside, and that European capital was not satisfied, because part of the land was miri and an important part was waqf. Neither could have its title taken or be expropriated easily, and transfer was difficult. The government could legislate for miri land. Waqf stood outside its reach. As the entry presents it, Europeans wanted miri land and waqf turned into goods that could be put on the market, and waqf land expropriated and mortgaged against credit. It says that after Western insistence the Sublime Porte issued the law extending transfer and the regulation on waqf buildings and rented waqf property, and so opened the sale of waqf that it had long promised, and that the founding and the work of the ministry played an important part in that. The entry attributes these points to Bernard Lewis and Edouard Engelhardt. Their books were not read for this record. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

## What was joined to it
First, in 1244 AH (1828), came the waqfs under the aghas of the gate, and in 1246 AH (1830) the waqfs under the bostancı başı, the topçu başı, the hazinedar başı, the kilercibaşı and the aghas of the new palace. In 1247 AH (1831) came the waqfs under the judges of Istanbul and the three towns and under the Haremeyn inspectorate. The entry understands that the waqfs formerly under the defterdar, the reis efendi and the agha of the Galata palace had already been joined. In the first five years the waqfs brought in reached 632. In the same year the ministry was given the waqfs of the kapudan pasha and the çavuş başı, and in 1248 AH (1832) the administration of the waqfs under the grand vizier. Then came fifteen waqfs in the hands of Davud Pasha, and the waqfs of Murad Hudavendigar and Bayezid I in Bursa. In 1251 AH (1835) came the waqfs of Karaosmanoğlu Hacı Hüseyin Agha and of Abu Ayyub al-Ansari in Bergama and its district, and a year later the water nezaret, which ran the running water of Istanbul, Üsküdar and Galata, most of it waqf. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

On 10 Rabi al-awwal 1254 AH (3 June 1838) the Haremeyn waqf ministry, already combined with the waqfs stipulated for the shaykh al-Islam, was joined to the imperial ministry. On the same date the ministry was combined with the mint ministry, and the nazir of the imperial waqfs entered the council of ministers. From the middle of 1250 AH (1834), trustees and accountants who were still in place were given a salary, and that class of waqf began to be annexed. Late in the reign of Abdülmecid (1839–1861) his waqfs were tied to the ministry by a rescript. The balances drawn for that annexation put the surplus of those waqfs at 4,177,315.5 kuruş. The ministry then took the supervision of every waqf in the empire except eight excepted items, and did their accounts. It became both trustee and nazir of the waqfs administered directly and of those treated as such, and the sole administrator of the annexed waqfs. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

## The offices
At the start the ministry had three departments. The kesedarlık wrote the decrees, reports and referrals. The zimmet deputy drew up the deeds of tax farms and leases, the debt bonds taken from the tax farmers and from the money-changers who stood surety for them, the farm prices, and the debt slips for rents. The sergi deputy received what came into the ministry treasury and paid the expenses. Each department was given two clerks and two apprentices. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

The entry says the structure kept changing. The largest changes came when the waqf nazirs entered the council of ministers in 1254 AH (1838), with the Tanzimat in 1255 AH (1839), and with the organisational regulations of 1297 AH (1880) and 1330 AH (1911). In the Second Constitutional years the centre, as the entry lists it, held the undersecretariat, a special council, a council of officials, the hospital of the Muslim strangers, and the general directorate of construction and repair, with a central technical board and provincial technical boards arranged by region. The general accounts directorate held the Anatolia, Rumelia and Arabian desks, and the directorates of supplies, of estates and endowed cash, and of the cashier. An inspection board covered nine regions on the provincial plan. There were also a legal advisership, the waqf court, a private bureau, and directorates for orders on charitable buildings, waqf real estate, waqf forests and land, learned institutions, waqf registers, personnel, papers, annexed waqfs, the museum of Islamic waqfs, and the Haremeyn. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

In the provinces it was decided, after the Tanzimat, that local councils under the judges of the place would administer the directly held waqf buildings and lands whose treasuries belonged to the imperial and Haremeyn waqf treasuries, and that the year's income would pass into the local state chests to be sent on to the waqf treasury. In 1258 AH (1842) it appeared that money collected over two years and delivered to the finance treasury for the waqf treasury had not been delivered. It was then decided that provincial waqfs would be run by officials whom the müşirs, the defterdars and the kaymakams chose from the local people and the customs staff. In 1261 AH (1845) it appeared that officials chosen locally could not administer the directly held provincial waqfs as intended. Independent directors were to be appointed from Istanbul, with enough clerks beside them. An instruction of fourteen articles, nine articles rewritten after the gedik clause was dropped, was put into force by a rescript of 27 Jumada al-awwal 1261 AH (3 June 1845). {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

The provincial service was first split into accountancies and directorates, by the size of the place and the amount of waqf there. Apart from district waqf offices, it reached 120 units: twenty-nine accountancies and ninety-one directorates. On 26 Shawwal 1296 AH (13 October 1879), to save on public spending, the accountancies stayed at twenty-nine and the directorates were cut to seventy-five. In 1280 AH (1863) the old regulation on the administration of waqf posts in the provinces and on the work of the waqf directorates was recast in nine chapters and fifty-six articles. The entry calls this the widest legal regulation of provincial waqf administration in the Ottoman period. It stayed in force until the Vakıflar regulation was accepted on 17 July 1936, under waqf law number 2762. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

## Revenue and what followed
The entry says the central ministry did not give the result that was wanted against scatter and abuse. Collecting the tithes of waqf villages and hamlets, and the prices of the tax farms, was given to the finance department. The share cut for the collection kept rising in favour of the state treasury. Later the whole surplus was taken, and 15 per cent of the total income was deferred, so that waqf revenue was held inside the finance treasury and waqf buildings fell into ruin. With Western encouragement and pressure, the means and income of the waqfs were moved into other sectors of the state, and then a legal settlement cancelled the debts between the state treasury and the waqfs both ways. The entry says the waqfs were left in debt and the trustees poor. Thousands of charitable buildings fell into ruin because running costs could not be met, and the services stopped. What stayed open stood by the help of people who gave. The entry also says that even in the nineteenth century, which it calls the age of the state's collapse, the waqf still covered about 16 per cent of Turkish employment and economic life, and that law and political decisions then damaged it widely. That percentage is taken from Öztürk's thesis, which the entry cites and which was not read for this record. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}

Under the constitution dated Rumi 1337 (1921), the ministry was set up in Ankara inside the government of the Grand National Assembly, as the Şer'iyye ve Evkaf Vekaleti. That ministry was abolished on 3 March 1924, and the waqfs became a general directorate under the prime ministry. {cite:tdv-evkaf-i-humayun-nezareti p.521-524}
