## Summary

The hassar plaits and sells the reed mat, the humbler covering that serves the mosque floor and the family courtyard alike {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVI, pp. 93-94} {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. III, p. 82}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa devotes a chapter of his hisba manual to the reed-mat makers and their dyes {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVI, pp. 93-94}, and Chalcraft records the trade still organized enough in 1907 for Cairo's mat-workers to strike as a guild {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 182-183}.

## The reed that may be dyed

No reeds but the qulzumi variety may be dyed, Ibn al-Ukhuwwa writes, for the others are rotted by dyeing and do not hold together {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVI, p. 93}. Only Cyprus madder may be used for the dyeing; logwood must not be used because it changes colour, and if anything acid falls on it it turns yellow and stays stained {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVI, p. 93}. If madder is occasionally dear and scarce, two-thirds madder to one-third logwood is permitted {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVI, p. 93}. The black dye for mats is made with iron-water and copperas {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVI, p. 93}.

## Dyed, dried and plaited

The mats should be dried on the spot in the dye-pit so as not to be weakened {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVI, p. 93}. The water used must be pure, for the matting is used in the mosques and men pray on it {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVI, pp. 93-94}. The mats are sewn up with regular flax thread, and no matting may be cut until it is properly plaited, otherwise the reeds dry and it turns out like a sieve {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVI, p. 94}. The matting runs by the square, or bayt; the best has a hundred stitches and the inferior pieces fewer {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVI, p. 94}. The criers who sell it are warned not to give short measure when they see strangers buying, to be truthful about what the merchant delivers to them, and not to cut the price {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVI, p. 94}.

## The mat in house and mosque

In Lane's description of a Cairo house, the paved durqa'ah is covered with a mat in summer, and with a carpet over the mat in winter {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. I, p. 18}. In the mosque, the pavement is covered with matting and rich and poor pray side by side, the wealthy man enjoying no distinction unless his servant brings and spreads a prayer-carpet for him {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. III, p. 82}.

## A living industry

Mat-making was still a widespread industry in the Egypt Chalcraft describes: the production of samar baladi, a mat made of a particular kind of reed, in Zaqaziq alone was worth about a hundred thousand pounds a year {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 115-116}. Mat makers used imported flax threads, and the industry suffered from foreign competition {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 119}.

## The mat-makers' strike

Within a week of the silk-weavers' strike of April 1907, Cairo's mat-makers struck too: the newspaper Al-Jarida reported a "New Strike" on 27 April 1907, when many workers of the mat-makers' guild, the ta'ifat al-husriyya, struck work and met in Manshiyya Square {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 182-183}. The workers were rumoured to be planning to attack the owners of some mat-making workshops, who petitioned the acting governor for precautionary measures; whether punishments or wage increases followed is not recorded {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 183}. Chalcraft notes that weavers and mat-makers had little disruptive power, since their workshops supplied goods in small quantities mainly to poorer customers {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 183}.

## Not yet covered

The sources give the mat-maker's dyes, plaiting and guild, but not the harvest and preparation of the reed itself, the looms or frames used, the basket-weaving side of the trade, or a named mat-maker {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVI, pp. 93-94} {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 182-183}.
