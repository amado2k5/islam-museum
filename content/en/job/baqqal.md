## Summary

The baqqal sold food and drink by retail — dates, raisins, figs, pistachios, hazel-nuts, honey, clarified butter and the dried goods of the store-cupboard {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 175-176; lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}. Evliya gives the grocers precedence in the procession of the guilds because they sell eatables and drinkables, and makes their patron Asim Ibn al-Nabbash, girded by Selman Pak, the first of all bakkals {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 175}.

## The first of the guilds

The grocers' patron lived a hundred and twenty years, was a favourite of the Prophet among his companions, and lies buried at Jerusalem near Abd-ul-jerrah {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 175}. In the procession the bakkals fixed tents on wagons, put on clean cloth, and adorned the tents with baskets of hazel-nuts, pistachios, raisins and figs, throwing them among the spectators {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 175-176}. Some dressed in robes made of onions and dates, carried rosaries of nuts, wore goats' horns on their heads and cried raisins and figs for sale {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 175-176}.

## The grocer's train

In the grocers' train marched the merchants of butter, the musk-soap sellers, and the eight hundred dried-beef merchants of five hundred shops whose shops stood outside the wood-gate at Kassim Pasha, Galata, Top-khanah and Scutari {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 175-176}. The leblebi merchants — four hundred men in a hundred shops, assistants to the chief of the grocers — passed roasting the chickpeas and grinding them into flour on small hand-mills, some kneading the flour with glue into a paste for the heads of clubs; leblebis, Evliya notes, are wholesome camp food for those with relaxed bowels {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 176}. After them came the Bakkal-bashi, head of the grocers, the rich grocers mounted on Arabian horses with pages and the eightfold Turkish music {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 176}.

## Three thousand articles

A second body of grocers, the Attaran, kept three thousand shops under their patron Hussam al-Din Ibn Abdullah of Basra {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 212}. The Egyptian grocers were established outside the prison-gate and passed on wagons loaded with baskets of ginger, pepper, cardamom, cinnamon, cloves, rhubarb, spikenard and aloes — three thousand articles altogether, whose qualities are enumerated, Evliya says, in the Tezkeret of David; all were kept in bottles and boxes {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 212-213}. Beside them marched the aloe-and-ambergris merchants perfuming their shops as they passed {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 212}.

## The Cairo market street

Lane's Cairo shows the same retail world on one street: the 'attar, druggist and perfumer who also sells wax candles; the tobacconist; the fruiterer; the seller of dried fruits; the oilman (zeiyat) who sells butter, cheese and honey as well as oil; the greengrocer; the butcher; and the baker to whom bread, meat and dishes are sent to be baked {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}.

## The muhtasib at the vegetable stall

Al-Shayzari's hisba covers the fresh end of the trade: the greengrocer must sell produce washed of manure and free of grass and weeds, and cut the roots off lettuce and radishes {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 40, pp. 133-134}. He must not wash fresh onions and garlic, because water increases their pungency and offensive smell, nor mix vegetables kept overnight with the day's picking, nor sell worm-eaten melons, cucumbers or figs, nor anything so over-ripe that its skin has broken {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 40, pp. 133-134}.
