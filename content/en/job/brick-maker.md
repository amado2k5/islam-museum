## Summary

The ajurri made bricks — sun-dried mud brick and kiln-burnt ajurr — the material on which the earliest Islamic building, from the Prophet's mosque in Medina to the walls of Ta'if and the streets of Ottoman Istanbul, rested {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1, the Medina mosque chapter}.

## Mud brick at Medina

Creswell records that the Prophet's mosque at Medina was built of sun-dried bricks made of mud from the valley of Baqi' al-Khabkhaba, and that the houses built for his wives Sawda and 'A'isha were likewise of mud brick {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1, the Medina mosque chapter}. Medina's houses were of unburnt bricks, and Umm Salama built an addition to her house with a wall of unburnt bricks {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1, the Medina mosque chapter}. The same fabric served the first garrison cities: Abu Musa al-Ash'ari reconstructed the mosque and the Dar al-Imara of Basra in sun-dried bricks in 17 AH {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1, the Basra mosque and Dar al-Imara section}.

## Libna and ajurr

The sources themselves keep the craft's two products apart: libna, the mud brick, and ajurr, the baked brick — the distinction Creswell finds in the account of the wall built around Ta'if {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1}. The brick-maker therefore worked two disciplines, the mould and the kiln, wherever the building culture rose beyond palm-trunks and reeds {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1}.

## The guild at Istanbul

In Evliya's procession the brick-makers (Kerpichjian) were five hundred men with thirty-five shops, and the makers of unburnt straw-bricks (Kupejian) a thousand men — the sun-dried and the kiln sides of the trade marching as separate corporations {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 619-620, pp. 230-231}.

## The bricks of Pir Pasha

In the suburb of Pir Pasha stood two hundred shops of bricklayers and brick-makers who worked the lime which, in the summer, is extracted from the bottom of the sea by Albanese divers {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 40-41}. This lime remains forty days exposed to the influence of the air, and is then worked by men who knead it with their hands and feet and make it into bricks; the houses of Constantinople, covered with these bricks, all show the peculiar red colour of the bricks of Pir Pasha {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 41}.

## Not yet covered

The sources fix the material's history and the guild census but not the kiln's fuel and firing days, the brick-tax on buildings, or the seasonal rhythm of the trade; the Pir Pasha account describes lime-mortar brickwork rather than fired clay brick {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1; evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 40-41}.
