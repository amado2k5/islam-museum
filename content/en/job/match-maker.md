## Summary

The fitilji twisted the slow match — the burning cord that fed fire to the matchlock. Evliya counts the match-makers (Fitiljian) at fifty men in thirty shops, marching in the arms-makers' procession twisting matches of seven cotton strings {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 270, pp. 152-153}. The gun they served is the matchlock (miltyk) described in Elgood's Bukhara chapter, its long fork attached to the barrel as a rest {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 p. 28}.

## Seven cotton strings

The match-makers (Fitiljian) are fifty men, with thirty shops. Their patron is the Prophet, because — Evliya says — he first burned a rope in the shape of a match {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 270, p. 152}. They pass twisting matches of seven cotton strings: seven strands of cotton laid into a cord that burns slowly and holds its fire, so that a musketeer can carry a lit match through a day of fighting.

## A munition, not an ornament

The fitilji's place in the procession marks the trade as an arm of war. After the match-makers come the head of the shaft-makers and the head of the gun-makers, with their seniors, sheykhs, kyayas and chaushes, mounted on Arabian horses and clad in armour; the assistants of the head of the gun-makers are all military {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 270, pp. 152-153}. The musket-makers themselves were a thousand men in four hundred shops in the same quarter of the procession {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 261, p. 180}.

## The lock it fed

The matchlock was the firearm of the east long after Europe had moved to flint. In Bukhara, Flindt records, the firearms in general use through the nineteenth century were the horse pistol (tupancha) and the matchlock (miltyk), the latter with a long fork attached to the barrel which served as a rest {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 p. 28}. The lock's jaws held the smouldering cord the fitilji twisted; without his slow match the finest miltyk was a club.

## A rope burned in the shape of a match

Evliya's patronage claim — that the Prophet first burned a rope in the shape of a match — is the guild's own way of saying its cord is as old as fire-making; it is the guild's story, not a documented history, and is recorded here as Evliya reports it {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 270, p. 152}.

## Not yet covered

The sources give the guild's numbers, patron, product and marching order, but not how the cord was treated to burn slow and steady (saltpetre steeping is assumed but uncited in these texts), nor prices, nor the craft outside Istanbul {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 270, p. 152; elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 p. 28}.
