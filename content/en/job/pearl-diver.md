## Summary

The ghawwas descended to the sea-bed on the diving-stone to gather the pearl-oysters, the core trade of the Gulf fisheries prosecuted for more than two thousand years {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, pp. 85-92}. Bahrein was the headquarters of the pearling fleet, and Pliny already named Catifa (El-Katif) the centre of an important fishery {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, pp. 85-90}. In the early twentieth century some 3,500 boats and about 35,000 fishermen worked the banks each season {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, pp. 88-91}.

## The ancient fishery

The pearl fisheries of the Persian Gulf are the most famous and valuable in the world, and have been prosecuted for more than two thousand years {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, p. 85}. Megasthenes described them about 307 BC, Isidorus of Charax and Nearchus noted them, Ptolemy placed the fishery at Tylos, the Roman name for Bahrein, and Pliny judged the pearls of Arabia within the Persian Gulf the most perfect of all {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, pp. 85-86}. In the ninth century the fisheries were noted by al-Mas'udi, in the twelfth by Benjamin of Tudela, and about 1336 by Ibn Battuta {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, p. 86}.

## The fleet and its crews

The pearling fleet employed about 3,500 boats, of which 1,200 of the best were owned at Bahrein, manned by approximately 35,000 fishermen {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, p. 91}. Beside the nakhoda or captain, often the boat's owner, the crew consisted of ghoas or divers, mainly Arabs and Sedees, and sebs or rope-tenders, usually Bedouins or Persians; each larger boat also carried a general utility man, the 'prayer-man', who relieved the sebs who stopped to pray {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, p. 91}. The fisheries are at their height from June to September, when nearly every person on the coast is interested in some capacity {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, p. 89}.

## The diving-stone and the day

Aside from a loin-cloth the diver is devoid of clothing; over each finger and thumb he wears a shield or stall of flexible leather against the sharp shells and coral, wearing out at least two sets each season {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, p. 92}. He descends feet foremost on an oval diving-stone of thirty to fifty pounds, suspended by a stout rope over outriggers and held by a slip-knot below the surface, two divers sharing one stone alternately {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, p. 92}. Closing his nostrils with a fitaam or nostril-clasp of flexible horn, he releases the slip-knot, sinks to the bottom, gathers what oysters he can in fifty seconds or more, then jerks the rope and is hauled up; each fisherman descends thirty or forty times in a day's work, and the shells of each diver are kept separate because the men work on shares {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, pp. 92-93}. After sunrise devotions and a light breakfast of dates or rice and coffee they begin; about noon they knock off for coffee, prayers and an hour's siesta; at sunset they eat dates and fish roasted over a charcoal fire {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, p. 93}.

## How long below

The average stay submerged at seven or eight fathoms rarely exceeds sixty seconds, though some remain seventy, eighty and even ninety seconds on special occasion; a fully substantiated instance records an Arab diver down 109 seconds in seven fathoms at Manaar in 1887, timed by the fishery inspector {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, pp. 93-94}. The authors weigh the older reports — Ibn Battuta's hour or two, Chardin's seven and a half minutes, Vernatti's quarter of an hour — and conclude the individual cases reported were extremely exceptional or the writers misinformed {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, pp. 94-96}.

## The oyster, the knife, the debt

Three varieties of pearl-oyster are fished: the mahar or Lingah oyster, which yields the finest pearls in greatest quantity, and the larger sudaifee and zinni {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, p. 96}. On the large boats the oysters lie on deck overnight and are opened the next morning with a curved knife, the miflaket {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, pp. 96-97}. The operations are financed mostly by Indian traders from Bombay who furnish capital for equipment and supplies and buy the pearls in gross lots — bearing hard on the fishermen, who derive little more than the crudest necessaries of life and are usually in debt to them {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, pp. 89, 98}.

## Today

The authors write that the fishery owes absolutely nothing to modern civilization in the method of securing the pearls: it is carried on practically as it was six hundred years ago, and probably without important variation for two thousand {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, p. 91}. What has changed since is the market around the diver: the cultured pearl and the oil economy, not the stone and the rope, ended the trade they describe {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, pp. 91-92}.
