## Summary
The kaghadi made paper from soaked and beaten fibres. The craft came to the Islamic world from China by way of Central Asia. The TDV encyclopaedia says the first mill in the Near East opened in Baghdad in 178 AH (794–795), that kinds of paper were named after officials and princes, and that mills followed in Syria, Egypt and al-Andalus. The Ottomans imported most of their paper for a long time, and the entry's first confirmed Ottoman mill dates from the eighteenth century. {cite:tdv-kagit p.163-166}

## Before paper
The entry begins with clay tablets, stone, metal, ivory and bone, then papyrus and parchment. The Arabic words for the materials it lists are rak for the thin white skin, and kirtas, qurtas or qartas for paper. It also gives mohrak and waraq for a white sheet. The entry says it is generally accepted that Ts'ai Lun invented paper in China in 105, though some push the date back two centuries, and that the origin of the word is disputed. {cite:tdv-kagit p.163-166}

## How it reached the Muslims
Under the Sasanians, the entry says, Chinese paper was costly and used only for some state papers. In Transoxiana the entry reports that the Chinese left the craft in Samarkand after the conquest, and that some researchers put its start there with the captives of Talas in 756. It adds that the museum should read this as the entry's account, since the Iranica assessment differs. The Hudud al-Alam is cited for Samarkand paper being exported worldwide. al-Jahiz is cited as naming only China and Samarkand as sources of paper. {cite:tdv-kagit p.163-166}

The entry says a man called Yusuf ibn Amr al-Makki is reported to have made cheap cotton paper in the Hijaz near the end of the Companions' time. It gives this as a report. {cite:tdv-kagit p.163-166}

## Baghdad, Syria, Egypt and al-Andalus
The entry calls the first Near Eastern mill the one opened in Baghdad in 178 AH (794–795) under Harun al-Rashid, on the proposal of [[person/al-fadl-ibn-yahya-al-barmaki|al-Fadl ibn Yahya]], a Barmakid. It says imitations followed in Damascus, Tripoli in Syria, Hama, Yemen and Egypt. {cite:tdv-kagit p.163-166}

The Fihrist is cited for six kinds of paper in use around 987: the Pharaonic, for Egypt, and those called after Sulayman ibn Rashid, Ja'far ibn Yahya al-Barmaki, Talha ibn Tahir, a second Tahir and the Samanid Nuh. In 1046 Nasir-i Khusraw found Tripoli's paper better than Samarkand's, and saw Cairo merchants wrapping goods in it. The entry says most of the people of Damascus made paper before the Crusaders destroyed the city in 1109, and that Ibn Battuta in 1326 saw paper shops there making a product in demand in European markets. {cite:tdv-kagit p.163-166}

For al-Andalus the entry cites al-Idrisi, who in the twelfth century reports the Muslims' mills at Xativa near Valencia as the first in Europe, with a paper called shatibi. Europe depended on the Near East and Spain until Fabriano made paper in 1276. {cite:tdv-kagit p.163-166}

## The fibre, the mill and the sheet
The entry says rags and rope were soaked a long time in limewater and then beaten in stone mortars with wooden pestles. Later a heavy stone turned by water power, like a flour mill, was used, and the entry says the Western term paper mill comes from this. Microscope work on early finds showed mulberry bark, hemp and rags. {cite:tdv-kagit p.163-166}

For the calligrapher and the illuminator, the sheet was coated with ahar, made with starch, egg white, alum, fish glue, lead white and rose petal. It was then burnished with flint or glass, and a mistake could be scraped off. The entry says that Eastern paper has no watermark, which makes undated sheets hard to date. {cite:tdv-kagit p.163-166}

## The Ottoman paper trade and mills
In the sixteenth century, the entry says, Ali's Menakib-i Hunerveran lists paper reaching Ottoman lands from Damascus, Samarkand, China, Iran and India. It ranks Damascus paper lowest, which the entry finds notable, since Damascus paper was known in the West as charta damascena. The entry says Eastern paper grew scarce from the sixteenth century and left the market in the eighteenth. Italian paper was imported from the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and the narh lists of 1600 and 1640 name Istanbul, Sultani, Abadi and other grades. {cite:tdv-kagit p.163-166}

The first confirmed Ottoman mill was founded by [[person/ibrahim-muteferrika|Ibrahim Muteferrika]], who set up a mill at Yalova in 1741 after the first Turkish press of 1729 and brought Polish papermakers. It closed after ten to fifteen years for lack of water, technical staff and against foreign competition. The Kagithane mill under Selim III lost about 1,500 kese and closed. The Beykoz mill opened in 1805 under the mint, aimed at English and Dutch quality and closed in 1832. A steam mill at Izmir ran from 1844 to 1846. The Hamidiye mill at Beykoz opened in January 1893 and, after a lawsuit, stood idle until 1912. {cite:tdv-kagit p.163-166}

## The Republic
The entry names the Izmit paper factory of 1936 and the later SEKA mills as the Republic's industry. It does not give an end date for hand papermaking in the Islamic world. {cite:tdv-kagit p.163-166}

## Not yet covered
The entry's bibliography was not read, nor the works it cites. The entry does not describe a guild of papermakers, their pir, their training, their mark or the tools of a mill by name. The museum has no person record for the Barmakid-era mill workers or for Ersoy's other named figures. {cite:tdv-kagit p.163-166}
