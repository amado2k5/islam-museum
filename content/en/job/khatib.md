## Summary

The khatib is the preacher who delivers the khutba — the address given to the congregation above all at the Friday and festival prayers {cite:tdv-hutbe}. Oratory was a widespread art in pre-Islamic Arabia, and in Islam the sermon became at once a literary form and a condition or complement of the act of worship {cite:tdv-hutbe}.

## The sermon's pillars

The jurists agree that the Friday khutba is obligatory: the Prophet never held the Friday prayer without a sermon, and the schools set its pillars — for the Shafi'is, praise of God, the blessing on the Prophet, the call to taqwa, prayer for the believers in the second sermon, and the recitation of a self-contained verse in one of the two {cite:tdv-hutbe}. The khatib must preach at the appointed time, in a state of purity for the Shafi'is, before an audience that can hear him — forty for the Shafi'is and Hanbalis, twelve for the Malikis, as few as one for the Hanafis — with a sit between the two sermons, brevity in the second, and for most scholars a staff or bow to lean on {cite:tdv-hutbe}.

## What makes a good khatib

From the Prophet's method of guidance the entry draws the preacher's qualities: sound knowledge before everything, sincerity, fervour and energy; proper dress, fine delivery and the maturity to engage his congregation; sermons that answer the community's needs, that invite rather than frighten, and that are kept short as the Prophet kept his short {cite:tdv-hutbe}.

## When the ruler stopped preaching

In the earliest times the caliph himself gave the Friday sermon and led the prayer; Ibn Khaldun records that the first to preach in a caliph's name was Abdallah ibn Abbas, Ali's governor at Basra {cite:tdv-hutbe}. Under the Abbasids the expansion of the empire and the weight of office withdrew the caliphs from the people: a religious scholar was appointed khatib, and the naming of the caliph in the sermon — with prayer for him — became the emblem of the caliphate {cite:tdv-hutbe}. A new caliph attended the first sermon of his reign and clothed the khatib in a robe of honour {cite:tdv-hutbe}.

## The sermon as a throne's seal

Because public allegiance was no longer taken in the mosque, the khutba took the bay'a's place: to have one's name read in the sermon was sovereignty, to drop a name was to depose {cite:tdv-hutbe}. Tahir ibn Husayn first had his own name read instead of al-Ma'mun's in 822; the Samanids refused to recognise al-Muti' rather than accept the Buyids' supremacy implied by the shared sermon; and women rulers too — Shajar al-Durr in Mamluk Egypt among them — had the sermon read in their names {cite:tdv-hutbe}. Nur al-Din Zangi wrote to his khatibs ordering that he be named only in the plain words he fixed and that their praise not exceed what he deserved {cite:tdv-hutbe}. In later centuries the sermon in the Ottoman sultan's name reached as far as Aceh, Java and the Indian subcontinent {cite:tdv-hutbe}.
