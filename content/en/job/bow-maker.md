## Summary

The qawwas made the bow — and at the top of his craft stood the composite bow of wood, horn and sinew, able to bend reflex without breaking {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 Latham & Paterson, pp. 78-79}. Latham and Paterson write that in the Muslim East the accomplished warrior was necessarily a proficient archer, trained to see his practice as a religious duty, and that the crafts supplying archers were esteemed enough for the sons of Ottoman sultans to be apprenticed to bowyers {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 Latham & Paterson, pp. 78-79}. In Evliya's Istanbul the bow-makers were five hundred men in two hundred shops, and Sultan Bayazid himself had been a bow-maker {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 332, p. 195}.

## A religious craft

In the Islamic lands, Latham and Paterson explain, the archer was taught that his training was a duty he discharged as the vicarious agent of the whole community, and the ruling classes were proud to be known as skilled archers — the evidence in miniatures where the bow appears in the hands of kings and governors, and in ornamental thumb-rings worn by men of substance {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 Latham & Paterson, p. 78}. Sultan Mehmet III, skilled in making thumb-rings, would fashion a ring himself and present it to a person he wished to honour {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 Latham & Paterson, p. 78}.

## Wood, horn and sinew

The composite bow begins as a wooden core that fixes its final form; the belly, compressed when the bow is drawn, carries a facing of horn, and the back, under tension, a backing of shredded sinew — the correct balance of the three components making a highly efficient weapon {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 Latham & Paterson, p. 78}. McEwen's dissection of an Indian chahar kham shows what that meant in the workshop: a core of nine pieces of rain-tree wood joined by fish-tail splices so the grain is never cut across, the belly side flattened in India but arched by the Ottoman Turkish bowyers, and core and horn grooved together to strengthen the glue {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 McEwen, pp. 90-92}.

## The sinew back

The greater part of the bow's power came from the sinew back: tendon (pay) taken mainly from the Achilles tendon of an ox, scraped clean, dried, hammered soft and dragged through a strong iron comb until it resembled fine white flax {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 McEwen, p. 91}. The fibres, three to fourteen inches, were sorted by length because the bending sections must be covered by a single length; the bundles were weighed — four dirhams for the handle, two and a half for each rigid gusheh end, four for each limb, seventeen dirhams for one layer — and a finished bow could carry up to three layers {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 McEwen, pp. 91-92}. The black water-buffalo horn, a quarter inch thick over the limbs, tapered to three-sixteenths at the handle {cite:elgood-islamic-arms-armour-1979 McEwen, p. 91}.

## The guilds of the bow

Evliya gives the bow-makers (Yaijian) five hundred men in two hundred shops at Sultan Bayazid, at the monument of Murad Pasha, at Galata and Scutari; their patron Muhammad al-Akbar son of Abu Bakr was girded by Ali in the Prophet's presence, and they adorned their shops with the bows called Sheptsah, Tozkoparan and Deli Forbad {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 332, pp. 195-196}. The arrow-makers (Okji), three hundred men in two hundred shops, claimed as patron Abu Muhammad Ibn Umran al-Qawwas, who had charge of the Prophet's bow and arrows and made arrows in time of peace; their shops showed bundles of arrows — Jeki, Puta, Asmaish, Pishrew, Pelenk, Khadenk, Nawek, Sehm and others {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 333, pp. 196}. The crossbow-makers (Zenberekjian), eighty men in ten shops, served the Janissaries' crossbow regiment and the exercise house on the At-maidan {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 334, p. 196}. Beside them marched the exercise-masters, the bowmen (Kemankeshan) under their patron Sa'd Ibn Abi Wakkas bending the bows called Munteha, Ailan and Sheddan, and the wrestling archers of the At-maidan {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 336-338, pp. 196}.

## The Ok-maidan

On the Ok-maidan, the arrow's-place, stood a convent of bowmen whose room hung with the arrows, bows and weapons of famous bowmen and wrestlers; outside, many columns marked the celebrated shots, the latest those of Sultan Murad IV, and the Ayetji-bashi's office was to strangle with the bow-string anyone who stole an arrow from where it had hit the mark {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 88-89}. The market of the bow-makers gave its name to a quarter and held the sebil of Kana'an Pasha {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 51-52}.
