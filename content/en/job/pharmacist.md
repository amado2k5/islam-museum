## Summary

The saydanan — the pharmacist — is the maker and seller of compounded medicines {cite:tdv-eczacilik}. The hisba manuals treat his shop as the most dangerous in the market: drugs and potions are of various natures and purposes, and a changed ingredient can harm the sick, so the muhtasib inspects the druggists' goods every week and learns to uncover their frauds {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 17, p. 64}. The craft split off from medicine under the Abbasids in the ninth century, opened its first pharmacy — so it is thought — in Baghdad in 148/765, and under the Ottomans passed from the attar's shop to the licensed eczahane and then to the state's own pharmacy school {cite:tdv-eczacilik}.

## The parting of the crafts

Saydele, the Arabic word for pharmacy, came in from Sanskrit, and the art was also called fenn-i saydelânî or fenn-i ispenciyârî {cite:tdv-eczacilik}. At first the physicians themselves ran it: they prescribed the medicines and prepared them from raw materials bought from the root-diggers and herb-men called kökçü and otçu, and from the attars {cite:tdv-eczacilik}. The separation of medicine and pharmacy happened in the Islamic world under the Abbasids in the ninth century, the pharmacists now counted distinct from the attars as well as from the physicians; the caliph al-Ma'mun brought the making of medicines under state control {cite:tdv-eczacilik}. The first pharmacy, little different in look from an attar's shop, is thought to have opened in Baghdad in 148/765 {cite:tdv-eczacilik}.

## The art of compounding

From the ninth century the pharmacies kept a separate drug-making laboratory, producing about seventy forms of medicine — powders, syrups, electuaries, pastilles (akras) and suppositories — and blending the ones taken by mouth with flavouring and perfuming agents to ease their taking {cite:tdv-eczacilik}. The sources divide medicines into the simple (el-edviyetü'l-müfrede) — the single "drog" that is also the raw material of the compound drugs — and the compound (el-edviyetü'l-mürekkebe), wider in use because of their many effects {cite:tdv-eczacilik}. The Muslim pharmacists brought new drug-forms and new drogs into medicine and developed the tools and techniques of making them: al-Zahrawi's Tasrif pictures, in its twenty-eighth treatise, wooden moulds for casting pastilles and a three-layered sieve for straining the infusions of herbs {cite:tdv-eczacilik}. Al-Biruni, reckoned the father of pharmacy, defined the pharmacist's profession and set out his duties plainly in the Saydana; Ibn al-Baytar counted some fourteen hundred herbal drogs, and Kuhin al-Attar's Minhaj al-dukkan was the druggists' handbook {cite:tdv-eczacilik}.

## The watched shop

The muhtasib intimidates the druggists, exhorts them and warns them of punishment, and inspects their drugs every week {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 17, p. 64}. The frauds are legion: Egyptian opium cut with horned poppy, with the sap of wild lettuce and with gum; Chinese rhubarb with the "animal rhubarb" used by veterinarians; bamboo concrete with bones burnt in a furnace; frankincense with rosin and gum; costus with elecampane roots; scammony with hardened latex sap, with ground horn kneaded in resin water, or with bean and chick-pea flour {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 17, pp. 64-67}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa counts the druggists among the most important of the muhtasib's charges, holds that only persons of knowledge and experience may sell, and requires that drugs be bought singly from the druggist and then compounded {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXV, pp. 38-39}.

## From attar shops to the eczahane

Evliya sorts the seventeenth-century Istanbul drug-trades into attars, sellers of medicinal waters, electuary-makers, rose-water sellers, amber-sellers, incense-sellers, sellers of medicinal oils, esrar-sellers, opium-sellers, tutty-sellers and herb-men; he counts about two thousand attar shops and three hundred itinerant attars, the wholesalers selling in the Mısır Çarşısı {cite:tdv-eczacilik}. The apothecaries proper — sellers of physic and sherbets — he passes at six hundred men with five hundred shops at Sultan Bayezid's mosque, near Khoja Pasha, in Meidanjik and at Galata, marching in the procession all clad in armour because their medicines are much wanted in the camp for wounded warriors {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer vol. 1 pt. 2, sec. 57, p. 118}. Pharmacies in the modern sense arise from the mid-eighteenth century, and multiply under the influence of the French and English military physicians and pharmacists who came to Istanbul in the Crimean War; in 1868 there were about fifty in Istanbul and ten in Üsküdar, run by Ottoman non-Muslims or foreigners, Muslims opening pharmacies only from the 1880s and the count reaching about two hundred by 1900, ten of them Turkish-owned {cite:tdv-eczacilik}.

## Learning the craft

Until the nineteenth century the pharmacist was made by master and apprentice, as in Europe {cite:tdv-eczacilik}. The first pharmacy teaching in the Islamic lands opened in 1839 as a three-year eczacı class in the Mekteb-i Tıbbiyye-i Adliyye-i Şâhâne, at first for military boarders posted afterwards to the military hospitals; civilian day-students were taken from 1858 {cite:tdv-eczacilik}. In that period apprentice-trained men could still open a shop if a board of Mekteb-i Tıbbiyye teachers examined them and granted the tezkire of a master pharmacist: such men were "second-class pharmacists", while diploma-holders of the school or of a foreign pharmacy school were "first-class" {cite:tdv-eczacilik}. The licensing of apprentice-trained men ended under the 1861 İspençiyarlık nizamnâme, applied from 1863; a Mülkiye class opened in 1867, and on the union of the military and civil medical schools in 1908 the two pharmacy classes were replaced by the Eczacı Mekteb-i Âlîsi, which from 1909 taught at Kadırga and remained Turkey's only pharmacy school until 1960 {cite:tdv-eczacilik}.

## Today

The pharmacist's shop of 1900 — reception before, laboratory behind a wooden glazed partition, porcelain drug jars from France on the shelves and poisons in special cabinets — already shows the modern pharmacy {cite:tdv-eczacilik}. As ready-made müstahzarât-ı tıbbiyye entered treatment, the pharmacist turned from a maker of medicines to a seller of them, and Ottoman-made "müstahzarât-ı tıbbiyye-i Osmâniyye" such as Pertev's syrup and Ali Süreyya's elixir were exported abroad {cite:tdv-eczacilik}.

## Not yet covered

The sources give the separation of the craft, the inspection regime and the Ottoman licensing, but not the working life of a named saydanan, the compounding books in daily use, women in the trade, nor pharmacists outside Baghdad, Cairo and Istanbul {cite:tdv-eczacilik} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch.
