## Summary
The nadim was the ruler's companion at the drinking gathering. He kept him company with poetry, song or wit, and he might also speak with him on a serious matter. The word means a cup companion and a friend, and mujalis and musahib are used in the same sense. Al-Jahiz traces the rules of this work, as it appears in the Umayyad and Abbasid courts, to Sasanian court custom. The Ottomans more often said musahib, though some of their sources still say nadim. {cite:tdv-nedim p.509-510}

## The word
The entry says the word means a cup companion and a friend, and then a person who keeps the ruler company by his wit, his knowledge and his skills. Mujalis and musahib are used in the same sense. The Ottomans preferred musahib, and nadim still occurs in some of their sources. The entry points for this to its article on the musahib, which was not read. It says the word is traced to the regret of Jadhima al-Abrash after he killed two nadims of his who became a proverb, Malik and Aqil. It points to al-Tha'alibi's Thimar al-Qulub, pages 182–183, and to al-Baghdadi's Khizanat al-Adab, volume 4, page 304, which were not read. The museum has no person record for Malik or for Aqil. {cite:tdv-nedim p.509-510}

From what Ibn Habib reports, the entry understands that in the pre-Islamic period each of the leading men of Quraysh had a nadim. It points to al-Muhabbar, page 173, which was not read. A man of one tribe who became a close companion of another tribe was counted as their nadim. The entry says that [[person/nuaym-ibn-masud|Nuaym ibn Masud]] was, in the pre-Islamic period, the nadim of Banu Qurayza, and it points to Ibn Hisham's Sira, parts 3–4, page 229, which was not read. {cite:tdv-nedim p.509-510}

## At court
A court nadim accompanied the ruler at the drinking gathering and entertained him with song, poetry or wit. The name of these gatherings is munadama. Al-Jahiz, in the Taj fi Akhlaq al-Muluk, says that Ardashir ibn Babak, a Sasanian king, set the first rules of the nadim's work and service, and that the same appears in the Umayyad and Abbasid courts under the influence of the old Iranian court. The entry points to pages 23, 24 and 25–26, which were not read. The entry takes the drinking gatherings of the Persian court in the Hebrew Bible, and the rules the officials there had to keep, as belonging with munadama, and it points to Esther 1:5–7. The text is not copied here. {cite:tdv-nedim p.509-510}

Al-Jahiz divides nadims into three classes: the great masters of music, the companions of learning and of standing, and then those who clown, play, perform and sing. Some rulers had a curtain between themselves and their nadims. The nadim showed his skill when the ruler asked, at a sign from the master of the curtain, in a set order and at the most suitable time. The entry places [[person/ibrahim-al-mawsili|Ibrahim al-Mawsili]], Ibn Jami and [[person/zalzal|Zalzal]] in the first class of the masters of music, and [[person/amr-al-ghazzal|Amr al-Ghazzal]] and Ishaq Barsuma in the second. Each class of musicians had its own repertory. The entry says that when Ishaq Barsuma was asked for a song of Ibn Jami's, he said he would perform it only if he were raised to the first class, and he was raised. It points to page 41 of the same book, which was not read. Yazid II abolished this ranking among nadims. The Abbasid court kept it. A nadim was not only a poet or a musician. Some of them had learning and culture in various arts. It is reported that Bahlul the wise and [[person/al-baladhuri|al-Baladhuri]] also served as nadims. The museum has no person record for Bahlul that matches this report, so the occupation is written only for al-Baladhuri, and only as a report, not as a dated appointment. {cite:tdv-nedim p.509-510}

## What was asked of him
The work continued under the Seljuks and in other Muslim states. Nizam al-Mulk, al-Rawandi, and Kaykavus ibn Iskandar, who according to one report was himself the nadim of the Ghaznavid sultan Mawdud ibn Masud, describe the qualities looked for in a nadim and the work he did. Their books were not read. On their account a nadim is well mannered and virtuous, able to keep a secret, of good character, well dressed, cheerful and cultivated, and especially able to write a fine hand. He knows poetry, medicine, astrology, drinks, games, entertainment gatherings, hunting, polo and wrestling. He is serious when the moment is serious, and witty when wit is called for. In danger he does not hold back from putting himself in front of the ruler. Nadims who could speak with the sultan on the most serious matters would, when the moment came, make him think with a wise saying, and at another time stir him with songs and poems. They played chess with him and tried to lift his grief. Some rulers showed their nadims great favor and gave them valuable gifts. The museum has no person record for Kaykavus ibn Iskandar. {cite:tdv-nedim p.509-510}

## Training and dress
The entry says a nadim received a special training before he took up the post. He was taught how to behave in the sultan's presence. If he had to warn him, he was told not to say it outright, but to bring an example from past rulers. The nadim's dress differed with the time and with other circumstances. Hilal al-Sabi says that the dress of munadama was, in general, a gold-embroidered turban, an inner robe, an outer robe called a mubattana, and an open-front jubba, with ornaments and scent. The entry points to Rusum Dar al-Khilafa, page 96, which was not read. Ibn al-Nadim sets aside a section of the Fihrist, from page 157 on, for books of the manners, the rules and the stories of nadims. That section was not read. {cite:tdv-nedim p.509-510}
