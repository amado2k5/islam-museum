## Summary

The mutakallim practises kalam, the discipline that takes Islam's first principles as its subject: establishing the creeds with decisive proofs and demolishing counter-arguments by rational method {cite:tdv-kelam}. Al-Farabi defined it as a debative faculty that confirms the beliefs and acts laid down by the religion's founder and shows the falsity of whatever contradicts them; al-Iji described it as the science that gives the power to prove religious beliefs with certain evidences {cite:tdv-kelam}.

## What the science is for

Kalam answers criticisms directed at the whole religion: the entry's own synthesis defines it as the science that determines Islam's principles for belief and conduct from the texts and grounds them by rational methods, noting that its task is not defence alone but proving the truth of the principles, drawing where relevant on the social and natural sciences {cite:tdv-kelam}. Taşköprizade gave it two conditions: that the principles be present in the Qur'an and sound Sunna, and that they be confirmed by rational method {cite:tdv-kelam}. The discipline was also known as tawhid, usul al-din, al-fiqh al-akbar, aqa'id and the science of nazar and istidlal {cite:tdv-kelam}.

## The first mutakallimun

The entry credits Jahm ibn Safwan as the first scholar to use the kalam method, putting reason before the texts and preparing the ground for the Mu'tazila {cite:tdv-kelam}. At Basra, al-Hasan al-Basri's student Wasil ibn Ata launched the Mu'tazila about 100-120 AH (718-738) by holding the grave sinner to stand between belief and unbelief, and he is credited with the first kalam book {cite:tdv-kelam}. The Mu'tazila then grew through Amr ibn Ubayd, Abu al-Hudhayl al-Allaf, al-Nazzam, al-Jahiz and the Jubba'is into a major school, splitting into Basran and Baghdadi wings whose scholars differed in detail but united on interpreting the texts in the light of reason {cite:tdv-kelam}.

## The Sunni masters

As the schools formed, conservative scholars — mostly hadith men — reacted against the kalam enterprise itself, and the emergent Sunni mainstream divided between the Salafiyya, who refused rational instruments altogether, and the Sunni kalam school of the Ash'ariyya and Maturidiyya {cite:tdv-kelam}. Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari, who had taken kalam lessons from the Mu'tazili Abu Ali al-Jubba'i and defended Mu'tazilism until the age of forty, left the school declaring its views contrary to both text and reason, and founded the Ash'ari line in the Middle East {cite:tdv-kelam}. Abu Mansur al-Maturidi, of Abu Hanifa's scholarly tradition, founded the second Sunni school in Transoxiana, perhaps earlier still; joined to the Hanafi legal school it came to hold the Muslim majority by itself {cite:tdv-kelam}.

## Mutakaddimun and muta'akhkhirun

The early period of the mutakallimun closed with al-Ghazali among the Ash'aris and Abu al-Mu'in al-Nasafi among the Maturidis; its characteristic was an Islamic rationalism rooted in the Qur'an, working with instruments like the analogy of the seen to the unseen, sebr ve taksim and the validation of proofs, rather than a philosophical metaphysics {cite:tdv-kelam}. Al-Ghazali opened the later period by bringing classical logic into the religious sciences and reconciling kalam with tasawwuf; after him, in the works of Fakhr al-Din al-Razi, al-Amidi and Qadi Baydawi, philosophical topics came to occupy more space than kalam questions proper {cite:tdv-kelam}. From al-Iji onward the sharh and hashiya tradition began, reaching its peak with al-Taftazani, al-Jurjani and al-Dawwani, which the entry marks as the science's period of stagnation {cite:tdv-kelam}.

## The new ilm-i kelam

In the nineteenth century, translations and books by Western-educated intellectuals carried materialist, evolutionist, positivist and Freudian criticisms into the Islamic world, and the classical science's proofs proved insufficient against them {cite:tdv-kelam}. Nearly all the scholars of the age felt the need for a rebuilt kalam: the 'new ilm-i kelam' associated with İzmirli İsmail Hakkı, Elmalılı Hamdi, Muhammad Abduh, Sayyid Ahmad Khan, Shibli Nu'mani and Muhammad Iqbal reopened the gate of ijtihad, partly set aside school partisanship and took up the old questions afresh, weighing God's existence by the argument from purpose and order and answering each new critique in turn {cite:tdv-kelam}.
