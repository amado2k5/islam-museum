## Summary

The jabbas coated walls and ovens with plaster: gypsum and lime burnt in the kiln, slaked, mixed into a paste and spread on the wall {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXIX, p. 95}. His craft depends on the lime, so the hisba manuals regulate the lime-burners beside him and bind the masons by oath not to take their bribes {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXIX, p. 95}. Evliya marches the plasterers through Istanbul at five hundred men with twenty-five shops, their plaster-mills turning as they go {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 610, pp. 230-231}, and in nineteenth-century Cairo plasterers were among the building workers who migrated from Malta, Italy and Greece {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 47}.

## The lime under the muhtasib

Ibn al-Ukhuwwa makes the masons swear not to take bribes or gifts from the lime-burners and makers of gypsum who wish their acquiescence when the lime is insufficiently burnt or of bad quality {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXIX, p. 95}. The mark of well-burnt lime is that it is yellow in the kiln before being ground; when mixed with water and placed in a vessel it dries quickly, otherwise it is adulterated, and no old lime may be used nor any but the best {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXIX, p. 95}.

## The plasterer's mix

The plasterers' own rule is about proportion: when plastering a house white they must not use too large an admixture of lime with the white plaster when it is made into a paste for ease of spreading on the walls, for with an excess of lime the plaster falls from the wall when the owner repairs it {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXIX, p. 95}. For the ovens, only clean earth mixed with pure water may be used as plaster; none may be used which is taken from the shops and mingled with blood and dung {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XIV, p. 31}.

## The plaster-mills of the procession

In the builders' train Evliya counts the plasterers (Alchejian) at five hundred men with twenty-five shops, then the mortar-makers four hundred with eighty shops, and the chalk-makers in their kinds: plain chalk-makers five hundred, greasy-chalk six hundred, marble-chalk forty and stone-chalk fifty, plus a thousand makers of the waterproofing loqun {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 610-617, p. 230}. As they pass, the plasterers put their plaster-mills in motion and the chalk-makers crunch chalk, whitening their faces and dress with it {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 231}. The whitewashers (Badanaji) carry in troughs marble chalk made into a wash and long poles tipped with brushes of swine bristles with which they whiten the walls {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 627, pp. 231-232}.

## The migrant plasterers

In the Cairo of the cotton boom, stone workers, plasterers, decorators and builders from Malta, Italy and Greece had skills which made their labour attractive to consumers who wanted work in European styles, and European artisans paid no professional taxes before 1891 {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 47}. Yet the local trade absorbed them: the census of 1897 put European subjects at only three percent of the numbers employed in the construction trades {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 47}. The patron's complaint survives too, the charge that ruins the underbidding builder: the stone is too rough, the marble weak, the plaster insufficient, the whitewashing one coat only, the gypsum cold {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 51}.

## Today

Gypsum plaster and lime wash still coat walls, and the jabbas's ancient test — does the coat hold when the wall is opened again — is the same one the Cairene proprietor made his workmen swear to {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXIX, p. 95} {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 51}.
