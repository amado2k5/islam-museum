## Summary

The kahveci traded, roasted, ground and served coffee — a guild of five hundred men in three hundred shops in Evliya's Istanbul, each merchant worth a thousand purses of capital in Egypt, Yemen, Sana'a and Aden {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 434, pp. 214-215}. A hundred mortars thundered for the trade at the Takhnus-khanah, and Lane found the Egyptian cup strong, unsugared and sometimes perfumed with ambergris {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 162; lane-modern-egyptians pp. 157-158}.

## A new invention

In the procession the coffee-merchants load some hundred loads of coffee on wagons and weigh it out by the quintal {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 434, p. 215}. Evliya confesses he does not know their sheykh, because coffee is a new invention — but the drinking of it, he says, comes from Sheikh Shazeli {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 434, p. 215}.

## The thundering mortars

The Inspector of the Takhnus-khanah keeps the place where the coffee is ground: three hundred men are employed in grinding coffee for all Constantinople, the tenth of which goes to the Public Treasury, while a cook of the Janissaries is appointed to watch for the preventing of all noise and riot {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 162}. A hundred mortars are always at work, their noise resembling thunder; for an occa of ground coffee three aspers are paid to the grinder and two to the Inspector, and three ovens roast the coffee at the establishment {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 162}.

## The butchers' objection

When the great Egyptian merchants of rice, hemp, coffee and sugar disputed precedence with the butchers, the butchers attacked their goods: coffee is an innovation, they said, which curtails sleep and the generating power in man; coffee-houses are houses of confusion; coffee has been by law declared illicit in the great collections of fetwas called Bezazieh and Tartar-khanieh, wherein everything that is burnt is declared illegal food {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 136-137}.

## The Egyptian answer

The merchants replied in money: the cargoes of their fleet coming from Egypt give annually no less than eleven thousand purses to the customs — and their precedence, they argued, must follow their contribution {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 138}. In the same debate the henna of Egypt and its sanctified use were defended alongside the coffee {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 137-138}.

## The cup of Cairo

Lane gives the Egyptian making of it: the coffee is made very strong, without sugar or milk; the small cup, the fingan, of porcelain or Dutch-ware and without a handle, is placed within a zarf of silver or brass {cite:lane-modern-egyptians p. 157}. In preparing the coffee the water is first made to boil; the coffee, freshly roasted and pounded, is then put in and stirred, the pot returned to the fire once or twice until it simmers, and poured while the surface is yet creamy {cite:lane-modern-egyptians pp. 157-158}. A little cardamom-seed is often added; the wealthy melt a carat of ambergris in the pot or fumigate the cup with the smoke of mastic {cite:lane-modern-egyptians p. 158}. To his customers the Cairo tradesman offers a pipe and a cup of coffee, which is obtained from the nearest coffee-shop {cite:lane-modern-egyptians p. 158}; and the officers of the Zabit, the chief of police, often visit the coffee-shops to observe the conduct and listen to the conversation of the citizens {cite:lane-modern-egyptians p. 119}.

## Not yet covered

The sources give the guild's numbers, the grinding establishment's fees and the cup's etiquette, but not the coffee-houses' keepers as a guild of their own, the price of a cup, the routes from Mokha, or the coffee trade outside the capitals {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 434, p. 215; lane-modern-egyptians pp. 157-158}.
