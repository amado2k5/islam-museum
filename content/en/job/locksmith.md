## Summary

The qaffal made locks and their keys — a trade Ibn al-Ukhuwwa calls an important one, for in it lies the security of wealth and protection for womenfolk {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVIII, p. 95}. In Evliya's Istanbul the locksmiths were a thousand men in five hundred shops {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 282, p. 185}, and in Lane's Cairo every door of the house carried the wooden lock called the dabbeh {cite:lane-modern-egyptians Introduction, pp. 19-20}.

## A trust, not only a trade

Ibn al-Ukhuwwa rules that carpenters who make locks must have appointed over them a man possessed of honesty and experienced in their craft, for it is an important one since therein lies the security of wealth and protection for womenfolk {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVIII, p. 95}. They must be forbidden to make a key after the pattern of another unless both are for persons who are partners and known for their honour {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVIII, p. 95}.

## The teeth of the lock

The head of the piece in which the movable inner teeth are fixed must not be pierced, lest they should be thrown out; the piece must be morticed in, a place for the teeth being hollowed out in it {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVIII, pp. 95-96}. The upper ends of the teeth should be squared, the other ends round and filed smooth and flat at the tip; similarly for the teeth of the key, so that the projecting parts of the lock shall not be harmed either from above or below {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVIII, pp. 95-96}. The locks themselves must be differentiated in pattern by one another {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVIII, p. 96}.

## The dabbeh of Cairo

In Lane's Cairo, every door was furnished with a wooden lock called a dabbeh {cite:lane-modern-egyptians Introduction, p. 19}. A number of small iron pins, four, five or more, dropped into corresponding holes in the sliding bolt as soon as it was pushed into the staple of the door-post; the key had small pins made to correspond with the holes, which pushed the pins up so the bolt could be drawn back {cite:lane-modern-egyptians Introduction, pp. 19-20}. The wooden lock of a street-door was commonly about fourteen inches long; those of the doors of apartments and cupboards about seven, eight or nine {cite:lane-modern-egyptians Introduction, p. 20}.

## The locksmiths' guild

Evliya counts the locksmiths at a thousand men with five hundred shops; their patron is David, and in the Prophet's time Aziz-ud-din Samahi, who was girded by Selman Pak and is buried at Shiraz — from him all locksmiths and those who work small articles in iron derive their skill {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 282, p. 185}. They adorned their shops with locks, keys, rings and other small articles in iron, some gilt, some tinned {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 282, p. 185}. The locket-smiths (Kilidjian), two hundred men with a hundred shops, had as their patron Zeid the Indian, a favourite of Selman Pak, whose tomb is in India in the port of Div {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 285, p. 185}.

## Precedence in iron

The farriers, a thousand men in a hundred and fifty shops, pretended to have the precedency over the locksmiths on the ground that theirs is one of the most indispensable guilds in wartime; at last they were ranked as the locksmiths' assistants {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 296, pp. 186-187}.

## Not yet covered

The sources give the wooden pin-tumbler lock and the guild's numbers, but not the iron lock trade of Anatolia and Syria in detail, the craft of the key-cutters as a separate guild, or the prices of locks {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LXVIII, pp. 95-96; evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 282, p. 185}.
