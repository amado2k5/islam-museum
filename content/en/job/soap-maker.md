## Summary

The sabuni boiled oil with alkali and salt water until it set into soap, and sold it to the baths and houses of the town {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 245, pp. 174-175}. The craft's raw material was watched by the muhtasib: the millers of the alkali plant and the sellers of the lote leaves used in washing had their own rules against adulteration {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIII, pp. 53-54}, and the bath floor itself was scrubbed of soap lest the bathers slip {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 35, pp. 104-105}. In Evliya's Istanbul the soap-makers marched as a guild of five hundred men with two hundred shops {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 245, p. 174}.

## Jemshid's tear

Evliya gives the craft a patron and an origin story: Jemshid, who lived a thousand years and contrived three hundred arts, long pondered how to fix oil into soap, until weeping in despair one of his tears fell into the soap-kettle and the soap instantly took consistency, so that he knew tears to be salt and found the means of consolidating the oil into soap by mingling salt water with it {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 245, p. 174}. If a living thing should fall into the soap-kettle while boiling, Evliya adds, it would be destroyed and immediately turned into soap {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 245, p. 174}.

## The soap towns

These merchants, Evliya says, bring the soap from the Syrian towns of Jericho and Ascalon and from Smyrna and Tenedos for the necessary provision of Constantinople {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 245, pp. 174-175}. The best and whitest soap, which has also a scent of musk, is that of Tripoli in Syria, Jerusalem and Nablus; white soap is also made at Silistria, Nicopolis and Widin, but it has a bad smell, being made from mutton grease, while the soap of Tripoli in Africa, carried to Constantinople in large vases, has an agreeable smell and cleanses well in washing {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 245, p. 175}. Behind the soap-makers march the merchants of musk-soap, a hundred men with forty shops, who carry their shops on horseback and sell different kinds of perfumed soap {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 246, p. 175}. In the procession the soap-makers adorn their shops with different kinds of soap and carry plates of it on their heads, crying for the crowd to take soap to cleanse themselves {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 245, p. 175}.

## The alkali plant

Ibn al-Ukhuwwa watches the raw material: the millers of the alkali plant must grind nothing but the flower as it stands, for some adulterate it with lupin, and he gives the test for the fraud {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIII, pp. 53-54}. The lote-leaf sellers, whose crushed leaves served the bathers in place of soap, must not grind the summer leaves without the winter leaves, which brighten the colour and increase the effectiveness, nor mix in willow, mulberry and other garden leaves; the test is to pound some in a bowl — if a white foam arises it is good, and if the foam is yellow there is adulteration — and a jarful of the pure leaf weighs a ratl and two Egyptian ounces {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIII, pp. 53-54}. Buckley's note records that the dried and crushed leaves of the lotus tree were often used by poor Egyptians as a substitute for soap {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 35, p. 105}.

## Soap in the bath

Al-Shayzari orders the bath attendants to scrub the tiles with a rough material so that no soap, lote leaves or marsh mallow sticks to them and makes the bathers slip, and he cites the ruling that leaves slippery soap on the floor of the bath a shared liability between the one who left it and the attendant who should have kept the bath clean {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 35, pp. 104-105}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa has the bath-man keep lote-leaves and rubbing-stones ready for those who need them, and thinks it a benefit if he appoints a lote-seller always at the hammam door {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLII, pp. 51-52}. In Lane's Cairo the mukeyyisati lathers the bather with leef, the white palm fibres brought from the Hejaz, and soap and warm sweet water, for the well water of the hanafeeyeh is brackish and not fit for washing with soap {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XVI}.

## Today

The soap-boiler's kettle survives in the same towns Evliya names, above all Nablus, and the questions the hisba asked — pure alkali, honest oil, a floor that does not slip — are the same ones a bath or soap inspector still asks {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 245, p. 175} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIII, pp. 53-54}.
