## The craft

The butcher — Arabic qassâb (the trade's proper name), jazzâr and lahhâm; Turks also said etçi — is the one who makes slaughtering animals his regular occupation {cite:tdv-kasap}. Since in nomadic societies nearly every man and woman can slaughter and skin, butchery arose as a settled-city trade {cite:tdv-kasap}.

## Qualifications

The hisba manuals require the butcher to be a Muslim, of age, sane and healthy {cite:tdv-kasap}. Umar ibn al-Khattab is reported to have ordered his commanders not to employ Christians in sensitive trades like butchery and money-changing — Muslims were available, and unbelievers could entangle Muslims in work that did not accord with Islam; the Ottomans likewise barred Jews from selling meat to Muslims, and dhimmis could butcher only for their own communities since they also sold pork {cite:tdv-kasap}.

## Supervised slaughter

Inspection began when the animal was bought: buying a live animal "against" a slaughtered one was forbidden, and the muhtasib or his man attended the killing — checking the tasmiya, that the beast was laid down gently and tied, the knife sharp and the place clean, and that other animals could not watch {cite:tdv-kasap}. Ali is reported to have forbidden blowing up carcasses before skinning, and Umar had a herald in Medina proclaim that no slaughtered animal be skinned until it had cooled {cite:tdv-kasap}. Butchers could not slaughter at the shop front to the public's annoyance, and the shop and its surroundings had to be kept clear of heads, intestines and bones so no smell or flies and vermin bred {cite:tdv-kasap}.

## Honest selling

The butcher could not show a fat animal and sell a thin or sick one, nor mix goat with mutton — goat meat was to be marked with saffron or cut on a separate block — nor mix cuts from different parts, nor sell stale and fresh together {cite:tdv-kasap}. He had to watch for jallâla animals (those habituated to filth and needing special feeding) and for pregnancy before slaughter {cite:tdv-kasap}. The manuals even prefer separating meat with a knife rather than a cleaver, since the cleaver splinters bone into the meat {cite:tdv-kasap}. Chopping blocks were of hard wood, washed, salted and covered with palm-fibre or matting so insects, cats and dogs could not foul them {cite:tdv-kasap}.

## The Ottoman guild

Ottoman practice at times separated sheep and goat butchers, required wire-mesh cupboards (tel dolap) and fixed shop dimensions, and controlled meat prices by narh — butchers colluding to raise prices were punished; in al-Andalus a price tag on the meat was mandatory {cite:tdv-kasap}. Butchers marched in the great guild processions: Nakkaş Osman's 1582 Surnâme-i Hümâyun shows them with their hanging meat, knives and blocks {cite:tdv-kasap}. Early twentieth-century municipality registers divide the trade into slaughterhouse men for cattle and sheep, market sellers, itinerant butchers who worked on animals' backs, liver sellers (ciğerci), shop journeymen and apprentices {cite:tdv-kasap}.

## Early history

Several Companions are reported to have practised butchery — Khâlid b. al-Asîd b. Abi'l-Îs al-Umawî, Kirâm, al-Zubayr, Amr b. al-Âs and Âmir b. Kurayz — and there were butchers among the enslaved as well {cite:tdv-kasap}. Al-Kettânî, who holds that every necessary craft has a Qur'anic basis, cites the verse of al-Mâ'ida on the slaughter of the living animal for the butcher's trade {cite:tdv-kasap}.
