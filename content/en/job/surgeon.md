## Summary

The jerrah — the surgeon — is the practitioner of cirâha, the "work of the hand" on wounds: he dresses injuries and boils, treats hernias, draws teeth, lets blood, circumcises and amputates {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §I, §II}. Because he cuts into the body, the hisba manuals examine him as they examine the physicians: he must know Galen's book on wounds and dressings, al-Zahrawi's book, and the anatomy of the muscles, veins, arteries and nerves he must avoid {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 37, p. 116} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLV, pp. 58-59}. Among the Ottomans he counted as a craftsman — trained in the palace craft corps, beside a master, or father to son — serving in hospital, palace, army or his own shop {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §II}.

## The name and the science

Cirâha derives from cerh, "wounding"; the term in its defined sense is found in Islamic sources from the ninth century, taking the place of the older al-'amal bi'l-yad — "the work of the hand" — used in the Arabic translations of the Greek medical books {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §I}. In the Prophet's time bleeding and cautery were practised and Abu Rimse is named as a skilled surgeon {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §I}. Hunayn ibn Ishaq translated Paul of Aegina's Epitomes, and 'Abbas ibn Sa'id al-Jawhari five treatises ascribed to the Indian physician Shanak, bringing Greek and Indian surgery and methods of anaesthesia to Muslim surgeons {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §I}. Al-Zahrawi's Kitab al-Tasrif devoted its thirtieth treatise, "Maqala fi 'amal al-yad", to surgery; it spread through the Islamic world and then the West, was taught as a textbook until the end of the seventeenth century, and pictured the surgical instruments {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §I}. Ibn al-Quf, reckoned the greatest surgeon of his age, wrote al-'Umda fi sina'at al-jiraha, known as the first medical book in the Islamic literature devoted wholly to surgical matters {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §I}.

## Learning the craft

The hisba books require the surgeon to be familiar with Galen's book known as Qatajanus, on wounds and ointments, and with al-Zahrawi's book on wounds, and to know human anatomy — the organs and the muscles, veins, arteries and nerves in them — so that he avoids them when he opens abscesses and removes haemorrhoids {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 37, pp. 116-117} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLV, p. 58}. In the Ottoman lands the surgeon was counted more a craftsman than a scholar: he learned in the palace ehl-i hiref corps, or beside a master surgeon, or inherited the craft father to son {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §II}. The Turkish hospitals kept separate establishments for surgeons and for kehhals, the eye-surgeons {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §I}.

## The surgeon's kit

The muhtasib expects him to carry a set of lancets — some with round heads, some square, some cross-cut — knives, a frontal hatchet, an amputating saw, an ear-piercer, a wen-cutter, a set of leeches, a case of dressings and a strip of cloth to wrap the ointments, and the storax or olibanum medicament that stops the flow of blood {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLV, pp. 58-59} {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 37, p. 117}. The manuals warn against cheats: a man slips a bone into a wound and then draws it out before a gathered crowd, claiming his cutting medicaments extracted it; others lay plasters of lime washed in oil, dyed red with red earth, green with turmeric and indigo, or black with powdered charcoal — the 'arif must keep an eye on them all {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 37, pp. 117-118} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLV, p. 59}.

## Palace, army and shop

Ottoman surgeons worked in the darüşşifas, the palaces, the army or the shops they kept as consulting-rooms {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §II}. The palace surgeons belonged to the ehl-i hiref corps, and besides their surgery examined the devşirme boys taken into the Enderun and the harem aghas {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §II}. Their chief was the sercerrahin-i hassa, or cerrahbaşı; the best of the palace surgeons were appointed to the army, and the masters and experienced to the darüşşifas, where a first surgeon drew about seven or eight akçe a day and a second surgeon three or four, and where gentleness and compassion toward the patients were required of them {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §II}. The ehl-i hiref wage registers record forty-five surgeons and five apprentices in 1525, seventy-nine surgeons and thirty-three apprentices in 1596, and some forty to fifty palace surgeons through the seventeenth century {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §II}. In Evliya's procession the surgeons of the shops are seven hundred with four hundred shops, marching with litters set out with tooth-drawing pincers, saws, lancets and the instruments of surgery, jesting as if they dressed wounded heads and broken arms and feet {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer vol. 1 pt. 2, sec. 56, p. 118}.

## Examination and licence

From time to time the hekimbaşı examined the surgeons, as he did the physicians, oculists and attars: the competent received a permit bearing the hekimbaşı's seal, and those who understood nothing of surgery were punished {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §II}. The first Turkish surgery book was Sabuncuoğlu Şerefeddin's Cerrâhiyye-i İlhâniyye of 1465, inspired by the surgical treatise of al-Zahrawi's Tasrif and pictured with miniatures of operations, cauterisations and instruments; Cerrah İbrahim's Alâim-i Cerrâhîn, translated from a Greek and Syriac compendium taken at Modon, added a section on arrow and gunshot wounds unknown to the other cerrahnâmes {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §I}.

## Today

Modern surgical teaching in Turkey begins with the Cerrahhâne-i Âmire founded inside the Topkapı Palace in 1832, merged with the Tıbhâne in 1836 and moved to the Otlukçu barracks as the Mekteb-i Tıbbiyye {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §I}. The guild surgeon of the procession, examined by the hekimbaşı and learned in Galen and al-Zahrawi, is the ancestor of the licensed modern surgeon; his craft has been transformed, not extinguished {cite:tdv-cerrahlik §I, §II}.

## Not yet covered
