## Summary

The corsair was the raider of the seas — the word coming from the Italian corsaro through Arabic kursân, plural karâsîne — who attacked enemy shipping and coasts outside the regular fleets, an activity both the Islamic and the Western worlds read as a legitimate mode of war through the Middle Ages {cite:tdv-korsan}. In the Islamic reading the corsair's work belonged to jihad: Ibn Khaldun uses the hadith phrase 'the ghazis of the sea' (guzâtü'l-bahr) for corsairs, and the same men whom Turkish history calls levend were the sea's answer to the land's akıncı raiders, joining the fleet's campaigns when needed {cite:tdv-korsan}. The word also carried its shadow sense: corsairing for mere plunder was harami brigandage — the Arabs' 'thieves of the sea' — a distinction that ran through the whole literature {cite:tdv-korsan}.

## The corsair's long history

Corsairing is old: the Aegean and Levant coasts saw it for millennia, Minoan merchantmen carried armed crews, and Muslim historians read antiquity into the Qur'anic narrative of Khidr scuttling a boat against a king who seized every sound ship {cite:tdv-korsan}. In 631 corsairs off Mecca's port of Shu'ayba were reported to the Prophet, who sent Alqama b. Mujazziz with three hundred men to clear their island base; raiding on the Red Sea and Gulf coasts was common, and the early naval expeditions under Umar's governor Uthman b. Abi'l-As were aimed partly at it {cite:tdv-korsan}. The conquerors built in the old Phoenician yards not only for the navy but for Muslims who wanted to go on gaza against the enemy corsairs {cite:tdv-korsan}.

## Andalus and the western sea

In the western Mediterranean the southern shores were the Muslim corsairs' zone and the northern the Christians': Arab and Berber seamen raided Sicily, Sardinia and Corsica from Musa b. Nusayr's governorship, and Muslim corsairs helped win Malta, Sicily, Pantelleria and the Balearics for the Umayyads of Cordova {cite:tdv-korsan}. The exiles of the 817-18 al-Rabad rising raided the central Mediterranean, settled Alexandria, and after Abdallah b. Tahir drove them out sailed under Abu Hafs Umar b. Shu'ayb al-Balluti with thirty to forty ships to take Crete in 827 {cite:tdv-korsan}. In 891 some twenty Andalusi seamen anchored in Saint-Tropez bay and built at Fraxinetum a corsair state that lasted more than eighty years and drew embassies between Abd al-Rahman III and Otto the Great; Ibn Khaldun records the Bougie sea-ghazis choosing brave crews for sudden descents on the French coasts and coming home with rich booty and captives {cite:tdv-korsan}.

## The Turkish sea-ghazis

The first Turkish corsair movement followed Manzikert under Çaka Bey; the Aegean beyliks of Karasi, Saruhan, Aydın and Menteşe bred the levent corsairs whose skill the Ottomans later drew on, Kemal Reis being the 'pîrimiz' of Piri Reis {cite:tdv-korsan}. The great Ottoman reises — the Barbarossa brothers, Turgut, Seydi Ali, Sinan, Salih and Murad — all came up through corsairing, worked the Mediterranean and pushed out into the Atlantic to the Channel, Norway's coasts, Iceland and the Antilles, serving while no standing state navy yet existed {cite:tdv-korsan}. Ottoman derya beys owed their coasts protection against corsairs, and the ahidnames granted mutual protection and compensation for corsair damage {cite:tdv-korsan}.

## Mirrors and enemies

Corsairing ran in both directions: the fleets based at Rhodes, Cyprus and Crete shook the Istanbul-Egypt sea road, the Adriatic Uskoks troubled the sixteenth century, the Maltese knights raided Ottoman shipping to the Syrian coasts into the eighteenth, and Cossack craft struck the northern Anatolian shores {cite:tdv-korsan}. English-French rivalry brought their corsair wars into Ottoman waters; the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries saw Greek raiders whose vessels the documents call 'korsan bahrisi', and Indian corsairs working Socotra against the India and China trade before the Portuguese came {cite:tdv-korsan}.

## Law and the end

Fiqh read the corsair's prize as lawful when the raid was sea-gaza — every ship counted as a castle and its besiegers shared the booty by siege rules — while plunder for gain was hırâbe, punished in proportion to the deed under the verse of those who fight God and His messenger; Venşerîsî obliges Muslims who find a cargo ship not the enemy's to hold it as a trust and return it {cite:tdv-korsan}. What had been a method of war became a crime when the 1856 Paris congress made corsairing an offence in the law of nations: any officer might seize the corsair ship, bring her in, and try her crew regardless of nationality {cite:tdv-korsan}.
