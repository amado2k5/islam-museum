## Summary

The shipwright (qalfat, kalafat ustası) built and repaired ships inside the dâr al-ṣināʿa — the state arsenal where war and merchant vessels were constructed {cite:tdv-tersane}. The trade entered Islamic history as a continuation of Byzantine yards, whose Greek and Coptic masters and workers the Muslims kept in place {cite:tdv-tersane}. The entry follows the arsenals from Muʿāwiya's day to the Turkish beyliks of Anatolia {cite:tdv-tersane}.

## The word and the institution

"Tersane" came into Turkish through Italian, from the Arabic dâr al-ṣināʿa ("house of the craft of the sea"); it took this name because in the age of the first Islamic arsenals, shipbuilding was the weight of industry {cite:tdv-tersane}. From the same Arabic came the Western arsenal, arsenale, arzenale, tarzna, darsena and darsanale {cite:tdv-tersane}. The entry notes that shipbuilding is as old as the sources take it — at least to Noah {cite:tdv-tersane}.

## The first arsenals

Once Syria and Egypt were taken, the Muslims had to prepare a naval power against the Byzantine fleet; captured Byzantine ships in Syrian and Egyptian ports formed the first nucleus, and the Byzantine arsenals with their masters eased the way {cite:tdv-tersane}. The first arsenal the Muslims themselves built stood on Rawza island in the Nile in 54/674, after a Byzantine raid on Egypt's coast — it was called Sınâatü'r-Ravza and the island Jazîrat al-ṣināʿa {cite:tdv-tersane}. Alexandria held the era's largest arsenal, and Muʿāwiya is said to have sent it cedar timber from the Lebanese mountains {cite:tdv-tersane}.

## Craftsmen and materials

Surviving papyri of the Umayyad governor Qurra b. Sharīk record his correspondence on ship construction, repair, cleaning and fitting-out, naming the kalafat master, the carpenter and the smith with the materials and needs they required {cite:tdv-tersane}. The Nile valley was poor in ship timber: wood for hulls and masts came from Lebanon, Syria, Yemen, southern Anatolia and central Africa, while pitch, tar, nails, iron, sailcloth and hemp rope were largely imported {cite:tdv-tersane}. When Muʿāwiya ordered the shipwrights gathered at Acre in 49/669, housing was built for the arsenal workers along the shore {cite:tdv-tersane}. For the first North African arsenal at Tunis, Abd al-Malik ordered his brother Abd al-Azīz to send a thousand Coptic shipwrights with their families at Hassān b. Nuʿmān's request {cite:tdv-tersane}.

## Rise and fall of the yards

The Tulunids renewed and enlarged the Rawza arsenal; Ahmad b. Tūlūn gave its direction to the mathematician-engineer Abū Kāmil, and a hundred ships were built for him there {cite:tdv-tersane}. The Fatimids raised a port and arsenal at Mahdia, then two yards in Egypt at Maqs and Rawza; al-Muʿizz had six hundred "unprecedented" warships built at Maqs {cite:tdv-tersane}. Under the Mamluks, Sultan Baybars spent part of his time in the arsenal and received Sicilian envoys there, carpenter's tool in hand, sitting among the timber with masters and emirs {cite:tdv-tersane}. Abd al-Raḥmān III built a great arsenal at al-Mariyya after the Fatimids burned his ships in its harbour, and al-Ḥakam II inspected and renewed it in 353/964 {cite:tdv-tersane}. In Anatolia the Seljuks built a tersane at Alanya after Kayqubād I took the city; Sinop and Antalya had yards too {cite:tdv-tersane}.

## The craftsman's place in the state

Arsenal management was state business: the Fatimids created a dīwān al-ʿamāʾir for it, and al-Maqrīzī calls its directors mutawallī al-ṣināʿa and wālī al-ṣināʿa {cite:tdv-tersane}. Saladin founded a dīwān al-ustūl for naval finances and fixed the sailors' salaries {cite:tdv-tersane}. But when the late Ayyubids let the navy rot, the word ustūlī ("sailor") turned from a title of pride into an insult — and the decline reached the arsenals and shipbuilding itself {cite:tdv-tersane}.
