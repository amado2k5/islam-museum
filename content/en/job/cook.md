## Summary

The tabbakh is the cook who prepares food and sells it from a shop {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 13, pp. 58-59}. His trade stood under the muhtasib's eye because he fed the public: al-Shayzari lays down rules of cleanliness and honesty for the cook's pots and measures {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 13, pp. 58-59}, Lane finds whole rows of cooks' shops in nineteenth-century Cairo {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}, and Evliya Çelebi marches the cooks of Istanbul through the great guild procession as an armed company of five hundred men {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 157, pp. 147-148}.

## The craft and its rules

Al-Shayzari's hisba manual orders the cooks to cover their vessels and protect them from flies and crawling insects after washing them with hot water and plant ash {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 13, pp. 58-59}. They must not cook goat meat together with mutton, nor camel meat with beef, lest someone recovering from an illness eat it and suffer a relapse; the sign of goat in the pot, he writes, is its dark colour, unpalatable smell and small bones {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 13, pp. 58-59}.

## The frauds of the kitchen

The muhtasib must watch the cooks because they adulterate the food: some pour off the fat and then return it to the pot so that it floats on the surface and deceives people into thinking there is plenty of meat {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 13, pp. 58-59}. Others thicken madira with flour, rice flour or fine semolina, bulk out bahata with taro, and thicken labaniyya with the dregs of oil or cornstarch — the sign of all of them, he says, is that the food turns brown {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 13, pp. 58-59}.

## The alchemy of cooking

Al-Shayzari cites a treatise called The Alchemy of Cooking, attributed to the philosopher Ya'qub al-Kindi, which described meat dishes cooked without meat, fried liver without liver, sausages without meat, omelettes without eggs and sweets without honey or sugar {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 13, pp. 58-59}. He refuses to say more for fear of teaching the irreligious how to adulterate food, and leaves the watch over such tricks to the muhtasib {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 13, pp. 58-59}.

## The cooks of Cairo

In Lane's Cairo there were many cooks' shops where kebab and various other dishes were cooked and sold, but it was seldom that people ate at them; shopkeepers generally sent for provisions when they could not conveniently prepare food in their own houses, and the cooks of these shops were called tabbakhs {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}.

## The cooks of Istanbul

Evliya counts the cooks (Ashjian) at five hundred men with ninety shops; their first patron he names as Keikawus and their patron in the Prophet's time as Sheikh Seif-ud-din Khoza'i, who brought the Prophet a dish of a sheep's head and feet cooked with vinegar and garlic {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 157, pp. 147-148}. At the procession the cooks march armed, adorning their shops with china dishes and golden tankards, dressing heads and feet and handing what they cook to the spectators from iron spears, crying that it is greasy, hot, vinegared and garlicked {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 157, pp. 147-148}. A separate corps, the running cooks of the At-maidan, raced barefoot across the square each morning for the best carcases of the Janissaries' sheep, and rose by seniority to the offices of chaush-bashi and Yenicheri-agassi {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 149, pp. 144-145}.

## Today

The tabbakh's trade is still living: the cookshop that sells ready dishes to take home survived in the market streets Lane describes, and its descendants are the grill shops and restaurants of the same cities {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}.
