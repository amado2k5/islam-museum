## Summary

The fassifsa'i was the craftsman who cut and set the glass mosaic — fusaifisa' — that sheathed the great Umayyad sanctuaries {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 pp. 217-240}. The mosaics of the Dome of the Rock and the Great Mosque of Damascus, gold-ground fields of trees, wreaths and architecture in glass, are the most celebrated survivals of the craft {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 pp. 217-240}.

## A craft older than Islam

Mosaic was an old Mediterranean craft before Islam: the Abyssinian governor Abraha, building his church at San'a', asked the Byzantine emperor for workmen, mosaics and marble — the credible original, Creswell argues, of the later stories told about al-Walid {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 pp. 150-151}. When Ibn al-Zubayr rebuilt the Ka'ba after the fire, mosaics and three marble columns were taken from that very church of San'a' for the new structure {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 pp. 150-151}.

## Al-Walid and the emperor's workmen

Waqidi, quoted by Ya'qubi, relates that al-Walid wrote to the emperor of Rum asking help in rebuilding the Prophet's mosque at Medina, and that the emperor sent a hundred thousand mithqals of gold, a hundred workmen and forty loads of fusaifisa', the work finishing in 90 AH {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 pp. 142-143}. Baladhuri's earlier account speaks of eighty Greek workmen and makes no mention of imperial help {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 pp. 142-143}. For Damascus, Ibn Asakir has al-Walid demand two hundred Rumi craftsmen on pain of invasion; van Berchem shows these stories amplify century by century and cannot bear examination {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 pp. 234-235, 240}.

## How the cubes were made

Al-Umari, writing in the fourteenth century, gives the recipe: fusaifisa' is glass which is gilded and then overlaid with thin glass — the kind called mashar — while polychrome fusaifisa' is made of paste {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 p. 237}. Old tesserae are uniform and equal in size; new ones vary — the surest sign by which original work is told from repair {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 p. 237}.

## A Syrian workshop

Glass mosaic was still being manufactured in Syria in the fourteenth century: al-Umari records that a great quantity was recently made for the Umayyad Mosque, though chests of it stored away were damaged in the fire of 740 AH (1339) {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 p. 237}. The same writer had earlier described the Sakhra's exterior covered, above its marble, with gold mosaic of plant patterns up to the gutters {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 pp. 226-227}. Mamluk restorers too kept the craft alive: Baybars renewed the Sakhra's mosaics, and the thirteenth-century glass cubes survive, though inferior to the eighth-century work {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 pp. 226-227}.

## Reading the craft in the wall

Van Berchem, comparing her own observations with those of the Italian mosaicists who repaired the Dome's interior, could trace where original Umayyad cubes still stood, where Mamluk hands had patched them, and where plaster had replaced the lost cubes altogether {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 pp. 218-222}. The surviving mosaic band alone runs some 240 metres around the octagonal arcade, its double line of Kufic inscription in gilded cubes on a deep blue ground {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 p. 222}.

## Not yet covered

The texts name the material, the patrons and the buildings, but no guild of fassifsa'in, no wages and no workshop rules come down to us; the makers themselves remain anonymous behind their cubes {cite:creswell-early-muslim-architecture-vol1 pp. 217-240}.
