## Summary

The qabbani weighed for the market: the public weigher whose balance stood between seller and buyer, his office older than any guild — the hisba writers make control of weights and scales the muhtasib's first duty {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 3, p. 39; ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. X, p. 27}. Evliya shows him at Istanbul's custom-house, silk-khan and mint {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 162-163, 165-166, 170-172}; Chalcraft shows the qabbani of Alexandria still weighing at the port's stations under a guild shaykh in 1871 {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 32, 97-101}.

## The first duty of the market

Ibn al-Ukhuwwa opens his chapter on scales and measures with the true balance: the most accurate scales have the two sides equal, the pans balanced and the aperture for suspension in the middle of the beam, the nail made of hard steel so it moves easily {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. X, p. 27}. Al-Shayzari says the weights are the bases of commercial dealings and an integral part of sales, so the muhtasib must verify their size that transactions may take place without fraud; his translator notes that whatever else changed in the office, the concern with correct weights, measures and scales always remained its primary one {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 3, p. 39}.

## The weigher's cheats

The manuals read like a weigher's own confession. Scales must be kept free of oil and dirt, brought to rest before weighing, never suspended from the hand, and the pans never touched by the thumb with fraudulent intent {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. X, p. 27}. The tricks have names: blowing into the pan that holds the gold, a fine strand of hair fastened to one side, and the balance called al-Mu'addi, whose beam is steel but whose tongue is soft iron that can be inclined {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. X, pp. 27-28}. The Coptic steelyard can be fraudulently altered and its use is disapproved for heavy merchandise; the muhtasib himself keeps the test weights — pebbles in bags of palm-fibre or coarse canvas, stored where neither moisture nor dust can reach them {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. X, p. 28}.

## The house of measures

Al-Shayzari adds the fraud from the other side: dealers soak grains of wheat and barley in oil, bury the ends of needles in them and dry them in the shade, until the grains look as they did before — the muhtasib must check the balance weights when their owners are not expecting it {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 4, p. 43}. Under the Fatimids the standard weights and measures were housed in the Dar al-'Iyar, where the accuracy of every part of the scales was checked; merchants were summoned there from time to time to bring their scales, weights and measures for testing, and a deficient instrument was destroyed and its owner compelled to buy a true replacement — a custom Salah al-Din confirmed and which still stood in the Fatimid-descended states {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 4, p. 43}.

## Scales of the custom-house

In Evliya's Istanbul the weigher is a state office. To the inspector of the great custom-house are attached writers, weighers, brokers, bankers and searchers of bales, and the quintal-men (Kantarji) pass in the procession on wagons, weighing bales of merchandise and writing them down to the account of different merchants {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 170-172}. The third of the city's inspectors is the Mizani-harir, the inspector of the weighing of silk: all silk must be weighed before him, and he receives a tenth {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 162}. Even the mint has its weighers, who weigh everything and keep an exact account {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 165-166}.

## The qabbani of Alexandria

The guild was still alive under Isma'il. Chalcraft lists weighers (qabbani) among the trades of public importance for which codified regulations were drafted {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 32}, and the weighing taxes of the 1860s and 1870s — on food, on meat in Alexandria, on wood — made the weigher a revenue office too {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 41-42}. In 1871-72 the porters of the weigh stations (ta'ifat 'attalin qabbaniyya) of Alexandria petitioned the governorate: the weighers were hiring outsiders instead of guild porters to carry goods to and from the port weigh stations, where goods were given an official weight to avoid disputes between merchants and buyers {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 97-98}. The authorities answered that proper procedure required the shaykh of the ta'ifa to be summoned — the weighers' shaykh included — so the guild could explain its own custom {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 100-101}. In Daqahliyya the shaykhate of the weighers itself was contested: al-Jawhiri held the weigh station by force while the court had returned the post to Amin Ibrahim {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 96}.

## Not yet covered

The sources give the weigher's rules, tricks, offices and disputes, but not his day's wages or fee per bale, the making and repair of the great beam-scales themselves (the kantarjian weight-makers are a separate guild), or the weighers of the smaller provincial markets {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley chs. 3-4, pp. 39-43; evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 170-172; chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 97-101}.
