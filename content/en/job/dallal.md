## Summary

The dallal — from dalâla, "showing the way", and matched by the Persian-derived simsar — stood between buyer and seller until the bargain was done, or cried news through the markets {cite:tdv-dellal}. The Islamic city held two of them: the trade dallal who closed sales for his dellaliye fee, and the news dallal who proclaimed tidings and sultanic orders; in fiqh the same calling stands as the simsar's contract of hire {cite:tdv-dellal} {cite:tdv-simsar}.

## Names and first markets

The simsar is a Persian word that appears in hadith, and the Prophet addressed the community described as the simsars of Baqi as "merchants" and bade them avoid oaths and empty talk, the text on which most jurists rest the lawfulness of brokerage {cite:tdv-simsar}. Arabic also named the roaming broker tavvāf, the settled one cellās, and the broker of slaves and animals nakhkhās; women brokers called dellâle carried textiles and jewels door to door into the harems of Egypt {cite:tdv-simsar}. In custom the words could divide the work: the simsar brought the customer to the goods, the dallal carried the goods in search of a customer {cite:tdv-simsar}.

## Fees and law

Under the Fatimids dallals might broker only certain goods, and under the Mamluks they took two per cent of each sale, paying half of it to the state as tax {cite:tdv-dellal}. Mehmed II's kanunname fixed the fee for trade goods at one per cent, and elsewhere the Urfa sanjak rule charged one Ottoman akçe per hundred on sold animals and the Damascus rule took ten eşrefîs per chest of coral, half of it for the dallal {cite:tdv-dellal}. The fiqh of the trade is the contract of hire: the parties, the service and the fee must be known and lawful, a broker may not deal in forbidden goods, and the schools differ over whether a percentage of the sale is a valid fee — the Hanafis tending to forbid it, Malik and Rabi'a allowing it as cu'âle {cite:tdv-simsar}. The kanunname also gave the simsars power to dismiss a corrupt dallal and take his berat {cite:tdv-dellal}.

## The two callings

The Ottoman news dallals served under the dellâlbaşı on daily wages, announced the deaths and accessions of sultans as state officials, and were sent out by rebels too, to draw the crowd to their side {cite:tdv-dellal}. The trade dallals specialised by goods — emlak, bedestan, fish-market, cart, slave (esirci), raw-silk and horse-market dellals — and could not broker outside their own craft {cite:tdv-dellal}. In mid-seventeenth-century Istanbul Evliya counted two hundred trade dellals in the outer bedestan and seventy in the new, the 1640 narh register gives a hundred and twenty each in the bezzazistan and the flea market, and fifteenth-century Tokat had twenty-six, twenty-one of them Muslim {cite:tdv-dellal}.

## The crying of the sale

In Lane's Cairo auctions were held in the suqs on stated days once or twice a week, run by the dellals, hired by private sellers or by shopkeepers; they carried the goods up and down announcing the sums bid with cries of "harâj" {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. The dellal's work reached beyond the market: he found the merchant looking for a buyer or the man looking for goods, handled second-hand sales especially from estates, and in the palace and the rich konaks the dellâle women sold cloth and dresses {cite:tdv-dellal}.

## Guild and decline

In the great towns the dallals formed guilds whose emins kept them honest, and outsiders were forbidden to practise — in Egypt even the fortress guards were barred from working the market or acting as dallals on pain of losing their pay {cite:tdv-dellal}. The news calling died of itself with the press and the telegraph, while the trade calling went on under the name of simsarlık — the modern commission broker {cite:tdv-dellal}.
