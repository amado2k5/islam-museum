## Summary

The daqqaq stood between the grain-merchant and the baker: he sold flour and fed the mills and bake-houses, and the hisba books gave him the same chapter as the seller of unground wheat {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 5, pp. 45-46}. In Evliya's Istanbul procession he marched three thousand strong, white from face to foot with his own flour {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 83, pp. 124-125}.

## Clean grain, sweet flour

Al-Shayzari orders the flour-merchants to sieve the dirt out of the wheat before it is milled and to remove all the darnel and dust, and to sprinkle a little water over the wheat while it is being milled, which makes the flour white and wholesome {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 5, p. 46}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa adds that the millers must cleanse the grain of earth before they grind it, and that the sieves must be renewed periodically {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, p. 85}.

## The adulterations

The muhtasib watches the flour itself: perhaps the merchants have mixed in sieved barley flour, bean flour or chick-pea flour, flour milled on an uneven millstone, flour mingled with darnel, or the dust of the mill; if he has doubts about them he makes them swear an oath {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 5, p. 46}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa likewise has the flour examined against chick-pea flour, and forbids milling on the worn particles of the millstone, which cause harm to men {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, p. 85}.

## Beasts, jars and hoppers

The mill-beasts are not forgotten: they must be permitted to rest each day and night, and none may be employed to grind more than a wayba at a time {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, p. 85}. The muhtasib inspects the scales and weights used for flour and for money, the measures and the jars; the flour-receptacles must be sound, or the flour is lost on the roads to the purchaser's loss — the tillis sack holds three battas, each of fifty ratls {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, p. 85}. Flour is received and delivered by weight; for every jar the name of its owner and his dwelling is written and placed in a gourd tied to the handle, so that it is not confused with others, and one person's flour is never mingled with another's in the mill-hopper {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, p. 85}.

## The daily quota and the deliveryman

For the common good the muhtasib stipulates that the merchants produce a daily quota of flour and distribute it to the shops and bakeries {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 5, p. 46}. The millers must also be compelled to mill flour for people to consume in their homes, since most people avoid eating bread from the suq; and the men who deliver this flour must be honourable and trustworthy, for they enter men's houses and converse with their children and slave-women {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, p. 85}.

## Three thousand white men

In the Istanbul procession the flour-merchants (Unilekjian), who furnish the mills with corn and the bake-houses with flour, numbered three thousand men — for the greatest part Egyptian peasants — clad in armour, their faces, eyes and dresses all over white with flour {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 83, pp. 124-125}. They passed on wagons with the flour-bags in their hands, crying "Allah yansur es-sultan" — may God give victory to the Sultan; their patron is Ramazan, the son of Sheikh Shadeli {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 83, p. 125}.

## Not yet covered

The manuals fix the chain from sieve to doorstep but give no milling fees, no water-rights for the mills, and nothing on the flour trade outside the great cities; the Ottoman evidence is a single guild entry {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 5; ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI; evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 83}.
