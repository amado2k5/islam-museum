## Summary

The hanna' sold the henna leaf and its powder — the kena of the Istanbul shops — in a guild of fifty-five men and fifteen shops in Evliya's procession, and under a hisba chapter of their own in Ibn al-Ukhuwwa {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 121, p. 139; ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LIX, p. 89}. Lane shows where the powder ended: bound all night on Egyptian hands and feet {cite:lane-modern-egyptians pp. 87-88}.

## Packets of kena

The henna-merchants are fifty-five men with fifteen shops; their patron is Imam Attar from Belbeis, a disciple of Anas ibn Malik {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 121, p. 139}. They are occupied in their shops in making up packets of henna, called kena, some of which they distribute amongst the spectators {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 121, p. 139}.

## A cargo of Egypt

Henna sails home with the Egyptian fleet: on their return the ships bring rice, lentils, hemp, sugar and henna {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer p. 136}. At the custom-house the porters lift, by machinery, loads of cotton or henna weighing from forty to fifty quintals — a weight, Evliya says, to make one lose his wits {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 170-171}.

## The Egyptians' answer

When the butchers mocked the Egyptian goods and said a paste of lavvadan root could dye nails and beards just as well, the merchants answered: it is impossible for henna to be found anywhere better than in Egypt, and the use of it is sanctified by the Sunna — to which, they said, there is no reply {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 137-138}.

## The sifting test

Ibn al-Ukhuwwa gives the henna-sellers a chapter of their own {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LIX, p. 89}. Over them is appointed a man of their own trade who knows their deceptions; he shall compel them not to sell any henna not free of all faults and free from sand and coarse flour {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LIX, p. 89}. Adulteration with sand and hot oil can be detected by sifting, when the sand or flour remains on the sieve — and the adulterated kind is heavier {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. LIX, p. 89}.

## A night in linen

Lane writes the use the sellers supply: the females of the higher and middle classes, and many poorer women, stain parts of their hands and feet with the leaves of the henna-tree, which impart a yellowish-red or deep-orange colour {cite:lane-modern-egyptians p. 87}. The henna is prepared merely by being powdered and mixed with a little water into a paste; spread in the palm and on the parts to be dyed, the hand is bound tight with linen for a whole night, and the colour, which does not disappear until after many days, is generally renewed after about a fortnight or three weeks {cite:lane-modern-egyptians pp. 87-88}. Some ladies then apply a paste of quicklime, common smoke-black and linseed-oil, which converts the tint of the henna to a black or blackish-olive hue {cite:lane-modern-egyptians p. 88}.
