## Summary

The wali — from a root meaning 'near to, in charge of' — was the official the ruler appointed to govern a city or province outside the capital; his charge was a wilaya, and the larger region an eyalet {cite:tdv-vali}. Early on he was called emir or 'amil, since the conquerors of a place usually became its governors and fiscal officers {cite:tdv-vali}.

## The deed of appointment

A wali was installed by a written deed — 'al-ahd', 'al-kitab' — much like a modern decree, which spelled out his powers, his duties and what the province's people could expect {cite:tdv-vali}. The Abbasids distinguished appointment by the caliph's free choice (imarat al-istikfa) from governorship seized by a strong man who then demanded confirmation (imarat al-isti'la), and between 'general' walis answerable for nearly everything and 'special' ones of limited remit {cite:tdv-vali}.

## What he did

His brief ran wide: lead the prayers, teach the religion in the mosques, command the army in war, make treaties after conquest, collect taxes, pay the officials, distribute booty, send the treasury's share to the centre, decide the fate of captives, settle Muslims in new quarters, punish criminals, run judicial affairs and keep order {cite:tdv-vali}. Over time pieces of this were handed to other city officers, and in some periods the governorship shrank to little more than 'commander of war and prayer' — emir al-harb wa'l-salat {cite:tdv-vali}.

## Qualities and limits

The required qualities were competence, trustworthiness, justice, mercy and no greed for office; caliphs weighed the province's own wishes in the appointment {cite:tdv-vali}. Walis served without fixed term until dismissed, resigned or expelled; they drew a state salary, and to prevent abuse they were forbidden gifts and commerce {cite:tdv-vali}. They lived and worked in the dar al-imara, the government house usually built beside the market and the mosque {cite:tdv-vali}.

## Inspection

Oversight ran through the ruler's inspectors or the ruler himself: the wali could be summoned to the capital or confronted with his accusers in his own city, complainants could reach the inspectors or the sovereign directly, and the deed was the yardstick {cite:tdv-vali}. A wali under accusation was suspended pending judgement; unexplained growth in the wealth he had declared on taking office could cost him his post and half or all of his property, and serious offences could end in hudud punishment {cite:tdv-vali}.

## From Medina to the empires

The Prophet appointed governors preferring local men, often newly converted former chiefs, sending Companions to make up what they lacked in knowledge; Abu Bakr kept his governors, Umar institutionalised their appointment, deeds and audit, and the reproach heaped on Uthman's kinsmen-governors showed how dangerous the office's misuse could be {cite:tdv-vali}. Under the Umayyads, ruling decentralised, the walis' names were spoken more than the caliphs'; they chose their own officers, struck silver in their own names and took an administrative advance on top of salary {cite:tdv-vali}. Under the Abbasids power ebbed to centrally appointed officers — the finance 'amil, the barid, the mazalim judge — while Persians and then Turks entered the service, and the office carried on into the later dynasties and the Ottoman eyalet system {cite:tdv-vali}.
