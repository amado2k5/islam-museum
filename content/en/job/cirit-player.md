## Summary

The cirit player rode the blunt-javelin war-game: two mounted teams of ten to fifteen threw the metre-and-a-half oak clubs — cirit, also called çevgen, from the Arabic cerîd, a stripped palm-stick — at one another in a running chase of about an hour and a half in two halves {cite:tdv-cirit}. A Central Asian war-game requiring mastery of riding and throwing, cirit was the favourite sport of every Turkish state and of the Turk-influenced Abbasid court; the Ottomans made it an organised branch, the sultans played it, the janissaries drilled it, and it lost its standing when their corps fell {cite:tdv-cirit}.

## Rules of the little war

A rider carried a straight oak stick about the thickness of an ordinary lance and roughly 1.5 metres long; the teams drew up a hundred metres apart, and play opened when one galloped toward the enemy line, hurled his cirit at a chosen horseman and fled — the game unfolding as a running chase of mutual charges {cite:tdv-cirit}. Hitting a rider scored, hitting a horse lost points; the highest score went to the man who caught an incoming cirit in the air, and high points also to the one who closed on his foe and, in place of casting, 'showed him mercy' with a feint {cite:tdv-cirit}. The figures were the horses' sudden starts and checks and turns, the riders emptying the saddle to let a cast pass, stooping without dismounting to take a javelin from the ground, and above all the airborne catch {cite:tdv-cirit}. By custom a wound or a death in play did not stop the game and gave no ground for complaint — it counted an honour to the player's house, above all to his father; the entry calls cirit, plainly, a little war in which riders and horses alike needed the most serious training {cite:tdv-cirit}.

## At the Ottoman court

After Bursa fell, Orhan Gazi endowed a nearby field for horse-races and cirit games; under Yıldırım Bayezid and Çelebi Mehmed the passion grew, and once the palace began teaching cündilik — horsemanship — cirit became an organised branch of sport {cite:tdv-cirit}. Ahmed I and Murad IV played it themselves; Murad IV kept up to four hundred specially trained horses for the game, and the sources credit his thrown cirit from the Eski Saray to the foot of the Bayezid mosque's minarets, and from Aleppo citadel over the moat onto Saraçhane {cite:tdv-cirit}. The game ran for days at Mehmed IV's 1675 circumcision wedding at Edirne and was a fixture of the Tulip Era; as the janissaries' chief game and drill it lost its former standing after their corps was abolished, and survives in Anatolia mainly as an equestrian-club display and in the Central Asian steppe as a living game {cite:tdv-cirit}.

## The Egyptian gereed

Lane saw the same game among the peasants of Upper Egypt: two mounted parties of twelve, twenty or more riders faced off about five hundred feet apart at weddings, circumcisions and the saint-tomb sacrifices {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. One rode out to challenge; a rider of the other party, carrying four or five heavy six-foot gereeds — freshly cut lower palm-sticks, blunt at both ends and cast with the small end foremost — pursued him at full gallop and threw them one after another at his head or back, sometimes wounding terribly, while the challenged man caught them, warded them with arm or sheathed sword, or outran them {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. Lane calls it a sport recalling the old tournaments, a game of the early Bedawees kept by the Nile tribes lately settled, and notes that three men and a mare were once killed at it within an hour in the plain of Thebes {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.
