## Summary

The seyyah is the professional traveller: the man who makes the journey itself his work, recording the lands, trades, guilds and shrines he passes through for the benefit of those who stay behind {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v1 §5-6}. The Ottoman world produced its fullest example in Evliya Çelebi, who tells us he chose the life of a traveller after a dream, styled himself 'the well-travelled' (seyyah) and gave forty years to it {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer intro}.

## The dream

Evliya reports that in his twentieth year, on the night of Ashure 1040 (1631), he saw the Prophet in a dream and, asking for the power of speech (şefa'at), was told instead 'seyahat' — travel {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v1 §5}. His father and his circle of prayer-companions then laid on him a course of Qur'anic and calligraphic training, and he set out in 1631 to wander his native Istanbul before all else, writing down its quarters, markets and masters {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v1 §5-6}.

## The itinerary

From Istanbul the road ran with Ottoman officers and on his own errands: to Bursa with the governor Ketenci Ömer Pasha's people, to the Caucasus and Azerbaijan, to the Crimea and the Black Sea, to the frontiers of the Austrian wars, to the Aegean islands, Egypt and the Sudan, to Mecca and Medina on the pilgrimage road, to Persia, Baghdad and the eastern provinces, and back repeatedly to Rumelia, Bosnia, Albania and Hungary {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer vol.1-10}. He wrote, as he went, the ten volumes of the Seyahatnâme, the largest single travel record the Islamic world left {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v1 intro}.

## What a seyyah carried

The traveller's kit was his education: grammar, tafsir, hadith and calligraphy — Evliya had learned the art of handwriting under masters and earned a place among the court's companions {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v1 §5}. What he gathered was the raw material of a hundred trades: the Istanbul guild processions he describes in such detail, the workshops and their officers, the shrines, inscriptions and oddities of every town {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 171-189}.

## The craft of description

The Seyahatnâme is itself the traveller's product: a systematic account of cities entered, baths and buildings measured, trades counted, languages sampled and notable dead noted in their tombs {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer vol.1}. Evliya's own words give the method — he kept his eye on whatever was strange, wonderful or useful, and asked about it {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v1 §6}.

## After Evliya

The seyyah's tradition ran on in the Ottoman lands and beyond: the court traveller, the pilgrim-chronicler, the consular dragoman and the late-Ottoman journalist-traveller all trace to the same working pattern — go, see, write {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v1 intro}. The office survived into the age of printed travel literature, which the Seyahatnâme itself joined in the nineteenth century {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v1 intro}.
