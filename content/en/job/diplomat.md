## Summary

The envoy is his sovereign's messenger to another sovereign: he carries the letter and the gifts and brings back the answer, and his person carries an old sanctity — the envoy is not killed and not insulted {cite:tdv-elci}. Muslim diplomacy began at Medina: the Prophet sent Amr b. Umayya al-Damri to the Najashi to frustrate the Quraysh embassy demanding the surrender of the emigrant Muslims, and after Hudaybiya he sent six envoys with his call to the rulers of his age — the Najashi, Heraclius, the Muqawqis, Khosrow, the governor of Busra and the ruler of Yamama {cite:tdv-elci}. The exchange flourished under the Umayyads and Abbasids with Byzantium, and under the Fatimids, Seljuks, Ghaznavids, Ayyubids, Mamluks and Mongols, and under the Ottomans it became a choreography of reception in which the envoy's welcome displayed the power of the state {cite:tdv-elci}.

## The envoy at the Prophet's court

The Prophet's exchanges while still in Mecca cannot be called diplomacy; the real thing began at Medina when he sent Amr b. Umayya al-Damri to the Najashi to defeat the Meccan bid to recover the emigrants {cite:tdv-elci}. After Hudaybiya in 6 (628) he sent six envoys to the rulers, all departing the same day; Ibn Hudayda's al-Misbah al-mudi counts forty-eight envoys sent on various errands {cite:tdv-elci}. In 9 (630) the delegations came to Medina to declare their Islam, so many that the year was called "the year of the delegations" {cite:tdv-elci}. He received them at the mosque's "column of the delegations" in his finest dress and told his senior Companions to dress likewise, ordered them met at the border and escorted to the city, built them a lodging house and worked on it himself, and among his deathbed instructions was not to neglect the envoys' care {cite:tdv-elci}.

## The envoy's sanctity and his choosing

The envoy is not killed and not insulted: the Prophet told Musaylima's envoys that had they not been envoys he would have had them beheaded, and he wrote to Yuhanna b. Ru'ba and to the ruler of Ayla that to turn back his envoy would count as a cause of war {cite:tdv-elci}. He chose his envoys for fitness of character and looks, power of persuasion, quick understanding, honesty, learning and eloquence, preferred men who already knew the country and its language, and ordered Zayd b. Thabit to learn Hebrew {cite:tdv-elci}. A political envoy need not be a Muslim — Amr b. Umayya was sent before his conversion — while a religious envoy had to be an observant Muslim {cite:tdv-elci}.

## Under the caliphs and the dynasties

The exchange continued under the Rashidun and thickened under the Umayyads with Byzantium, with treaties and annual payments; the commander al-Muhallab b. Abi Sufra said a man's letter shows his reason and his envoy shows his opinion {cite:tdv-elci}. Under the Abbasids the embassy joined commerce and learning: al-Ma'mun sent a mission to Byzantium to bring back the classics for translation, and contacts thickened under the caliph al-Ta'i'-Lillah {cite:tdv-elci}. The Fatimids dealt on many fronts with Byzantium: al-Mansur-Billah made peace with Constantine VII in 952 on condition a mosque be built at Reggio under Byzantine protection where captive Muslims might pray {cite:tdv-elci}.

## Turkic diplomacy

In the old Turkic states foreign affairs was an important department run mostly by interpreters and by officers called bitikçi, ılımgacı and tamgacı, and the chief relations were with China, then Byzantium and the Sasanians {cite:tdv-elci}. The Muslim Turks adopted the Islamic model: Nizam al-Mulk's Siyasatname asks of the envoy that he know the manners of serving rulers, speak little but boldly, travel much, and that the real aim of sending envoys is to gather news of other states — their history, geography, army, politics, religion and the welfare and loyalty of their peoples {cite:tdv-elci}. The Qarakhanid mirror Kutadgu Bilig describes the envoy as among the pick of mankind: intelligent, learned, distinguished, brave, well-spoken, a reader of books, a poet, a speaker of many tongues {cite:tdv-elci}. Among the Ghaznavids a court officer called resuldar received the envoy, lodged him and presented him to the sultan, and Seljuk embassies were usually headed by kadis {cite:tdv-elci}. Tughril Beg sent word to the caliph al-Qa'im in 1038 that they had seized rule from Mas'ud of Ghazna for failing in his duty, then reported that the khutba was read for the Abbasid caliph wherever they ruled (1043); the caliph sent the qadi al-qudat Mawardi to him as envoy {cite:tdv-elci}.

## Ayyubids, Mamluks and Mongols

Under Zengi and Saladin no standing envoy officer existed; men were commissioned as needed — Imad al-Din al-Isfahani, the Egyptian chief khatib Shams al-Din al-Ba'labakki, the Damascus chief qadi Ziya al-Din al-Shahrazuri {cite:tdv-elci}. Mamluk Egypt, midway between the Far East and the Mediterranean basin, saw constant envoy traffic: Ilkhanid, Golden Horde, Timurid, Ottoman, Byzantine and papal, and the Golden Horde embassy of 1314 arrived with 174 persons and precious gifts {cite:tdv-elci}. Among the Mongols envoys were always men — once the Georgian king David V sent a woman to negotiate — and Sultan Berkuk had some of Timur's envoys killed when he raided Mamluk lands {cite:tdv-elci}.

## The Ottoman embassy

From their beginnings the Ottomans dealt with Byzantium, the other beyliks and neighbouring states; Venice made the first treaty with them in 1408 {cite:tdv-elci}. The foreign envoy on Ottoman soil was a guest under the Islamic aman: the state saw to his road and his expenses, at times even before he crossed the border, but he was imprisoned if war opened between the two states {cite:tdv-elci}. Venice opened the first resident embassy at Istanbul in 1454, followed by Poland, Russia, France, Austria, England and Holland; the Ottomans kept no resident envoys in the West until Selim III opened permanent embassies in London, Paris and Vienna in 1793 {cite:tdv-elci}. They made the envoy's reception a display of power: goods from the Imperial Treasury, the Mehterhane and the Privy Chamber were set out as ornaments, the envoy's audience was timed to the "galebe divanı" on pay day, and his gifts were presented a day ahead and registered in the protocol books {cite:tdv-elci}. They sent ad hoc envoys for accessions, congratulations, peace terms, frontier disputes and answers to letters, and profited both from the rivalries of the resident Western ambassadors at Istanbul and from their own intelligence networks of converts and merchants {cite:tdv-elci}. When they resolved on resident missions they could not find Muslims who knew European languages and leaned on non-Muslim embassy dragomans — some of whom betrayed them — until the Sublime Porte's Translation Office (Bâbıâli Tercüme Odası) opened in 1821 to train Muslim diplomats; permanent embassies resumed in 1834 and never lapsed again {cite:tdv-elci}.
