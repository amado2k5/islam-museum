## Summary

The nakkash is the craftsman of painting and decoration: the title, derived from the root naksh — to paint in several colours, to ornament with needle or tool — originally embraced the engraver, the carver and the illuminator, but in history it came to mean above all the miniature painter {cite:tdv-nakkas}. In the book-arts workshop the nakkash's name covered the whole range of specialists: the müzehhib who gilded, the siyahkalem who worked in black pen, the musavvir who painted the pictures, the şebihnüvis, meclisnüvis, cetvelkeş, renkzen and tarrâh {cite:tdv-nakkas}. The standing of figural depiction in the law shaped the craft's fields: the jurists' discussion channelled Muslim art toward calligraphy, illumination and marbling {cite:tdv-resim}.

## Named after their materials

Early Islamic sources call the nakkashes after what they worked: nakkāsh al-fidda for silver, nakkāsh al-zahab for gold, nakkāsh al-sikka for the coin dies, nakkāsh al-hijara for stone {cite:tdv-nakkas}. The reports on the Prophet's signet ring led al-Kattani to argue a nakkash was already at work in Medina {cite:tdv-nakkas}. The musician Ibn Surayj's son-in-law Abu Mas'ud Sa'id is remembered as a nakkash who made stone cooking-pots at Abu Qubays, the Ash'ari theologian Abu Ja'far al-Sulami was a silver-nakkash, and the Persian slave Abu Lu'lu'a practised nakkash-work beside smithing and carpentry {cite:tdv-nakkas}.

## The eastern workshops

At Herat a book-arts academy (kitabkhana) trained many nakkashes and reached its brightest period under Sultan Husayn Bayqara (1470-1506); Bihzad, counted the greatest master of the miniature, gave his finest works there {cite:tdv-nakkas}. His line ran back through Pir Seyyid Ahmed of Tabriz to Ustad Cihangir of Bukhara, who had studied under the Chinese nakkash Gung — masters training masters across the courts {cite:tdv-nakkas}. Tabriz, Shiraz, Isfahan, Bukhara, Samarqand and Herat stayed great miniature centres through the Akkoyunlu, Karakoyunlu and Safavid periods; their masters include Sultan Muhammad, Muhammadi of Herat, Shahkulu who passed to the Ottoman palace, the woman nakkash Bibijah, Mir Musavvir, Agha Mirak, Dust Muhammad, Mirza Ali, Mir Sayyid Ali, Muzaffar Ali, Shaykh Muhammad and Abd al-Samad {cite:tdv-nakkas}.

## The Mughal atelier

Humayun brought Tabrizi masters home from exile, and under Akbar the Mughal atelier flourished; his vizier Abu'l-Fazl al-Allami devoted a chapter of the A'in-i Akbari to the nakkashes, from which we learn that Hindu and Muslim painters worked side by side — Abd al-Samad, Mir Sayyid Ali, Husayn Nakkash, Basawan, Keshav Das, Agha Riza Jahangiri, Farrukh Beg, Miskin, Manohar, Mir Hashim, Bisan Das, Bichitr, Abu'l-Hasan Nadir al-Zaman, Govardhan, Payag and Mansur Nadir al-Asr, famous for his nature studies {cite:tdv-nakkas}. Under Aurangzeb the palace workshop lost its earlier importance, but nakkashes outside the court carried the art on {cite:tdv-nakkas}.

## The Ottoman nakkashan corps

Ottoman nakkash-work began in Amasya and Bursa and developed at the Edirne palace, where masters thought to have come from Shiraz worked beside local ones; after the conquest of Istanbul, Sinan Bey painted Mehmed II's portraits, Şiblizade Ahmed of Bursa and Abdullah Nakkash illuminated the Shahnameh-i Melik Ümmi, and the masters Selim I brought from Iran and Egypt formed a new style recalling Herat {cite:tdv-nakkas}. The nakkashes became one of the chief divisions of the ehl-i hiref: a wage register of Rabi' II 932 (January-February 1526) names twenty-nine nakkashes and twelve shakirds under chiefs — sernakkashan, kethüda, serbölük and the oda heads — with masters like Shahkulu, Melek Ahmed, Hasan ibn Mehmed and Hasan ibn Abdülcelil at the head {cite:tdv-nakkas}. As kapıkulu they marched with the army on campaign, were paid every three months from the wage registers, and were appointed on the hazinedarbashi's recommendation and the reisülküttab's tezkere; when the palace workshop lacked a man for a commission, a master was hired from the market, and at the feasts the sultan rewarded the nakkash who brought his special work with a kaftan or coin {cite:tdv-nakkas}. Evliya calls them the esnaf-ı nakkashan-ı musavveran and says they had no pir because depiction was proscribed — forty men in four shops in his day {cite:tdv-nakkas}. The corps was abolished among ten idle divisions by the nizamname of 1826 {cite:tdv-nakkas}.

## The image in the law

The RESİM entry records the long legal discussion: sûret and tasvir stand in the classical texts for image and depiction, the prohibition of figures is argued from the danger of idolatry and from rivalling the Creator, and the jurists' reasoning held the ban to attach where the image leads to shirk or is made an object of veneration — an analysis that reads much figural craft as mubah when that cause is absent {cite:tdv-resim}. Whatever the verdicts, the practical effect the entry names is the one the museum's other crafts show: the restriction steered Muslim artists toward other channels of beauty, and the arts of calligraphy, illumination and marbling grew in the space it left {cite:tdv-resim}.
