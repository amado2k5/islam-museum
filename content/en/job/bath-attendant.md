## Summary

The hammami is the owner or keeper who runs a public bath: he sees to heating the water, the fuel, the fees and the staff {cite:tdv-hamam II}. In the Islamic and Ottoman city the bath had a fixed place; many baths were waqf income-buildings that served the congregation of the mosque at the heart of the complex {cite:tdv-hamam I}. The entry sets out the working staff inside the bath — the tellak, the natar and the other servants — and the kadılık rules that bound them {cite:tdv-hamam II}.

## The word and the institution

The entry derives hamam from the Arabic root for heating and heat; the hamam is "the heated place" and then "the bathing place" {cite:tdv-hamam I}. Its history section follows the bath from Greek and Roman baths into the cities of Islam, noting that in Roman baths the services were done by slaves, and that baths spread through Muslim cities until they became part of the urban fabric and daily life {cite:tdv-hamam I}. In the Ottoman period many baths were endowed because they brought a good income and served the mosque congregation {cite:tdv-hamam I}.

## The bath's staff

In women's baths the customers were served by the natar; in men's baths the washer was the tellak or dellak {cite:tdv-hamam II}. Men's baths also had other servants called hamam uşakları, among them the kulhancı, meydancı, peştamalcı, çıkmacı and kahveci {cite:tdv-hamam II}. There were also laundry workers who washed the towels, destimals and peştamals and hung them to dry on the kafes {cite:tdv-hamam II}.

## Guild and discipline

The entry reports Evliya Çelebi's guild lore: the hamamcılar's pir is Muhsin b. Osman, the tellaks' pir is Ubeyd-i Mısrî and the natars' pir is Mansur b. Osman {cite:tdv-hamam II}. For eighteenth-century Cairo it cites Raymond's archive study, which identifies a hamamcılar loncası among the city's baths {cite:tdv-hamam I}. The kadılık nizamnamesi put bath workers under chained surety, made the senior servant, the sernöbet tellak, answerable for them all, and fixed the tellaks' and natars' duties to customers and the fees for services {cite:tdv-hamam II}.

## Fiqh and earnings

Most jurists permitted entering the bath provided the awrah is covered, and in principle permitted building, selling, leasing and operating baths {cite:tdv-hamam III}. Those who were hesitant about the bath keeper's earnings looked at the looseness over covering; their stance can be read as a warning that keeps people from the harm rather than a ban on the trade itself {cite:tdv-hamam III}.

## Today

The name "Turkish bath" spread in the West, but the entry observes that many such institutions had no real link with the actual hamam {cite:tdv-hamam II}. Public baths still exist in Turkey and elsewhere, yet the hammami's trade and its servants have been transformed by water in the home and modern standards of hygiene {cite:tdv-hamam II}.

## The bath and society

The bath was a social room as well as a place to wash: a single bath opened to women by day and men by night, while the çifte hamam kept separate sections for each {cite:tdv-hamam II}. Women brought food and ate it on the göbek taşı, and the bath gained fixed places in life's passages — the gelin hamamı before the wedding, the kırk hamamı for the mother on day forty, and the güvey hamamı for the groom {cite:tdv-hamam II}.

The fire brigades had a share of it too: after a fire the tulumbacı came to the bath, the owner charged them nothing by day and closed the bath to them by night, and Istanbul bath owners kept the custom of giving iftar to their leaders in Ramadan {cite:tdv-hamam II}. Bath gear was also art: the peştamal, towels, bath bowls and nalın, the clogs made of walnut, boxwood, ebony and sandal and inlaid with mother-of-pearl and tortoiseshell {cite:tdv-hamam II}. From the fifteenth century baths became winter salons for divan poets, and the poems written about them were called hammâmiyye {cite:tdv-hamam II}. In Mevlevi usage, "taking him to the bath" marked the end of the retreat: the meydancı washed the dervish and dressed him anew to earn the saka post, and the "hamam seal" was a paper stamped with the word hamam that dervishes gave the keeper instead of coin {cite:tdv-hamam II}.
