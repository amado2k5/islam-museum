## Summary

The sandukji made the boxes, chests and baskets that stored the household's goods — leather trunks in Istanbul, mother-of-pearl inlaid boxes and small cabinets in Cairo {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 400, p. 208; chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 80-83}. Two sources, two centuries apart, show the same small craft corporation: a guild that marched in armour in Evliya's procession, and one that in 1868 was still fighting its own shaykhs over the tax register {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 400, p. 208; chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 80-83}.

## The Seped-Sandukchi of Istanbul

In the great procession of the guilds, Evliya counts "the makers of leather baskets and trunks (Seped Sandukchi)" at one hundred and five men with thirty shops; they fill their shops with different kinds of baskets and trunks, and "pass all clad in armour" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 400, p. 208}. The entry is short — three lines — but it fixes the craft's shape: a small specialist guild beside the halter-makers and the sumpter-saddlers, working leather into the containers that carried grain, kit and household goods {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 400, p. 208}.

## The al-Khidr box makers of Cairo

Two hundred years on, the guild appears in a petition. In December 1868 the "people of the al-Khidr box makers' guild" (ahali ta'ifat al-sanadiqiyya al-khidr) — artisans who made furniture of some kind, either ornamental boxes inlaid with mother-of-pearl or small cabinets — complained to the Interior Ministry that the guild's leaders and senior members were trying to foist most of the professional-tax burden onto the poorer and weaker members, a scam in which the local tax office was also involved {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 80-81}.

## The register and the closed shops

The box makers opened with the regulation they knew should govern them: "The assessment of the professional tax is according to the level of income and taxation raised from craftsworkers is according to the tax brackets fixed by the Government bureaus" {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 80}. Everywhere else it held, they said, "except in our guild, whose condition has deteriorated, and whose poverty is evil and which now suffers from incapacity" {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 81}. The tax office had fixed different rates and the leadership had fraudulently sealed the register affirming official brackets; when the Sar Mi'mar — the "head of construction" — investigated, he found the truth at variance with the leaders' claims {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 81}. Meanwhile the office had closed the workshops to force payment, demanding 125 piastres a year of eight-year-old apprentices paid only a piastre and a quarter a day — every shop, they said, carried two such journeyman-apprentices (sunna'in ashraq), taxed on both {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 81-82, 63}. "Even if we sell all our capital," they wrote, "then this would not come to half of the assessment" {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 82}.

## A small craft's two centuries

Between the armoured basket-makers of the procession and the petitioning cabinet-makers of Khedivial Cairo runs the ordinary life of a minor guild: a patronless band of a hundred, filling thirty shops with leather trunks; then, under Isma'il's tax-raising state, the same small corporation discovering that its register could be sealed against it, its workshops closed, its eight-year-old apprentices assessed as masters — and learning, as Chalcraft notes, a new kind of politics addressed to ministry, council and governorate rather than to the old elite patrons {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 400, p. 208; chalcraft-striking-cabbies pp. 80-83}.
