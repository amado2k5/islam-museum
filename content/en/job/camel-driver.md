## Summary

The cameleer — the jammāl of the Arabs, the deveci of the Turks, the sarbân of the palace — drove the animal the Arabs called the sefînetü's-sahrâ, the ship of the desert {cite:tdv-deve}. Evliya counts a thousand camel-drivers (Shuturban, Sarban) attendant on the pilgrimage caravan in the Istanbul procession, clad in armour and richly dressed, playing drums and fifes; their patron is Weis-ul-Karni of Yemen, buried in the town of Earn {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer}.

## The Janissary cameleers

The Ottomans used the Persian word şütür/üştür for the camel and named the Janissary cameleers the şütürbân; by the mid-seventeenth century twenty-five of the cemaat ortas were cameleer companies, their commander's post — the şütürbân ağa — reached from the yayabaşılık, and the most senior was called başdeveci {cite:tdv-deve}. These men were granted zeâmets of 28,000 akçe in the first half of the sixteenth century, and a promoted agha went on to the haseki or to be Rumeli agha of the Acemi Ocağı {cite:tdv-deve}.

## The palace sarbân

The palace camel-keepers were the sârbân or sârvân under a sârbânbaşı; the Has Ahır held a thousand katars of camels in the mid-seventeenth century, drawn from all over Anatolia and sorted by sex — the sârbân-ı mâde looked after the females, the sârbân-ı ner the males, with further hands for the gear and other needs {cite:tdv-deve}.

## Camels on campaign

The army's camels were taken into care and fattened before a march — the cannoneers' clerk Abdülkadir Efendi notes they were greased in the İznik district before the eastward campaigns, "until the üştürs are greased in the customary way the loads cannot be carried" {cite:tdv-deve}. His figures give 800 katars (5,600 camels) for the 1597 Vasıt campaign and 1,200 katars (8,400) for the 1621 Polish campaign; the state also hired cameleers from the Turkmen tribes, among whom the buğurcu families stood obliged to breed camels for it {cite:tdv-deve}.

## The caravan's gear

Heavy caravans loaded each camel with five or six kantars, light ones with three — the kantar running to 44 okka, about 57 kilograms {cite:tdv-deve}. A thread through each camel's nostril was knotted to the rump of the animal ahead so that it could be loosed quickly in trouble, and a bell hung on the last camel told the driver, going ahead or alongside, whether the katar was moving soundly {cite:tdv-deve}. The pack-saddle, the hamut, came soft or on a wooden frame — the Arabs' big rahl and the small kateb used to school two-year-olds to the load — and on it rode the hevdec or mahfe, the little chamber carried for women's journeys, from which Aisha directed her army at the Battle of the Camel {cite:tdv-deve}.

## The driver's songs

The camel endures load, cold, heat, hunger and thirst long, but heals hard when sick or wounded, so it took special care: before the march, püse — tar mixed with fat — was rubbed on its rump and where the girths pressed, and it was fed grass, thorns and the bran-and-salt dough called lop {cite:tdv-deve}. Gentle and fond of music, the camel was driven to the hidā songs that hold a place in Arabic literature; among the Companions, Anjasha was remembered as skilled in them {cite:tdv-deve}.
