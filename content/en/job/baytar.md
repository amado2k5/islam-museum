## Summary

The baytar is the veterinarian and farrier: he treats the illnesses of the beasts of burden and shoes their hooves {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 33, p. 99}. Al-Shayzari calls his science honourable and harder than the medicine of men, for the animal cannot speak its sickness; its ailments are known only by touching and looking {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 33, p. 99}. In Evliya's Istanbul the farriers march in two guilds — five hundred horseshoe-smiths with two hundred shops and a thousand farriers with a hundred and fifty — among the most indispensable guilds of the imperial camp {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer vol. 1 pt. 2, secs. 272, 290, pp. 181, 187}.

## An honourable science

Veterinary science is an honourable one which philosophers have written about in their books and on which they have composed numerous treatises {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 33, p. 99}. It is more difficult to treat the illnesses of animals than those of men, because an animal cannot tell of its sickness and pain: information about its ailments is got only by touching and looking, so the veterinarian needs skill and understanding of the illnesses of animals and their treatment {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 33, p. 99}. No one should practise it who has not a religion to stop him harming the animal by inexperienced blood-letting, cutting or cauterization, leading to its death or injury {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 33, p. 99}.

## The farrier's hands

The veterinarian examines the animal's ankle and inspects its hoof before paring: if the hoof is crooked he pares the opposite side until it is even; if the foot stands straight he uses small nails behind and large in front, reversing them if it stands the other way {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 33, p. 100}. He must not pare the hoof into the flesh, nor set the nails so loose that the shoe moves and lets in stones and sand, nor so tight that he tortures the animal — hammered shoes stick best to the hoof, soft shoes take hard nails, and thin nails are better than thick {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 33, p. 100}.

## Opening a vein

If the animal needs a vein opened, the veterinarian holds the scalpel between his fingers with the handle in his palm, leaving half a fingernail's length of blade exposed, and delicately cuts the vein on its upper side {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 33, p. 100}. He must not cut until he has stopped the vein with his finger — above all the jugular veins, dangerous because they lie beside the gullet: for those he binds the animal's neck firmly so they stand out, then does as he intends {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 33, p. 100}.

## The book of the diseases

The people resort to the veterinarian when they cannot agree on what the illness is; the learned have counted in the books of veterinary science three hundred and twenty illnesses of the animals {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 33, p. 100}. Among them are tightness in the gullet, nostril-disease with and without discharge, madness and weakness of the brain, stomach worms, colic and gripes, cold and hot coughs, blood bursting from the anus and the penis, throat disease, difficulty in urinating, pain in the joints, stones caught in hooves, swelling of the foot, whitlows, split hooves, facial paralysis, watering eyes and toothache {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 33, p. 100}. He must know the treatment of each and its reason, and which leave lasting damage and which do not; the muhtasib must not neglect to test him on all of it and to watch what he does with the people's animals {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 33, pp. 100-101}.

## The guilds of Istanbul

Evliya passes the horseshoe-smiths (ahengeran-i na'l-kesen) at five hundred men with two hundred shops — patrons David and Abu Muslim — making horse-shoes in their shops, one of the most useful and necessary guilds in a camp {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer vol. 1 pt. 2, sec. 272, p. 181}. The farriers proper (na'lbenjian) he counts at a thousand men with a hundred and fifty shops: their patron is Khizr, who shod the Prophet's mule Duldul unseen, and the chief of farriers in the Prophet's time Abu'l-Russem Essemak Beitari, girded by Selman and buried at Hormuz — this is Evliya's guild lore, told as he tells it {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer vol. 1 pt. 2, sec. 290, p. 187}. They claimed precedence over the locksmiths on the ground that they are indispensable in war time, and were at last ranked as the locksmiths' assistants; in the procession they show a complete array of horse-shoes of the Bosnian, Syrian, Vicegrad and Hersek kinds and the other instruments of farriery {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer vol. 1 pt. 2, sec. 290, p. 187}.

## Today

The baytar's craft has split and been transformed: the farrier shoes hooves still as a blacksmith's trade, while animal medicine is the licensed profession of veterinary medicine — but the muhtasib's frame, a man of religion tested on what he does with the people's animals, is still the public's expectation {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 33, pp. 99-101}.

## Not yet covered

Al-Shayzari gives the craft's duties and Evliya its Ottoman guild numbers, but not the baytar's fees, his apprenticeship, the army veterinary service, the horse-doctors of the countryside, nor veterinarians outside the hisba cities {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 33, pp. 99-101} {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer vol. 1 pt. 2, secs. 272, 290}.
