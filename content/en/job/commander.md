## Summary

The serdar — Persian ser 'head' with the -dâr suffix, 'the one who holds the lead' — was the army commander's title used above all by the Turks and passing from them into Arabic {cite:tdv-serdar}. In Iran it ran as an honorific — serdâr-ı zafer, serdâr-ı ceng; in India it named the heads of the small eighteenth-century states, the Indian officer of a unit under the British ('serdâr-log'), and a grade of distinction, 'serdâr-bahâdur'; in Egypt and the Sudan 'sirdar' was the British commander of the nineteenth century {cite:tdv-serdar}. Under the Great Seljuks, the Anatolian Seljuks and the Ilkhanids the equivalent terms were sipehsâlâr, serleşker, subaşı and serasker {cite:tdv-serdar}.

## The Ottoman serdar

In Ottoman usage the serdar was usually the vezir, beylerbeyi or sancak beyi commissioned to a province or a sancak — and sometimes the kapudan pasha at the fleet's head {cite:tdv-serdar}. Handed the serdarlık berat that made his office formal, he prepared the campaign on the centre's orders, carried wide powers of appointment, dismissal and dirlik-granting, and until the eighteenth century could even affix the tuğra to blank decree papers when the sealed stock ran out — a power Kemankeş Mustafa Pasha's withdrawal of it provoked Nasuhpaşazâde Hüseyin's revolt in Erzurum {cite:tdv-serdar}. His retinue took janissaries, armourers, gunners and cavalry with a defterdar or treasury clerk for the finances and a tezkireci doing a reîsülküttap's work; on the march he held divan and heard cases, and his campaign spending was not charged against him {cite:tdv-serdar}.

## The serdâr-ı ekrem

When the sultan did not campaign in person the commanding grand vezir was the serdâr-ı ekrem: his authority swelled, senior officials marched in his suite, the sultan handed him the sancak-ı şerif at the Davud Pasha field for western campaigns and the Doğancılar Meydanı for eastern {cite:tdv-serdar}. On campaign the serdâr-ı ekrem appointed, granted and dismissed without asking the centre, held divan in the winter quarters, could confer a vezirate or pass a political death sentence — Kara Mustafa Pasha had the Budin governor Uzun İbrahim Pasha executed at Vienna in 1683 — and his telhis dispatches were never refused, lest his standing with the army break {cite:tdv-serdar}. His registers were kept apart: campaign appointments were written 'ordudan tayin edilmiştir', 'appointed from the army' {cite:tdv-serdar}.

## Other uses and the aftermath

The word also meant 'head of a corps': a serdâr-ı ulemâ appears under Mehmed II, a 'kır serdarı' kept the intercity roads safe, and the veteran officers sent by the yeniçeri ağası to keep order in small towns were the yeniçeri serdarı {cite:tdv-serdar}. The older serasker parted company and became the title of the Asâkir-i Mansûre-i Muhammediyye's supreme commander after the janissary corps was abolished; the serdâr-ı ekrem name was revived in the nineteenth century for non-grand-vezir seraskers like Ömer Lutfi Pasha in the Crimean war of 1853 and Abdülkerim Nâdir Pasha in the Russian war of 1876 {cite:tdv-serdar}. Romanian took the word from Turkish for a military commander, cavalry pasha and boyar {cite:tdv-serdar}.

## What the title was not

The office was a commission, not a craft: the serdar's berat defined his province or campaign, Lutfi Pasha grudged serdarlıks scattered among sancak beyis and beylerbeyis and held the grand vezir should watch the serdar, and Âlî Mustafa Efendi argued the padishah should lead the campaigns himself rather than send serdars {cite:tdv-serdar}.
