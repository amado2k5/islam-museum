## Summary
The tanner treated animal hides until they could be used. The TDV encyclopaedia says the early Arabs tanned with acacia pods, pomegranate peel and alum, and that Cordoba and Morocco became leather centres from which the Western names cordovan and moroccan come. The jurists ruled on the hides of dead animals and of the pig. In Ottoman Istanbul the tanners had a guild and a set place for sharing out hides, and in Anatolia their craft is attached to Ahi Evran. {cite:tdv-deri p.174-178}

## The word and its sources
The short entry DEBBAG defines the tanner as the craftsman who works hides into a usable state and points to the entry on leather. The substance is in that entry, in three parts: Nebi Bozkurt on the history, Mustafa Baktir on the rulings, and Zeki Tekin on leather-working among the Turks. {cite:tdv-deri p.174-178}

## Hides and tanning among the early Arabs
The entry says the early Arabs ground the leaves or fruit of the acacia in stone mills brought from the Akik valley, and also used pomegranate peel and alum. It lists leather terms such as kash and tiraf for hide tents, and rakk for parchment. Yemen, Sa'da and Taif were centres, and Mecca had a tanning trade. The entry names two wives of the Prophet who worked leather, [[person/sawda-bint-zama|Sawda bint Zam'a]], skilled in Taif-style work, and [[person/zaynab-bint-jahsh|Zaynab bint Jahsh]], who sold leather goods and gave the proceeds in charity. These are the entry's accounts, and the sources it cites were not read. It also names Sa'd al-Qaraz, a Medina muezzin, probably called after trading qaraz. {cite:tdv-deri p.174-175}

## What the jurists said
Baktir reports that all agree the hide of a properly slaughtered edible animal is pure without tanning, and that the hide of a dead animal can be used only after tanning. The majority hold that the pig is forbidden and its hide cannot be purified. Abu Yusuf among the Hanafis and Sahnun and Ibn Abd al-Hakam among the Malikis hold that tanning cleans it. The Hanafis allow the hides of all animals except the pig after tanning. The Hanbalis and most Malikis do not hold that tanning purifies the hide of a dead animal, because of a hadith about the Prophet's last days, and the entry notes that critics found defects in that hadith and that a contrary hadith says tanning purifies. All agree that tanning must dry, clean and remove the smell. {cite:tdv-deri p.175-176}

## Centres of the craft
The entry says the techniques developed at Cordoba and in Morocco, and that the Western terms cordovan and moroccan, maroquin, come from them. Cairo and Aleppo were also centres. In the thirteenth century Aleppo's tanneries paid more tax than all its other workshops together, in the entry's words. Under the Seljuks Diyarbakir and Kastamonu are named, and under the Ottomans Istanbul, Edirne, Kayseri, Ankara, Bursa, Manisa, Tokat and Konya. Tavernier is cited for red morocco at Diyarbakir, yellow at Mosul, black at Urfa and the finest at Tokat. {cite:tdv-deri p.175-178}

## The workshop in Istanbul
For eighteenth-century Istanbul the entry describes the hides washed in plenty of water and laid in lime pits, with hair and flesh scraped off by a special knife. They were layered with gall, acorn cups or the dung of pigeon, hen and dog, and scraped again, again and again until the quality was reached. The leather was softened with tallow or fish oil, dyed and sold. A workshop was two or three storeys, with a water-powered mill for grinding the acorn cups, and wells. It held fifteen to twenty or more workers under one master, with apprentices and journeymen. {cite:tdv-deri p.176-178}

At first the workshops stood outside the towns, on the coast or where rivers left the settlement. As towns grew, the smell and the waste became a health problem, and an 1838 quarantine regulation ordered them planted with trees or moved. The entry gives Evliya Celebi's count of some 700 workshops in Istanbul in the seventeenth century, chiefly at Kazlicesme, Kasimpasa and Uskudar. {cite:tdv-deri p.176-178}

## Guild, patron and the state
The managers of the tanners' guild shared the raw hides at a set place according to each workshop's capacity, and finished leather went mostly to the sewers' guild in the Mercan market. The tanners had first to supply the arsenal, the powder store, the foundry and the military band. Prices of raw and semi-finished hides were fixed by a commission and recorded in the qadi registers, and export needed state permission under a monopoly. The entry says a Muslim offender was jailed and a non-Muslim sent to the galleys. For Anatolia, the entry says various studies show Ahi Evran as the originator of tanning and calls him the pir of the tanners. The museum has no person record for him. {cite:tdv-deri p.176-178}

The entry also notes that containers for food and drink were often made of leather, so tanners came under the market inspector's rules, and it cites Ibn al-Ukhuwwa's Ma'alim al-Qurba on this. That book was not read. {cite:tdv-deri p.175-176}

## Decline
After the Tanzimat ended the monopoly and the shop privilege, the tanners lost their rights and foreign buyers took the hides. A reform commission of 1864 led to the Tanners' Company of September 1866, which was short-lived. Small factories opened in the 1850s, and the Beykoz factory of Mahmud II became a state enterprise in the Republic. The Kazlicesme tanneries moved to Aydinlikoy and Kazlicesme is now a park. {cite:tdv-deri p.176-178}
