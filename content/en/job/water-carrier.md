## Summary

The saqqa carried and sold drinking water in the streets and to the houses of the Islamic city {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, pp. 318-322}. In Cairo, where the well water was brackish, a numerous body of sakkas drew the city's supply from the Nile in leather skins on camels, asses and their own backs {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 319}. Hisba rules guarded the purity of what they drew and the safety of their animals {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 40, pp. 133-134}, and in Istanbul Evliya counted fourteen hundred town water-carriers and eight thousand on foot {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 80-81, pp. 123-124}.

## Carrying the Nile

Because the well water of Cairo was slightly brackish, numerous sakkas made their livelihood supplying its inhabitants with Nile water: during the months after the canal opened they drew from the canal that runs through the city, and at other times from the river {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 319}. The camel carried the rei, a pair of wide bags of ox-hide holding three or four kirbehs; the ass, or the sakka on foot, bore the goatskin kirbeh {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, pp. 319-320}. His general cry was "O may God compensate me", and for a goat's skin of water brought a mile and a half or two miles he earned scarcely more than a penny {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 320}.

## The street sellers

Many sakkas supplied water to passers-by: the sakka sharbeh, whose kirbeh had a long brass spout, poured into a brass cup or an earthen kulleh for whoever would drink {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 320}. A more numerous class, the hemalees, were mostly Rifa'ee or Beiyoomee darweshes exempt from the firdeh income-tax; the hemalee carried on his back the porous earthen ibreek that cools the water, sometimes offered kulleh water scented with orange-flower water to his best customers, kept a sprig of naring in the jar's mouth, and hung a wallet at his side for the bread or food the poor gave him instead of coin {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, pp. 320-321}.

## Water as charity

At moolids and other festivals the water-carriers were paid by visitors to distribute water to passers-by, a cupful to whoever desired, a work of charity called tesbeel done for the saint's sake; the carriers so employed were generally allowed to fill their ibreeks and kirbehs at a public fountain, since they asked nothing of those they supplied, and they chanted a cry inviting the thirsty to the gift and praying paradise and pardon for its giver {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, pp. 321-322}.

## The water-carrier under hisba

Al-Shayzari orders the owners of water-bags and skins to wade far into the river, away from the bank and the dirty places; they must not draw near an animal watering place, a public drinking place or the outlet of a bath, but go upstream or far downstream {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 40, pp. 133-134}. A new skin is first used for days to carry water for building clay, and only when the tan and pitch taste has gone may he sell it for drinking {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 40, p. 134}. Their animals must carry bells or clappers so that the blind, children and the inattentive are warned, must not be overloaded, driven fiercely, beaten severely or left standing loaded in a courtyard, and must be fed enough {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 40, p. 134}. Al-Maqrizi, quoted in the introduction, adds that the muhtasib's deputies ordered the water-carriers to cover their skin vessels with cloth, gauged their loads at twenty-four buckets of forty ratls, and made them wear short blue trousers that kept them covered {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley introduction, pp. 33-34}.

## Istanbul's sakas

In Evliya's procession the water-carriers of the town, unlike the military sakas of the corps, numbered fourteen hundred; they carried water from the city's nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine fountains to where it was needed, their patron Selman of Cufa and their chief the head of the Imperial water-carriers, their meeting place near Aya Sofiyah, and they passed distributing water from horses decked with flowers {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 80, p. 123}. The water-carriers on foot numbered eight thousand, wearing black leather jackets, carrying jacks on their backs and crystal, china and golden cups from which they gave drink in remembrance of the martyrs of Kerbela, sprinkling the roads and the crowds as they passed, followed by the colonel Saka-bashi and the head of the Imperial water-carriers {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 81, pp. 123-124}.

## Today

Piped water in the house replaced the saqqa's daily round: the trade survives mainly in its charitable and ceremonial forms — the tesbeel of the moolids and the saqi of the festivals — rather than as the city's water supply {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, pp. 319-322}.

## Not yet covered

The sources describe the carriers and their rules but name no individual saqqa and give no wage series beyond Lane's penny a skin. The Ottoman military sakas of the Janissary corps, only touched here, deserve their own treatment {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 80-81, pp. 123-124}.
