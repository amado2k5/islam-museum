## Summary

The tahhan is the miller who grinds the town's grain and delivers its flour {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, pp. 28-29}. Because bread begins at his millstone, the hisba manuals put him under the muhtasib's eye: Ibn al-Ukhuwwa orders the grain cleansed, the sieves renewed and the flour weighed, and al-Shayzari lists the adulterations the inspector must catch {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, pp. 28-29} {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 5, pp. 45-46}. Evliya Çelebi counts nearly a thousand horse-mills among the guilds of Istanbul and marches the millers in the procession {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 82, pp. 124-126}, and in modern Egypt milling and baking still ranked among the chief food trades {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 17}.

## The miller's rules

Ibn al-Ukhuwwa orders that the millers cleanse the grain of earth before they grind it and that the sieves be renewed periodically {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, pp. 28-29}. Al-Shayzari gives the same rule to the flour merchants, who must sieve the dirt out of the grain and remove all the darnel and dust before it is milled, and adds that a little water sprinkled on the wheat while it is being milled makes the flour white and wholesome {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 5, pp. 45-46}. They may not hoard grain, nor mix bad wheat with good nor old with new, and if washing is necessary the grain must be well dried and each type of wheat sold separately {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 5, pp. 45-46}.

## The flour that must be honest

The muhtasib examines the flour against admixture with the flour of chick-peas and the like, and stops the milling on the worn particles of the millstone, which cause harm to people {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, pp. 28-29}. Al-Shayzari adds the other suspected frauds: sieved barley flour, bean flour, flour milled on an uneven millstone, or flour mixed with darnel or the dust of the mill — and if the muhtasib has doubts he makes the millers swear an oath {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 5, pp. 45-46}. For the common good he stipulates that they produce a daily quota of flour and distribute it to the shops and bakeries {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 5, pp. 45-46}.

## Flour to the door

Ibn al-Ukhuwwa has the muhtasib assign the quantities the millers must deliver to the bakers' shops each day, and compels them to mill flour for people's houses too, since most people avoided eating bread from the market {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, pp. 28-29}. The men who carry this flour must be honourable and trustworthy, for they enter men's houses and converse with their children and female slaves {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, pp. 28-29}. Flour is received and delivered by weight; on every jar the owner's name and dwelling are written and placed in a gourd attached to the handle, and one person's flour must never be mingled with another's in the mill-hopper {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, pp. 28-29}. The scales, weights, measures and jars are all inspected, and the receptacles must be sound lest the flour be lost on the roads; the standard sack, the tillis, is three battas of fifty ratls each {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, pp. 28-29}.

## The mill animals

The animals employed for grinding must be permitted to rest each day and night, and none may be made to grind more than a wayba at a time {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, pp. 28-29}. Evliya preserves the same scruple inside the guild ceremony: every guild has a patron saint except the horse-millers, who have none, because no saint can afford his patronage to men who torment animals in that way — and there is no enjoying quietly the bread whose flour was ground by horse-mills {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 89, p. 126}.

## The millers of Istanbul

Evliya counts the millers (Deghirmenjian) at nine hundred and eighty-five horse-mills served by nine thousand eight hundred men; they construct mills on wagons which are put in motion by the wheels of the wagon and grind flour as they go, and at the procession they throw flour on the spectators crying "God make your face white" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 82, pp. 124-125}. Of windmills he says they alone were in use in the Prophet's time and still supply Mecca and Medina; watermills were a later invention, the first erected in Egypt in the Caliph al-Hakim's time, and Constantinople had only four, driven by the winter overflow of the conduit of the Forty Fountains {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 82, pp. 124-125}. The horse-mills, he adds, dated in the Ottoman Empire from the conquest of Buda, where Sultan Suleiman found one {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 89, p. 126}. With the millers march the flour-merchants, three thousand men, for the most part Egyptian peasants clad in armour, their faces, eyes and dresses white with flour, and the corn-purifiers, three thousand persons attached to the mills and bake-houses who separate the grains so skilfully that the falling corn traces words on the ground {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 83-84, pp. 125}.

## Today

The trade persisted into the modern city: in the count of Cairo's crafts before the 1860s, the food trades — especially milling and baking — occupied most of the artisans after the textile workers, in small workshops with relatively simple tools {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 17}. Mechanised milling later transformed the work, but the hisba questions the manuals ask — clean grain, honest flour, rested animals, a labelled jar — are the same ones a mill inspector asks today {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XI, pp. 28-29}.
