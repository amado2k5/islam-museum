## Summary

The haddad hammered iron into the tools and fittings of daily life {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 271, p. 183}. Al-Shayzari polices the honesty of the forge, above all against selling soft iron as steel {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 32, pp. 98-99}, and Evliya gives Constantinople's smiths a thousand shops and the right to march ahead of the following guilds, since the imperial camp could not do without them {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 271, p. 183}.

## Honest iron

The blacksmiths must not hammer out knives, scissors, pincers and the like from soft iron and sell them as steel, for that is fraud {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 32, pp. 98-99}. They must not mix straightened-out used nails with the newly forged, and knives, scissors and razors are to be made only of pure steel {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 32, p. 99}.

## The smith's neighbours

The same hisba chapter binds the coppersmiths: they must not mix copper with the habag that goldsmiths and silver-smelters extract, for it hardens the copper until a vessel breaks like glass, nor mix broken old vessels with unused copper — each must be smelted and worked separately {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 32, pp. 98-99}.

## The smiths of Constantinople

In all four jurisdictions of the capital the smiths had no less than a thousand shops {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 271, p. 183}. As their handicraft was among the most indispensable in the imperial camp, not only for the artillery but in every respect, they obtained precedence over the guilds that followed them {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 271, p. 183}. In the procession they passed on large wagons, their shops adorned with flowers, striking iron on the anvil and crying "O David"; some carried iron rods, some hammers on their shoulders {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 271, p. 183}.

## Patrons of the forge

In Evliya's guild lore the smiths' first patron is David, buried near Jerusalem beside his son Suleiman, and their patron in the Prophet's time was Abuzeid Moslem Haddadi, girded by Selman in Ali's presence; all smiths, he writes, derive their lineage from him, and his tomb is at Sana'a in Yemen {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 271, p. 183}.

## The guild's branches

The smiths marched with their fellow ironworkers: the farriers, five hundred men with two hundred shops, shoemakers of horses and a necessary guild in a camp; the nail-smiths, many of them Armenians from Divrighin, whose trade mattered in a city of frequent fires; the smiths of the small boot-nails, two hundred men at Churkurhamam by the mosque of Sultan Mehmed; and the weight and quintal makers {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 272-275, p. 183}.

## Today

The village and city smithy has been largely replaced by machine shops and manufactured hardware; the haddad survives in farriery, decorative ironwork and repair, a transformation of the trade the sources describe {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 271-275, p. 183}.

## Not yet covered

The sources give the smiths' guild face and the hisba rules against fraud but no account of furnaces, bellows or named smiths; the cutlers, armourers and locksmith guilds Evliya lists separately remain uncovered here {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 271-275, p. 183}.
