## Summary

The nahhas is the coppersmith who smelts, casts and hammers copper and brass into the pots, cups and mortars of the kitchen and the market {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 32, pp. 98-99}. Al-Shayzari polices the metal itself — what may be mixed into copper and how scrap may be reused {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 32, pp. 98-99} — while Evliya Çelebi numbers the kettle-makers of Istanbul among the great guilds, four thousand men behind a factory built by Mehmed II {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 297, pp. 187-188}.

## The rules of the metal

Al-Shayzari forbids the coppersmiths to mix copper with a substance called habag, which the goldsmiths and smelters of silver extract when they smelt; it hardens the copper and makes it less malleable, so that a drinking vessel or mortar moulded from it breaks easily, like glass {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 32, pp. 98-99}. They must not mix pieces of broken copper containers with unused copper: each must be smelted separately and worked separately {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 32, pp. 98-99}.

## The kettle-makers of Istanbul

Evliya counts the kettle-makers (Kazanjian) at four thousand men with nine hundred shops; their factory, a great building raised by Mehmed II, stood within the gate of the Ayazmah, and their patron was Abu Habib Mohay-ud-din Bokhara, girded by Selman {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 297, pp. 187-188}. In his telling the first to make iron kettles and pans was David, and Abu Habib made the first copper one, for from Adam down to David the pots were all earthenware {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 297, pp. 187-188}.

## Smelters and polishers

With the kettle-makers march the purifiers of brass, sixty-five men in four manufactories at the towers of the Flour-hall, whose bellows of buffalo's skin are each worked by ten men and raise such a heat that no one can come near; they pass on wagons melting brass as they go {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 298, p. 188}. A hundred and five polishers of brass kettles pass polishing vessels on wheels, and five hundred kettle-merchants in three hundred shops — mostly Persians, Evliya notes, crying their wares in their own dialect — adorn their stands with every kind of brass vessel {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 300-301, p. 188}.

## Neighbours in the row

The coppersmiths shared their chapter with the blacksmiths, whose rule al-Shayzari gives in the same breath: they must not hammer knives, scissors and pincers from soft iron and sell it as steel, nor mix straightened-out nails with newly forged ones {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 32, pp. 98-99}. The closeness of the two trades — one shaping metal cold on the anvil, the other pouring it molten — is the shape of the metalworkers' row itself {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 32, pp. 98-99}.

## Today

The nahhas's trade is still living in the old coppersmiths' bazaars, where vessels are tinned, engraved and repaired by hand, though factory cookware has taken most of the market the kettle-makers once filled {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 297, pp. 187-188}.
