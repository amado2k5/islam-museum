## Summary

The sabbagh is the dyer who colours thread and cloth for the public {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 28, p. 92}. His vats sat inside the market order: al-Shayzari's hisba manual lists the dyers' frauds and the trust rules for the garments in their keeping {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 28, p. 92}, Lane names the sabbagh among the trades of the Cairo market streets {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}, and Evliya Çelebi marches eight hundred dyers through Istanbul's guild procession, dyeing linen as they go {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 502, p. 224}.

## The craft and its frauds

Al-Shayzari reports that most dyers of red silk, and of other thread and material, dye with henna instead of madder in their shops: the dye looks good and bright, but when it is exposed to the sun its colour changes and its brightness fades {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 28, p. 92}. Others darken cloth with oak apples and vitriol when they want the colour of kohl; put into large containers, it comes out a uniform jet-black, but the colour changes after a short time and the dye wears off — all of this is fraudulent and the muhtasib must stop it {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 28, p. 92}.

## The customer's clothes

Because the dyer held other people's property, the manual binds him to account: dyers must write the customers' names on their clothes in ink so that none get confused, and the muhtasib must show their dye-work to their 'arif {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 28, p. 92}. At holidays, festivals and celebrations most dyers and marandajun — those who dye cloth black with the vitriol called yarnadaj — alter the people's clothes and rent them out to those who want to dress up, a breach of trust the muhtasib must prevent {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 28, p. 92}.

## The dye-street of Cairo

In Lane's Cairo the sabbagh stood in the long list of market trades, between the tailor and the darner {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}.

## The dyers of Istanbul

Evliya counts the dyers (Sabbaghan) at eight hundred men with five hundred shops; their patron is Amir Ben Abdullah the dyer, who was girded by Selman Pak, lived to a hundred and thirty-four and is buried in Yemen {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 502, p. 224}. They march in the procession dyeing linen and hanging it on ropes {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 502, p. 224}. Beside them pass the dyers' beaters (Tokmakjissi), seven hundred men with one hundred shops, who dress the dyed cloth with their mallets — men whose name became a curse, for wits wishing a man ill would say "the dyer's mallet to you" {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 503, p. 224}.

## Today

The sabbagh's craft is transformed: synthetic dyes and factory dyeing took over most of the work, though hand dyers still hang skeins and cloth to dry in the dye-souks of older cities {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 502, p. 224} {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}.
