## Summary

The wāʿiẓ is the exhorter — the preacher of wa'z, counsel and reminder — distinct in this museum's records from the khatib who delivers the obligatory Friday khutba and from the qāss who tells the tales, though the work historically blurred {cite:tdv-vaaz}. The Prophet gave wa'z and counsel a place in his mission and told exemplary stories within his sermons {cite:tdv-vaaz}. After the killing of Uthman tale-telling spread into preaching, until the hadith scholars objected and demanded licence and qualification of the preacher {cite:tdv-vaaz}.

## Exhortation and its bounds

The TDV entry treats wa'z — exhortation — beside qasas and tadhkir: the sermon that moves hearts rather than lays down law {cite:tdv-vaaz}. Its history of the qussās runs from the caliphate of Umar to the craft's decline by the fourth Islamic century, and it names the standing witnesses of the debate: Ibn al-Jawzi's and al-Suyuti's books against the storytellers' fabrications {cite:tdv-vaaz}.

## The preacher in Lane's Cairo

Lane describes the officers of a Cairo mosque of the 1830s: a nazir, the waqf's warden, appoints the religious ministers and the inferior servants {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. The larger mosques keep two imams — the khateeb who preaches and prays before the congregation on Friday, and the imam ratib who recites the five daily prayers; in most smaller mosques one imam performs both {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. On Friday the murakkee opens the doors at the foot of the pulpit-stairs and hands the khateeb a straight wooden sword, which the preacher holds, point on the ground, as he sits on the top step and then rises to deliver the exhortation called khutbet el-wa'z — composed, Lane notes, in rhyming prose {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. The muballighs stationed on the dikkeh chant the responses {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.

## Under the muhtasib's eye

Ibn al-Ukhuwwa's hisba manual makes the preacher a subject of market-oversight too: if the khatib wears a black robe in which silk predominates, or grasps a gilded sword, that is a misdemeanour to be denounced, while black alone is merely less liked than white {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy}. The same chapter forbids stepping over the backs of the congregation after the call to prayer, and orders the muhtasib to denounce an imam who prolongs the ritual beyond what the weaker can bear {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy}.

## Appointment and permission

The sources agree that preaching was never a free stage: the scholars held that no one might preach kisas without the emir's licence and set conditions on the preacher himself {cite:tdv-vaaz}. In Lane's Cairo the mosque's religious ministers owed their places to the nazir of the endowment {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. And the sovereign's appointment had legal force — if the ruler appoints an imam for Friday worship, Ibn al-Ukhuwwa writes, he has the first right to officiate even if more learned persons are present {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy}.

## Three trades at the minbar

The museum keeps three preaching trades distinct, as the sources keep them: the khatib of the prescribed khutba, the qāss of the tales, and the wāʿiẓ of counsel and reminder {cite:tdv-vaaz}. Lane's Friday scene shows how they met in one ritual — the khateeb's second address in the service is itself named the sermon of exhortation, khutbet el-wa'z {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.
