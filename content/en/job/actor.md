## Summary

The orta oyunu actor plays in the open air, in the middle of the people, in a round space ringed by spectators — no stage, curtain or décor — in the traditional Turkish theatre also called kol oyunu, meydan oyunu, meydân-ı sühan and zuhûrî {cite:tdv-orta-oyunu}. It is an improvising actor's theatre: the play has no written text, its kanava outline lives in the players' heads led by the Pîşekâr, and the words are spoken impromptu, so that the article calls it a "field of speech" rather than of action, played with everything happening before the audience's eyes {cite:tdv-orta-oyunu}.

## First attestations

The play's beginnings are argued: Râşid Mehmed's history mentions a play called "Yazıcı" on the 1703 events, but a karagöz play of that name also exists, so the first sure attestations are the surnames — Lebîb's Sûrnâme of 1834 for Saliha Sultan's wedding names it, and Hızır's of 1836 for the circumcision feast of Abdülmecid and Abdülaziz shows it known in the first half of the century and common from its second half {cite:tdv-orta-oyunu}.

## Elements of the play

The performance joins four elements: the temsil, the improvised dramatic piece mimicking the types of Turkish society; music — zurna and çifte nağra with songs and türküs; dance, carried by the çengi women dancers, the köçek boys dancing in female manner, and the curcunabaz clowns who burlesque the dancers; and the muhâvere, the comic dialogue opened by an "anahtar" or "dişi söz" cue and answered with the "erkek söz" {cite:tdv-orta-oyunu}. The fifth element is taklit, the mimicry of the empire's peoples — Arab, Albanian, Armenian, Georgian, Kurd, Laz, Greek and Jew — who became the play's stock types {cite:tdv-orta-oyunu}.

## The meydan and its heroes

The play is held in a meydan, a round space ringed by the audience: the tent or screened spot holding the players' clothes is the "sandık odası," the entry is the "kapı," Kavuklu's little screen-workplace is the "dükkân," and the latticed screen called "yeni dünya" stands for house or hamam, while the men's seats are the "mevki" and the women's the "kafes" {cite:tdv-orta-oyunu}. Its two heroes are the Pîşekâr — at once player, stager and author — and the Kavuklu, the play's chief comic, standing to each other as Hacivat to Karagöz; around them move the types from the Kayseri and Eğin men, the Laz and the Rumelian, to the Acem, the Arab, the Jew, the Greek and the zenne players of women's parts {cite:tdv-orta-oyunu}.

## How a night runs

A play has four parts: the giriş, where the Pîşekâr enters to his own zurna air with the "pastal" clapper and opens the show; the muhâvere in its arazbar and tekerleme sections, bringing Kavuklu and his dwarf or hunchback companion to quarrel with Pîşekâr; the fasıl — the named piece, "Büyücü," "Gözlemeci," "Hamam," "Ferhat ile Şirin" — in which the jobless Kavuklu is set up in a trade and the types pour in; and the bitiş, where Pîşekâr begs the audience's pardon for their faults, announces the next play and place, and the zurna plays them out with "Ey gaziler" or the İzmir march {cite:tdv-orta-oyunu}. Cevdet Kudret counted eighty-three plays in the repertory {cite:tdv-orta-oyunu}.

## The mimics' companies

Evliya's Istanbul already swarmed with the actor's ancestors: at every imperial circumcision, wedding or victory feast two or three hundred singers, dancers, comics and mimics played day and night, sometimes earning a thousand piastres at a private wedding — the money collected in the half-drum after each dance {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}. His twelve companies — the kols — held pure mimics among the boys of Balata and the Seven Towers, companies famous for "stripping their admirers" and for acting the parts of bakers, tribute-collectors and silver-searchers {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}.

## Decline

From the Tanzimat era's second half the intellectuals' passion for Western theatre pushed the traditional stage aside — it was counted no theatre at all, even harmful — though Teodor Kasap defended the native tradition with a Molière adaptation headed "Orta Oyunu Bir Fasıl," and the Hungarologist Kúnos argued a national Turkish theatre would grow from karagöz and orta oyunu; the article closes by naming the last great players — Kavuklu Sepetçi Ali Rızâ, Pîşekâr Âsım, Kavuklu Hamdi Efendi, Pîşekâr Küçük İsmâil Efendi — as the play waned {cite:tdv-orta-oyunu}.
