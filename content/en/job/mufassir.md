## Summary

The mufassir is the scholar whose craft is tafsir, the explanation and interpretation of the Qur'an {cite:tdv-tefsir}. The science teaches how the Qur'an's meanings are opened and its rulings drawn out by means of the language sciences, the Qur'anic sciences, the transmitted sciences and the method sciences, and it presumes that whoever does this work knows all these fields, first of all language and the sciences of the Book {cite:tdv-tefsir}.

## A science with a heavy toolkit

The classical definitions collected in the entry list the exegete's kit: sarf, nahiv and balagha among the language sciences; the occasions of revelation, abrogating and abrogated verses, and the muhkam and mutashabih among the Qur'anic sciences; hadith and history among the transmitted sciences; and logic and usul al-fiqh among the method sciences {cite:tdv-tefsir}. Definitions by al-Zarkashi's synthesis, Abu Hayyan al-Andalusi and al-Jurjani all underline that tafsir must rest on a firm basis and that its practitioner must stand on broad knowledge {cite:tdv-tefsir}. The entry also stresses a spiritual qualification: to approach the Qur'an as mere human speech is counted the greatest obstacle to interpreting it correctly, and those without faith are described as limited in their share of tafsir {cite:tdv-tefsir}.

## The first exegete and the three schools

There is no dispute that the Qur'an's first exegete was the Prophet, who corrected his Companions' misreadings, interpreted verses directly and answered questions, and from him the Companions took the science and passed it to the Followers {cite:tdv-tefsir}. Of the Companions, Ibn Mas'ud and Ibn Abbas are known to have interpreted nearly the whole Qur'an using what reached them from the Prophet {cite:tdv-tefsir}. Three teaching houses of the craft formed in the Followers' time: the strongest, the Meccan school, rested on Ibn Abbas — for whom the Prophet prayed that he be taught the ta'wil — with Sa'id ibn Jubayr, Mujahid, Ikrima, Tawus and Ata ibn Abi Rabah among his circle; the Medinan school went back to Ubayy ibn Ka'b through Abu al-Aliya, Muhammad ibn Ka'b al-Qurazi and Zayd ibn Aslam; and the Iraqi school, as strong as Mecca's, carried Ibn Mas'ud's line through Alqama, Masruq, al-Hasan al-Basri, Qatada and Ibrahim al-Nakha'i {cite:tdv-tefsir}.

## From spoken lessons to written books

Tafsir first lived as spoken commentary: the Followers' own authored tafsirs are few, yet the writing down of tafsir as books preceded the hadith collections, and al-Hasan al-Basri's public tafsir lessons and Qatada's tafsir discussions were much consulted in the first two centuries {cite:tdv-tefsir}. The first complete Qur'an commentary to survive was written by Muqatil ibn Sulayman, who died in 150 AH, and his work shows how much the craft had already absorbed, including the Isra'iliyyat material the community had come to tolerate {cite:tdv-tefsir}.

## Two ways of practising the craft

The craft settled into two methods {cite:tdv-tefsir}. In riwaya tafsir — commentary by transmission, also called ma'thur tafsir — the exegete's own sources are the Qur'an itself and the Prophet, then the Companions who lived the revelation, and he works through the chains of report {cite:tdv-tefsir}. In diraya tafsir — commentary by insight, also called ra'y or rational tafsir — the mufassir is more active, reasoning within the sources he holds, a mode that multiplied in the Followers' period and later produced the distinct Sunni, Mu'tazili, Shi'i, Ibadi and Sufi commentarial literatures the entry surveys {cite:tdv-tefsir}.

## The modern exegete

In the modern period the craft kept its book-form but widened its methods: the entry lists rational-social commentaries such as Rashid Rida's Tafsir al-Manar, literary commentaries like Bint al-Shati's al-Tafsir al-bayani, 'scientific' commentaries seeking harmony between the Qur'an and modern science, and thematic (mawdu'i) tafsir much pursued in academic institutions {cite:tdv-tefsir}. Tafsir is now an academic department in its own right — in Turkey alone, by 2007 the tafsir chairs had produced 560 authored and translated books, 1,430 articles and hundreds of theses — and a large scholarly literature in both the Islamic world and the West studies the exegetes and their methods {cite:tdv-tefsir}.
