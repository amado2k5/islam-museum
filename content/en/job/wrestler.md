## Summary

The wrestler — the pehlivan of Persian name, the küştigîr or güreşçi of Turkish, the musâri' of Egyptian Arabic — was the hero of a sport the entry calls as old as human history, and one of the few jobs whose patron was himself a wrestler {cite:tdv-gures}. Wrestling stood beside riding, shooting, running and swimming as the Prophet's commended war-sports; he wrestled himself and defeated the famous pehlivan Rukana ibn Abd Yazid, who made conversion conditional on being beaten in the ring {cite:tdv-gures}. From the palace pehlivans of the Abbasids the craft passed to the Seljuk and Ottoman tekkes, the huzur güreşleri before the sultan, the Kırkpınar fair-games, and the guild of three hundred greased wrestlers Evliya watched in the imperial procession {cite:tdv-gures} {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}.

## An ancient sport

Egyptian wall-paintings of the second millennium BC already show systematic wrestling figures, and the Greeks and Romans practised it; among the ancient Turks it was at once war-drill, festival game and a measure of manliness, so that the rulers, princes and viziers who patronised it made it a palace sport beside riding and archery {cite:tdv-gures}. Turkish girls are even recorded wrestling — one made her suitor's hand conditional on beating her at racing, shooting and wrestling, in the Book of Dede Korkut {cite:tdv-gures}. In the Jahiliyya the fairs hosted matches; Umar, famous for wrestling at the Ukaz fair, once lost to Khalid ibn al-Walid with a broken leg {cite:tdv-gures}.

## The Prophet and the first pehlivans

The Prophet encouraged archery, riding, running and swimming as war-sports and wrestled himself; Rukana ibn Abd Yazid demanded defeat in the ring as his condition for accepting Islam, and the Prophet threw him {cite:tdv-gures}. The sources remember the Companions' boys wrestling at the annual review and Hasan and Husayn wrestling in the Prophet's presence, and his saying that the real pehlivan is not the one who throws men but the one who masters himself in anger {cite:tdv-gures}. The tradition gave the craft its patron: the pehlivans' pir is Hamza, called the lion of God and His Messenger and counted the master of the martyrs {cite:tdv-gures}.

## From palace payroll to tekke

Under the Abbasids famous pehlivans were hired into the palace — the caliph al-Amin wrestled a lion, al-Mu'tadid killed one to show his strength — and the Buyid Mu'izz al-Dawla staged hours-long matches before crowds in Baghdad, the winners taking money and robes of honour {cite:tdv-gures}. The Seljuks fused the Turkish game with Persian and Islamic rules: the salawat, the du'a and the name of Hamza entered the bout, the Persian word pehlivan entered Turkish, and wrestlers' tekkes rose in Konya and many cities {cite:tdv-gures}.

## The Ottoman order

Orhan Bey opened the first wrestlers' tekke inside Bursa's citadel, Murad I the second at Edirne, Murad II a third at Manisa, and Istanbul kept the Pehlivan Şücâ and Pehlivan Demir tekkes, their expenses carried by founders' vakıf revenues {cite:tdv-gures}. The tekke was a club, not a tariqa: an authoritative, literate 'şeyh' headed it, the resident wrestlers were called 'derviş' without Sufi ties, and training ran on the grass meydan and a winter hall {cite:tdv-gures}. Bayezid II gathered a wrestlers' corps in Istanbul from his Amasya years; the ehl-i hiref register of Selim I's reign lists a cemaat-i küştigîran whose Ali Küçük drew 1,000 kuruş and trained the others {cite:tdv-gures}. Evliya counts three hundred pehlivans of the wrestlers' guild, greased and girt only in leather, turning their tricks before the emperor at the Alay Köşkü in the public procession, rewarded by skill — and he records that Sultans Abdülaziz and Abdülhamid II's reigns saw Koca Yûsuf, Kara Ahmed, Adalı Halil and Kurtdereli Mehmed carry 'strong as a Turk' into Europe and America {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2} {cite:tdv-gures}.

## The games and the craft

Turkish wrestling had three named forms: karakucak, where the fall to both shoulders decides; yağlı, the oil-wrestle in the leather kispet with its five grades from deste up to baş; and the aba güreşi of the south and southeast {cite:tdv-gures}. The huzur güreşleri — 'presence bouts' before the sultan at weddings, festivals and biniş outings — paid the winners in ihsan: Bayezid II gave one pehlivan 3,000 and another 1,500 kuruş in 1504 {cite:tdv-gures}. The Kırkpınar games on the Edirne meadow, named for the forty ghazis of legend, ran under the elected Kırkpınar ağası who bought next year's office at auction over a lamb, while the cazgır — usually an old pehlivan — praised each wrestler aloud and led the pir's du'a; three straight years as başpehlivan made the golden belt a permanent prize {cite:tdv-gures}. Evliya gives the craft's own count of the art: forty arts, seventy rounds, one hundred and forty tricks, and the wrestler's prayer he himself used to recite when Murad IV stripped to wrestle at the Chemen-sofa {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v1}. In 1830s Cairo Lane watched the musâre'een strip to their drawers and oil their bodies, wrestling mostly for pay at festivals and processions {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.
