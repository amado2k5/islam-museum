## Summary

The dâyeh — the midwife — is the woman called to the house when a birth is near: she brings her own birthing chair, seats the labouring mother on it, and afterwards keeps the seventh-day rites of the subooa' beside her {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII, birth-festivities section}. Lane's account is of Cairo in the 1830s; the picture he gives — a woman of the neighbourhood whose equipment is a single decorated chair and whose pay is the nukoot collected in a water-bottle tray — is the fullest the packet's sources give of the craft {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII}.

## The birthing chair

Two or three days — sometimes more — before the expected time of delivery, the dâyeh conveys to the house of the woman who requires her assistance the "kursee el-wilâdeh", a chair of a peculiar form upon which the patient is to be seated during the birth {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII}. The chair is covered with a shawl or an embroidered napkin, and flowers of the henna-tree, or roses, are tied with an embroidered handkerchief to each of the upper corners of the back; thus ornamented, the chair — the property of the dâyeh — is carried before her to the house {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII}.

## After the birth

In the houses of the rich, and of those in easy circumstances, the mother after delivery is placed on a bed and usually remains on it from three to six days; poor women in the same case seldom take to a bed at all, and after a day or two resume their ordinary occupations, if not requiring great exertion {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII}. On the morning after the birth two or three dancing-girls called Khawals, or Ghâzeyehs, dance in front of the house or in the court — the festivities for a son always greater than those for a daughter {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII}.

## The subooa'

On the seventh day after the birth — the "Yum es-Subooa'" — the mother's female friends pay her a visit, and the mother, attended by the dâyeh, sits on the kursee el-wilâdeh in the hope that she may soon have occasion for it again, her doing this being considered propitious {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII}. At the same time the dâyeh, or another woman, sprinkles on the floor of each room a mixture of salt and fennel-flower seed, or salt alone, placed the preceding night at the infant's head, saying as she does so "The salt be in the eye of the person who doth not bless the Prophet", or "The foul salt be in the eye of the envier" — a ceremony considered a preservative for child and mother from the evil eye {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII}.

## The dâyeh's pay

After the nukoot — the coins tied in handkerchiefs — are given for the child, others are given for the dâyeh: the water-bottle placed at the child's head the night before — a dorak for a boy, a kulleh for a girl, its neck bound with an embroidered handkerchief — she takes, puts upon a tray, and passes to each of the women, who put their nukoot for her, merely money, into the tray {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII}. At weddings too she has her due: the family's dâyeh, with the khâtibehs and the bath-attendant, is each presented with a piece of gold stuff, a Kashmir shawl or striped silk, which they sling over the left shoulder as they ride on asses, drums before them, to invite the town's women to the bride's bath — these women are called the mudinât {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII, marriage-festivities section}.

## Standing of the craft

Lane gives the dâyeh no guild, no shaykh and no written art: her knowledge is the midwife's knowledge kept among women, and her standing in the quarter is shown by the fact that the family sends her out, dressed in the gifted silk, to invite its guests {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII}. The mother is regarded as religiously impure for a period after childbirth — in most cases among the people of Cairo forty days, the "Niffâs" — at the expiration of which she goes to the bath {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII}.

## Today

The neighbourhood dâyeh with her decorated chair has been transformed into the licensed midwife and the obstetric profession; the rites of the subooa' she kept — the chair, the sprinkled salt, the bottle passed for coins — survive, where they survive, as custom rather than as a craft {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XXVII}.
