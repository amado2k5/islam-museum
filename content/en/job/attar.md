## Summary

The attar keeps the druggist's and perfumer's shop: musk and ambergris, aloes-wood and frankincense on one side, the simples and compounded drugs on the other {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318} {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley chs. 17-18, pp. 64-74}. The hisba manuals treat his trade as the most dangerous in the market, because an adulterated drug can harm the sick; al-Shayzari devotes his longest chapters to the druggists' and perfumers' frauds and orders a weekly inspection {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 17, pp. 64-68}, and Ibn al-Ukhuwwa confines the trade to men of knowledge and experience {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXV, pp. 38-39}. In the Ottoman cities the attar was a merchant of standing: Lane lists him among the principal trades of Cairo {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}, Evliya gives him guilds in the procession {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 417-418, p. 212}, and in the 1870s the head of all Cairo's merchants was a Mahmud al-'Attar {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 79}.

## The most dangerous shop

Ibn al-Ukhuwwa counts the makers of drugs and resins among the most important of the muhtasib's concerns, and rules that only persons of knowledge and experience may sell drugs {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXV, p. 38}. Drugs should be bought separately from the druggist and then compounded {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXV, p. 38}. Al-Shayzari opens his chapter with a prayer for the inspector: the swindles of the apothecaries are so many they cannot all be known, they are more harmful to people than anything else, and the muhtasib should note them in the margins of his register, intimidate the druggists, exhort them, warn them of punishment, and inspect their drugs every week {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 17, pp. 64-65}.

## Frauds of the drug chest

Among the adulterations al-Shayzari catalogues: Egyptian opium cut with horned poppy, wild-lettuce sap or gum, each betrayed by its smell, texture and taste {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 17, p. 65}; Chinese rhubarb replaced by the Syrian rawand of the veterinarians, the true root being red, odourless, light and worm-free {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 17, pp. 65-66}; bamboo concrete mixed with bones burnt in a furnace — the test is water, where bone sinks and bamboo concrete floats {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 17, p. 66}; and frankincense with rosin and gum, which smoke and smell when thrown on the fire {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 17, p. 66}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa's abstract adds the tabashir test, the adulteration of male frankincense with gum and colophonium, and the kinds of tamarind {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXV, pp. 38-39}.

## The perfume of musk

In the perfumers' chapter al-Shayzari details the making of false musk {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 18, pp. 69-70}. Some fill the musk bags with emblic myrobalan bark, Indian pepperwort and shadiran kneaded with diluted pine resin, four dirhams of the mixture to one of musk, then seal the bags with resin and dry them over the oven {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 18, p. 69}. Others make bagless musk of aristolochia, ramik and dragon's blood, or of spikenard, aloes-wood filings, cinnamon and cloves at equal weight; others mix in lead pellets the size of peppercorns dyed with ink, which cannot be noticed until the musk is crushed {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 18, pp. 69-70}. The tests are intimate ones: the muhtasib opens the bag and puts it to his lips — musk that bites like fire is genuine — or chews a little, spits it on white cloth and shakes; what shakes off without staining is pure {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 18, pp. 69-70}.

## Ambergris and the perfumer's conscience

Ambergris is faked from cuttlefish bone, black resin, beeswax, sandarac and nutmeg, or compounded and left a while in a horse's stomach before being blended with the real thing; the pieces are sometimes sprayed with sandarac, so their tips must be burnt to test them {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 18, pp. 70-71}. Al-Shayzari declines to record the secret frauds, fearing that someone without religion would learn them and swindle the Muslims, and commends whoever tears up and burns the Alchemy of Perfume, the treatise that taught them {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 17, p. 68} {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 18, pp. 69-74}.

## The attar of the cities

Lane places the druggist and perfumer ('attar) among the principal trades of the Cairo market streets, and notes that he also sells wax candles {cite:lane-modern-egyptians ch. XIV, p. 318}. Evliya's procession passes the aloe and ambergris merchants at one hundred men with forty shops, perfuming their shops as they march, and the perfume merchants at thirty men with twenty-five shops, smoking the air with frankincense, spikenard and other perfumes {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer secs. 417-418, p. 212}. In the Egypt of the professional tax, the sartujjar — head of all the merchants of Cairo — was one Mahmud al-'Attar, whose petition for exemption by custom was granted in the late 1870s {cite:chalcraft-striking-cabbies p. 79}.
