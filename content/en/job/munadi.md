## Summary

The munadi was the voice of state and community before announcing machines: he cried orders, proclamations and calls to gather in the markets, streets and mosques, and mobilisation in war, standing at governors' doors to proclaim their edicts. The oldest of the public spoken-word offices, it branched into specialised trades — the muezzin specialised in the call to prayer when Bilal ibn Rabah was appointed at Medina {cite:tdv-muezzin}, and the ruler's messenger specialised in carrying the command where it was sent {cite:tdv-elci}.

## In the Prophet's time

Quraysh already had a crier from Banu Adi ibn Ka'b who announced what had to be made public {cite:tdv-elci}. At Medina the Prophet's munadi cried the general mobilisation (al-nafir) on the day of the Confederates and the summons 'al-salatu jami'a' on the great occasions; the crier of the conquest also proclaimed safety at the entry into Mecca {cite:tdv-elci}. From this general crier the muezzin's own profession separated once the wording of the adhan was fixed in year one or two of the Hijra {cite:tdv-muezzin}.

## What he cried

His work fell into fields: proclaiming the ruler's decrees, fees and fines; announcing community affairs — losses found, occasions; war cries of mobilisation and marching; and crying goods in the markets, which the auction-dallals took over {cite:tdv-muezzin}. In the Ottoman order the tellal was one of the known guilds of 'the people of the craft', carrying decrees through quarters and markets {cite:tdv-muezzin}.

## His conditions and image

He was chosen for a strong clear voice the far could hear and the near could understand, for a faithfulness that never changed or shortened the word, and for an eloquence that read the written and shaped the spoken. He carried a staff, banner or drum marking his approach, and stood at the palace or market gate or on a rooftop so that the whole quarter heard {cite:tdv-muezzin}.

## His decline

The spread of printing from the eighteenth century, then the newspapers, cut the cities' need of the munadi; then radio, then television, then the platforms replaced the walking voice entirely {cite:tdv-matbuat}. The trade's trace survives in the announcer, the newsreader, the station herald and the master of ceremonies — the office did not die, it moved from the body to the machine.
