## Summary

The singer is the voice of the performance: the hânende of Turkish music, the mugannî and muganniye of the Arabs, the gûyende and kavvâl of Persian usage {cite:tdv-hanende}. Before Islam the professional art of song was carried by the kaynes — the singing-girls, also called dâcine, mudcine and karîne — and the debate over gınâ, its permissibility and its instruments, runs through the whole fiqh literature {cite:tdv-musiki}. In the Ottoman fasıl the hânende read the gazel, beste, nakış, kâr and yürük semâi under the serhânende's lead; in the Cairo of Lane's years the celebrated ʿAwâlim sang from behind the hareem lattice, and Evliya's singers' guild counted its patron among the Companions' circle {cite:tdv-hanende} {cite:lane-modern-egyptians} {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}.

## The names of the craft

Hânende — Persian "one who reads, a reader" — named until recent times the men and women who joined solo or ensemble performance with their voices; Arabic offered mugannî and muganniye, Persian preferred gûyende beside kavvâl, and the great Abdülkâdir-i Merâgî was himself called Abdülkâdir-i Gûyende for his singing {cite:tdv-hanende}. Cantemir divided performance into the music of breath and the music of strings: hânendelik is the breath-side, and he sorted the fasıl into hânende fasıls — gazel, beste, nakış, kâr and yürük semâi in order — sâzende fasıls, and the mixed fasıl where both join, ending on a hânende's gazel {cite:tdv-hanende}. The word has now yielded to "ses sanatkârı," the voice artist {cite:tdv-hanende}.

## Leading the fasıl

The ensemble's direction belonged to the singers: the serhânende — hânendebaşı, başhânende — led the fasıl beating the usul on the def, a role resembling a choir-master yet different, for he sang with the others, and the harmony was held not by watching a leader's signs but by the hânendes and sâzendes listening to each other directly {cite:tdv-hanende}. With no programme fixed in advance, the başhânende also ordered the repertory to conditions, and within the sünûhat the hânendes read each work in the voice-range that suited them {cite:tdv-hanende}. A fine voice wanted real musical learning beside it — Abdülkâdir-i Merâgî held that hânendes bearing both win the more esteem among learned mûsikişinas {cite:tdv-hanende}.

## The kayne and the muganniye

The Arabic books record that artful song existed before Islam and grew through professional singing women: these were the kayne, plural kiyân — the word literally meaning a slave-girl — also called dâcine, mudcine and karîne, while mugannî named those who sang provocatively, dragging and cutting the notes {cite:tdv-musiki}. The scholars' argument over the ruling of gınâ and the instruments filled a literature of its own — a full section of Nuwayrî's Nihâyetü'l-ereb treats every side of the question, and al-Ghazâlî reports a refutation written against those who opposed gınâ — so the singer's trade always stood inside a debate it survived {cite:tdv-musiki}.

## The guild of singers

Evliya gives the Constantinople singers their own section: their patron is Hamza ben Yetim, girded by Selman, who sang naʿts with Bilal the Abyssinian in the Prophet's presence and lies buried at Taif {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}. He names among the famous the Imperial favourite Tokatlı Dervish Omar Gulsheni — "my singing-master in the Imperial Harem in the time of Sultan Murad IV," a hundred and forty years old, who took the little-belled half-drum and sang the word "Dost" at the top of his voice, and who gave the world new life with his compositions in the sultans' favourite makam Sigah {cite:evliya-narrative-hammer-v2}.

## The ʿAwâlim of Cairo

Lane found the female professional singers of Cairo called ʿAwâlim — "learned females," literally, though he derives the word rather from the old word for a singing-girl — hired for hareem festivities, singing from the latticed takyeşe adjoining the saloon or from a screened window when male guests sat below {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. The most celebrated of them charmed him beyond any music he had heard; he knew sums above fifty guineas collected for a single ʿālmeh from the guests at a merchant's entertainment, such was the effect of an accomplished voice at the height of the hearers' excitement {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}. A few among them, he adds, deserved the name "learned" for some literary accomplishment; an inferior class danced in the hareem, which is how travellers misapplied "almeh" to the common dancing-girls {cite:lane-modern-egyptians}.
