## Summary

The hajjam is the cupper and phlebotomist who opens veins and sets the cupping glass {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, pp. 107-108}. Because his lancet touches the body, the hisba manuals treat his craft almost as a branch of medicine: no one may practise phlebotomy without attested knowledge of the anatomy of limbs, veins, muscles and arteries {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, p. 107} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, p. 54}. Both manuals give him the same rules of training, consent, equipment and caution, and the muhtasib binds him by oath to consult the physicians in the dangerous cases {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, pp. 54-55} {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, pp. 107-108}.

## Learning the craft

Whoever wants to learn phlebotomy must practise on the veins of chard leaves until his hand is steady {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, p. 107} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, p. 54}. The phlebotomist must not do manual work that callouses the ends of his fingers and blunts the touch by which he finds the veins, and he must care for his eyes with strengthening salve {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, p. 107} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, p. 54}. The lancet must never cut the wrong vein, a muscle or an artery, for many limbs have been destroyed that way {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, p. 107}.

## Consent and caution

He may not bleed a slave without the owner's permission, nor a minor without the guardian's, nor a woman who is pregnant or menstruating {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, p. 107} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, p. 54}. The bleeding is done only in a lit, public place, with a sharp instrument, and never while the operator is agitated {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, p. 54}. The muhtasib exacts a pledge and a covenant that they will not bleed anyone under ten conditions — a patient under fourteen, the very old, the emaciated, the shaking or flabby, the anaemic, the chronically ill, those of a cold temperament and those in great pain — except after consulting the physicians; five further cases, such as a patient just after intercourse, a bath or a heavy meal, demand similar care {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, pp. 107-108} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, pp. 54-55}.

## The moment of bleeding

There are two situations: the optional bleeding, done in the early morning when digestion and evacuation are complete, and the compulsory bleeding at the urgent moment that allows no delay {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, p. 55} {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, p. 108}. Afterwards the patient must not eat to repletion or over-exert himself but should lie down, taking care not to sleep, for sleep weakens the limbs; if the hand swells, the other hand is bled if possible {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, p. 55} {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, p. 108}.

## The cupper's kit

The phlebotomist keeps a number of lancets, a sinew to tie the arm, musk in pods and pastilles to revive a fainting patient, rabbit fur, and a styptic cream of storax, aloe, myrrh, dragon's blood and vitriol for haemorrhage {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, pp. 108-109} {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, p. 55}. The tip of the lancet is rubbed with oil so the incision hurts less, though it heals more slowly; the lancet is held between thumb and middle finger with the forefinger left free to feel, and not used in a sudden movement {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, p. 55} {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, p. 109}. The incision is made wider in winter so the blood does not congeal, and narrower in summer so the patient is not too quickly overcome {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, p. 55} {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, p. 110}.

## The map of the veins

The muhtasib examines the phlebotomists on the veins they may open {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, p. 55}. Of the veins in the head, two called waswaq prevent procreation when bled, and the muhtasib swears the phlebotomists not to bleed them {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, p. 55}. The veins of the arm are the cephalic, median, basilic, cubital, usailim and axillary: the cephalic is the healthiest to bleed; the median is dangerous for the muscle beneath it; the basilic has an artery below whose blood cannot be stanched if cut; the salvatella is bled lengthwise and the cubital obliquely {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, pp. 55-56} {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, pp. 109-110}. Of the leg, the manuals name the vein of the sciatic nerve at the ankle or between the little toes for gout, the saphena on the left side for haemorrhoids and the organs below the liver, the vein inside the knee, and the vein behind the Achilles tendon {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, pp. 55-56} {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, p. 110}.

## Today

Cupping survives as a living practice, carried on by practitioners of traditional medicine; the manuals' frame of consent, a clean sharp instrument and knowing which veins may be touched is still the frame of the craft {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XLIV, pp. 54-56}.

## Not yet covered

The hisba chapters describe the inspected phlebotomist at his work, but not the wet-cupping that followed the incision, the cupper's fees, his shop or bath-side pitch, nor how he was apprenticed beyond the chard leaf {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley ch. 36, pp. 107-110}.
