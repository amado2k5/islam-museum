## Summary

The levent was the fighting sailor of the Ottoman fleet; the word came from the Italian levantino, "eastern soldier", which the Venetians applied to sea-soldiers drawn from the Albanian and Croatian shores, and it passed into Persian and Turkish as levend {cite:tdv-levent}. In Turkish and Persian it also took darker meanings — vagrant, drunkard, ill-tempered — beside "lithe, handsome, well-built" {cite:tdv-levent}.

## Sea levents

In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries the levents were the fighting men of the galleys; because they worked free or half-free they were linked with corsairing, and the kanunnames already note them as an unruly sea force {cite:tdv-levent}. Their status settled in the seventeenth century: youths raised on the western Anatolian and Marmara coasts were enrolled in the fleet as levents and called levend-i Rûmî, while levents for the light Danube flotilla were levied on the river banks {cite:tdv-levent}.

## Shipboard numbers

After 1648, as galleons multiplied, the kalyoncu and levents took the place of the azebs: a frigate carried eighty to a hundred levents, a galleon two, three or four hundred by its size, a river kancabaş thirty and the shallow brolik seven {cite:tdv-levent}. Every ship had its leventler ağası and leventler çavuşu; in battle the levents attacked in their own boats, and in service they drew wages {cite:tdv-levent}.

## Discipline ashore

Corsairing levents attacked merchant ships and plundered the coasts, which loaded the word with its bad name; the state answered by binding each levent to a surety and keeping the surety-less from coming ashore {cite:tdv-levent}. Part of the corps lodged in the khans of Istanbul as "yerli levent", and when they kept causing disorders the kapudan-ı derya Hoca Süleyman Paşa built levent barracks in Istanbul proper, Üsküdar, Galata, Beşiktaş, Hasköy and Eyüp; the barracks on the Bosphorus heights later became the Levent farm {cite:tdv-levent}.

## The land levents

The same name covered the retinues kept by vezirs and beylerbeys — sekban, sarıca, volunteer men — and when such a master died or fell his men became "kapısız levent", masterless levents {cite:tdv-levent}. In late-sixteenth-century Anatolia economic want and the long wars cast these armed bands loose; they robbed the country until the state declared open season in 1688, ran inspections, executed those caught, and by the early nineteenth century had abolished the status altogether {cite:tdv-levent}.

## Today

The word survives as a district name of Istanbul — the Levent farm of the barracks — while the trade of the sea-soldier has passed to the modern navy's ratings {cite:tdv-levent}.
