## Summary

The oud maker built the short-necked lute that is the model instrument of Islamic music — from the crescent wood ribs of the bowl to the wound strings {cite:tdv-ud}. The craft took its form in Abbasid Baghdad, and Ziryāb carried it to al-Andalus, where it became the ancestor of the European lute {cite:tdv-ud}. The entry ends with the nineteenth-century Istanbul workshops that both sold ouds and taught playing {cite:tdv-ud}.

## The instrument and its name

The oud is the prototype of every short-necked lute in the world — the European lute, the Istanbul lavta, the Chinese pipa, the Japanese biwa, the Romanian kobza are all descendants of the oud or of its ancestor {cite:tdv-ud}. The first oud-like instrument was probably made in ancient Egypt (dynasties 19-29, 1320-1085 BC), and an Elamite clay figure of the eighth century BC shows a similar instrument {cite:tdv-ud}. The Sasanians called it "barbat" (goose breast), and its chest was of skin {cite:tdv-ud}. The Arabic word ʿūd means agarwood; the first ouds' chests may have been made of it, and Farmer holds the instrument was named for its wooden chest {cite:tdv-ud}.

## How the craft developed

Arab sources say that until Zelzel (d. 174/790) the oud's bowl and neck were carved from a single block of wood, and that the separate neck was Zelzel's innovation; after him the bowl was presumably built by joining wood ribs rather than hollowing {cite:tdv-ud}. Fārābī played the oud and made changes to it, and the "destan" fret-bands were abandoned toward the end of the tenth century {cite:tdv-ud}. Ziryāb of al-Andalus (d. 238/852) spread the eagle-feather plectrum and played a role in the oud's passage into Spain {cite:tdv-ud}.

## How an oud is built

The bowl is formed of about twenty crescent-shaped wood ribs; the short flat neck is set into the body with a wedge (takoz) and narrows toward the pegbox, which draws a long thin "S" curve and takes the tuning pegs — called "ears" — from the side {cite:tdv-ud}. The chest is a spruce or fir board about one millimetre thick, braced beneath by laths called "balkon", usually pierced with three round holes (two small ones at the sides) covered by carved ornaments called "rose" (gül) or "lattice" (kafes) {cite:tdv-ud}. The strings run from the glued string-holder on the chest — which is also the main bridge — over the nut at the neck-pegbox joint, and wind onto the pegs {cite:tdv-ud}. The two lowest courses (nevâ and gerdâniye) were gut before nylon; the rest were silk wound with silver or copper {cite:tdv-ud}.

## Workshops and makers

The oud was much favoured in the Ottoman palace in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, abandoned in the seventeenth, and re-entered the classical Turkish instrumentarium in the late nineteenth {cite:tdv-ud}. In this return the chest lost its juniper side-panels and the string count rose from five to six, while Istanbul filled with oud workshops and music shops that sold instruments and taught playing at once {cite:tdv-ud}. The most famous of the makers was the Greek-born Manol Usta {cite:tdv-ud}.

## From maker to player

Craft and performance overlap: Şerif Muhittin Targan, student of Ali Rifat Bey (Çağatay) from whom he took his first oud lessons, developed the oud's technique on European virtuoso models and passed it through the Baghdad conservatory to the Beşir brothers Cemil and Münir and Selman Şükür — spreading it across the Arab world {cite:tdv-ud}. The Iraqi-trained Cemil Beşir in turn taught a style in Baghdad that shaped generations of oud players {cite:tdv-ud}.
