## Summary

The sha'ir is the one who voices knowledge, intuition and emotion in measured, harmonious speech: the dictionary sense of shi'r already carries the idea of knowing a thing in all its fineness, perceiving it by feeling {cite:tdv-sair}. In pre-Islamic Arabia the poet stood as his tribe's spokesman, defender and sometimes leader, and the office could earn him the title sayyid; a tribe without a poet was counted worthless {cite:tdv-sair}. The philologists ranked the poets in generations from Ibn Sallam al-Jumahi onward, and the craft carried itself forward through each poet's own râvîs, who memorised and transmitted his verse in a master-apprentice method {cite:tdv-sair} {cite:tdv-siir}.

## The poet of the tribe

In Jahiliyya society the poet voiced the tribe's feelings, satirised the enemy tribe and proclaimed his own tribe's superior qualities — he was, the entry says, the tribe's sole source of strength {cite:tdv-sair}. Arabs believed the poet received his knowledge from a supernatural source, an invisible companion (karin) or jinn who inspired him, and his gift could make him sayyid within his tribe {cite:tdv-sair}. Inspiration was thought to need real triggers: war, anger, fear, wine, desire, longing, love, grief, violent excitement {cite:tdv-sair}.

## The fairs and the contest

Jahiliyya poets composed in the Quraysh dialect recognised as the common tongue, though their own dialects coloured their verse, and they competed at the seasonal fairs before arbiters who were themselves authorities on poetry {cite:tdv-sair}. The tradition handed down in Ibn Abd Rabbih's al-Iqd al-Farid names the seven Mu'allaqat poets — Imru' al-Qays, Tarafa, al-Harith ibn Hilliza, Amr ibn Kulthum, Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma, Antara ibn Shaddad and Labid ibn Rabi'a — as the winners of these contests, though other sources give different names and numbers {cite:tdv-sair}.

## The generations of the poets

From the end of the second (eighth) century the philologists divided the poets into ranks; Ibn Sallam al-Jumahi wrote the standing book on the subject, splitting the Jahiliyya poets into thirteen classes and the Islamic poets into ten {cite:tdv-sair}. Abu Ubayda and al-Asma'i had earlier sorted poets as jahili, islami and mukhadram — those who lived across the two eras — and Abu Tammam's Fuhul al-shu'ara keeps the same threefold frame {cite:tdv-sair}. In the long Abbasid period poets were classified further by whether their verse could serve as linguistic proof, giving the categories muwallad, muhdath and muta'akhkhir, with Bashshar ibn Burd counted the first of the muhdath poets {cite:tdv-sair}. Some historians also graded poets by quality: the hinziz who wrote well and transmitted poetry, the muflik who wrote well without transmitting, the ordinary sha'ir, and the shu'rur whose verse was worthless {cite:tdv-sair}.

## How a poet was made

The entries describe a craft learned inside the tradition: many poets are known to have grown up through the poetry-experience of memorising and reciting the ancients' verse, as the râvîs did, and by remaining long at the side of an elder poet to be formed by him {cite:tdv-sair}. In the transmission of ancient Arabic poetry the master-apprentice method was the rule: each poet's own râvî or râvîs memorised and handed on his verse, and râvilik stood as a school with a tradition of its own {cite:tdv-siir}. In the divan literature the same formation continued through the nazire custom, by which a young poet wrote in conscious imitation of an acknowledged master {cite:tdv-sair}.

## The poet after the classical age

The three sections of the entry follow the poet from the Arabic tribes into the other Islamic literatures — Persian, Turkish and Urdu — where the office took on new shapes at the sultans' courts and in the Sufi lodges, and down to the modern period in which poetry continues as the craft of the salons and the printed page {cite:tdv-sair} {cite:tdv-siir}.
