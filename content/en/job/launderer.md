## Summary

The qassar washed, beat and finished other people's cloth and clothing — the man who returned linen white from the river or the fulling trough {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 415, p. 217; pp. 86-87}. Because he held strangers' property, the hisba manuals bind him with the rules of the deposit: Ibn al-Ukhuwwa forbids him to steal from the stuffs entrusted to him, to wear them or let his workmen wear them, or to pledge them, and requires the owner's name to be written on every article {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXI, pp. 44-45}.

## The trust of other people's clothes

The fuller's temptation was the pile of valuable garments in his shop: Ibn al-Ukhuwwa orders that he take nothing from them, wear none of them himself, permit none of his workers to wear them, and never pawn them, marking each piece with its owner's name {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXI, pp. 44-45}. The repairers around him were bound in the same way — a valuable garment coming from a fuller or cloth-beater might not be repaired except in the owner's presence, and embroiderers might not transfer embroidery from one garment to another {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXI, p. 44}.

## When the garment is destroyed

The manual then works the legal problem the fuller's trade creates every day: a garment washed, then destroyed while still in his possession — who pays, and does the fuller still earn his fee for the work done {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXI, p. 45}. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa distinguishes the cases where the damage came from a third party, from the owner himself, or from an employee {cite:ibn-ukhuwwa-maalim-levy ch. XXXI, p. 45}.

## The washermen of Istanbul

In the guild procession Evliya counts the washermen at five hundred men in three hundred shops, their patron unknown to him; they passed on wagons, washing linen as they went {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 415, p. 217}. On the river of Kaghid-khanah the washermen washed shirts and other linen without soap, yet they came out extremely white after having been washed twice, and Indian merchants brought their bales there to dip them once in the flood {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer pp. 86-87}.

## The stain-cleaners

A second, smaller guild worked beside them: the stain-cleaners (Lekejian), twenty men in ten shops on the place of Bayazid, who possessed the art of taking stains out of precious cloth without hurting the colour {cite:evliya-seyahatname-hammer sec. 416, p. 217}.
