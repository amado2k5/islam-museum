## Summary

The caliph — khalifa, 'one who comes after' — was the head of the Islamic polity, the man who succeeded the Prophet in managing the community's worldly affairs {cite:tdv-hilafet}. He was also addressed as amir al-mu'minin, 'commander of the believers', a style introduced from Umar's reign, and the office itself was called the imama, or 'the greater imamate' to distinguish it from leading the prayer {cite:tdv-hilafet}.

## What the word meant

The term rests on succession: the caliph represented the Prophet's authority in everything except prophethood — applying the rulings of religion, ordering public affairs, embodying the sovereignty that belongs to God or to the community of believers {cite:tdv-hilafet}. He was called 'khalifat rasul Allah', the Prophet's successor; when people tried 'khalifat Allah' — God's own deputy — Abu Bakr is reported to have rejected the address, saying he was the successor of God's messenger, not of God, and Umar and Umar b. Abd al-Aziz refused it likewise {cite:tdv-hilafet}.

## Choosing a caliph

The first accession set the pattern of consent: at the Saqifa of Banu Sa'ida, before the Prophet had been buried, the ansar moved to pledge to Sa'd b. Ubada, but Abu Bakr, Umar and Abu Ubayda joined the meeting, and after debate the pledge went to Abu Bakr — the 'special bay'a', followed next day by the 'general bay'a' in the mosque {cite:tdv-hilafet}. The article weighs the Sunni–Shi'i dispute over that choice and notes what the electors actually weighed: Abu Bakr's Quraysh standing, age, precedence in Islam and closeness to the Prophet {cite:tdv-hilafet}. Umar came by Abu Bakr's testament, Uthman by a six-man shura, Ali by the pledge of a divided city — and each route fed a later doctrine of legitimacy {cite:tdv-hilafet}.

## The conditions jurists set

From the eleventh century the al-ahkam al-sultaniyya literature codified what a caliph must be: Muslim, male, adult, sane, free, Qurashi by the dominant view, sound in body, knowledgeable enough to make ijtihad, capable of running affairs and defending the realm {cite:tdv-hilafet}. The jurists also discussed his removal — a caliph who openly violated the rules of religion could be deposed — and held that he had no divine power, that he represented the umma's sovereignty and answered to God and to the Muslims for his office {cite:tdv-hilafet}.

## From election to dynasty

Mu'awiya's accession in 41/661, the 'year of unity', turned the caliphate into monarchy: he secured the succession for his son Yazid, and bay'a became hereditary, passing from father to son or between branches of the house {cite:tdv-hilafet}. The Umayyads took the style 'khalifat Allah' to demand unconditional obedience, and scholars who held the true caliphate to have ended with the Rashidun still counselled obedience to avoid civil war {cite:tdv-hilafet}. The Abbasids kept the hereditary system but grounded legitimacy in descent nearer the Prophet's line; al-Mansur founded Baghdad as the seat, and from Mu'tasim's day Turkish commanders began to make and unmake caliphs, a tutelage the Buyids and Seljuks then made formal {cite:tdv-hilafet}.

## Ottoman sunset

Under the Ottomans the caliphate passed to a sultanate that joined temporal and claimed spiritual authority in one house; the article's second section follows the Ottoman claim — sultans styling themselves caliph within their own lands, then the title's wider assertion and its eventual abolition {cite:tdv-hilafet}. With abolition the oldest office in Islamic government ended as a working institution, though its long debate — election or heredity, religious authority or political necessity — remains the frame for Islamic public law {cite:tdv-hilafet}.
