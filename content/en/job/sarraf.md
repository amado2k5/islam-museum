## Summary

The sarraf weighed gold and silver on fine balances, knew their purity and their worth, and changed one coin for another for a fee; the name comes from the root sarf, "to sell gold for silver", and sayraf, sayrafî and nakkād were also used, while early Iraq called the money-changer shashkale, a word taken from Himyarite {cite:tdv-sarraflik}. From the markets of prophetic Medina the trade ran through the whole Islamic world, and under the Ottomans it grew into the financial system itself, until the state banned money-changing outright in 1916 {cite:tdv-sarraflik}.

## Scales and shops

Before coinage, people used gold and silver in ingots, and the grain of barley was the unit of weight in the Mesopotamian codes; the Prophet is recorded asking a wezzān to weigh the dirhams he paid for a garment, and the markets then held fee-taking weighers beside the sarrafs {cite:tdv-sarraflik}. The sarraf's skill lay in judging fineness: the scholars of hadith compared the testing of a report to the submitting of gold and silver to the money-changers, and the Turkish proverb runs that only the sarraf knows the worth of gold {cite:tdv-sarraflik}. Their stalls stood where change was needed — the markets, the much-visited places, the customs posts; in Jerusalem their tables reached into the courtyard of al-Aqsa itself {cite:tdv-sarraflik}.

## A trade of temptations

Money-changing was a trade that invited fraud, and the manuals devote whole pages to its tricks: al-Jawbari tells of the sarraf with a magnet in his ring who played the balance against the customer, of sealed weights opened and made heavier or lighter, of quicksilver used to spoil the standard of gold {cite:tdv-sarraflik}. Al-Shayzari opens his chapter on money-changers with the warning that the trade is dangerous to the religion of the ignorant, for a man who does not know the law of usury will transgress it {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley}. Gold for gold and silver for silver had to change hands in equal weight and on the spot; profit was lawful only when gold was sold for silver, and credit in such dealings was unlawful {cite:shayzari-nihaya-buckley}. For these hazards Muslims largely left the calling to others: in Medina the money-changing and goldsmith crafts lay mostly in Jewish hands, and caliph Umar wrote to his governors that Jews and Christians should be barred from butchery and money-changing on account of the usury in their dealings {cite:tdv-sarraflik}.

## Bankers of the empire

In the Ottoman lands the sarrafs appear from the fifteenth century, a group born of the medley of local and foreign coins whose price differences made a trade of exchange {cite:tdv-sarraflik}. Beyond buying and selling coin they moved and guarded money, brokered house sales, ran the finances and debts of statesmen, managed the funds of cash pious foundations and orphan trusts, and from the 1760s financed the treasury with short-term loans, above all in wartime {cite:tdv-sarraflik}. Their strongest field was the tax-farm: every mukataa farmed by emanet had to name its sarraf, who supplied capital to the emin and the mine masters and settled accounts after harvest, and in the iltizam system the sarrafs paid the instalments to the treasury and stood surety on both sides, which made them a pillar of the whole structure {cite:tdv-sarraflik}. Small-capital sarrafs kept to coin and bullion sales to the goldsmiths, while the treasury sarrafs, the "tailed sarrafs", held great capital and state-recognised standing, and the nineteenth century added the licensed corner sarrafs of Istanbul, cut from seventy-eight to twenty in 1854 {cite:tdv-sarraflik}.

## Guild and Berat

No one with capital could simply practise: sarrafs worked under the sultan's berat inside the gedik system, and from the late seventeenth century they formed an esnaf guild with its kethüda, yiğitbaşı, elders, masters, journeymen and apprentices {cite:tdv-sarraflik}. Istanbul counted forty sarrafs in 1714, seventy-two licensed ones in 1740, and a hundred and thirty-seven gedikli and unlicensed together in 1761; orders were issued from time to time against outsiders practising, and a dead sarraf's berth generally passed to his son {cite:tdv-sarraflik}. Because they financed the treasury and the tax-farms the sarrafs were counted among the askeri class and allowed a rate of interest a little above the market's fifteen per cent; the great houses kept their offices in the han rooms of Galata and became the "Galata bankers" {cite:tdv-sarraflik}.

## Decline and abolition

The financial changes of the mid-nineteenth century — paper money in 1840, shares and bonds — made the Istanbul sarraf-bankers of the Baltazzi, Zarifi, Kamondo and other families a financial bourgeoisie that helped float the first banks and placed the state's European loans {cite:tdv-sarraflik}. The government's suppression moved in steps: the sarraf gediks were abolished in 1860, the shops shut after the panic of December 1861, and in 1916 the Tevhîd-i Meskûkât law abolished the coin rates and banned sarraflık, under penalty of three months to two years in prison {cite:tdv-sarraflik}. So great had the trade become in the Ottoman markets that The Times's Istanbul correspondent proposed in 1877 that the city be renamed "Sarrafopoli" {cite:tdv-sarraflik}.
