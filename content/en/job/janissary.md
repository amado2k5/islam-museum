## Summary

The janissary was the Ottoman army's standing, salaried infantryman — the "new soldier" (yeniçeri) drawn first from the state's one-fifth share of war captives and then from the devşirme levy of Christian boys {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. The corps grew from roughly a thousand under Orhan's successors to more than fifty thousand on the seventeenth-century payrolls, stood in 196 ortas under its own ağa and divan, and passed from the empire's decisive strike arm to its standing obstacle to reform, until Mahmud II destroyed it in the Vak'a-i Hayriyye of 14 June 1826 {cite:tdv-yeniceri}.

## From captives to the devşirme

No firm founding date can be given for the corps; the need for more soldiers after Orhan's conquests led to the use of prisoners of war, paid and intended for permanent service, where the older yaya and müsellem levies had proved undisciplined {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. Under Murad I the Acemi Ocağı was founded at Gelibolu on the reported counsel of the kazasker Kara Halil Efendi and Karamanlı Kara Rüstem, and the Gelibolu lads served on the strait transports before being admitted to the "new soldier" corps {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. The first intake ran on the pencik — the fifth of the captives that by religious rule fell to the state — collected by the akıncı judges, the pencikçi and the pencik emini {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. Captive lads then gave way to boys levied from the empire's own Christian subjects, whom the records call "lads on the Turk": handed to Anatolian and later Rumelian households — peasant farmers, townsfolk, even hospital service — for five to eight years of Turkicisation, then brought into the Acemi Ocağı under the Anadolu and Rumeli ağaları {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. A 1395 letter of the metropolitan of Thessalonica describes boys taken to change their faith and forget their tongues, while registers also record the rarer volunteer, like Istoban of Sokoloviçe in 1498, entered at his own wish {cite:tdv-yeniceri}.

## Novices at the gate

The acemi oğlanı — the "torba oğlanı" of the registers — belonged to the state through a status scholars still debate: passage "out to the gate" (kapıya çıkma) into the janissary corps has been read as manumission from a form of slavery, and the legal nature of the devşirme boy's standing is among the entry's open questions {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. Sources place the novice's service at fifteen to twenty years, though need shortened it, and not every graduate joined the infantry: the registers show acemis drafted to palace gardens, kitchens and stables, to the fleet's ships, the arsenal and the waterworks, and in 1570 as paid labour on the Selimiye at Edirne {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. The white börk set on the boy's head when he returned from the Turk cannot be traced to Hacı Bektaş — the saint died long before the beylik rose — and the janissary-Bektashi tie, though later formalised with a Bektaşi baba seated in the 94th Cemaat Orta, belongs to the corps' later centuries {cite:tdv-yeniceri}.

## Ortas and barracks

The corps answered to the yeniçeri ağası and his ağa divanı, an autonomous jurisdiction in which even the sultan's writ ran through the ağa, punishments and executions carried out in secret {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. In Istanbul the janissaries lodged in the Eski Odalar and Yeni Odalar — wooden barracks closed to non-soldiers around the Orta Cami, which doubled as the corps' council chamber and was burned with them in 1826 {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. The battalion was the orta and its commander the çorbacı; the 196 ortas settled in three divisions — the 101 cemaat or piyâdegân ortas of Mehmed II, the 34 sekban ortas raised from the palace's hunting-dog keepers, and the 61 bölük or ağa bölükleri begun under Bayezid II {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. Each orta kept a solidarity chest (orta sandığı) fed by pay deductions and lent at interest, meals were shared through the orta's cooks, and the members called one another "yoldaş" — while overturning the orta cauldrons in the square, the kazan kaldırma, became the corps' sign of revolt {cite:tdv-yeniceri}.

## Arms and pay

At Kosovo in 1389 some two thousand janissaries fought with the bow; they met firearms under Murad II, fielded ten musketeers at the Novobrdo garrison by 1455, and at Mohács in 1526 four thousand of them won the day by rolling volley fire while the artillery missed {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. A 1552 order bound them to weekly musket drill, though by the early seventeenth century drill survived mainly before great campaigns; the long arquebus they handled so well at Mohács was outranged at Haçova in 1596 {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. Pay was the ulûfe, disbursed quarterly for reasons never recorded — Koçi Bey reckoned the whole salaried kul class had swollen from 92,000 to 200,000 by his day — and the demands for raises, accession donatives and bonuses hardened into custom after 1446 and 1481 {cite:tdv-yeniceri}.

## From strike arm to standing revolt

In the sixteenth-century field army the janissaries were the elite kept most carefully from losses, reserved for the final blow after the timariot sipahis had softened the enemy {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. The same corps deposed and killed Osman II in 1622, toppled Mustafa II in the 1703 rising, debated turning from the dynasty to elect a "republic" of its own, and ended the Tulip Era in 1730 {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. Their drift into the market — Selânikî notes the shopkeeper shortage when the corps marched on campaign in 1596 — made the askeri tradesman whose military edge dulled while his political weight grew {cite:tdv-yeniceri}.

## The Vak'a-i Hayriyye

Mahmud II, who had watched the corps overturn a long execution list even at Alemdar Mustafa Pasha's death in 1808, met the final rising in June 1826 with the people urged to bring their own cauldrons into the street {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. The corps was abolished on 14 June 1826; the Bektaşi order, bound to it in ceremony, shared its fate, the barracks were burned, and the new Asâkir-i Mansûre-i Muhammediyye took the field under the serasker {cite:tdv-yeniceri}. The article closes with Beydilli's judgement: the janissaries began as the instrument of the state's rise and ended, five hundred years on, as the armed obstacle the state removed by blood — and the legend of the corps as a people's guard does not survive the data {cite:tdv-yeniceri}.
