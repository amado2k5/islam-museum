## Summary
The grand vizier was the chief officer of the Ottoman state as the sultan's deputy, and the title of the head vizier. The office continued the vizierate of the Turkic Muslim states, above all the Seljuks. He first worked inside the palace, in the place called the Kubbealtı. The office was later called the Paşakapısı, then the Sadaret Dairesi, and last the Sublime Porte. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

## The title
Until the late sixteenth century the usual phrase was vezir-i azam. From sadr, "the highest", the titles sadr-ı ali and sadr-ı azam then appear. A campaign narrative of the reign of Murad II uses both veziriazam and sadrazam. Later sources generally keep veziriazam. The word sadrazam becomes common from the second half of the sixteenth century. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

## From the Çandarlıs to Mehmed II's law
The entry says that little is known of the office before the middle of the fifteenth century. One report has Orhan give a pasha's rank to Lülü, a man of the palace, so that a man from outside the learned class became second vizier and Sinaneddin Yusuf thereby became grand vizier. The entry calls this a weak report. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

When the viziers became more than one under Murad I, [[person/candarli-kara-halil-pasha|Çandarlı Kara Halil Hayreddin Pasha]] came to the fore. He had come from the learned class, and had been kazasker and then vizier. The entry says he performed a kind of chief vizierate. He died in 1387. His son [[person/candarli-ali-pasha|Ali Pasha]] carried on his father's work until his own death in 1406. The divan then had three viziers, and this continued under Bayezid I and Mehmed I. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

Under Mehmed II the standing and the powers of the office began to be fixed. His organisation code names the vezir-i azam outright, as the head of the viziers and the commanders and as the sultan's absolute deputy in every matter, and as the first man in ceremony. The entry reads this as the sultan handing his power, on the administrative side, to his deputy. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

## Where they came from
Until the conquest of Istanbul the grand viziers usually came from the learned career. From the middle of the fourteenth century to the conquest, apart from one or two exceptions, the Çandarlı family held the office for more than seventy years, rising from kazasker to vizier to grand vizier. The entry says this produced, in Ottoman society, the view that the sultanate belonged to the house of Osman and the grand vizierate to the Çandarlıs, so that the office looked hereditary. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

After the conquest Mehmed II put men of devshirme origin forward, broke families that might rival the dynasty, and built a service of slaves bound to himself. For two centuries the slave system was so settled that the appointment of a man of Turkish origin was sometimes opposed on the claim that the office belonged to the devshirme. The entry names this unrest at the appointments of [[person/candarli-ibrahim-pasha-saghir|Çandarlı Ibrahim Pasha]] (d. 905 AH / 1499) and [[person/piri-mehmed-pasha|Piri Mehmed Pasha]] (d. 939 AH / 1532). After the devshirme-origin Mahmud, [[person/rum-mehmed-pasha|Rum Mehmed]], Ishak and [[person/gedik-ahmed-pasha|Gedik Ahmed]] pashas, Mehmed II brought [[person/karamani-mehmed-pasha|Karamanî Mehmed Pasha]], of an old Turkish family, to the office. Between Mahmud Pasha and Ipşir Mustafa Pasha (1453–1654), seventy-one grand viziers served, and only nine were of Turkish origin. Their time in office came to sixteen years in all. From the middle of the seventeenth century, when the devshirme rule fell out of use for political reasons, many Turks raised in the Enderun could reach the office. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

Under the first ten sultans the chief viziers were men who had learned the work in the provinces and at the centre, on campaign and off it. Süleyman made an exception for Makbul Ibrahim Pasha (d. 942 AH / 1536), raising him, against custom, from head of the privy chamber to the governorship of Rumelia, then to the vizierate and the grand vizierate, before he had that career. The entry calls this rare in the first two hundred years. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

From the second half of the seventeenth century the Köprülü family stands out, with seven grand viziers. The entry names [[person/koprulu-mehmed-pasha|Mehmed Pasha]] and [[person/fazil-mustafa-koprulu|Fazıl Mustafa Pasha]] among them. It was the first family after the Çandarlıs to last in the office. The entry sees here the start of a change in the sultans' sixteenth-century hold on administration. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

In the eighteenth century the grand viziers stand out as diplomats as well as soldiers. The entry names Ali Pasha (d. 1128 AH / 1716), who took back the Morea ceded to Venice at Karlowitz in 1699 and was killed on the Austrian fronts; Nevşehirli Damad Ibrahim Pasha (d. 1143 AH / 1730); [[person/hekimoglu-ali-pasha|Hekimoğlu Ali Pasha]] (d. 1171 AH / 1758); Yeğen Mehmed Pasha; Hacı Ivaz Pasha; [[person/halil-hamid-pasha|Halil Hamid Pasha]]; and [[person/koca-yusuf-pasha|Koca Yusuf Pasha]]. After Karlowitz more of them came from the bureaucracy. Among these were Koca Ragib Pasha, who had conducted the Karlowitz talks as reisülküttab, [[person/rami-mehmed-pasha|Rami Mehmed Pasha]], Damad Ibrahim Pasha and Halil Hamid Pasha. The entry says that grand viziers from the scribal service were looked down on for a long time. Under Selim III and Mahmud II it names Izzet Mehmed Pasha and Yusuf Ziya Pasha among those who led reform. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

## Appointment and the seal
In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries the custom, when a grand vizier was dismissed, died or was replaced, was to give the office to the second or third vizier of the imperial divan. It was sometimes given instead to a governor of a great province who was not a vizier of the dome, or to a serdar at the front. In the seventeenth century men were appointed from the janissary aghaship, the kethüda of the grand vizier, the master of the horse, the kethüda of the gatekeepers and the çavuşbaşı, without the custom. The entry points the examples to Hadikat ül-vüzera and its supplements, which were not read for this record. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

Appointment straight to the grand vizierate, without first being a vizier, was against the rule. A diploma of the vizierate was given first, and the grand vizierate was then conferred by an imperial rescript. The sign of appointment and dismissal was the imperial seal. Giving it was appointment. Taking it back was dismissal. The seal was sent to the divan, or the man was summoned and the sultan gave it himself. If the new grand vizier was on campaign or in a province, the kethüda of the gatekeepers or the master of the horse carried it with a large suite. In the eighteenth century there are cases of the sultan taking an auspicious hour from the chief astrologer before giving the seal. From that century the seal was no longer sent to the provinces. It was given in Istanbul, at the audience. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

A man brought from a governorship was represented in the palace by a deputy until he arrived. When Yusuf Ziya Pasha was first appointed from the governorship of Erzurum in 1798, Kapıcıbaşı Derviş Abdullah Pasha stood in for forty-five days with the rank of vizier. At the second appointment, from Aleppo in 1809, Çarhacı Ali Pasha stood in for 113 days. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

In the seventeenth century it became the custom for the shaykh al-Islam to be present when the new grand vizier was received. The grand vizier was dressed in two furs, one faced with seraser and one plain, and the seal was given. The shaykh al-Islam was dressed in the white fur. The shaykh al-Islam, the viziers, the kazaskers and the senior men of the learned and military careers then congratulated him, and he gave robes to those who were due them. He then received the men of the divan and of the Paşakapısı. The ambassadors came on the day set for them. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

Some dismissals had a cause that required them, and many did not. The entry says that İsmail Hami Danişmend usually gives the reasons in his list, and that the list is not always right. The list was not read for this record. The entry counts, among the main causes, old age, weak judgement, harshness, quarrelsomeness, want of courage, and above all failure on campaign. Many who were dismissed for failure on campaign also paid with their lives. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

## The absolute deputy
Mehmed II's code says the grand vizier is the absolute deputy in every matter. The code of Tevkiî Abdurrahman Pasha, from the middle of the seventeenth century, lists the powers: absolute deputyship for the sultan in religion and the state, keeping the order of the sultanate, carrying out the hadd, retaliation, prison, exile, discretionary punishment and execution, hearing cases, applying the rulings of the sharia and of custom, removing oppression, governing the country, and granting provinces, sanjaks, timars, trusteeships, imamates and preacherships, that is, the learned and military posts. This was confirmed again at each appointment. Ahmed II told Bozoklu Mustafa Pasha that the affairs of the state were entrusted to his judgement, and ordered him to dismiss whom he should dismiss and appoint whom he should appoint, and then to report. Selim III wrote to Şerif Hasan Pasha that the sultan should give his absolute deputies full leave and independence. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

Until the late sixteenth century the grand viziers were left alone in their work. Later the palace, and in some reigns the valide sultan and the aghas, intervened. The entry says that the grand viziers' own weak judgement had a large part in the loss of their authority. When the office was offered to [[person/koprulu-mehmed-pasha|Köprülü Mehmed Pasha]], in a time when the sultan's authority was weak and the palace and the soldiers intervened, he had his conditions accepted first, and so kept interference out of his work. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

A grand vizier leaving as serdar-i ekrem tried to disable men in Istanbul who could work against him. Before [[person/fazil-mustafa-koprulu|Fazıl Mustafa Pasha]] marched on Belgrade in 1690, the agha of the Abode of Felicity, Hacı Mustafa Agha, who had influence over Süleyman II, was dismissed at his request, with the support of the shaykh al-Islam, the kazaskers and some senior soldiers. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

## Appointments, pay and the market
What occupied him most was appointments across the country. Every appointment, dismissal and promotion, including those of the learned career, and excepting the Enderun, was by his buyruldu. Appointments in the Enderun were made on the proposal of the agha of the Gate of Felicity to the sultan. Outside campaign time he took the sultan's approval for the business of a vizier, the shaykh al-Islam and a kazasker. Posts were given for a term. If the term was extended, that was ibka or mukarrer. If the post was given to someone else, that was tevcih. Appointments were made together at the start of Shawwal, after the feast at the end of Ramadan. Posts outside the learned career were by the grand vizier's direct submission. Posts in the learned career were by the shaykh al-Islam's proposal and then the grand vizier's submission, three approvals. He sent two lists, one for keeping men in place and one for new grants, in a telhis. The sultan changed what he wished. The grand vizier sent the list again and took the approval, then issued the ruus and recorded them, and robes were given at the Paşakapısı to those who were in Istanbul. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

He could grant timars of less than 6,000 akçe by one akçe without submitting them to the sultan, as Mehmed II's code says. Turning a zeamet into a timar, or a hass into a zeamet, or turning either into waqf or private property, needed the sultan's will and the nişancı's entry in the registers of the Defterhane, and the grand vizier drew the tuğra of the decree. Paying the kapıkulu wages every three months was among the heaviest of his duties. The janissaries were paid at the imperial divan, and the other soldiers at the Paşakapısı. When he had done it, a rescript of thanks, a fur and a dagger were sent to him. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

Inspection was part of the work. He went out "on the round" with the judge of Istanbul and a large suite, walked the markets and checked prices. Lütfi Pasha, in the Asafname, tells the grand vizier to stay close to the fixed prices. That book was not read for this record except through this entry. Tradesmen found at fault were punished on the spot. He also inspected the arsenal. Great waqfs stood under his supervision, and he turned them over to one of his men. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

## Justice and the councils
He was to hear cases and give judgement so that justice was done in time. The Tevkiî code calls him the head of all cases, of the sharia and of custom. He divided what reached the imperial divan. He settled the customary and administrative cases himself, and sent the legal ones to the kazaskers. Administrative cases and timar cases that did not need the great divan he heard in the afternoon divan, which was his own. He heard cases in the imperial divan, the afternoon divan, the Wednesday divan and the Friday divan, and in the divans held in the towns on the road when he left for campaign or returned. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

The imperial divan was the most important, and in name it was the sultan's divan. After it came the afternoon divan at the grand vizier's residence. The reisülküttab, the first and second tezkirecis, the viziers' tezkirecis, the çavuşes, many clerks and the interpreters attended. It was open to anyone. On Friday morning he held a divan at the Paşakapısı with the kazaskers of Rumelia and Anatolia, weighted toward cases of the sharia and of custom and the business of the learned career. On Wednesday morning he held a divan with the judges of Istanbul, Galata, Üsküdar and Eyüp for the cases of the people of Istanbul. The beylerbeyi and the sanjak beys in the city used to visit him before that divan. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

After the imperial divan he went in to present his telhis on set days. Lütfi Pasha insists that the grand vizier's submission should be accepted and not sent back. From the reign of Murad III face-to-face meetings grew fewer, and the telhis sent to the palace, answered by an imperial rescript, grew more common. Ali Mustafa Efendi criticised this loss of independence sharply, and the entry reports that criticism. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

The reisülküttab prepared the telhis. It was then written in plain language and in nesih and sent. The sultan wrote above it words such as "I have seen it", "let it be given", "I have given it", "prepare it", "it is not the time", "may you be denied it", and "it shall not be", and it was then carried out. The grand vizier's letters to other officers were called buyruldu. The shaykh al-Islam, the defterdar and the beylerbeyis could submit a telhis to the sultan only through the grand vizier. On letters to foreign heads of state and to the voyvodas of Wallachia, Moldavia and Transylvania he drew the signature called the pençe, and sealed the middle of it. Letters to foreign heads of state were sent in satin bags. From 1832, under Mahmud II, the grand vizier sent a sadaret note to the chief secretary of the Mabeyn and received the sultan's will as a marginal note from that secretary, instead of addressing the sultan directly by telhis. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

## Campaign, income and the deputy
On campaign the grand vizier, as serdar-i ekrem, had full power, including killing, retaliation and exile. Makbul Ibrahim Pasha was sent ahead on the Iraqayn campaign with very wide powers given by the sultan himself, and was called serasker sultan. The sultan's rescript addressed him as deputy of the sultanate, high-ranking serasker, and grand vizier of the caliphal presence. When Abdülhamid I called [[person/koca-yusuf-pasha|Koca Yusuf Pasha]] in before a campaign, he told him that he was his independent absolute deputy in the affairs of religion and the state and the interests of the realm and the people, and his serdar-i ekrem. As a sign of these powers, blank papers bearing the tuğra were given to the grand vizier beforehand, and he wrote decrees and diplomas on them. On return he might have to account for what he had done. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

His income was a hass of very high yield, collected by his voyvodas. Mehmed II's code puts it at "twelve times a hundred thousand", 1,200,000. A century later the Asafname assigns the grand vizier a yearly hass of 1,200,000 akçe, which could rise to 2,000,000, and with gifts and rewards to twenty-four yük at most. The income later rose. Besides the hass he had pishkesh, caize and gifts. When tribute and pishkesh came to the sultan, a share came to the grand vizier, the defterdar and the viziers of the dome, by rank. He took a caize on the great appointments, and was usually the richest officer after the sultan. The booty of a serdar-i ekrem was very large. Rüstem Pasha, Koca Sinan Pasha and Nasuh Pasha are remembered for their wealth. In 1843, under the Tanzimat, the hass, the pishkesh, the caize and the perquisites were abolished and the grand vizier was given a salary. On retirement a hass of 150,000 akçe a year was assigned. From the seventeenth century a retired grand vizier was given a hass or a place as arpalık. The cost of his household was heavy. He might have 500, 1,000, or sometimes 2,000 people of the household. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

When he left as serdar he left a vizier in Istanbul called the deputy of the grand vizierate, or the deputy of the imperial stirrup. The entry does not fix when this office matured. It says it spread on campaigns from the sixteenth century. The deputy was meant to be one of the grand vizier's own trusted men, so as not to undo his work, though sometimes an opponent was chosen. The deputy went with the grand vizier before the sultan and was told his duty. He summoned and chaired the imperial divan. The Friday and Wednesday divans met at his residence. The afternoon divan, being the grand vizier's own, met where the grand vizier was. He kept his own registers, because the main registers went to the front. The basic decisions were taken by the serdar at the front. The deputy could grant waqf posts, clerkships and lower offices, and take some measures for Istanbul and the country. Like the grand vizier he walked the markets and could inspect the arsenal. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

## Rivalry and the last years
The grand vizierate was the highest office after the sultanate, and viziers competed for it, often at the state's expense. In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries the rivalry among viziers of devshirme origin was bitter. The entry names the quarrel, under Murad III, between his two Albanian-origin grand viziers, Koca Sinan Pasha and Ferhad Pasha, and the split it caused among the men of state and in the bureaucracy. The janissaries and the kapıkulu were often stirred in secret to raise a revolt. A grand vizier also had to be careful with those who had influence over the sultan: a princess of the house, the valide sultan, or the girls' agha. Marriage into the dynasty was one way of keeping power. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

Under the Tanzimat the grand viziers were usually civil officials who had learned French and who put European diplomacy first. Of the last thirty-nine, ten had studied wholly or partly in Europe, and many had been trained in the Translation Office of the Porte. Before the office they had held ministries several times and served abroad. Mustafa Reşid Pasha came to the office six times. With his pupils [[person/mehmed-emin-ali-pasha|Mehmed Emin Ali Pasha]] and [[person/kececizade-fuad-pasha|Keçecizade Fuad Pasha]] he gave the office a new kind of standing, and Ali Pasha's way of working it was treated as a rule for a long time. The constitution of 1876 does not use the phrase "absolute deputy". Articles 27 and 28 say that the sultan gives the grand vizierate and the shaykh al-Islam's office to men he trusts, and that the council of ministers meets under the grand vizier to consider internal and external questions. After Reşid, Ali and Fuad, grand viziers were changed very often under Abdülhamid II, and the great powers and their ambassadors took a hand in appointment and dismissal. From 1876 there were forty-eight changes. The entry says this rapid change was understood as a reply to the ultimatums the powers gave the Porte. In 1838 the word başvekil was used for a short time instead of grand vizier. It ended when Abdülmecid appointed Koca Hüsrev Pasha grand vizier. On 4 November 1922 the last grand vizier, Ahmed Tevfik Pasha, resigned. The office ended in practice, and the prime minister of the Ankara government took it up. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

Lives of the grand viziers were written as a series. Osmanzade Ahmed Taib's Hadikat ül-vüzera and its supplements are the standard book, but they do not treat the men by function or by powers. İbnülemin Mahmud Kemal's book on the last grand viziers gives full lives of thirty-seven men, from Ali Pasha to Ahmed Tevfik Pasha. Most of it rests on oral report and memoir, and the entry finds it more satisfactory. It also names Mehmet Zeki Pakalın's book on the last grand viziers and prime ministers. The chronicles record appointments and dismissals, what they did in war and peace, ceremonies, the reception of ambassadors, and the news of death. Lütfi Pasha's Asafname is set apart, among books of advice to viziers, because it rests on experience. It was not read for this record except through this entry. The entry faults Danişmend's list for pressing the ethnic origin of the grand viziers, for belittling the successes of those of devshirme origin, and for accusing them in places of disloyalty to the state. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}

## Not yet covered
The bibliography of the entry was not read, nor Hadikat ül-vüzera, nor the Asafname, nor the codes of Mehmed II and Tevkiî Abdurrahman Pasha in their own texts. The museum has no person record for several men the entry names as grand viziers, among them Makbul Ibrahim Pasha, Koca Sinan Pasha, Mustafa Reşid Pasha and Ahmed Tevfik Pasha, so they are not written as holders. The picture the entry captions from a French book of 1688 was not brought in. Its licence is not on the museum's list. {cite:tdv-sadrazam p.414-419}
