## Summary

The ship's captain commanded the vessel at sea: in the enumeration the historians give, the rubbân or reis was the captain, set beside the nâhudâ who owned the ship but need not sail on every voyage {cite:tdv-gemi §1}. Around them stood the dîdebân lookout, the muallim who handled the charts and technical instruments and fixed the course, and the iştiyâm who advised the passengers {cite:tdv-gemi §1}. In the Gulf pearl fishery the nakhoda was often the boat's own owner, commanding divers, rope-tenders and the 'prayer-man' who relieved them at prayer {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, p. 91}.

## The officers of the medieval ship

Al-Mas'udi notes two kinds of men on a ship, those who administer and those who serve: the nâhudâ was the ship's owner, whose presence on a voyage was not obligatory; the rubbân or reis was the captain; the dîdebân kept watch; the muallim determined the route by charts and technical instruments; the iştiyâm advised the travellers {cite:tdv-gemi §1}. Al-Maqdisi lists the same personnel as the rubbâniyyîn or captains, the eşâtime or passenger-advisers, the riyâziyyîn who read the charts and laid the course, the vükelâ or commercial agents and the tüccâr or merchants {cite:tdv-gemi §1}. On the Mediterranean ships the crew was called nevâtiyye, the nûtî sailors — the word from the Greek nautés — together with the rüesâ, and the foreman of the crew bore the title kāidü'n-nevâtiyye {cite:tdv-gemi §1}.

## Two fleets, one craft

War and trade ships were of different types, yet each served the other's purpose when needed, and there was a close relation between the trading and the fighting fleets: the experienced hands trained on merchant ships also took their places on the war ships {cite:tdv-gemi §1}. Some of the medieval vessels were very large — eleventh-century sources give one a mast of twenty-three metres and a 300-ton capacity, while Mediterranean ships carrying five hundred passengers are attested in the same period {cite:tdv-gemi §1}.

## The reis of the galley

On the Ottoman kadırga the captains and the fighting men stood on the small half-decks ringed with ropes at bow and stern; the crew numbered about 330 — a hundred warriors, a hundred and ninety-six oarsmen, twenty ropemen, two helmsmen, a sail and two oar repairers, two caulkers, two carpenters, two ship's boys — and over all of them the reis, who commanded with chart and compass {cite:tdv-gemi §2}. The largest oared flagships, the baştardas of the kaptan paşa, carried some 500 oarsmen, 216 fighting men and the rest sailors, gunners and servitors, the whole crew reaching 600 and more {cite:tdv-gemi §2}.

## The nakhoda of the pearl banks

On the pearling boats of the Persian Gulf the nakhoda, or captain, was often the owner of the boat {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, p. 91}. Under him served the ghoas, the divers, mainly Arabs and Sedees, and the sebs or rope-tenders, usually Bedouins or Persians, who attended the divers and did the other work; each larger boat also carried a general utility man called the musalli, the 'prayer-man', who relieved the sebs who stopped to pray {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, p. 91}. The fishery he commanded ran practically as it had six hundred years before: the fleet of some 3,500 boats and 35,000 fishermen worked the banks from June to September {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, pp. 89-91}.

## The command that was learned

The sources describe the ranks of the ship rather than its training: what the muallim brought was skill in charts and instruments, what the reis brought was the authority of command, and the pool in which both were formed was the trading fleet, whose seasoned crewmen the war fleets also drew upon {cite:tdv-gemi §1}. In the Gulf the nakhoda rose within his own boat's economy — captain and owner in one — while the financing of the fishery, mostly by Indian traders furnishing capital for equipment and supplies, stood over him and his crew {cite:kunz-book-of-pearl-1908 ch. VI, pp. 89-91, 98}.
