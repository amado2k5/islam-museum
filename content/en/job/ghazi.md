## Summary

The ghazi — plural guzât — was the fighter 'in the path', the active participle of gazâ, to raid, war and wage jihad, which the Turks above all used as the equivalent of mücahid {cite:tdv-gazi}. The word stands once in the Qur'an in plural and is praised there by implication beside martyrdom, and the hadith uses ghazi and guzât freely, nearly always for those who fight in God's way {cite:tdv-gazi}. The Prophet's words on the ranks of shahid and ghazi raised the maxim 'if I die a shahid, if I return a ghazi', which the entry credits with first-rank influence on the Islamic conquests {cite:tdv-gazi}.

## The ribat volunteers

As Islam spread, ghazi passed from the name of every Muslim who fought for the faith to, in a narrower sense, particular groups in the cities and the army {cite:tdv-gazi}. The nomads of the Central Asian steppe, when harsh winters killed their herds, came down on the towns and took by force what was refused; the Umayyad administration answered by raising volunteer corps the early sources call ghazis and building fortified ribats outside the cities to house them {cite:tdv-gazi}. The ghazis' work became the guarding of towns and caravans and calling the non-Muslim nomads to Islam — with the shaykhs and dervishes of the ribats volunteering alongside — until some bands turned to plunder and the word soured for a time before recovering its old sense {cite:tdv-gazi}.

## The Anatolian frontier

The eleventh-century Turkish incursions into Anatolia were led by ghazis; the emirs who completed the conquest after Manzikert — Danishmend, Mengücük, Süleyman Şah — are remembered with the title, and those settled on the Seljuk-Byzantine marches were the 'uç Türkleri', a ghazi community in which the rank ran father to son as an ocak station, provided the son proved himself a good cengâver {cite:tdv-gazi}. Aşık Pasha's list for the alp eren was a strong heart, courage, arm strength, zeal, a good horse, a special dress, a bow, a good sword and spear, and a fit companion {cite:tdv-gazi}. The Turkish 'alp' can be read as the native equivalent of ghazi, later smoothed under Sufi influence into 'alp eren'; Osman Gazi's companions counted both Abdurrahman Gazi and the alp-titled Konuralp {cite:tdv-gazi}. Dervishes coming with the Mongol-era migrations lent the ghazis spiritual fire, and the Anatolian ghazis of the beyliks represented the seyfî arm of the futuwwa — Mehmed Bey of Aydın was ceremonially made 'sultan of the ghazis' by Sultan Veled at Konya {cite:tdv-gazi}.

## From calling to title

Seljuk b. Dukak won 'al-Malik al-Ghazi' for jihad against the non-Muslim Oghuz; under the Ghaznavids the ghazis played a leading part in the India campaigns, with a Sâlâr-ı Gāziyân Abdullah Kara Tegin in charge of their direction under Sultan Masud {cite:tdv-gazi}. Ghazi became the standing honorific of successful commanders and then rulers: Peçuylu İbrâhim calls Süleyman the Lawgiver by it, Mahmud I received it for the fall of Tabriz in 1732, Mustafa III for repelling the Russians from Khotin in 1769, and Abdülhamid I took it by fatwa in 1788 with firmans ordering it read in the khutbas {cite:tdv-gazi}. Abdülhamid II bore it on his tughra and his coins after the war of 1877-78, and he conferred it on Osman Pasha of Plevna and on Ahmed Muhtar Pasha of the eastern front {cite:tdv-gazi}.

## A name that outlived the frontier

In Turkish culture the gazâ spirit bred its own literature — the gazanâme or gazavatnâme genre, like the maghazi books of Arabic — and gazi served as a personal name for men and, in the feminine Gaziye, for women; the Crimean khans produced three Gazi Girays, and Antep took the title into its name for the heroism of the Independence War {cite:tdv-gazi}. Mustafa Kemal received the ghazi title with the marshal's rank from the Grand National Assembly on 19 September 1921 after Sakarya, and the name still marks Turkey's veterans of the Independence, Korea and Cyprus wars {cite:tdv-gazi}.
