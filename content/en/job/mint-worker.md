## Summary

The darrab is the mint craftsman who struck coin in the darphâne — also called dâr al-darb and dâr al-sikka: gold dinars, silver dirhams, copper fals {cite:tdv-darphane}. The mint was a state house, where the sâhib-i ayâr answered for the coin's weight and fineness, and it was usually run on three-year tax-farm (iltizam) contracts {cite:tdv-darphane}.

## Word and origin

Darphâne is the Persian-Turkish name; dâr means house, darb the striking of coin, sikka the die and then the coin itself — the word even gave Venice's 1284 gold zecchino its name {cite:tdv-darphane}. The first Islamic coinage came under Abd al-Malik b. Marwān (685-705): al-Ḥajjāj struck the dirham at Kufa on his order, the caliph struck the dinar at Damascus, and Egypt's governor Abd al-Azīz struck dirhams at Fustat (694-697) {cite:tdv-darphane}. Before that, Byzantine and Sasanian coins had simply been restruck in existing mints with small changes {cite:tdv-darphane}.

## Inside the mint

Under Süleyman the Magnificent and Selim II more than seventy Ottoman mints were active {cite:tdv-darphane}. Inside worked the masters (üstat) and their apprentices: the entry names the mid-eighteenth-century Egypt mint crew with their pay — the sebbâk melter on 750 pâre a month, the haddâd who repaired the iron parts of the striking tools, the meddâd bar-drawer, the kattâ cutter, the gassâl washer, the polisher, the assayer (muayyir/sâhib-i ayâr), the hallâl who refined gold with nitric acid, the körükçü bellows-man, and the zincirci who struck the inscribed collar on both faces {cite:tdv-darphane}. The sâhib-i ayâr took a share of each nevbet — the furnace-load of metal for striking — and paid masters and apprentices from it {cite:tdv-darphane}. Outside, the hakkâk or sikkegen die-cutters served centre and provincial mints alike, with provincial dies sent out from the centre {cite:tdv-darphane}.

## The striking process

Metal was melted in crucibles to mint fineness, cast into bars, flattened to coin thickness, cut into squares of the coin's diameter and clipped round into blanks (pul) {cite:tdv-darphane}. The blank went between two dies: the lower "kürsü" fixed anvil-like on a block, the upper "çelik" a rod bearing the obverse design, hammered on its head {cite:tdv-darphane}. In 1689 the Venetian convert Cerrah Mustafa built the first mechanical press — the rakkas: a screw holding the çelik, lowered onto the kürsü by swinging an iron bar weighted at both ends — moving striking from hammer to screw {cite:tdv-darphane}.

## The iltizam administration

Ottoman mints were farmed out as mukātaa for three-year terms (tahvil); gold, silver and copper striking each counted as a separate farm, often held by one mültezim at different prices {cite:tdv-darphane}. If no bidder came, the mint ran as emanet, a public enterprise, with state emins checking the mültezim's clerks {cite:tdv-darphane}. In the 1546 tahvil, the Jewish-born Yasef b. Yasef offered 1,552,506 akçe for the Istanbul mint's gold, silver and mangır striking, naming his own staff — himself as clerk, his son İsak as clerk of the gold mint, Hayreddin as emin, the Armenian-born Abdullah as sâhib-i ayâr {cite:tdv-darphane}.

## Coinage policy and the worker

The state profited from striking itself: when a new ruler banned his predecessor's coins, the sarrafs, brokers and mübâyaacıs swept the old akçes at scrap price and fed them to the mint, and the difference between face and bullion value was the state's gain {cite:tdv-darphane}. A silver nevbet was fixed at 13,165 dirhams, of which 165 dirhams went as "fire's share" and 120 as workers' wages, leaving 12,000 dirhams struck per nevbet {cite:tdv-darphane}. As American silver flooded in and costly mines closed, the mints struggled for metal, akçe fineness fell, and by the seventeenth century only the major centres still worked {cite:tdv-darphane}.

## Today

Striking moved to the press in 1853 and to mechanical printing in 1911; after the Republic the mint was renamed Darphâne-i Millî and later merged into the Finance Ministry {cite:tdv-darphane}. The darrab's hand trade was transformed: the mint survives as a state institution, but with machines rather than hammers {cite:tdv-darphane}.
