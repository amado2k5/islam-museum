## Summary
Abu al-Qasim Ahmad ibn Abdallah Ibn al-Saffar al-Ghafiqi — pupil of Maslama al-Majriti at Cordoba — wrote a comprehensive treatise on using the astrolabe titled "Tuhfat al-Tullab fi al-Amal bil-Asturlab"; Sa'id al-Andalusi described it as clear and comprehensible, it was translated into Latin about 1134, and it was used by Alfonso X's astronomers, remaining in European use into the fifteenth century {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 14} {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}. This is an organised instructional guide, not the invention of the instrument itself.

## What it is
An instructional treatise on how to use the astrolabe — the flat instrument used to tell time, find directions and measure star heights — arranged in chapters covering solar adjustment, solar and stellar altitude, day and night hours, prayer times, the qibla, and hour conversion {cite:arabic-latin-corpus-saffar-astrolabe}. The idea is not to invent the astrolabe — it existed before Ibn al-Saffar — but to provide a comprehensive, organised guide collecting the ways of using it in clear chapters {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}.

## The world before it
The astrolabe had passed into the Islamic world from the Greco-Hellenistic traditions two centuries before Ibn al-Saffar's time, and it already had earlier literature of use in Arabic and Latin. Ibn al-Saffar did not invent the instrument nor was he the first to write on it; he was the pupil of Maslama al-Majriti at Cordoba, writing his treatise in a specialised scientific milieu {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 14}.

## The story
Ibn al-Saffar was born at Cordoba and trained in Maslama al-Majriti's school — one of the most important centres of the exact sciences in al-Andalus — where he taught arithmetic, geometry and astronomy and produced disciples {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}. He then moved to Denia on the eastern coast because of civil war and lived there until his death in 426 AH/1035 CE according to the Azhar catalogues {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 14} {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}. It was in this environment that he wrote his astrolabe treatise.

## The maker(s)
Abu al-Qasim Ahmad ibn Abdallah Ibn al-Saffar al-Ghafiqi, nicknamed "Ibn al-Saffar" (son of the coppersmith) because his father was a metalworker, an Andalusi astronomer {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}. His Muslim identity is documented by his being a pupil of Maslama al-Majriti and an author in Arabic in al-Andalus, and Sa'id al-Andalusi mentions him in his Tabaqat {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}. His Sunni label is the museum's declared frame for a scholar in the Sunni milieu of al-Andalus {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}.

## How it works
The treatise does not invent a new instrument; it explains how to use the existing one: each chapter treats one problem — such as adjusting the astrolabe on the Sun to determine the time, or measuring a star's altitude, or computing day and night hours, or finding the direction of the qibla — with the practical steps for each problem {cite:arabic-latin-corpus-saffar-astrolabe} {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}. Most important, according to the Biographical Encyclopedia, it gives a value for the qibla at Cordoba of 30 degrees south of east, which agrees with the azimuth of the rising Sun at the winter solstice {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}.

## The spread
The book spread widely: it was translated into Latin in two versions — one by Johannes Hispalensis and one by Plato of Tivoli, who called it "the best Arabic treatise I have ever read" — translated into Hebrew by Jacob ben Makhir, and into Old Spanish, used by Alfonso X's astronomers, and remained in use in Europe until the fifteenth century {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}. Johannes Hispalensis's Latin version misattributed the treatise to his teacher Maslama al-Majriti because its last chapter is borrowed from his zij, so later scholars thought it the teacher's work {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}.

## What it was built on
The treatise was built on the accumulated heritage of the astrolabe: earlier literature of use in Arabic and Greek, and Maslama al-Majriti's school which inherited the Sindhind books and its zijes {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 14}. Ibn al-Saffar did not come from nothing; he was Majriti's pupil and author of other astronomical works.

## What was built on it
The Latin, Hebrew and Old Spanish translations carried his approach into Europe, and its use at Alfonso X's court proves its arrival at European centres of learning {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}. We establish no direct lineage here to later astrolabes or to modern timekeeping technologies {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}.

## Impact on daily life
The astrolabe was a daily instrument in the medieval period — for determining prayer times, telling the hour, and finding the qibla — so the treatise was a practical guide for anyone wanting to use the instrument. Its spread into three languages and its use at a king's court show it was a practical reference, not mere theory {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}.

## How it changed history
It changed history in that it carried the way of using the astrolabe from Arabic into Latin, Hebrew and Spanish at an early stage of scientific translation, proving that "books of instrument operation" were a bridge between Andalusi and European science {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}. He did not invent the instrument nor was he the first to write on the astrolabe, but he organised its use in one comprehensive book that kept being read for centuries.

## Who invented it?
We do not claim that Ibn al-Saffar invented the astrolabe — the instrument is centuries older than him {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}. What is documented is that he authored a comprehensive and clearly written treatise on its use, and that this treatise is his most famous work and the most widely translated of his works; this is an improvement in instrument literature, not priority for the instrument itself {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 14}.

## Today
Today astrolabes are no longer used in daily life; they have become museum and antiquarian objects {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}. The treatise's trace remains in the tradition of later astronomical instrument books and in the memory of the translations that carried Andalusi science to Europe {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}. The accompanying image is an Andalusi astrolabe from the Metropolitan Museum's open access — a contextual family example, not a page of his treatise {cite:met-open-access}.

## Surviving examples
The direct witness is the Arabic text edited by Millás Vallicrosa in 1955, the Latin translations by Johannes Hispalensis and Plato of Tivoli, and the Hebrew translation by Jacob ben Makhir {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}. A manuscript description of the treatise also exists in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina astronomy catalogue {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 14}. The museum displays no original page of the treatise today.

## In culture
This book represents a model of "books of operation" in the Islamic tradition — books explaining how to use an instrument, not only its theory. Its spread into three languages shows that al-Andalus was a bridge of translation between Arabic and European science {cite:bea-ibn-al-saffar}.

## For young readers
The astrolabe was a flat instrument that helped tell time, directions and star heights. Ibn al-Saffar wrote a treatise explaining how to use it step by step; its translations reached Europe and were still read there centuries after his death.
