## Summary
A Syrian military writer's treatise of about 1280 contains the earliest clear written account known to the historian Partington of purifying saltpetre with wood ashes and crystallisation, together with some seventy-one pyrotechnic compositions — the earliest documented gunpowder chemistry in the Islamic world. It mentions no gun and no propellant use {cite:partington-greek-fire-gunpowder-1960}.

## What it is
A written record: the Kitab al-Furusiyya wa'l-manasib al-harbiyya of al-Hasan al-Rammah, preserved in two Paris manuscripts with coloured figures. Its pyrotechnic sections document incendiary, rocket, firework and smoke compositions, and a method for refining the saltpetre they depend on {cite:partington-greek-fire-gunpowder-1960}.

## The world before it
Islamic war already used naphtha and artificial fire — fire-pots thrown by mangonels, fire-lances, incendiary arrows — and China already knew gunpowder compositions, whose ingredient names and firework types the treatise itself carries {cite:partington-greek-fire-gunpowder-1960} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The story
Al-Rammah, who died in 1294-95 still in his thirties, compiled his book for the masters of furusiyya and the artificers 'for the advancement of Islam'. In it he describes taking saltpetre solution, treating it with wood ash so the unwanted salts fall out, and crystallising the clear liquid — the process Partington calls the first clear account he knew of refining saltpetre {cite:partington-greek-fire-gunpowder-1960}.

## The maker(s)
[[person/hasan-al-rammah|Al-Hasan al-Rammah]] is the named compiler; Partington notes the recipes were probably family and workshop material collected over time, some marked 'tested' — a codifier of inherited craft, not a lone inventor {cite:partington-greek-fire-gunpowder-1960}.

## How it works
The treatise describes dissolving raw saltpetre in warm water, adding prepared wood ash to precipitate the calcium and magnesium salts that spoil the powder, then crystallising the clear liquor — a purification chemistry, set down in words rather than passed hand to hand {cite:partington-greek-fire-gunpowder-1960}.

## The spread
The book belongs to the Mamluk furusiyya manuscript culture of Syria; two copies survive in Paris, and Partington judged the manuscript was probably prepared for confidential official use — a craft text that did not aim at a public {cite:partington-greek-fire-gunpowder-1960}.

## What it was built on
The older incendiary tradition of the Islamic world and, plainly, on Chinese material: the mixtures, the coloured smokes and the firework names echo Chinese practice, although the text does not call saltpetre 'snow of China' {cite:partington-greek-fire-gunpowder-1960}.

## What was built on it
Mamluk firearms appear in the record within a generation; the treatise documents the pyrotechnic stage they grew from. The purification method it describes is the one later powder-makers used to obtain pure nitrate {cite:partington-greek-fire-gunpowder-1960}.

## Impact on daily life
Its practical arts were the fireworks, signal smokes and siege-fires of its age — spectacle and war, not household craft. The museum describes the record; it does not teach the recipes {cite:partington-greek-fire-gunpowder-1960}.

## How it changed history
It fixed gunpowder chemistry in an Islamic-world text two generations before Mamluk cannon — a datable written edge between Chinese invention and the gunpowder empires {cite:partington-greek-fire-gunpowder-1960}.

## Who invented it?
Not al-Rammah: gunpowder is Chinese and older. His book is the earliest documented Islamic writing of the compositions and of saltpetre purification — a record of transmission and systematisation, honestly so {cite:partington-greek-fire-gunpowder-1960}.

## Today
The treatise is studied as a landmark in the history of pyrotechnics; its manuscript is in Paris, its text edited and discussed since the nineteenth century {cite:partington-greek-fire-gunpowder-1960}.

## Surviving examples
The two Paris manuscripts — BN ancien fonds 2825 and fonds Asselin 643 — with their coloured figures of engines and devices {cite:partington-greek-fire-gunpowder-1960}.

## In culture
The book belongs to the furusiyya literature — the chivalric-military arts taught in the hippodromes of Cairo and Damascus in the Bahri Mamluk age {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
About seven hundred years ago a young Syrian wrote a book about horsemanship and war. In it he wrote down how to clean a special salt that makes fireworks and coloured smoke — the first time anyone in his world wrote that down clearly, so far as historians know. He did not invent the powder; he wrote down what the workshops knew.
