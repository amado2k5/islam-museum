## Summary
The Nizamiyyas were not Islam's first madrasas. This record follows the contribution of a network sponsored by [[person/nizam-al-mulk|Nizam al-Mulk]], joining teaching, accommodation and books to endowments supporting teachers, students and staff, as described by TDV {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. The [[building/al-madrasa-al-nizamiyya-baghdad|Baghdad Nizamiyya]] opened in 459 AH/1067 CE, but Nishapur's preceded it {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. More than a century later Ibn Jubayr saw the Baghdad institution and described the city's schools' endowments and maintenance of teachers and students {cite:rihla-ibn-jubayr-1907 scan p. 193}. The museum does not call this invention of education, universities or ijaza.

## What it is
The madrasa here is an institution with teachers, books, accommodation and continuing income, not merely a name for a scholarly gathering. TDV describes Baghdad's complex with rooms for teachers and students, classrooms, a mosque, library, dormitory, dining room and bath {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. “Nizamiyya” joins institutions in several cities, not one building repeated with identical dimensions and plans {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}.

“Network” describes the patronage programme and shared name and model, not an assumed modern education ministry or a common examination across cities. The encyclopedia identifies different teachers, endowments and dates, with some institutions following the model after its patron's death {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}.

## The world before it
TDV rejects the claim it reports from some historians that the Nizamiyyas were Islam's first madrasas, and explicitly says Baghdad was not even the earliest Nizamiyya {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. Learning therefore does not begin at this school's gate, nor does endowment become the vizier's invention.

The explicit financial link is [[invention/waqf|waqf]]: land, markets and shops whose income supports teaching and maintenance. TDV describes these properties in Baghdad, and Ibn Jubayr connects the schools' endowments with their people's livelihood {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191} {cite:rihla-ibn-jubayr-1907 scan p. 193}. This does not mean Umar's particular endowment sent money to Baghdad.

## The story
TDV places the programme in a policy of strengthening Sunni teaching and preparing state personnel under Alp Arslan and Malikshah, in competition with Fatimid religious teaching as its author interprets it {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. This is an attributed interpretation, not a licence for the museum to speak of other traditions in an opponent's language.

A site east of the Tigris was chosen for Baghdad, with Abu Sad al-Kashi supervising construction. Work began in Dhu al-Hijja 457/November 1065; the school opened on 10 Dhu al-Qada 459/22 September 1067 according to the encyclopedia {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. The data stores only the year, explaining that it dates the Baghdad exemplar, not the whole network's beginning.

According to TDV's narrative, Abu Ishaq al-Shirazi did not attend the opening after hearing the land had been wrongfully acquired. Ibn al-Sabbagh gave the first lesson; al-Shirazi took over twenty days later after the vizier's and students' insistence and the caliph's request {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. The property question and refusal remain part of the story, rather than a celebration without tension.

## The maker(s)
Nizam al-Mulk commissioned foundations, endowed them and selected teachers; he did not author every lesson or invent learning. His TDV biography describes hadith activity, support for Ash'ari and Shafi'i scholars and promotion of Sunni teaching: this documents affiliation rather than inference from name or Persian origin {cite:tdv-nizamulmulk pp. 194–196}.

Al-Kashi had the construction-supervision role, al-Shirazi and Ibn al-Sabbagh the teaching role. In 484/1091 the vizier appointed [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|Abu Hamid al-Ghazali]] to teach in Baghdad {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. [[person/imam-al-haramayn-al-juwayni|Al-Juwayni]] belongs to Nishapur, where the survey reports he taught for over twenty years, not Baghdad's first chair {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}.

## How it works
The institution connects four parts in TDV's account {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}:

1. **Assigned income.** Land, a market, shops and a bath supported teachers, students and staff.
2. **Posts.** Teacher, preacher, librarian, reciter and Arabic and grammar instructor, alongside administration and service staff.
3. **A place to learn and live.** Rooms, lessons, books, lodging and food, not a passing lesson alone.
4. **Endowment conditions.** Baghdad's foundation was for Shafi'is, including Shafi'i requirements for some service posts, with management assigned to the vizier and his descendants.

TDV lists Qur'an and its sciences, hadith, Shafi'i law and legal theory, Ash'ari theology, Arabic, mathematics and inheritance calculation among the subjects, with hours varying by season, teacher and subject {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. It reports admission above age twenty and a four-year course: these are its historical statements, not automatic equivalence to a modern university degree.

## The spread
The encyclopedia describes Nizamiyyas at Nishapur, Isfahan, Balkh, Mosul, Basra, Herat, Merv and Amul {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. Some are better documented than others: it gives no exact foundation date for Nishapur, says information about some attributed sites is insufficient, and places some foundations after the patron's death as followers of the model {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}.

The record therefore draws no route starting in Baghdad and passing through every city in an invented order. Nishapur preceded it; this is a multi-centre programme, not the journey of one architectural plan {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}.

## What it was built on
The Nizamiyyas inherited the madrasa institution and used waqf to connect property with educational income {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. The diagram keeps the two antecedents separate: earlier madrasas and endowment. Its arrows do not claim every Baghdad condition was unprecedented.

Ibn Jubayr's witness is later than construction: he describes about thirty schools in eastern Baghdad, calls the Nizamiyya the greatest and most famous and notes its renewal in 504 AH {cite:rihla-ibn-jubayr-1907 scan p. 193}. He witnesses the institution at his visit, not the opening in 459.

## What was built on it
TDV says later schools bore the Nizamiyya name because they followed the model, while noting insufficient information about some sites {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. That is the extension recorded here.

The passages read give no documented chain to a particular European university or priority for inventing certificates and degrees. The article therefore does not equate scholars' ijaza with a modern university qualification, nor treat every contemporary “Nizamiyya” as a surviving administrative branch of the vizier's programme.

## Impact on daily life
The tangible effect in Ibn Jubayr's account is that endowment income reaches teachers and provides students with their maintenance {cite:rihla-ibn-jubayr-1907 scan p. 193}. Learning is connected to the cost of lodging and livelihood, not desire for knowledge alone.

TDV describes a rich library, reports about six thousand volumes and students rescuing books from the fire of 510/1116, then rebuilding and enrichment with Caliph al-Nasir's books in 589/1193 {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. The number is a reported estimate, not a modern inventory; books belong to the institution's life alongside rooms and food.

## How it changed history
TDV connects Baghdad's prestige with scholars competing to teach there, formal appointments and support for Sunni teaching, with explicitly Shafi'i endowment conditions {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. Multiple subjects do not make the foundation confessionally unrestricted.

The travel narrative adds a public scene: Ibn Jubayr witnessed a gathering at the Nizamiyya attended by Sadr al-Din al-Khujandi, describing the audience and preaching {cite:rihla-ibn-jubayr-1907 scan p. 202}. This illustrates the building in use, not a complete timetable or proof that every educational policy succeeded.

## Who invented it?
**The Sunni witness.** Ibn Jubayr names Nizam al-Mulk as the Nizamiyya's builder and describes the relationship between Baghdad schools' endowments, teachers and students {cite:rihla-ibn-jubayr-1907 scan p. 193}. The passage does not say the vizier invented schools worldwide.

**The encyclopedia study.** TDV reports and rejects some historians' first-madrasa claims, distinguishing Nishapur's chronological priority from Baghdad's fame and its construction and opening dates {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. “First” is not repeated as fact.

The attribution is improvement of a multi-city endowed institutional programme. The record adopts no disputed priority claim: an honorific invention title does not replace the limits of the evidence.

## Today
TDV places continued Baghdad teaching in the fourteenth century, mentioning al-Firuzabadi as a teaching assistant then, but cannot date its end {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. The Mongol invasion is not automatically chosen as a certain closure date.

Accounts differ about damage in 656/1258, while the survey records floods, repairs and later decline {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. The legacy is institutional and historical; no single administration continuing from the eleventh century to today is claimed.

## Surviving examples
Images of scan pp. 193 and 202 of Ibn Jubayr's travels, Brill 1907, were read: the first describes schools and their endowments, the second a Nizamiyya gathering {cite:rihla-ibn-jubayr-1907 scan pp. 193, 202}. Neither is the foundation's original endowment deed.

The photograph shows Baghdad's later Mustansiriyya, which TDV dates to 631/1234, not the Nizamiyya or a reconstruction of it {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}. Its courtyard and arcades provide visual context for the city's madrasa history only.

## In culture
Ibn Jubayr admires the institution as Baghdad's greatest and most famous madrasa and praises the schools' and hospitals' endowments {cite:rihla-ibn-jubayr-1907 scan p. 193}. His admiration remains attributed, not converted into a measurable global ranking.

TDV questions the chronological possibility of the story that the vizier, Hasan Sabbah and Umar Khayyam studied together because their ages differ; it is not used as a dramatic explanation of the network's origin {cite:tdv-nizamulmulk pp. 194–196}.

## For young readers
Imagine wanting to study in a distant city: you need a teacher, a book, somewhere to sleep and food. Sources describe Nizam al-Mulk linking schools with endowments helping to provide these things {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191} {cite:rihla-ibn-jubayr-1907 scan p. 193}. He did not invent school: he developed institutions with funds, posts and conditions, including Shafi'i conditions in Baghdad {cite:tdv-nizamiye-medresesi pp. 188–191}.
