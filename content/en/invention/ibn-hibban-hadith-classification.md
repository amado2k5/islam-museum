## Summary
Ibn Hibban al-Busti arranged all the Prophetic tradition he had collected into five major divisions — commands, prohibitions, reports, permissions and reports of actions — branching into four hundred types, as his preface states; he made this "following the arrangement of the Qur'an" in its division, and gave the reason as easing retrieval and understanding for the reader {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 56-59} {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 109} {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 112-113}. This is an original and documented classification system in the history of hadith books, not an invention of the subject of hadith itself.

## What it is
A division-and-classification system for the Prophetic tradition collected by Ibn Hibban in his book known as Sahih Ibn Hibban. The idea is that every prophetic report is assigned to one of five major divisions according to its rhetorical type: commands, prohibitions, reports, permissions, and actions. Each division then branches into smaller types, and the total of all types is four hundred {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 56-59} {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 109}. With this system the book acquires a fixed structure from which the student can request a chapter and a type.

## The world before it
Before Ibn Hibban, hadith books had arranged their material in different ways: the Musnad ordered by companion, the Sunan by legal topics, and the two Sahihs by varied chapters not unified under a single comprehensive division. Ibn Hibban himself was a pupil of Abu Bakr Ibn Khuzayma and followed him in committing to the bare sahih {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 6, 13}. But none of these arrangements offered one system that described all prophetic reports by a comprehensive rhetorical division as Ibn Hibban did. In his own preface he bears witness that the routes of reports had multiplied and people's knowledge of the sound among them had dwindled, because they were preoccupied with fabricated books and with memorising errors and reversals, until the sound report was neglected and left unwritten while the false one was treasured {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 58-59}.

## The story
Ibn Hibban al-Busti — Abu Hatim Muhammad ibn Hibban — was born in Khurasan, sought knowledge around the turn of the year 300, wrote in Syria, the Hijaz, Egypt, Iraq, the Jazira and Khurasan, and served as judge of Samarqand {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 506-508}. He collected much hadith and saw that its arrangement needed a clearer system than earlier books offered, so he planned to divide the tradition into five divisions "following the arrangement of the Qur'an" — that is, on the model of the Qur'an's division into parts, surahs and verses — and declared in his preface that his aim was to ease retrieval and understanding {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 56-59}. On this arrangement he ordered his whole book, which he named "al-Musnad al-Sahih ala al-Taqasim wa-al-Anwa'" — the sound musnad on divisions and types {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 15}. In his preface he also records that he wrote from more than two thousand shaykhs between Isbijab and Alexandria, yet narrated in this book only from about a hundred and fifty, the book's reliance resting on some twenty shaykhs on whom he had mastered the traditions {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 21}.

## The maker(s)
Ibn Hibban al-Busti, the hadith master and jurist, who died in 354 AH/965 CE according to al-Dhahabi in Mizan al-I'tidal {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 508}. His Muslim identity is documented by his being one of the imams of hadith, his judgeship at Samarqand, and his Arabic works; his Sunni label is the museum's declared frame because his work stood inside the Sunni tradition of hadith sciences, while his personal positions remain a recorded dispute on his card {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 506-508} {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 51-59}.

## How it works
The rule is simple: every prophetic report enters one of five major divisions according to its nature — if it is a command it goes to the commands division, if a prohibition to the prohibitions, if a report of an event to the reports, and so on through to the prophetic actions. Each division is then subdivided into finer types until the total reaches four hundred {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 56-59} {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 109}. The author describes the five divisions as equal, agreed in division and non-contradictory: the commands God commanded His servants, the prohibitions He forbade them, His reports of what needs to be known, the permissions He allowed, and the Prophet's actions done exclusively by him {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 58-59}. This division makes searching the book a matter of type rather than a scattered text, which is what the author stated as the purpose.

## The spread
The original arrangement of the divisions did not survive as Ibn Hibban ordered it; the amir 'Ala' al-Din 'Ali ibn Balban later re-arranged the book into books and chapters in the circulating editions while preserving Ibn Hibban's text precisely and fixing the hadith titles verbatim {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 17}. Nevertheless the five divisions remained the reference for understanding the book's original structure, and the critics ranked Sahih Ibn Hibban after the two Sahihs and before the Mustadrak {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 11}. The book reached later readers through a connected isnad: Abu al-Hasan al-Bahhathi transmitted it from Ibn Harun al-Zuzani from Ibn Hibban, Abu al-Qasim al-Shahhami read it, and the hafiz Ibn 'Asakir carried it with the connected chain as Yaqut records in Mu'jam al-Buldan {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 24}.

## What it was built on
The system was built on the Qur'an's precedent in its organised division, on the hadith profession's work of collecting and classifying reports, and on Ibn Hibban's own experience with his teacher Ibn Khuzayma's sahih {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 56-59} {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 6}. His preface also employs the conditions for narrators and knowledge of criticism and vindication — inherited tools of hadith science {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 112-113}. He justified the arrangement by observing that the earlier compilers of the sunan and the jurists who discussed them had gone deep into citing routes for reports and repeated words for memorizers, which made students rely on the book and left quoters without direct acquisition; Ibn Hibban examined the sahihs so that memorization would be easy for students and comprehension not hard for those who quote {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 58-59}.

## What was built on it
The idea of rhetorical division of reports remained a reference for those who came after Ibn Hibban in collecting and directing traditions; his book was later re-arranged into chapters but stayed one of the relied-upon books in the Sunni tradition {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 11} {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 506-508}. Later scholars produced works on it: Siraj al-Din Ibn al-Mulaqqin abridged it, and the hafiz al-Haythami singled out the "Mawarid al-Zam'an fi Zawa'id Sahih Ibn Hibban" on its excess over the two Sahihs {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 20-21}. We establish no later system here claiming direct descent from his five divisions.

## Impact on daily life
Its direct effect on people's daily lives is limited; it was a tool for students of knowledge, not for the general public. But through his book it influenced the way the tradition was preserved and studied in schools, making access to prophetic texts clearer through a declared system {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 56-59}. The editor Shakir comments on Ibn Hibban's aim in the memorization device that the device did not succeed for its first purpose; instead it succeeded only too well in making the book hard to consult, and this may be one factor in the rarity of its copies {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 16}.

## How it changed history
It changed history in that it added to the hadith-book heritage a comprehensive rhetorical classification system unmatched in a single book, and justified it explicitly by ease of retrieval; it did not invent the subject of hadith or criticism and vindication, but it organised a huge corpus into a navigable structure {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 56-59} {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 109}. Al-Suyuti in Tarbiyat al-Rawi called Ibn Hibban's ordering "invented" — neither on chapters nor on musnads — named it "al-Taqasim wa-al-Anwa'", attributed it to his knowledge of theology, grammar and philosophy, and recorded that he was accused of zandaqa, nearly sentenced to death, then exiled from Sijistan to Samarqand {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 15-16}.

## Who invented it?
What is documented is that Ibn Hibban designed this division himself and declared it in his preface; we do not claim he was the first to classify hadith — the Musnad, the Sunan and the two Sahihs preceded him {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 56-59}. The novelty is the declared fivefold rhetorical division that placed every report in one of four hundred types; this is documented originality, not absolute priority for the science of classification.

## Today
Today there are digital and topical classification systems for hadith, but we do not establish here that Ibn Hibban's divisions are their direct source; the division remains a witness that regulating large texts was an intellectual concern long before computers {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1}. ## Surviving examples
The most important witness is the book itself in the Shakir edition, which preserves Ibn Hibban's preface with its five divisions and four hundred types {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 56-59}. His other books on criticism, vindication and weak narrators are also recorded in the biographies {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 506-508} {cite:thiqat-ibn-hibban}. Among the early copies the editor describes is the one in the Ahmad III library in Istanbul, number 347, copied by Ahmad ibn Yahya ibn 'Asakir and completed in Jumada I 739, read in the Sacred Mosque {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 25-26}.

## In culture
This system represents a model of the hadith scholars' concern with knowledge organisation in the medieval period: collecting reports was not enough — they had to be arranged in a structure that could be learned and searched. It is also an example of the "composition on the model" tradition in which scholars followed the Qur'an's pattern in their books {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 pp. 56-59}. The editor notes that Ibn Hibban was not prevented from a true index except by the absence of printing, and that the amir 'Ala' al-Din made a true index for Ibn Hibban's book by placing the number of the type and division beside each hadith {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 18}.

## For young readers
Hadith books had gathered reports in one ordering, but Ibn Hibban wanted a clearer one: he divided them into five great divisions — commands, prohibitions, reports, permissions and actions — up to four hundred types, so a reader could find what he sought more easily.
