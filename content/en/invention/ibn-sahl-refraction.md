## Summary
Around the year 984, a scholar attached to the Buyid court of Baghdad wrote a treatise on burning mirrors and lenses that contains something no earlier text anywhere contains: a constant geometrical ratio governing how light bends when it passes from one medium into another — the rule we now call Snell's law. Abu Sa'd al-'Ala' ibn Sahl used that ratio not as a curiosity but as a design tool, deriving lens shapes that focus parallel rays to a single point. The work was reconstructed and published by the historian of science Roshdi Rashed in 1990 and 1993 {cite:rashed-geometrie-dioptrique-1993} {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sahl-en}.

## What it is
A proved law of refraction and its application. Ibn Sahl's construction takes the lengths associated with an incident ray and its refracted continuation and shows their ratio is fixed by the two media — mathematically equivalent to the sine ratio written three and a half centuries later as Snell's law. He then put the law to work, designing 'anaclastic' lenses — plano-convex lenses curved along a hyperbola — that bring parallel rays to a true focal point without geometric error {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sahl-en}.

## The world before it
Ptolemy's Optics had recorded refraction as a table of measured angles: useful numbers, no rule. Greek and early Arabic writers knew burning mirrors — the legend of Archimedes' mirrors was old — but mirrors reflect, and a reflecting surface is a simpler geometry than a refracting lens. In tenth-century Baghdad, the translation movement had made Ptolemy's book available, and mathematicians such as al-Quhi and al-Kindi's successors worked on conics and light, yet refraction remained an empirical list {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sahl-en}.

## The story
Ibn Sahl (c. 940-1000) worked in the Baghdad of the Buyid emirs. His treatise survives imperfectly: a three-folio piece in Damascus (al-Zahiriyya MS 4871, titled On the Burning Instrument) and a much longer but badly damaged manuscript in Tehran (Milli MS 867, titled The Book of Burners by a later hand). Between them they preserve the full arc of the work: the refraction theorem, then the lens designs, then parabolic and ellipsoidal mirrors, biconvex lenses and techniques for drawing hyperbolic arcs. Nothing else in the medieval record does this {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sahl-en} {cite:rashed-geometrie-dioptrique-1993}.

## The maker(s)
One man: Abu Sa'd al-'Ala' ibn Sahl. Of his life almost nothing is known — even his name gives no certain country or creed, and Rashed says as much. What survives is the work itself, and it is enough: the first serious study of Ptolemy's Optics by a Muslim scholar, and a treatise that stands at the head of the Arabic optical tradition some thirty years before Ibn al-Haytham's Book of Optics {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sahl-en}.

## How it works
Light changes speed when it crosses from air into glass or water, and the change bends its path. Ibn Sahl's figure fixes the geometry: the ratio between the hypotenuse drawn on the incident side and the hypotenuse on the refracted side is constant for a given pair of media — the ratio of their refractive indices. Turn that around and you can compute the surface a lens must have: he showed a plano-convex lens, curved as a hyperbola, collects all rays parallel to its axis into one focal point {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sahl-en}.

## The spread
The treatise itself did not spread widely — two manuscripts survive, one incomplete — and the law had to be rediscovered in Europe. But its idea spread through the discipline: within a generation Ibn al-Haytham made optics a science in its own right, and the whole line of Arabic dioptrics — lenses, burning instruments, the rainbow — follows from the opening Ibn Sahl made {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sahl-en} {cite:rashed-geometrie-dioptrique-1993}.

## What it was built on
Ptolemy's Optics, translated into Arabic, gave him the phenomenon and the measurements; the geometry of conic sections, cultivated in Baghdad since the Banu Musa and Thabit ibn Qurra, gave him the tools; and the older burning-mirror literature gave him the question {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sahl-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What was built on it
Ibn al-Haytham's Book of Optics — written about thirty years later — is the monument raised on this foundation; EI2's history of Arabic mathematics places Ibn Sahl in the company of Thabit ibn Qurra, al-Quhi and Ibn al-Haytham himself {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. At a longer remove: Snell's law (1621), Descartes' formulation, and every lens designed since {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sahl-en}.

## Impact on daily life
None directly in the tenth century — this was court mathematics, aimed at theory, not at spectacles for readers. Its daily-life impact came centuries later, through every lens ground to the ratio he proved: eyeglasses, telescopes, microscopes, cameras {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sahl-en}.

## How it changed history
It is the earliest surviving quantitative law of geometrical optics in any tradition — the moment refraction stopped being a list of measurements and became a theorem. The history of optics used to begin at Ptolemy and resume at Ibn al-Haytham; Rashed's edition showed there was a working law between them, written in Baghdad around 984 {cite:rashed-geometrie-dioptrique-1993}.

## Who invented it?
Abu Sa'd al-'Ala' ibn Sahl — documented, not legendary. His treatise survives under his name in two manuscripts, edited by Rashed in 1993. The scholars cited in the record — Hogendijk and Sabra, Wolf, Zghal and colleagues, A. Mark Smith — describe him as the discoverer of the law of refraction, while noting honestly that the compact 'sine law' phrasing is the modern reading of his ratio construction {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sahl-en} {cite:rashed-geometrie-dioptrique-1993}.

## Today
Snell's law is taught in every physics classroom; the history books increasingly attach Ibn Sahl's name to its first proof. His treatise exists as Rashed's edition and French translation — the manuscripts themselves remain in Damascus and Tehran {cite:rashed-geometrie-dioptrique-1993} {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sahl-en}.

## Surviving examples
The work survives as text, not artefact: Damascus, al-Zahiriyya MS 4871 (three folios, containing a section absent from the longer copy) and Tehran, Milli MS 867 (fifty-one folios, badly damaged). No instrument made to his design survives {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sahl-en}.

## In culture
In the history-of-science literature Ibn Sahl has become the emblem of a recovered priority: the man who anticipated Snell by six and a half centuries and whose existence was almost wholly forgotten until Rashed read the damaged folios {cite:rashed-geometrie-dioptrique-1993} {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sahl-en}.

## For young readers
When light goes from air into water, it bends — that is why a straw in a glass looks broken. A scientist in Baghdad named Ibn Sahl figured out the exact mathematical rule for that bend a thousand years ago, long before anyone else. He even used it to design perfect lenses that gather sunlight into one bright, hot point.
