## Summary

An astronomical model can change in two ways: the calculated body moves, but the elements describing its motion may also change. Historians attribute to [[person/al-zarqali|al-Zarqali]] an account of the solar apogee's own motion and a solar model with variable eccentricity, in a lost work summarising twenty-five years of solar observations according to the report presented by Roser Puig {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}.

This is a contribution to modelling, distinct from his [[invention/universal-astrolabe-al-zarqali|universal plate]] and [[invention/equatorium-al-zarqali|two-plate equatorium]], not a claim that he invented all solar astronomy. The museum presents the work through the scholarship actually read; it has not inspected the lost original or recovered all its calculations {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}.

## What it is

Apogee means the farthest point from Earth in the Earth-centred description of a body's motion; perigee is the nearest. The accompanying modern image explains that near/far distinction, but it is a generic Earth-centred teaching drawing, not al-Zarqali's solar diagram {cite:commons-apogee-psf description-and-history}.

Puig identifies two linked features: an independent motion of the solar apogee and variable eccentricity in the solar model. These statements do not attribute Kepler's elliptical orbital law or modern gravitational dynamics to the medieval author {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}.

A rate for one changing element is not a complete daily solar ephemeris. Puig separates theoretical works, tables, an almanac and instruments; keeping those categories distinct also prevents counting the same contribution repeatedly under different names {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}.

## The world before it

Astronomical observations and calculations had a long inherited history. MacTutor describes the Toledan Tables as combining material from al-Khwarizmi, al-Battani and Ptolemy with observations by al-Zarqali and his colleagues, in a group containing Muslim and Jewish scholars {cite:zarqali-mactutor Toledan-Tables paragraph}.

That setting makes neither every table entry original nor earlier astronomers dispensable. It also does not establish that every ancient and Arabic model used one identical apogee convention: this account stays with the specific change attributed to al-Zarqali rather than constructing an unsupported universal account of earlier astronomy {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}.

The biography distinguishes years spent observing from the later work summarising the observations. A probable treatise date must therefore not become the beginning of every observation, while an early observation must not automatically become the completion date of the later theory {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}.

## The story

Al-Zarqali appears in the historical accounts as an instrument maker and engraver working in [[place/tulaytula|Toledo]]. Puig reports Marrakushi's testimony that he observed there in1061 and Ibn al-Ha'im's attribution of twenty-five years of solar observations; these are reports conveyed by the modern biography, not an observation notebook inspected here {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}.

Puig probably dates the lost solar work to approximately1075–1080 and gives alternative titles, *On the Solar Year* or *A Comprehensive Epistle on the Sun*. Its contents are known through Arabic and Latin secondary sources, so the museum does not select a certain title and imply that a surviving original is available {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}.

This is not his separate fixed-star-motion treatise, dated approximately1084–1085 and surviving in Hebrew translation. Similar astronomical subjects do not make the survival history of one text evidence for the survival of the other {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}.

## How it works

The central distinction is between calculating a body's changing position and allowing the element defining the apogee's direction to change. Puig gives the apogee its own motion of about one degree in279 Julian years, alongside variable eccentricity; the biography supplies neither the lost treatise's full equations nor an executable reconstruction {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}.

The reported unit can be checked with modern paper arithmetic. One degree contains sixty arcminutes and each arcminute sixty arcseconds, making3600 arcseconds. Dividing3600 by279 gives approximately12.9032 arcseconds per year, or0.00358423 degrees annually: a modern conversion of the reported rate, not a decimal notation attributed to the author {cite:zarqali-puig-bea solar-work paragraph}.

Multiplying the unrounded rate by279 returns3600 arcseconds. By contrast, the12.04 arcseconds/year repeated in the museum's older biography corresponds to approximately299.003 years per degree. These quantities are not numerically interchangeable; this check does not resolve the historical discrepancy or establish what the lost text actually said {cite:zarqali-puig-bea solar-work paragraph}.

An arcsecond is an angular unit, not one second of elapsed clock time. “Per year” describes how that angle changes with time; confusing the two types of second changes the meaning even when every written numeral is copied correctly {cite:zarqali-puig-bea solar-work paragraph}.

## The spread

Puig describes the variable-eccentricity solar model as influential in the Maghrib and Latin Europe until Copernicus's time. This is a historian's account of prolonged reception, not a list of surviving originals or evidence that everyone in those regions used an unchanged calculation {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}.

MacTutor also discusses the almanac's circulation and adaptation for later dates, but that is another work. Its surviving languages and later revisions cannot simply be reassigned to the lost solar treatise, nor does the tables' popularity establish transmission of every theoretical detail {cite:zarqali-mactutor solar-work-and-Almanac paragraph}.

## Who invented it?

The narrow answer is that Puig attributes the apogee's own motion and a variable-eccentricity solar model to al-Zarqali. MacTutor repeats the lost-work account and one-degree-in279-years rate; neither contribution needs a headline claiming that he first knew everything about an orbit {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260} {cite:zarqali-mactutor solar-work paragraph}.

Writing about the Sun also does not make him the author of Kepler's laws. MacTutor cautions against a comparable exaggeration concerning Mercury, a separate subject; this account keeps solar theory, the equatorium's Mercury geometry and modern orbital explanation distinct {cite:zarqali-mactutor Mercury discussion}.

## Today

NASA's modern explanation distinguishes Earth's axial precession, the changing orientation of its orbital ellipse and changes in eccentricity. The comparison is useful because “motion” alone does not specify whether a body's position, a path's shape or an axis or path's orientation has changed {cite:nasa-milankovitch-cycles Eccentricity-Obliquity-Precession}.

It remains an explicitly modern comparison, not a literal translation of al-Zarqali. NASA's approximate cycles are not used to certify the historical rate or to claim that he supplied gravitational dynamics or predicted Earth's climate; each account has its own questions, vocabulary and evidence {cite:nasa-milankovitch-cycles Precession} {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}.

## For young readers

Draw an exaggerated oval on paper, place an Earth point away from its centre, and mark near and far positions. This models what perigee and apogee mean; it is neither a scale drawing nor a reproduction of al-Zarqali's solar model {cite:commons-apogee-psf description}.

Copy the direction joining the positions onto a transparent sheet and turn it a little. Turning that line is different from moving a dot along a fixed path; the activity explains a distinction, not a historical experiment or a real annual amount of precession {cite:zarqali-puig-bea solar-work paragraph}.

For a paper calculation, divide3600 by279 and multiply the unrounded answer by279. Returning to3600 checks that the units survived; no telescope or direct view of the Sun is needed, and this is not an observing procedure {cite:zarqali-puig-bea solar-work paragraph}.
