## Summary

Al-Tasrif directs the surgeon, for internal wounds, to thread made of animal gut — material the body absorbs, so no second opening is needed to draw it out. That instruction is the earliest written record read of an absorbable suture, and the idea still closes internal wounds today under names like vicryl and PDS {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## What it is

A suture material and its use: gut string — made from processed animal intestine — strong enough to hold tissue, perishable enough to disappear. For surface wounds ordinary thread came out later; inside the body, a thread that dissolves was the only safe option {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## The world before it

Sutures were old — linen, silk, plant fibre had closed wounds for millennia. But every such thread had to be removed or festered; inside the body that meant either leaving a foreign body or cutting again. No earlier surgical text read specifies an absorbable thread.

## The story

In Cordoba's caliphal court around 1000 CE, al-Zahrawi compiled his surgical treatise for practitioners "unable to compile." Among its instrument drawings and operations stands a quiet material note: for internal suturing, use gut {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973} {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861}.

## The maker(s)

Abu al-Qasim al-Zahrawi (c. 936–1013), the court physician-surgeon of Cordoba, recorded as Muslim on his person record. Whether he devised gut sutures or codified existing practice is unsettled — the text is his.

## How it works

Gut is protein: the body's own enzymes slowly digest it while the wound knits. Thread strength outlasts the critical healing days, then the material is absorbed — mechanical closure, then disappearance {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## The spread

Through al-Tasrif's Latin transmission the material entered European surgery, where catgut — refined, sterilised — remained standard absorbable suture into the twentieth century.

## What it was built on

On the ancient suturing craft and on knowledge of gut as a strong, workable animal material — strings for instruments and binding were already familiar.

## What was built on it

The whole absorbable-suture line: chromic catgut in the nineteenth century, then synthetics (vicryl, PDS, monocryl) that still disappear inside healing tissue.

## Impact on daily life

It made internal repair survivable: closing deep tissue without leaving something that must be cut out again meant fewer deaths from reopened wounds and retained thread.

## How it changed history

It introduced the absorbable principle into the surgical record — the idea that a repair material should vanish when its job is done, now a design goal across implantable medicine.

## Who invented it?

For the written record: al-Zahrawi, in the sources read. Gut as material was old; its documented prescription as internal suture begins here {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## Today

Real gut is largely replaced by synthetics with tuned absorption times — but every absorbable stitch inherits this record's principle.

## Surviving examples

## In culture

Catgut sutures are a standard exhibit in the "Muslim surgery" narrative of the Golden Age.

## For young readers

If you stitch a deep cut, how do you get the thread back out? A surgeon in Cordoba a thousand years ago answered: use a thread that melts away inside. Doctors still use disappearing stitches today — they started with that idea.
