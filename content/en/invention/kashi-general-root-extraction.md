## Summary

In the [[work/miftah-al-hisab-kashi|Key to Arithmetic]], [[person/al-kashi|Ghiyath al-Din al-Kashi]] treated numerical extraction of roots of general orders, not only square roots. MacTutor reports Luckey's comparison of his procedure with a special case of the later Ruffini/Horner methods. It also retains Rashed's qualification: earlier procedures existed in the school of al-Karaji, particularly al-Samawal. This is development and integration of inherited computation, not the first root known to humanity {cite:kashi-mactutor root-extraction}.

The subject differs from [[invention/kashi-sine-one-degree|the sine of one degree]], with its particular cubic problem, and from [[invention/al-kashi-pi-calculation|circumference calculation]]. Here the question is how to recover a number from one of its powers. The museum's examples are independently written modern explanations, not numbers or table layouts copied from an unexamined manuscript {cite:kashi-mactutor roots-and-sine}.

## What it is

Root extraction reverses raising a number to a power. The positive cube root of eight is two because2³=8; many other inputs do not yield a whole-number answer. Then a numerical procedure needs an approximation and some account of what that approximation guarantees. Iranica describes general root extraction with the square-root procedure as a special case {cite:iranica-kashi calculation}.

This is not automatically a solver for every algebraic equation. Writingxⁿ=A specifies a particular problem; a later comparison with Horner does not turn it into the entire general polynomial algorithm. Our secondary accounts also do not disclose every pen movement, column arrangement or historical precision setting. ## How it works

Here is an independent modern teaching example, not a literal recovery of the historical table. Seek the positive cube root of ten. Since2³=8 and3³=27, it lies between two and three. “Near two” is insufficient if we want a stated accuracy. We compare powers at closer bounds and report an interval rather than an unexplained string of digits.

A modern identity explains the effect of an increment:if a is an estimate and b an increase,(a+b)³=a³+3a²b+3ab²+b³. Keeping just the first term does not account for the change. Doubling a cube's edge also does not merely double its volume. A new decimal digit can be tested using integer powers after scaling; this illustrates the mathematics without attributing our notation to the author.

At three decimal places,2154³=9993948264 and2155³=10007873875. Compare both with10000000000,which is ten multiplied by1000³. Consequently2.154≤the positive cube root of ten<2.155. The interval width is0.001,and the truncated lower value2.154 falls short by less than that. This is not a claim that the lower value is the nearest-rounded answer or that a cube's error equals its root's error.

The bound works because cubing increases on positive magnitudes. We explicitly select the positive branch:an even-power equation with positive input can have two real solutions, while the principal-root convention selects the nonnegative one. These modern distinctions prevent confusion; we do not claim that each was worded identically in al-Kashi's chapter.

## For young readers

If a cube has volume eight cubic units, its edge is two length units because2×2×2=8. Do not mix the volume unit with the edge unit, or assume doubling an edge doubles volume only once. This is a modern exercise about roots, not an artefact we measured or an experiment attributed to al-Kashi.

Now ask about volume ten. Its edge is not exactly two, since two cubed is eight. The interval between2.154and2.155narrows the answer beyond a broad guess.
