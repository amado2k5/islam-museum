## Summary
Al-Battani gathered in one chapter of his Zij a method for computing planetary application quantities — the angular measures of closeness between planets and the Sun, Moon and stars — and wrote a separate epistle of the same title for the vizier Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Muhammad ibn al-Furat, whose title matches chapter LIV of the Zij on the projection of rays {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 28} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum ch. LIV}. The epistle is lost today; the chapter remains as its carrier in the translated Zij {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum}. The museum records the contribution as a documented computational improvement inside the zij tradition, not as the invention of the concept of application itself.

## What it is
In astrological-astronomical usage, an "application" is the approach of one planet to another body in the sky, and the "quantities of application" are the angular distances or arcs that separate them. What al-Battani produced is an organised chapter in the Zij that treats these quantities computationally, paired with a standalone epistle of the same title written at his vizier's request, whose purpose was to verify the correctness of the computed quantities rather than merely to transmit them {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum ch. LIV; p. 28}. The idea rests on an older heritage in the books of judgments, but its form here is a zij-style computation addressed to the reader who wants a correct quantity.

## The world before it
The doctrine of application and its quantities was known before al-Battani in the Greco-Arabic traditions; Ptolemy's Tetrabiblos, as transmitted in the Arabic tradition, mentions application arcs and their quantities for the planets, and Nallino compares al-Battani's treatment in this chapter with Ptolemy's parallel chapter {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum ch. LIV}. In Islamic tradition, zijes were crowded with tables and computational problems, but not every zij set aside a documented chapter for this subject together with a standalone verification epistle. Al-Battani himself had already refined sine computation and interpolation in other chapters of his Zij {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum Arabic pp. 13-17; Latin pp. 9-11}.

## The story
Al-Battani worked at Raqqa in the Abbasid third century AH and was among the greatest astronomers of his age, also a critic {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor biography}. In his Zij he made a chapter dealing with application quantities, and he wanted the subject to receive attention beyond what the general tables gave, so he wrote a separate epistle titled "Verification of the Quantities of Applications" commissioned by the vizier Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Muhammad ibn al-Furat; Nallino notes that the epistle's title is the same as the title of chapter LIV of the Zij on the projection of rays {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 28}. We know neither a day nor a session for writing the epistle, and the epistle itself no longer exists; what remains is the chapter in the Zij and what its editor says about it.

## The maker(s)
Al-Battani — Abu Abdallah Muhammad ibn Jabir ibn Sinan al-Harrani al-Raqqi — a Muslim astronomer of Raqqa, who died in 317 AH/929 CE according to the biographical sources {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor biography}. He is credited with the application-quantities chapter in his Zij and the lost epistle addressed to the vizier; his Muslim identity is documented by his Arabic work and Nallino's argumentative presentation, and his Sunni label is the museum's declared frame rather than a personal madhhab testimony {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu}.

## How it works
The chapter treats a specific computational problem: how to compute the "quantities of application" between a planet and the body whose approach is being measured. The core of the term in application contexts is the size of the arc or angular distance between them, and computing it requires spherical geometry and tables — hence the separate chapter in the Zij and the dedicated verification epistle. We do not possess the epistle's text, but its title and its parallel with Ptolemy's chapter in the Tetrabiblos prove that it concerned computing these quantities with precision rather than merely presenting them {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum ch. LIV; p. 28}. The museum's accompanying image is a modern teaching triangle illustrating the general principle; it does not rebuild a page of the Zij.

## The spread
The Zij carrying the chapter was translated into Latin in the Middle Ages and afterwards, which is what allowed Nallino in the nineteenth century to edit and publish it with his notes {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum} {cite:battani-mactutor reception}. The standalone epistle did not survive, so we do not know whether it was separately copied or translated; what we know is that the chapter remained part of the translated Zij read by medieval astronomers {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum}.

## What it was built on
The subject was built on the inherited doctrine of application from the Greco-Arabic books of judgment, and on the computational tools of the zij that al-Battani himself was refining through his sine tables and interpolation {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum ch. LIV} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum Arabic pp. 13-17}. The epistle itself was built on the Zij, its title being the title of one of its chapters, as Nallino pointed out {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 28}.

## What was built on it
The chapter remained inside the Zij that passed into Latin, becoming part of the text read by medieval astronomers; the standalone epistle was lost before we could find a copy, so we establish no direct descendant for it {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu Kitab al-Zij}.

## Impact on daily life
This chapter had no direct effect on people's daily lives; it was a scientific tool for computing quantities in the books of astrology and astronomy read by specialists. Its value lies in documenting the Zij author's care for computational accuracy to the point of writing a separate epistle to verify it — evidence of a verification culture prevailing in the Abbasid school {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum ch. LIV; p. 28}.

## How it changed history
History is not changed by what changes nothing in later practice, and here we do not claim that al-Battani's chapter created the schools of application computation; but documenting a standalone verification epistle for a partial subject of the Zij is a witness to methodological maturity in writing astronomical tables, and to "verification" being a declared value in this tradition {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 28} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum ch. LIV}.

## Who invented it?
We do not know who invented the concept of application and its quantities; the doctrine is older than al-Battani in the Greco-Arabic astrological traditions, as Nallino's comparison with Ptolemy's chapter shows {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum ch. LIV}. What is documented is that al-Battani organised the subject in a zij chapter and wrote a separate verification epistle at his vizier's request; this is an organised improvement on an existing heritage, not priority for the concept {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 28}.

## Today
Today angular distances between celestial bodies are computed automatically in astronomy and navigation software, and we establish no direct lineage here from al-Battani's chapter to those programs; the chapter remains a historical witness to the precision of ancient astronomical computation {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu Kitab al-Zij} {cite:battani-mactutor reception}. The accompanying image is a general teaching triangle from 2007 that explains the principle and does not represent a page of the Zij {cite:commons-trigonometry-triangle Description}.

## Surviving examples
The direct witness is chapter LIV of the Zij on the projection of rays, as edited and published in Nallino's Latin translation {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum ch. LIV}. The standalone epistle is attested by the bibliographic notices Nallino cites, but its text does not exist {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 28}. The museum displays no original page of the chapter today.

## In culture
This chapter embodies a model of scientific patronage in the Abbasid age: an astronomer devotes a chapter of his Zij and a separate epistle to a single computational subject, at the request of his vizier who was a patron of learning {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 28} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}. It is also an example of how the "astrological" books of astronomy were written in precise computational language, not superficial predictive language.

## For young readers
Planets sometimes appear close together in the sky. Al-Battani gathered in one chapter of his Zij a way to compute the size of such closeness, and wrote a separate epistle to the vizier to verify those quantities; the epistle is lost and the chapter survives in the Zij.
