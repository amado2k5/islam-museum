## Summary

[[person/al-zahrawi|Abu al-Qasim al-Zahrawi]] describes a drill for a stone lodged in the urethra, the urine passage beyond the bladder, in the surgical book of *al-Tasrif*; Abdel-Halim and colleagues render its name Al-Mishaab {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1288}. The encyclopaedia was completed around 390 AH/1000 in the context of his Andalusian medicine associated with [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]], not a manufacturing date for an authenticated surviving object {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133} {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191}. The account concerns reducing a stone rather than removing it whole, not invention of surgery or every kind of stone fragmentation {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1287–1289}. The photograph provides context from a later instrument manuscript, not a confirmed picture of this drill {cite:commons-zahrawi-instruments metadata}.

## What it is

The translated text describes a metal part with a sharp triangular end and a wooden handle, worked by hand rather than a motor, in chapter 60 on extracting stones {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1284, 1288}. It must be distinguished from al-kalaleeb, the bladder-stone grasping and crushing forceps discussed earlier in the study {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1286–1288}. A shared aim of fragmentation does not make these the same instrument: their location and mechanism differ {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1286–1289}.

## The world before it

Urinary-stone treatment did not begin with al-Zahrawi: the study compares ancient and Greco-Roman traditions, followed by [[person/al-razi-abu-bakr|al-Razi's]] treatments {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1284–1287}. These include the difficulty of removing a large body through a limited opening, and the scoops, forceps and size-reduction methods historians discuss {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1286–1288}. This is context for the problem, not evidence that all those predecessors used this urethral drill; a bladder procedure cannot be transferred to the urethra simply because both involve a stone {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1287–1289}.

## The story

The witness is a passage in a medical book, not an object bearing a maker's signature and manufacturing date; the 2003 study translates a 1993 Riyadh Arabic edition {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1284}. In the translated passage, the author reports trying the method and avoiding incision in some cases, then gives an alternative when it is infeasible {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1288–1289}. The historians take this as evidence of experience, but it supplies no patient names, trial count or follow-up: the author's reported experience remains distinct from a modern clinical series {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1288–1289}.

Nor does TDV's biography establish a princely commission for this drill: early biographical information is sparse, and court connections belong to later testimony {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191}. No patron, workshop or invention scene should be supplied where the evidence gives none {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191}.

## The maker(s)

Al-Zahrawi was an Andalusian physician and surgeon whose 30-book encyclopaedia concludes with surgery and instrument illustrations; his work extends beyond this one tool {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133}. His documented role here is describer, with the transmitted report of experience, while the modern historians regard the device as an improvement in the treatment tradition {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1288–1289}.

The medical paper explicitly calls the named author a Muslim surgeon, the documented identity basis rather than his name or country {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1283}. Sunni is the museum's explicit default for this Andalusian context when no other tradition is named, not a personal legal-school declaration supplied by the biography {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191}.

## How it works

The mechanical idea is to concentrate the hard tip's action on a stone with manual rotation, making a hole that weakens its bulk rather than relying on removal of the intact body {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1288}. The text connects a metal tip with a wooden handle, not an electric motor or imaging system {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1288}. This is a historical explanation of principle, not instructions for using an instrument on a person.

The study glosses Foulaz with an English expression suggesting stainless steel; this record does not adopt that modern metallurgical label or infer an untested alloy composition {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1288}. Nor does it invent anatomical sections or dimensions: the edge, handle and shape are textual information, not measurements of an authenticated surviving object {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1288}.

## The spread

Documented circulation is clearer for the book than for instrument numbers: TDV identifies Gerard of Cremona's 12th-century Latin translation of the surgical book and subsequent copies and editions {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133}. It distinguishes these from translations of pharmaceutical and theoretical sections; every translation of part of *al-Tasrif* is not proof of transmission of this drill {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133}.

It also describes Sabuncuoglu's 1465 Turkish version as an adaptation with additions, not an identical copy {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133}. These witnesses provide neither a count of makers or patients nor a map establishing that every reader manufactured every instrument {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133}.

## What it was built on

The commentary situates al-Zahrawi after earlier stone surgery and al-Razi, distinguishing extraction, removal of portions and drilling {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1286–1289}. These are related problems, not one technique: the browser's chain represents intellectual context, not a proven sequence of objects passed from hand to hand {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1286–1289}. The passage establishes neither a named smith nor a particular prototype copied for the drill {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1288}.

## What was built on it

The 2003 authors compare the idea with 19th-century European drills and instruments, including Civiale's, regarding them as developments of drilling and fragmentation {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1289}. That is those historians' interpretation; this record has not independently examined every book or object in their comparison {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1289}. Similarity of principle alone is not a transmission document, nor are later bladder instruments automatically identical to the described urethral drill {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1288–1289}.

## Impact on daily life

The human problem in the passage is obstructed urine flow, and the stated aim is avoiding incision when fragmentation is feasible {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1288}. The move to an alternative when an obstacle prevents it shows a limit, not a promise of cure in every case {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1288–1289}. The museum does not turn this into a success rate, mortality reduction or treatment cost: those measurements are not supplied by the passage {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1288–1289}.

## How it changed history

The book preserved a tool's relationship to its medical problem, with descriptions and drawings that could travel with the text; translations and editions show enduring interest in the surgical book {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133}. The modern study reads the passage as evidence of initiative and experience, not merely a compilation of names {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1288–1289}. This concerns documenting and teaching knowledge, a narrower claim than saying one device alone created all modern urological surgery {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1289}.

## Who invented it?

The source supports attributing this specific description to al-Zahrawi, and the historians call the instrument an original contribution; their abstract's “founder of lithotripsy” language is broader than the question this record settles {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1283, 1288–1289}. The chapter distinguishes instruments and locations, and the commentary itself discusses older accounts of bladder-stone fragmentation {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1287–1289}. The museum therefore publishes a described improvement without adjudicating priority for every fragmentation technique across civilizations {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1287–1289}.

## Today

We encounter this tool through surgical history and books, not a current treatment prescription; comparisons with later drills do not constitute present-day clinical approval {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1288–1289}. The record also distinguishes a surviving text from an original metal object: its sources identify no authenticated drill made by al-Zahrawi for this purpose {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1284, 1288–1289}.

## In culture

The biography lists European forms of the author's name, including Albucasis, associated with his book's reception {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191}. The sources read establish no particular film or novel appearance for this drill; none is invented here {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191} {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1288–1289}.

## For young readers

The stone here is a small hard lump stuck in the urine passage; al-Zahrawi described a tool intended to make it smaller rather than remove it whole {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1288}. We study this in an old book and its drawings, not as an experiment to perform on someone: historical medical knowledge, not treatment advice.
