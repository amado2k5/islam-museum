## Summary
"Al-Manhal al-adhb al-zulal fi hall al-taqwim wa-ru'yat al-hilal" is a short treatise by Shihab al-Din Ibn al-Majdi, an Egyptian astronomer and timekeeper in Cairo, dealing with two practical problems: finding the beginnings of the Arab months by calculation, and the methods of sighting the crescent. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

The treatise is not a discovery of a new theory of calendar or sighting; the subject is older than Ibn al-Majdi. His documented contribution is gathering in one treatise what timekeepers knew about computing months and sighting the crescent, in a concise form fit for the mosque timekeeper who needs a quick answer, not a long dissertation. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33} {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

Its context suggests it was written in the early ninth century AH, around the start of the fifteenth century CE, within Ibn al-Majdi's own lifetime. His writings kept being read and commented on in Egypt into the late nineteenth century. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33} {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

## What it is
This record describes a "method": gathering a timekeeper's calendrical knowledge into a short treatise fit for daily use. The treatise is not a device or an independent table, but a teaching book linking an astronomical computation to a visual observation. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

The two problems it treats are exactly the two on which the Islamic lunar calendar rests: when does the month begin, and is the crescent seen? {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33} {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

"Hall al-taqwim" in the title means finding solutions to the problem of fixing the months; "ru'yat al-hilal" means the methods by which the timekeeper searched for the crescent after sunset. The treatise joins the two because they are two faces of one question: the start of the lunar month. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

## The world before it
Before Ibn al-Majdi, muwaqqit science was established: timekeepers in mosques fixed the hours, astronomers wrote on eclipses and the visibility of bodies, and schools such as Ibn al-Shatir's in Damascus and al-Mardini's in Egypt transmitted this knowledge generation after generation. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

Ibn al-Majdi himself learned miqat under Jamal al-Din al-Mardini, who was in turn a pupil of Ibn al-Shatir of Damascus, so he entered the science through an ancient teaching chain, not a vacuum. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

The problem itself - when the lunar month begins and when the crescent is seen - was an institutional daily question: the Hijri calendar needs each month's start so people know their seasons and worships, and crescent sighting is a condition in fixing the beginning for many jurists. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

## The story
Shihab al-Din Ahmad ibn Rajab ibn Taybugha al-Majdi al-Qurazi al-Shafi'i was born in Cairo around 767 AH/1366 CE, raised there on a traditional religious education in Quran, hadith, fiqh and Arabic, then mastered arithmetic, geometry, the algebra of inheritance, theoretical astronomy and miqat. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography} {cite:tdv-ibnul-mecdi treatise list}

He held the post of timekeeper at al-Azhar Mosque and head of teachers at the Jannibakiyya madrasa, so his daily work was fixing the hours in Egypt's greatest mosque. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

The Turkish encyclopaedia entry records that he was probably of Turkish origin through his grandfather Taybugha, who gave him his name, that he read Shafi'i fiqh under Salih ibn Umar al-Bulqini and Ibn al-Mulaqqin, and that his lessons on al-Mawardi's al-Hawi al-kabir drew religious scholars of different schools. {cite:tdv-ibnul-mecdi}

In this post he wrote his many astronomical treatises — the source counts more than thirty works in the field — among them writings on the astrolabe, quadrants and arcs, auxiliary tables easing the computation of planetary positions that Kennedy and King investigated, and the treatise "al-Manhal al-adhb al-zulal" that is our subject. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33} {cite:tdv-ibnul-mecdi}

The treatise is brief: it does not prolong itself in theoretical foundations but goes straight to "finding the beginnings of the Arab months by calculation and the methods of crescent sighting" - the didactic manner that marked his books, for he was a teacher esteemed by his pupils and successors in Cairo astronomy, described by the source as one of the major Egyptian astronomers of the first half of the fifteenth century CE. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33} {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

## The maker(s)
Ibn al-Majdi is "Ahmad ibn Rajab ibn Taybugha al-Majdi al-Qurazi al-Shafi'i", an astronomer and polymath of Cairo. His role here is "improver" and "describer": he gathered miqat knowledge into a treatise; he did not invent the problem of sighting or the calendar. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

His Muslim identity is strongly documented: his name carries "al-Shafi'i", his education was Islamic religious training in fiqh, hadith and Quran, his work was timekeeping at al-Azhar mosque - all documented evidence, not inference from a name. {cite:tdv-ibnul-mecdi treatise list} {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

He was an exception among the astronomers of his Mamluk age: most avoided astrology because of their ties to religious institutions, yet he wrote on astrology too - showing he was a broad scholar, not only a timekeeper. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

## How it works
The treatise works on two parallel paths. The computational path: the reader learns to find month beginnings by calculation - locating the Sun-Moon relationship and conditions that make a month begin on a given day, using the astronomical tables and computational methods known in miqat. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

The observational path: the reader learns the methods of crescent sighting - the conditions that make the crescent visible after sunset: its altitude, thickness, duration above the horizon, and weather factors - knowledge the muwaqqit tradition passed down generation after generation. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33} {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

The treatise does not give a single table but "solutions" to the calendar problem: it gathers different methods so the timekeeper can choose what suits his place and conditions - a practical manner matching the function of one who fixes a mosque's hours every day. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

## The spread
The treatise did not spread far geographically; its spread was temporal and readership-based: Ibn al-Majdi's writings kept being read and commented on in Egypt into the late nineteenth century, some four and a half centuries after his death. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

His pupils carried his sciences: the biographical entry records that virtually all his younger contemporaries and successors in Cairo astronomy were his pupils at one time or another, so miqat, sundials and quadrants passed to later generations of Egyptian astronomers. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

The Turkish encyclopaedia adds that his zijes - not only this treatise - were widely used in Egypt into the nineteenth century, to which later astronomers added supplements, evidence that his learning stayed alive in Egyptian muwaqqit practice centuries after his death. {cite:tdv-ibnul-mecdi} {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

His writings on time computation and auxiliary tables became a reference in Egyptian muwaqqit practice, entering the teaching of muwaqqit students in schools and mosques until the tradition neared its end. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

## What it was built on
The treatise rests on three layers: first, the miqat teaching he received from Jamal al-Din al-Mardini, pupil of Ibn al-Shatir - a scientific lineage from Damascus to Cairo. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

Second, the astronomical books he read: zijes, calendars, and works on the astrolabe and quadrants by his predecessors in the Damascus and Cairo schools. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography} {cite:tdv-ibnul-mecdi}

Third, institutional practice: his daily work as a timekeeper at al-Azhar made him know what a muwaqqit actually needs, so he framed his treatise for speed and accuracy, not philosophy. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

## What was built on it
Built directly on it: Ibn al-Majdi's pupils who carried his sciences to later Cairo astronomy, and his books whose readers and commentators persisted in Egypt into the late nineteenth century. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

Built on it also: the continuity of the "muwaqqit method" - the manner of joining computation to observation in one practical treatise that remained a model for later timekeeping books in Egypt and Syria. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

Alongside our treatise, his auxiliary tables al-Durr al-yatim, which ease the computation of planetary positions and were investigated by Kennedy and King, stayed popular in Egypt into the nineteenth century and inspired later, more extensive sets built on the parameters of his zij - though they are not our treatise's subject. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

## Impact on daily life
Its effect was immediate and daily: every mosque in Cairo needed someone to fix prayer times, and every Muslim needed to know when his month began. A short treatise like "al-Manhal" answered both questions at once. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33} {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

The lunar-month calendar is no luxury but organises the seasons of worship: Ramadan, Hajj and the festivals are all bound to month beginnings, so any error in fixing them corrupts worship. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

Thus Ibn al-Majdi's treatise was a small but critical institutional tool: it put in the timekeeper's hand what he needed to perform his religious function accurately. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

The biographical encyclopedia confirms that crescent visibility is a matter of prime importance to Muslim religious practice because the Islamic calendar is lunar, which makes muwaqqit treatises on the subject institutions rather than scientific hobbies. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

## How it changed history
The treatise did not change theoretical astronomy; what it changed was "the teaching of muwaqqit": it proved that timekeeping science could be condensed into one practical treatise fit for teaching and daily use, instead of remaining in massive books only specialists could read. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33} {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

It is also an example of the "institutional scholar": an astronomer working in a mosque, not an observatory, so his writings yield applications rather than theories - a pattern that would persist in Egypt into the nineteenth century. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

In the history of the Islamic calendar, the treatise is recorded among the books that documented muwaqqit practice in fixing the months, part of the muwaqqit treatise tradition of the Damascus and Cairo schools. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33} {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

## Who invented it?
The precise answer: not an "inventor" but an "author-describer". Ibn al-Majdi did not invent month computation or crescent sighting; muwaqqits had done that for centuries before him. What he did was gather these practices into one short treatise bearing his name, in the Mamluk age. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33} {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

That is why his attribution is recorded as "improvement", not "original": the real contribution is the methodical summary and the joining of computation and observation in a single text - something not every timekeeper did with the same clarity. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

His Muslim identity is firmly fixed: Shafi'i by name, religiously educated, a mosque timekeeper at al-Azhar, with Arabic sources needing no further proof. {cite:tdv-ibnul-mecdi treatise list} {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

## Today
The treatise is not used practically today; modern astronomical tables and published crescent-visibility criteria have replaced it. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

But it witnesses the old timekeeper's method: how computation was joined to observation, and how the question "when does the month begin?" was answered before computers existed. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

Reading it today is useful to historians of muwaqqit and to teachers of Islamic history who want to understand how Muslims managed their calendar before the printed one. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

## Surviving examples
The treatise survives in manuscripts: the Azhar manuscript described by the Bibliotheca Alexandrina catalogue, which enabled the current description of it. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

No independent printed edition of it is known in the sources read; our knowledge of it comes from manuscript catalogues and descriptions, not a critical edition. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33} {cite:tdv-ibnul-mecdi treatise list}

Its account is carried in Ibn al-Majdi's work lists in both the Turkish encyclopedia and the Shadharat al-Dhahab entry, confirming its continued presence in bibliographic tradition. {cite:tdv-ibnul-mecdi treatise list} {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 268}

## In culture
"Al-Manhal" did not become a popular icon, but it is an icon in the history of muwaqqit: in catalogues and literature of timekeeping books its name appears alongside the treatises of his predecessors in the Damascus and Cairo schools. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33} {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

Its very name "al-Manhal al-adhb al-zulal" means "the sweet clear spring" - a beautiful rhetorical name meaning the book is a sweet source for one working on the problem, not a dry technical label. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

In Ibn al-Majdi's biography, the treatise is cited with his other works as evidence that he "played an important role as a didactic author": he wrote for students, not only specialists - indeed his former pupil Sibt al-Maridini praised his treatise on sexagesimal arithmetic, fundamental to astronomers' work, as the only satisfactory treatment he knew. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography} {cite:tdv-ibnul-mecdi}

## For young readers
Imagine a Muslim scientist named Ibn al-Majdi whose job was to tell people when each lunar month begins. He wrote a small treatise called "al-Manhal al-adhb al-zulal" teaching how to compute a month's start by calculation and how to search for the small crescent after sunset. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}

He was not discovering new astronomy; he gathered what timekeepers knew into one place so others could use it. He was like a good teacher: he did not invent the lesson but arranged it in a way the pupil could understand. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography}

That is why his treatise kept being read in Egypt into the late nineteenth century: people needed someone who could tell them when their month began. {cite:islamsci-ibn-al-majdi-bea biography} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 33}
