## Summary

Four visible circles do not mean only four letters. [[person/al-jazari-engineer|Al-Jazari]] describes a chest whose shared closure becomes ready to open through twelve letter settings, three in each circular group {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.199–201}. This is CategoryVI chapter3, not a claim to invent every coded lock. The author explicitly acknowledges earlier makers of two-, four- and six-letter locks {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.199}. This entry brings together the assembly, drawing limitations and a separate teaching calculation, not a security certification. More letters do not make a chest impossible to open by means outside its intended operation.

## What it is

A composite chest lock has four groups of nested components in its lid and a shared plate underneath; the circles carry sixteen selected letters from the twenty-eight-letter Arabic alphabet {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.199–201}. Each group has three readable settings. Counting one visible handle as one choice would omit the components beneath it. The pieces connect a letter position at the surface to a position inside, combining their conditions in one closure {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.200–201}.

The entry covers the complete chest assembly, not the next chapter's door bolts or a separate invention for every disc and lug {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.201–202}. Its purpose is to explain the relation between knowing a setting and moving a closure, not to supply a fabrication drawing or instructions for interfering with someone else's lock.

## How it works

Each group contains nested circles and shafts with three readable settings: one pointer against the lid's circle, another against a circle above it, and a third against a smaller circle {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.199–201}. The visitor need not memorize component names to understand that a surface setting relates to a corresponding internal piece. Three indications per group explain the chapter's twelve-letter title.

Beneath the lid these settings connect to three discs with peripheral notches. Their alignment permits movement of the matching plate element, with the condition repeated across all four groups {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.200–201,268}. Think of “this, and this, and this too”: one correct group is insufficient if the others remain unmatched. That is a teaching comparison, not identification as a computer or electrical circuit.

The closure is shared, not four separate chests. A divided plate and central handle connect the settings to retaining or releasing the chest's latch {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.201,268}. Function is explained without component dimensions or disassembly steps. Hill's discussion of changing the selected letters concerns owner-controlled maintenance, not an experiment proposed on existing locks {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.269}.

Mathematically, assuming sixteen independent choices per position gives4096 arrangements for three positions and16^12 for twelve. This is a modern teaching derivation from the described count, not a number quoted from the author or a measurement of examined hardware {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.199,201,269}. No guessing time or protection guarantee follows. Material quality, assembly and closure structure are separate questions; Hill himself notes a physical-protection limitation {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.269}.

## What it was built on

The description builds on earlier letter locks acknowledged by the author and on connecting a readable letter with an internal rotating component's position {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.199–201}. ## How it changed history

Its historical value lies in detailing how many conditions combine in one closure, and in showing that written mechanical devices extend beyond water, timekeeping and moving displays {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.199–201}. It also presents an author explaining his work within a craft whose predecessors he acknowledges, rather than a solitary hero creating the field from nothing {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.199}.

Critical reading makes material knowledge open to questions. Hill identifies omitted or disproportionate components and uses the Dublin witness in some comparisons {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.268}. ## For young readers

On paper only, draw four groups of three spaces: twelve in total, explaining the chapter rather than reducing it to four spaces {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.199,201}. Give each space two simple game symbols, circle or square. Three spaces make eight arrangements; four such groups make4096. This is a modern two-symbol counting game, not the lock's sixteen letters or its historical total.

Choose a drawn pattern, marking each group that matches it. The story is complete only when all four agree. Change one symbol to distinguish one condition from their conjunction, without claiming to model metal friction or security. No real locks, tools, keys or force, and no experimenting with other people's property: paper is enough.
