## Summary
The saqiya — the animal-driven chain-of-pots wheel — is pre-Islamic, but Islamic engineers turned it into a machine family: al-Jazari's composite ox-plus-water-wheel design, the first known crank in a saqiya, and the earliest implied minimising of intermittence, with the wheel still watering Egypt, India and Spain centuries later {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en} {cite:hill-engineering-ehas-1998}.

## What it is
An endless chain of pots on a vertical wheel over a well, driven through right-angle gearing by an animal walking a circle on the surface: the chain dips, fills, climbs and empties — ten to twenty metres of lift where the shaduf manages three {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en}.

## The world before it
The geared waterwheel is ancient: a Ptolemaic tomb painting of the second century BC shows the saqiya already developed to the point that modern Egyptian devices are virtually identical, and the Indian araghatta of the Panchatantra is the eastern candidate for its origin {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en}.

## The story
The Islamic chapter is engineering refinement: at the Artuqid court al-Jazari designed a composite machine — an ox pacing the roof of an upper reservoir turns the saqiya while water released below falls onto scoop pallets — and another of his machines carries the first known crank on a saqiya, with the drive arranged to smooth the stream's intermittence {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en} {cite:needham-scc-v4p2-1965}.

## The maker(s)
The type has no inventor this side of antiquity; the improvers are named engineers — al-Jazari at Amid, and three centuries later Taqi al-Din, whose al-Turuq al-saniyya opens with two animal-driven water-raising machines in the same line {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en} {cite:wikipedia-taqi-al-din-en}.

## How it works
The animal circles a horizontal cogged wheel; its teeth mesh a vertical lantern pinion on the well-wheel's axle; the axle turns, the pot chain rotates, each vessel rising full to tip into the channel — al-Jazari adds a second power input, falling water, and a crank to keep the drive smooth {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en} {cite:hill-engineering-ehas-1998}.

## The spread
From Upper Mesopotamia the improved saqiya ran through the medieval Islamic world — Damascus since the thirteenth century — into Egypt, al-Andalus and the Maghreb, and east toward India, where the "Persian wheel" name recorded the same machine {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en}.

## What it was built on
The Ptolemaic geared wheel and the shaduf it replaced, the Indian araghatta possibility, and the noria — the sister machine that substitutes river flow for the animal {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en}.

## What was built on it
The Andalusi irrigation tradition itself: Spain's aceña wheels and acequia channels carry the saqiya's name and mechanism into Spanish fields; motor-driven versions turn the same gearing today {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en}.

## Impact on daily life
Hundreds of thousands of wheels watered the Nile valley and delta: gardens, bathhouses like the royal one at Qusayr Amra, city supplies — the daily sound of creaking wood and falling water was the saqiya's {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en}.

## How it changed history
It is the clearest case of Islamic engineering perfecting an inherited machine — gear train, dual power, crank, flow continuity — and handing it on so completely that the Spanish word for an irrigation canal is still the Arabic one {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en} {cite:hill-engineering-ehas-1998}.

## Who invented it?
No one in the Islamic period — the saqiya is inherited technology. The record's makers are its improvers, al-Jazari first among them {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en} {cite:jazari-hill-1974}.

## Today
Electric pumps are replacing it, but animal saqiyas still work in Egypt, India and Spain, and the word survives in Spain's water law and landscape {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en}.

## Surviving examples
Working wheels in the Nile valley and the Iberian countryside; al-Jazari's designs in his manuscript's miniatures; the Damascus saqiyas documented in use since the thirteenth century {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en}.

## In culture
The wheel entered the language itself: saqiya for the machine, acequia for the Spanish canal, and in Morocco the saqqaya as the public fountain {cite:wikipedia-saqiyah-en}.

## For young readers
Imagine an ox walking in a circle, turning big gears, and with them a long chain of pots dipping into a well and climbing back full — that is how Muslim farmers watered their fields for centuries with a tireless machine.
