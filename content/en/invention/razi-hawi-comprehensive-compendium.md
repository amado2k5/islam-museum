## Summary

Al-Hawi is the largest medical-gathering project attributed to Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi: an encyclopedia in which he gathered what he saw in the whole of medicine — diseases, causes, drugs, treatments — together with material on earlier authorities and their transmitted sayings {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}. This is a comprehensive gathering project, not a first scientific invention; it is the transformation of scattered knowledge into a single referable work {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}. It is read in the Dar al-Kutub al-Ilmiyya edition on scan 3, where the author's preface describing the book's scope appears {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}.

## What it is

Al-Hawi — also called Kitab al-Hawi fi al-Tibb, the Book of the Comprehensive — is a massive work in medicine that al-Razi composed by gathering what he found of medical material in his earlier writings and sources {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}. In its preface he says the book gathers the diseases of the human body and their treatment together with the sayings of earlier authorities {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}. This scope makes al-Hawi an encyclopedia in the full sense: not a treatise on a single subject nor an epitome of one book, but a systematic gathering of all the medical knowledge al-Razi saw {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya pp.1–3}. The work appears in Ibn al-Nadim's Fihrist among al-Razi's books {cite:isaw-nlm-hawi-1094 fol.317v–318r}, and a Baghdad manuscript dated 30 November 1094 survives from it {cite:isaw-nlm-hawi-1094 fol.317v–318r}.

## The world before it

Before al-Hawi, medical books were individual or limited encyclopaedias: treatises on a single subject, epitomes, or translations of Greek and Syriac medicine into Arabic {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya pp.1–3}. Al-Razi himself had already written many medical treatises before al-Hawi — including a book on the diagnosis of smallpox — so al-Hawi was the harvest of that production, not its beginning {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}. It is therefore not credited with inventing smallpox medicine nor inventing any single drug that appears in the book; only the system of gathering is attributed to it {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}.

## The story

Al-Hawi's place in al-Razi's biography is a gathering place: after decades of scattered medical composition, he collected his work into one comprehensive book {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}. The preface gives no precise date for beginning or finishing, and the Fihrist offers only a broad context — the eighth/ninth century AH — because the exact completion is unsettled {cite:isaw-nlm-hawi-1094 fol.317v–318r}. The pages read are the Dar al-Kutub al-Ilmiyya edition, and scan 3 opens the preface describing the book's scope {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}. We do not claim al-Razi wrote al-Hawi in a single sitting or that it was printed exactly as he wrote it; we establish that the text reached us with this scope {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya pp.1–3}.

## The maker(s)

**The declared Sunni frame:** the person record presents a debated religious identity in Sunni-first order; Britannica records conflicting readings about his religious identity without verdict {cite:britannica-al-razi opening}, and claims that he was a Shi'i, an atheist or a Christian circulate but remain unsettled {cite:sep-abu-bakr-al-razi} {cite:tdv-razi-ebu-bekir}. **The transmitted contrary reading:** some modern writers describe al-Razi as a pioneer of experimental medicine or place his birth in Tabaristan rather than Persia; these readings are presented from the sources actually read {cite:sep-abu-bakr-al-razi} {cite:tdv-razi-ebu-bekir}. The museum infers neither al-Razi's faith from his parents nor his school denomination from a patron or a teacher, and keeps the earlier attributed affiliations in the maker's record {cite:britannica-al-razi opening}.

Attribution here is to al-Razi as the author of the text, not to whoever translated or commented on this presentation; al-Hawi is an independent work in the Arabic-Islamic heritage {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}. This attribution does not imply al-Razi invented everything in the book; the gathering is comprehensive and the materials are older than him {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}.

## How it works

**First: the declared scope.** The preface says the book gathers the diseases of the human body and their treatment together with the sayings of earlier authorities {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}. This scope is the whole programme: not a treatise on one subject but a comprehensive gathering of medicine {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya pp.1–3}.

**Second: gathering the materials.** Al-Hawi contains materials from al-Razi's earlier writings and from the books of earlier authorities — Greek, Syriac, Indian and Arabic — in one system {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya pp.1–3}. Every material enters the book as transmitted medical knowledge, not as a claim of new invention {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}.

**Third: ordering the chapters.** The book arranges its materials into chapters and sections on medical topics — from anatomy to diseases to drugs to surgery — so the encyclopedia becomes referable rather than a mere storehouse of texts {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya pp.1–3}. This ordering is the method in al-Hawi: a conscious compositional decision that makes knowledge searchable {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}.

**Fourth: preserving the sayings.** Al-Hawi keeps the sayings of earlier authorities attributed to them — as the author says — so earlier opinions remain visible inside the new text {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}. This preservation makes al-Hawi a precious secondary source for studying ancient medicine, not only a reference for contemporary reading {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya pp.1–3}.

## The spread

Al-Hawi appears in Ibn al-Nadim's Fihrist among al-Razi's books {cite:isaw-nlm-hawi-1094 fol.317v–318r}, and a Baghdad manuscript dated 30 November 1094 survives from it {cite:isaw-nlm-hawi-1094 fol.317v–318r}. A book's spread does not prove that every reader applied it or recorded its results; the specific evidence here is that the work survives in the Fihrist and in a dated manuscript, not a complete map of al-Razi-medicine citation practice across the centuries {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya pp.1–3}. We therefore do not fill the gaps of reception with an invented chain of institutions or pupils {cite:sep-abu-bakr-al-razi}.

## What it was built on

Al-Hawi builds on al-Razi's earlier medical production — his treatises on specific diseases — and on the Greek-Syriac-Indian medical heritage translated into Arabic {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya pp.1–3}. Every material in it has an older origin; al-Hawi is the point of gathering, not the origin of all knowledge {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}. Read outside this frame it loses its historical meaning {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya pp.1–3}.

## What was built on it

Modern sources discuss that al-Hawi remained a medical reference in the Islamic world for centuries, but that does not prove it became a universally followed protocol {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. The similarity of medical-documentation questions to modern references does not prove direct descent from this work {cite:sep-abu-bakr-al-razi}. For the same context, the question of similar problems is not proof of lineage {cite:britannica-al-razi opening}.

## Impact on daily life

The directly demonstrable impact is organisational: it made the scattered medical knowledge of many books into a single referable work {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}. This ordering can make the reader more aware that medicine is an organised field of knowledge, but it is not by itself evidence of more accurate diagnosis in any given population; the book offers no accuracy figures or clinical results attributed to its application {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya pp.1–3}. The daily contribution described is the organisation of questioning, not a diagnostic guarantee {cite:sep-abu-bakr-al-razi}.

## How it changed history

Al-Hawi shows that gathering medical knowledge is part of the history of written medicine: al-Razi did not content himself with a treatise on one disease, but arranged a comprehensive work that makes every medical material searchable in one place {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}. This ordering inside the Islamic medical context that lasted centuries gives it real historical weight without turning medical history into a race for the title of first to gather knowledge {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. The precise estimate keeps the medical heritage it used and the distance between a reasonable historical argument and verification tools it does not describe {cite:britannica-al-razi opening}.

## Who invented it?

What is proven is that al-Razi presented this work in al-Hawi, not that a survey of every earlier text proves he was the first person to gather medical knowledge into one reference {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya pp.1–3} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. The record therefore treats the contribution as systematic and an improvement inside an older heritage, not as the sole origin of all medical documentation {cite:sep-abu-bakr-al-razi}. This is not the invention of smallpox medicine nor the first system of disease names; it is material al-Razi read and gathered in an encyclopaedic order {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}.

## Today

Visitors can read al-Hawi as a history of the idea that medical knowledge can be gathered into one reference, while remembering that the materials here are not instructions for diagnosing self or others {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya pp.1–3}. ## In culture

The idea that medical knowledge can be gathered into one book can tempt the reader into imagining a direct ancestor of modern references, while each chapter's explanation reveals historical meanings broader than or different from that analogy {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya pp.1–3}. Medical-documentation questions can be appreciated without hiding the frame in which they were phrased {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. History here is an encounter with a document, not a modern image projected onto its author {cite:britannica-al-razi opening}.

## For young readers

**A modern museum example, not an experiment from al-Hawi:** imagine you have a box holding thousands of papers about disease and treatment, and every paper comes from a different book. Al-Razi wanted to put them all in one book that would be easy to search; he gathered them and arranged them into chapters and sections. It was a huge project, but it was an ordering of knowledge, not a medical prescription. We do not use his method today, and we do not say it was right or wrong by our standards {cite:hawi-razi-dar-kutub-ilmiyya p.3}.
