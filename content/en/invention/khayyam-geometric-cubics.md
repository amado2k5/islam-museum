## Summary

[[person/umar-khayyam|Umar Khayyam]] did not start algebra from nothing or provide the general radical formula now associated with cubic equations: his contribution here is a systematic geometric treatment of fourteen irreducible cubic forms under positive-coefficient conventions {cite:sep-umar-khayyam sec.4.1}. A problem becomes a choice of two curves, construction of their intersection, and proof that a length obtained from it meets the requirement {cite:khayyam-woepcke-1851 pp.32–34}. We present a methodological improvement with predecessors and limits, not a competition that makes everyone before and after him disappear.

## What it is

A cubic problem contains the cube of an unknown, perhaps alongside its square, a linear term or a known number; Khayyam distinguishes forms according to the terms present and the side on which they appear, working with positive magnitudes {cite:sep-umar-khayyam sec.4.1}. Fourteen forms do not mean fourteen unrelated subjects: the classification fits geometric algebra's language and conventions, rather than a modern list of every possible sign combination {cite:sep-umar-khayyam sec.4.1}.

The relevant curves include circles, parabolas and hyperbolas, with the intersection of two determining a required length; the circle is not decoration beside an equation solved independently {cite:sep-umar-khayyam sec.4.1}. A geometric solution is a construction with proof, whereas a radical solution supplies an algebraic expression involving operations such as square and cube roots. Sharing a numerical answer does not make these the same kind of solution.

## The world before it

Geometric treatments of quadratics belonged to older traditions; Stanford places Khayyam after Greek and Babylonian work and after al-Khwarizmi and Thabit ibn Qurra, so connecting an unknown magnitude with a geometric figure was not his solitary invention {cite:sep-umar-khayyam sec.4.1}. Thabit belongs in the predecessor story: we neither reclassify him as a Muslim maker to meet the browser's criteria nor erase him from the history.

For cubics, the entry names al-Mahani's reduction of an Archimedean problem to an equation, al-Khazin's conic solution of a particular case, and al-Layth's non-exhaustive treatment; it reports that Khayyam himself knew earlier solutions of four cases {cite:sep-umar-khayyam sec.4.1}. The difference between a particular case and a wider classification identifies the contribution, rather than a claim that nobody had previously encountered a cubic. These names do not establish a personal teacher-pupil chain.

## How it works

Take one case from the French translation, in which a cube and linear terms equal a number, and explain it in modern notation, not letters claimed to be Khayyam's own {cite:khayyam-woepcke-1851 pp.32–34}. Let `x³ + a²x = b`, with positive `a` and `b`. Choose the parabola `x² = ay` and a circle with horizontal diameter `b/a²`, written `y² = x(b/a² − x)`. This is a teaching rewrite of one intersection case, not a recipe for all fourteen forms {cite:khayyam-woepcke-1851 pp.32–34} {cite:commons-khayyam-cubic-kuiper description}.

Substituting for `y` gives `x⁴/a² = xb/a² − x²`, hence `x(x³ + a²x − b) = 0`. Care is essential: the origin lies on both curves but does not solve the original cubic when `b` is positive. We choose the positive, nonzero intersection; dividing out `x` then shows that its horizontal coordinate meets the requirement. This is a mathematical condition, not an optional visual detail.

For a modern example chosen here for teaching, not numbers claimed from Khayyam's text, set `a=2`, `b=16`: the cubic is `x³+4x=16`, the parabola `y=x²/2`, and the circle has center `(2,0)` and radius2. Both pass through `(2,2)`, giving the horizontal length `x=2`; verification is direct: `8+8=16`. The translated text instead proves the result with lengths and proportions: page33 names the parabola, semicircle and perpendicular projections, not this modern numerical example {cite:khayyam-woepcke-1851 pp.32–34}.

This example does not prove that Khayyam's treatment encompassed everything now known about roots: MacTutor and Stanford explain limits in his handling of multiple positive solutions, and positive geometric lengths are not a theory of complex numbers {cite:khayyam-mactutor algebra} {cite:sep-umar-khayyam sec.4.1}. We preserve both the construction's achievement and its limits rather than sacrificing either to an easier story.

## For young readers
