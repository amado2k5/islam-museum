## Summary

[[person/al-razi-abu-bakr|Abu Bakr al-Razi]]'s *Book of Secrets* poses a practical problem: how can names of materials, instruments and activities become retrievable knowledge? Its opening announces materials, then instruments, then operations; earthy materials receive six nested classes {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 p.2}. The contribution is an organized handbook and classification, not invention of matter or all chemistry. Ferrario reads it as a practical guide with a distinctive organizing principle, not merely a long list of names {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 pp.27–29}.

## What it is

This is an alchemical book, belonging to an older art whose aims and explanations do not coincide with present chemistry; Ferrario examines its ingredients, apparatus and operations in that setting {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 pp.24–29}. Valuing knowledge organization does not validate every explanation or certify successful transmutation. We distinguish what a text says, how its author arranges it, and whether its contents are correct by current knowledge. Answering the second question does not settle the third.

Here we read Arabic opening pages1–3 in the Tehran1964 volume, not an autograph or an independent collation of every manuscript {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 pp.1–3}. Despite its usefulness in making the text accessible, Ferrario warns that this edition cannot be considered a reliable critical restitution {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 p.25}. Uncertain readings and differences between lists therefore remain visible rather than being silently corrected into a modern textbook.

## The world before it

Thinking about substances did not begin with this work: the preface names the author's earlier books and mentions Jabir ibn Hayyan {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 p.1}. A mention witnesses the text's claimed reference point, not, by itself, a personal meeting or apprenticeship or authentication of everything attributed to Jabir. With predecessors acknowledged, the better question becomes which choices and arrangements are visible here.

Ferrario discusses earlier historians' search for the beginning of “scientific chemistry” and later scholarship's move away from a single inevitable line towards our science {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 p.25}. We should not assume that symbolic writing contains no practice, or that plain language guarantees successful replication. Knowledge history needs aims, practice and language together, not just awards assigned to famous winners.

## The story

The opening presents the work as a response to Muhammad ibn Yunus's request, gathering the art's knowledge for his use {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 p.1}. This is the author's voice as transmitted by the edition, not independent minutes proving a meeting's time and place. A small textual issue is revealing: the read page says the pupil came from Bukhara, whereas Ferrario's translation gives Balkh {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 p.1} {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 p.26}. We do not invent one harmonized geographical fact.

The record uses a broad tenth-century historical setting, not a precisely dated composition {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 pp.24–25}. Baghdad and Rayy are biographical context, not proof of where each section was written {cite:britannica-al-razi biography}. The catalogue's1343[1964] dates the modern Iranian edition:1343 here is Solar Hijri, not a lunarAH date for al-Razi's writing {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 catalogue}.

## The maker(s)

Ferrario treats *Secrets* among al-Razi's genuine works and separates it from spurious *On Alums and Salts*, associated with his name in some transmission {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 pp.25–26}. Similar titles or the appearance of his name do not turn every alchemical text into one work. Nor do we automatically equate the modern volume's appended *Sirr al-Asrar* with that spurious book {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 catalogue}.

**Sunni frame:** This is the declared presentation frame, not proof of the individual's school. Britannica explicitly calls al-Razi a Muslim philosopher: an individual basis, not an inference from name or country {cite:britannica-al-razi biography}.

**Academic reading:** Adamson discusses hostile accounts, contrary evidence and the limits of attributing religious views {cite:sep-abu-bakr-al-razi sec.5}.

**Kaya's reading:** Mahmut Kaya defends a deist interpretation; that interpretation remains his, not the museum's verdict {cite:tdv-razi-ebu-bekir pp.479–485}. Classification skill and medical fame do not resolve this disagreement.

## How it works

First distinguish the level of the question. Page2 divides materials into earthy, vegetable and animal kinds; only the earthy branch receives the six classes: spirits, bodies, stones, vitriols, boraxes and salts {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 p.2}. These are neither six equally ranked classes covering everything nor six elementary elements. Removing the upper level changes the structure even if the six names survive correctly.

The spirits list contains mercury, sal ammoniac, sulphur and arsenic; the seven bodies include gold, silver, iron and kharsini {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 p.2}. These are names within an art, not laboratory purity certificates. Nor should “spirits” be read as a religious judgment on matter: Ferrario relates the classification to substances' behaviour under changed conditions and in combination with other ingredients {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 p.27}. Technical meaning comes from context, not a word's most familiar meaning elsewhere.

“Stones” is not a present geological mineral list: glass is included among its names {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 p.2} {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 p.27}. Recognizing a familiar word therefore does not establish identical systems. A substance can appear in two arts but occupy different positions because different practical questions organize the classifications.

Page2 lists thirteen stones, seven vitriols, six boraxes and eleven salts, with the salt list continuing onto the following page {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 pp.2–3}. These counts describe the read list, not a standardized chart for every copy and every work. Page3 discusses six bodies apart from kharsini after the previous list of seven; we retain this detail rather than arbitrarily changing the number {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 p.3}.

Kharsini particularly illustrates the limits of identification. Ferrario discusses uncertainty about it and hypotheses connecting different traditions, but finds no substantial evidence establishing a Chinese-alchemy link {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 pp.27–28}. We therefore assign neither a certain modern formula nor a transmission map on the strength of the name. Likewise, not all “boraxes” are the modern compound borax, and vitriol colours alone do not guarantee one chemical identity {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 pp.27–28}.

The handbook's next step is not an endless addition of names. It introduces instruments and organizes practical discussion around operations adapted to ingredients; Ferrario calls its organizing principle operation-centred {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 p.2} {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 p.29}. In present explanatory language, one can look up an activity and then the material-specific case. This describes retrieval structure, not recipes or certification of their accuracy and safety.

## The spread

Ferrario records Arabic copies in European and Indian libraries and Gerard of Cremona's twelfth-century Latin translation of the first two sections, preserved in Paris manuscript6514 {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 p.25}. That is specific partial transmission, not a demonstrated translation of every recipe or measured influence in every workshop. We have not inspected the Paris manuscript ourselves; the scholar's report is not presented as our own examination.

Textual circulation also differs from the circulation of a famous name. Ferrario discusses how most witnesses of *On Alums and Salts* do not name al-Razi, despite the association in some Latin transmission {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 pp.29–30}. Fame in another language does not authenticate every later attribution. A handbook's organization may help readers even without a famous author's name: a question about knowledge use rather than national competition.

## What it was built on

The preface acknowledges earlier works and a selection gathered for a learner; it does not claim to abandon all previous experience {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 p.1}. Innovation can lie in organizing existing knowledge, making it findable and distinguishing a general category from an individual example. That differs from discovering a previously unknown substance, but it is more than changing a title.

We draw no dependency edge to another museum invention merely because instruments resemble each other or two books mention one material. Nor does every name in the preface establish a teacher-pupil relationship. Such a connection requires specific evidence. Honest graph-making includes leaving an unsupported relation empty rather than filling it with an attractive route.

## What was built on it

Partial translation witnesses continuing interest, not automatic parentage of every later classification {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 p.25}. The Latin manuscript's existence does not itself establish a link to the periodic table. Educational comparison and a demonstrable borrowing between authors are different claims.

Ferrario supplies a counterexample to oversimplification: sal ammoniac is a spirit in *Secrets*, but a salt in *On Alums and Salts* and also in al-Razi's genuine *Introductory Instruction* {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 p.28}. One classification difference alone therefore cannot prove a book spurious. Authorship requires the combined evidence, not treating every altered placement as a copying offence or conclusive proof of another author.

## Impact on daily life

The handbook's structure addresses learning and retrieval: what is the material, with which apparatus is it discussed, and under which activity can its account be found? Ferrario explains these organizing choices by comparing the two guides {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 pp.27–29}. Potential historical usefulness does not prove that every recipe worked or every reader could replicate it. The selected pages provide no trainee counts, workshop-output measures or demonstrated reductions in material costs.

We do not turn this into a home-remedy story or children's laboratory. Hazardous preparations, quantities and heating procedures are not reproduced. Historical classification can be explained without inviting visitors to buy unknown substances, taste them to identify an old name or assume that an ancient recipe is safe. Reading industrial history and performing its instructions are different activities.

## How it changed history

The inspectable value is a text bringing materials, tools and work into an explicit order; Ferrario identifies organization and clarity as important to its place in alchemical writing {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 pp.25,29}. Visitors can examine a particular page rather than rely on a “father of chemistry” medal. Structure can be described and compared even while a reading or substance identity remains unsettled.

Ferrario quotes early twentieth-century praise for an exceptional scientific beginning and places it within the search for precursors of current science {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 p.25}. We attribute that praise rather than use it to bypass modern analysis or declare all chemical history born on one page. A bounded achievement is more precise than praise that absorbs both predecessors and successors.

## Who invented it?

**Sunni frame:** The museum presents the contribution with an individual Muslim-description basis and visible religious disagreement, not an unavailable personal-school certificate {cite:britannica-al-razi biography} {cite:sep-abu-bakr-al-razi sec.5}.

**Historian's reading:** Ferrario separates the genuine book from the *Alums and Salts* attribution and questions reducing alchemical history to universal firsts {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 pp.25–29}.

**Limit of the read text:** The opening establishes organization and classification, not worldwide priority for every classification or discovery of every listed material {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 pp.1–3}. We record documented organizational improvement, without certifying successful transmutation or invention of every laboratory operation.

## Today

These categories witness historical questions about materials; they are not current identification or safety labels. An old occurrence of “salt” does not entitle us to assume table salt or safety. Ferrario's discussions of sal ammoniac, kharsini and colour names show why a name's history is not itself a substance definition {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 pp.27–28}.

The enduring lesson concerns knowledge organization: a category may change with a guide's purpose or scope. We can understand that change without mocking earlier writers or making them modern in every respect. The museum makes the distance between systems intelligible rather than concealing it behind the newest familiar terminology.

## In culture

Priority stories sometimes condense a book into first-scientist/first-laboratory slogans; Ferrario shows how “scientific beginning” language developed in earlier scholarship {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 p.25}. We can appreciate clear organization without inheriting every older judgment. Nor does an appealing classification narrative settle disagreement about the author's religion {cite:sep-abu-bakr-al-razi sec.5} {cite:tdv-razi-ebu-bekir pp.479–485}.

Distinguishing al-Razi's work from texts carrying his name opens a wider story: authors, copyists, translators and readers can all affect knowledge transmission {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 pp.25–29}. That does not erase the author; it prevents every later sheet from being treated as one unchanging voice.

## For young readers

The book arranged materials, instruments and operations, with some groups nested inside larger ones {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 p.2}. Imagine word cards under headings and a larger heading above them. Ask why a card belongs there and whether its place would change with a different question. This is our modern teaching example, not a manuscript game. Use paper and words only: do not touch, mix or heat unknown materials. The old guide is for historical reading, not experiment instructions.
