## Summary
Nasta'liq is an elegant Persian script that grew in Shiraz and Tabriz in the second half of the fourteenth century from a merging of two older hands: the clear, balanced naskh and the swift, connected ta'liq {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. Called the 'bride of scripts' because it gathered the finest traits of Arabic calligraphy, it became the preferred hand for Persian poetry in Iran and India {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. Legend credits Mir Ali Tabrizi after a dream, while scholars describe a gradual development rather than a single invention {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27} {cite:titley-persian-miniature-painting-1983 p. 27}.

## What it is
Nasta'liq is a style of Arabic script in the Persianate tradition, marked by long slanting, connected letters that flow as if they were a single wave along the line {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. It combines the legibility of naskh — the book and Qur'an hand whose proportions Ibn Muqla codified — with the speed of ta'liq, the chancellery hand written for swift abbreviation {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 25–27}. The result is a hand more beautiful and more mobile than naskh, and more balanced and legible than ta'liq, so its masters named it the 'bride of scripts' {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. It is still taught and practised today in Iran, Pakistan and India for Persian poetry and Urdu {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}.

And it is distinguished from the other scripts by its lateral slope: the letters tilt and rise toward the right, so the writing seems to flow from the top of the line downward {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. This slope is not a flaw but the very core of its beauty: it makes the whole line look like one continuous stroke without gaps, while remaining readable to the trained Persian eye {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}.

## The world before it
Before nasta'liq, Persian calligraphers wrote in established styles codified in the tradition: naskh for books and Qur'ans, ta'liq for the sultan's diwans and correspondence, and riqa', thuluth and diwani for documents and proclamations {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 25–26}. Crucially, ta'liq itself had grown in Iran around the late thirteenth century from adapting the Kufic and naskh hands to the speed demands of state correspondence, becoming a swift administrative hand {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 26–27}. The calligraphy schools taught each style in turn: the student began with naskh copied from models, then tried thuluth, diwani and riqa', each with its own verse texts and preserved exemplars {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 25–26}. Neither hand alone could carry poetry: ta'liq's speed ate its clarity, and naskh was slow and dry {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 26–27}. Thus the world before nasta'liq was open to a new hand that could join the diwan's speed with naskh's legibility.

## The story
Calligraphy histories tell that a leading calligrapher in Tabriz — Mir Ali Tabrizi — mastered naskh and ta'liq together until a third hand emerged joining their merits {cite:titley-persian-miniature-painting-1983 p. 27}. In the circulated craft legend, Ali ibn Abi Talib appeared to him in a dream and commanded letters like the wings of flying geese, and nasta'liq was born {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. Ekhtiar herself, however, distinguishes this legend from historical evidence: scholars establish that the script originated in the second half of the fourteenth century in Shiraz and Tabriz as a gradual merging of naskh and ta'liq {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. Titley gives a precise material witness: under the patronage of Sultan Ahmad Jalayir, Mir Ali Tabrizi perfected the elegant flowing Persian script and wrote Khvaju Kirmani's poems in it in 1396 — the earliest signed manuscript in his hand in this script {cite:titley-persian-miniature-painting-1983 p. 27} {cite:titley-persian-miniature-painting-1983 p. 224}.

## The maker(s)
The record does not give one confirmed inventor. Calligraphy books credit Mir Ali Tabrizi with perfecting nasta'liq at the court of Sultan Ahmad Jalayir in Tabriz and with the earliest signed manuscript in it, dated 1396 {cite:titley-persian-miniature-painting-1983 p. 27}. Ekhtiar describes him as one of the earliest leading masters of nasta'liq, followed in the line by Ja'far Tabrizi, then Sultan Ali Mashhadi, Mir Ali Haravi and Mir Imad al-Hasani {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. The fairest statement: a circle of Shiraz and Tabriz calligraphers developed the script across generations, and Mir Ali Tabrizi stands out as the first known name attached to it in the sources.

## How it works
Nasta'liq works by a doubled geometric-motor principle: the long slanted letter is written in a single extended stroke, and the connected letters hang from it as a bird's wings hang {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. The calligrapher keeps the inherited proportion rules — Ibn Muqla's module and its derivatives — so that letter height, width and position on the line stay governed {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 25–26}. There is no single shape for a letter: the forms of 'ayn, ya' and nun change with their position in the word and on the line, which is what makes mastering nasta'liq harder than naskh {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. It is written with a cutting pen — reed or bamboo — on absorbent paper, the angle and pressure of the pen producing the flowing line {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. The result is that a single line looks like one continuous stroke without gaps, while remaining readable to the trained Persian eye {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}.

## The spread
Nasta'liq spread from Shiraz and Tabriz to India under the Mughals, where it became the hand of royal diwans and calligraphers' albums, and from there to the Ottoman Empire, which adopted it in the art of the pen {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. In India it was preserved in albums such as the Shah Jahan Album, which holds signed nasta'liq folios {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 53–56}. In Iran it settled as the script of poetry and the diwan, in the Ottoman lands as a cultivated hand of albums and manuscript collections, and in Mughal India as the script of royal albums and numerals {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 53–56}. It travelled with merchants and travellers to Kabul, Delhi and Isfahan, becoming the first 'Persian' script mastered by calligraphers outside Iran itself {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}.

## What it was built on
Nasta'liq was built on two earlier hands: naskh, whose rules Ibn Muqla and the Baghdad school set in the fourth century AH, and ta'liq, which arose in Iran in the late thirteenth century {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 25–27}. It took balance and legibility from the first, speed and fluidity from the second. It did not abolish its ancestors but joined them: naskh remained for Qur'ans and scientific books, ta'liq for swift correspondence, while nasta'liq became the hand of the luxury poem and diwan {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 25–27}. Later descendants such as shikasta-nasta'liq — the faster 'broken' variant — were written in a single stroke for a whole word without lifting the pen {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}.

## What was built on it
On nasta'liq rose a whole heritage of poetic correspondence: the divans of Hafez and the classical Persian poets are written in it, and the albums of Persian and Mughal calligraphers gather its praise-pages {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27} {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 53–56}. In India and the Ottoman lands it carried Persianate taste across continents, becoming the emblem of the shared Persian-Turkic culture for six centuries {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 53–56}.

## Impact on daily life
Nasta'liq changed the look of every Persian poetic page: instead of a regular geometric block of text it looks like a living weave of connected strokes {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. Mastering it became the mark of a calligrapher's maturity: the student advances through the scripts in order — naskh, then thuluth, diwani and riqa' — and nasta'liq stands as the hardest and most prized of them {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 25–27}. In the arts it decorated manuscripts, album pages and book bindings across the Persianate and Ottoman worlds {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 53–56}.

## How it changed history
Nasta'liq changed the history of Arabic-Persian script structurally: it turned calligraphy from a copying tool into an independent art with its own albums, stages and masters {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. By carrying it into Mughal India and Ottoman domains it moved Persian taste across continents, and for six centuries it stood as the symbol of the shared Persian-Turkic culture {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 53–56}. Today it is still taught and practised in Iran, Pakistan and India, above all for Persian poetry collections, and it remains one of the most living scripts in the Muslim world.

## Who invented it?
The record proves no single inventor. The craft legend says Ali ibn Abi Talib commanded Mir Ali Tabrizi in a dream to make letters like geese's wings {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. Titley says Mir Ali 'perfected' the script at Sultan Ahmad's court and wrote the 1396 manuscript in it {cite:titley-persian-miniature-painting-1983 p. 27}. Scholars hold that nasta'liq 'originated' gradually in Shiraz and Tabriz in the second half of the fourteenth century as a fusion of naskh and ta'liq {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. The honest answer: it was developed by generations of Persian calligraphers of Shiraz and Tabriz, and Mir Ali Tabrizi remains the closest name to its first attestation in the sources.

Calligraphy books themselves do not know an 'inventor' in the modern sense: the craft was a collective heritage passed from master to pupil, and names are recorded for mastery, not invention {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 25–27}. Mir Ali Tabrizi is the nearest name to the first emergence in the sources, but the credit to him remains 'perfecting', not 'creating from nothing' {cite:titley-persian-miniature-painting-1983 p. 27}.

## Today
Nasta'liq is still written and taught in Iran, Pakistan and India; Persian poetry and Urdu divans are set in it in schools and universities, and calligraphy masters still train students in it {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. In Pakistan and India it remains the hand of Persianate divans and poetic collections. Because the script depends on proportion and movement rather than special tools, it has survived every attempt to replace it with print: every contemporary calligrapher learns it with pen and paper as his forebears did.

There are today calligraphy circles and festivals where students compete to master nasta'liq as their ancestors did {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. On social media, short poems are written in nasta'liq, photographed and shared, so the script lives on screens as it once lived on paper.

## Surviving examples
The earliest known signed witness is the manuscript of Khvaju Kirmani's poems that Mir Ali Tabrizi wrote in nasta'liq in 1396 under Sultan Ahmad Jalayir {cite:titley-persian-miniature-painting-1983 p. 27} {cite:titley-persian-miniature-painting-1983 p. 224}. A surviving image shows a folio of the 'al-Risala al-Sharif' manuscript copied probably in Tabriz in 878 AH/1473 CE for the Aq Qoyunlu sultan Ya'qub, its Arabic in gold nasta'liq with a smaller black nasta'liq Persian translation — early evidence of the script's maturity within decades of its emergence {cite:commons-nastaliq-tabriz-1473}. Museums and private collections preserve whole albums of Mughal and Ottoman calligraphers {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 53–56}.

## In culture
Nasta'liq entered the Persian cultural imagination: poets are remembered with the phrase 'so-and-so wrote in nasta'liq', and literary journals celebrate the finest hands {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. In Pakistan and India it remains the emblem of the shared Persianate literary culture carried across continents for six centuries {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 pp. 53–56}.

## For young readers
Imagine a script that looks like a flying bird: every letter is long and slanted, and the letters join like a goose's wings. That is nasta'liq, the beautiful Persian script born in Shiraz and Tabriz five hundred years ago. People say a calligrapher named Mir Ali perfected it at Sultan Ahmad's court, and calligraphy books tell of a dream that commanded it. Today this 'bride of scripts' is still written for Persian poetry in Iran, Pakistan and India.
