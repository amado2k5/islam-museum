## Summary
The obliquity of the ecliptic is the angle at which the zodiacal plane tilts from the celestial equator; it sets the Sun's greatest altitude at the summer and winter solstices. Al-Battani, the Abbasid astronomer of the tenth century CE, fixed this angle at about 23°35′ from his measurements of the year's four seasons, after the inherited Ptolemaic value of about 23°51′ had stood higher. This is not an invention of the angle but a documented improvement of its measurement through repeated observation. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 114}

The value he reached is very close to today's accepted figure, which testifies to the precision of his method. But the sources read give no single year for his derivation; al-Battani's observing career spanned decades in the ninth and early tenth centuries, so the date is recorded by century, not by day. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

To understand why this measurement mattered, it helps to know what the obliquity controls. It fixes how far north or south the Sun can climb at noon through the year, which in turn sets day length, season length, the height of the noon shadow, and the dates on which the Sun crosses the equator. A wrong obliquity propagates into every one of those quantities, so correcting it was not a cosmetic refinement but a correction of the frame on which calendars and ritual times rested. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112–114} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

## What it is
This is a record of a "measurement method", not an artefact: a set of astronomical observations that linked the lengths of the four seasons to the ecliptic's tilt. The idea is that the longest and shortest days fix the Sun's greatest declination, and from measuring the altitude differences between the solstices the obliquity angle can be derived. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112–114}

The numerical result 23°35′ is what the biography and astronomical summaries document; we do not know whether he measured it in one night or gathered it from scattered observations, because the sources do not say. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

It is also worth being precise about what the record does and does not contain. It contains a result and a method-level claim: repeated observation of the seasons, corrected comparison with the inherited value. It does not contain a surviving instrument, a dated observing log, or a step-by-step procedure we could reproduce today. The museum therefore presents this as a documented measurement contribution, with its limits stated, rather than as a reconstruction of a lost experiment. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

## The world before it
Before al-Battani, astronomers inherited Ptolemy's obliquity value of about 23°51′ and treated it as fixed. Astronomers of al-Ma'mun's circle in the ninth century also made an experimental measurement at San'a giving a close value of about 23°35′, which al-Farghani records in his book. Al-Battani did not start from zero; he came in a line that included the earlier Ma'munid measurement and the inherited Ptolemaic value. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 114} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

Al-Farghani notes that Ptolemy set values, that al-Ma'mun's work tested them, and that al-Battani continued on the same path with improvements. This sequence matters: each scholar built on a predecessor, and the measurement cannot be credited to one person in isolation. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112–114}

The contrast between the two earlier values is instructive. Ptolemy's figure came down through the Almagest tradition as a received constant; al-Ma'mun's astronomers, working under caliphal patronage in the ninth century, reportedly made a fresh measurement at San'a that landed near 23°35′. Al-Farghani preserves both numbers side by side, which lets a reader see that al-Battani's result was not a sudden leap but the latest in a line of tested values. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112–114}

## The story
Al-Battani observed the Sun and stars for decades at Raqqa and in the Antioch region, as the biography records. He measured the four seasons - the lengths of spring, summer, autumn and winter - and derived a tropical year of 365 days, 5 hours, 46 minutes and 24 seconds. From the same method he derived the ecliptic's obliquity of 23°35′. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

The biography says he "fixed" the inclination by his own method, but gives no details of his instruments or observation days in the text read. We invent no coordinates and no exact year; we keep to what the sources document. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

## The maker(s)
Al-Battani is Muslim according to his documented biography and sources, and his role here is "improver": a scholar who refined an inherited value through measurement, not the inventor of the angle itself. Ptolemy and the earlier Ma'munid astronomers remain predecessors and transmitters, not makers. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

He is not a religious scholar nor the inventor of a new instrument; he is a precise astronomer who measured and proved, and that is the role that matters for this record. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

## How it works
The astronomical idea: at the summer solstice the Sun reaches its greatest height north of the equator, and at the winter solstice its greatest southward dip. The difference between these altitudes is tied to the ecliptic's tilt angle. If you measure the four season lengths precisely, you can derive the obliquity without measuring it directly. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112–114} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

Al-Farghani explains that the obliquity is "the amount the zodiac inclines from the equinoctial"; al-Battani derived 23°35′, a value more accurate than Ptolemy's. We do not know whether he used an astrolabe, a quadrant or something else; the sources do not say, so we do not invent. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112–114} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

## The spread
Al-Battani's values entered later zij tables, and his book "al-Zij" was translated into Latin, carrying his results into Europe. The research read establishes no direct line from his measurement to today's instruments; but it does show his value was a reference in several astronomical traditions. {cite:battani-mactutor} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

The spread was therefore one of parameters rather than of a single object: what travelled was the corrected number and the method that produced it, embedded in zij tables and commentaries. Astronomers in later centuries either adopted 23°35′ or re-measured and adjusted it; the record documents the transmission of a value, not the diffusion of a machine. {cite:battani-mactutor} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

## What it was built on
It was built on Ptolemy's inherited value, on al-Ma'mun's earlier experimental measurement, and on the methodology of repeated seasonal observation. Al-Battani did not invent the angle; he improved its measurement and proved it systematically. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 114} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

## What was built on it
The obliquity underpinned zij calculations for calendars, eclipses and prayer times. Later astronomers used it without re-measuring until someone measured again. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

In practical terms, every computation that needed the Sun's declination - from predicting the equinoxes to fixing the times of noon and the seasons - quietly depended on a number like this. Correcting it by a fraction of a degree improved the whole chain of derived tables, which is why a single parameter could matter to so much downstream calculation. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112–114} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

## Impact on daily life
This measurement had no direct effect on ordinary daily life in its time; its effect was scientific, in the accuracy of calendars and seasons. But it is the basis for every calculation that depends on knowing the Sun's position and tilt. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

Indirectly, however, it touched daily life through the calendar: prayer times, the seasons of worship and the dates of festivals all rest on astronomical tables that quietly used parameters like this one. A fraction-of-a-degree correction propagated into tables used by communities far from Raqqa, even though no villager ever saw the instrument that produced it. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112–114}

## How it changed history
It proved that careful repeated measurement could change a fundamental parameter, making al-Battani's obliquity a zij reference. This records a stage in the history of astronomy: from inheriting a value to re-measuring it. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 114}

## Who invented it?
The ecliptic's obliquity was not invented in Islam; the documented contribution is lowering and fixing it through seasonal measurement. Al-Battani improved the value, the earlier Ma'munid astronomers measured it experimentally, and Ptolemy first set it. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 114}

## Today
Today the obliquity is known to vary between about 22.1° and 24.5° over a roughly 41,000-year cycle, so no single fixed value is correct for all time. Al-Battani's degree-and-minute figure remains a witness to his method's precision for his epoch, though it no longer matches the current value. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

Modern measurement reaches far greater precision through instruments and long observation series that al-Battani could not have had. His achievement is not that his number survives unchanged but that a tenth-century observer, working with the means of his time, produced a value within a fraction of a degree of the modern one - a reminder that careful method can approach truth long before instruments make it easy. {cite:battani-mactutor} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

## Surviving examples
There is no single "artefact" of the measurement itself; the legacy is zij texts and biographical books. Al-Farghani records al-Ma'mun's tested value, and the biography records al-Battani's result. These texts are the surviving witnesses. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 114} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

## In culture
Al-Battani is known in Islamic culture as one of the greatest astronomers, and his obliquity measurement is among his most famous contributions. But we do not exaggerate: the contribution is documented and bounded, not a legend. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

## For young readers
Imagine you measure the length of day at the year's longest and shortest days. The difference tells you how much the Earth tilts. Al-Battani measured that difference carefully and found the ecliptic's obliquity to be about 23°35′, very close to the number we know today. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112–114}

The lesson is that a careful measurement, repeated over years, can correct what was inherited - and that the correction, though small, changes everything built on it. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}
