## Summary
The sakk was a written document ordering payment of a sum to a named person, used by Abbasid commerce and administration within a family of instruments — [[concept/al-sakk-wal-hawala|sakk and hawala]], suftadja — that provided credit without moving coin {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. III pp. 303–305 HAWALA} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. VIII pp. 794–795 SUFTADJA}. The word itself is a Persian loan (chak) shaped to Arabic triliteral patterns in the lexica {cite:khwarizmi-mafatih-ulum-1930}.

## What it is
Sakk originally meant a sealed tablet, and in use a document by which its holder orders payment of a sum; the suftadja specialises in financing a merchant against delivery in another town, and the hawala transfers a debt from one liability to another {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. III p. 303 HAWALA} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. VIII pp. 794–795 SUFTADJA}. The name was later connected to the cheque/check, but the verbal and institutional chain into English requires documentation, not assertion {cite:quinn-roberds-check-2008}.

## The world before it
Before it, long-distance sale was covered by transported metal carried at the road's risk; the contracts of murabaha, salam and qirad provided the legal basis for moving a claim without moving money {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. III pp. 303–304 HAWALA}.

## The story
It reached its highest form in the Abbasid diwan, where the land-tax was booked by hawala on provincial collectors through documents the treasury issued, while coin moved from a different place and date {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. III p. 304 HAWALA}. From merchants to bureaux this was the non-cash mode of payment and receipt; local tax collectors accepted nothing but documentary payment {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. VIII pp. 794–795 SUFTADJA}. Al-Khwarizmi's Mafatih al-Ulum ⁦(compiler d. 387/997)⁩ records the definition and use of the sakk, one of the earliest written descriptions {cite:khwarizmi-mafatih-ulum-1930}.

## The maker(s)
No individual inventor is known; it was a collective development of merchants, tax agents and jurists. [[person/muhammad-ibn-ahmad-al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi the secretary]] is credited as its earliest descriptor {cite:khwarizmi-mafatih-ulum-1930}.

## How it works
A buyer writes a sakk ordering his agent or money-changer to pay a sum to the named seller or bearer; a suftadja involves a guarantor who receives in one place and pays in another; a hawala transfers a debt from transferor to transferee {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. III pp. 303–304 HAWALA} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. VIII pp. 794–795 SUFTADJA}.

## The spread
It spread through Islamic commerce and the state from Iraq and Khurasan to the Maghrib, and connected with later medieval Italian instruments (lettera di cambio) {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. III pp. 304–305 HAWALA} {cite:quinn-roberds-check-2008}.

## What it was built on
It was built on the commercial fiqh of salam, murabaha and qirad, and on the Romano-Byzantine law of obligations that Islam inherited {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. III p. 303 HAWALA}.

## What was built on it
Credit instruments and exchange practice, and eventually national banking; describing it simply as 'the Islamic cheque' is a broad claim that requires establishing the transmission chain {cite:quinn-roberds-check-2008}.

## Impact on daily life
It carried credit over distance without coin, easing long-range trade and tax administration {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. III p. 304 HAWALA}.

## How it changed history
It showed that monetary claims could travel on paper rather than metal; some economists regard it as an ancestor of the modern cheque around the Mediterranean, while the institutional lineage remains a matter of study {cite:quinn-roberds-check-2008}.

## Who invented it?
No name and no single event: it was collective institutional evolution, and the Persian-derived word itself signals an exchange network rather than a lone invention {cite:khwarizmi-mafatih-ulum-1930}.

## Today
The electronic payment order is a distant functional successor, not a direct descendant {cite:quinn-roberds-check-2008}.

## Surviving examples
Al-Khwarizmi's description in Mafatih al-Ulum, the EI2 articles HAWALA and SUFTADJA, and Geniza mercantile papers {cite:khwarizmi-mafatih-ulum-1930} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. III pp. 303–305 HAWALA}.

## In culture
The sakk became the diwan's name for the written order; its presence in correspondence shows its everyday use {cite:khwarizmi-mafatih-ulum-1930}.

## For young readers
Merchants once travelled without carrying silver: the seller wrote a paper promising that the buyer's agent would pay. The state paid soldiers' wages with such papers too. The paper was called a sakk, and some scholars compare it to the cheque we know today.
