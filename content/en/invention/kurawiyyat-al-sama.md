## Summary
The sphericity of the heavens is the astronomical doctrine that the sky is a 'sphere' turning on its pole, not a flat roof or a fixed dome {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 125–126}. Al-Farghani presents it in chapter two of his 'Elements of Astronomy' with a precise observational proof: all the stars appear from the east, rise gradually in one order in their motions, sizes and distances from each other until they reach the middle of the sky, then descend to the west on the same order and system, moving in parallel circles without difference in speed or slowness {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. This principle was a settled truth among the astronomers of the ancient nations before Islam — from the Greeks to the Indians — so al-Farghani restated it in Arabic within the Abbasid curriculum; he is an organiser-transmitter, not an inventor {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}.

## What it is
'Kurawiyya' — sphericity — in ancient astronomy means that the heavens are not a flat surface above the Earth but a huge sphere turning on two fixed poles, one toward the north and the other toward the south, as al-Farghani puts it: 'the heavens are, so to speak, a sphere, and their rotation for all that is in them of the planets is like the rotation of a sphere' {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. Inside this sphere the planets travel on parallel circles around the two poles: the stars nearest the pole trace small circles that never set, while the farther ones trace larger circles that rise and set {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. This principle is not merely a picture but the 'working frame' of everything that follows: distances, sizes, risings and settings are all built on the heavens being a sphere {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 125–126} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Today it is called the 'celestial sphere' in astronomy, still the basis of representation in star charts {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}.

## The world before it
Before al-Farghani, sphericity was among the 'settled points' of the astronomy of nations: Greek astronomy in the Almagest and the science of the spheres (hay'a), and Indian astronomy in its zij tables, both built on the celestial space being a sphere {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Al-Farghani himself notes in the same chapter that 'there is no disagreement among the scholars' that the heavens are a sphere turning on poles — a phrase that reveals an ancient consensus, not a new discovery {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. Before the astronomers, narrators, kings and sailors knew the sky practically: they knew the stars circle the northern pole, and that some never set — an observation directly tied to the parallel circles al-Farghani describes {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 125–126}. The world before al-Farghani was full of knowledge: what was missing was the clear Arabic formulation that placed the ancient consensus in a single teaching curriculum.

## The story
In chapter two of the 'Elements', al-Farghani states the principle then proves it: he begins by saying the heavens are 'in the manner of a sphere' — that is, in the form of a sphere — 'and their rotation for all that is in them of the planets is like the rotation of a sphere' {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. Then he gives the observational proof every stargazer knows: 'all the planets appear from the east and rise gradually in one order in their motions, sizes and distances from each other until they reach the middle of the sky, then descend to the west on that same order and system, moving in parallel circles without difference in speed or slowness' {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. This sentence — among the most honest in the book — sums up a complete visual observation: every star has its own parallel path, and the only difference between them is its position on these parallel circles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. Al-Farghani then continues in later chapters to set out the 'parallel circles' between the poles and the 'intersecting' ones around the equinoctial — the full geometric mapping of sphericity {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 125–126}.

## The maker(s)
The principle is neither an Islamic invention nor an al-Farghani discovery: it was a 'settled truth' among the astronomers of the ancient nations, as the TDV 'ilm al-falak' entry describes it — established in Greek and Indian astronomy before reaching Arabic {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Al-Farghani's role is clear in his book: he 'presented' the principle in his second chapter with its observational proof, and 'worked out' the parallel circles geometrically in the chapters that follow {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 125–126}. This is the role of 'organiser', not 'inventor': he gathered the ancient consensus into a clear school formulation {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Like the rest of the 'Elements' chapters, the chapter claims no priority — it describes what is 'settled' and proves it for learners.

## How it works
The principle works on three layers: first, structure — the heavens are a sphere with two fixed, unmoving poles, one toward the north and one toward the south {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. Second, motion — the planets travel on parallel circles around these poles, differing neither in speed nor in slowness, like lines of latitude on Earth but in space {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. Third, observation — every star rises from the east, climbs gradually in its own order, reaches the middle of the sky, then descends to the west on the same order and system, so the observer can predict its position at any hour of the night {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. The geometric paradox is that this 'system' is not arbitrary: each star takes its own parallel circle, so the heavens look like a 'drum' of circles stacked around the two poles — which is what makes sphericity predictable and computable {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 125–126}.

## The spread
Sphericity spread with the 'Elements' and the rest of Arabic astronomical books: chapter two of the book became a compulsory 'introduction' for all who studied astronomy, because everything after it — distances, sizes, motions, instruments — is built on the heavens being a sphere {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 125–126} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Later astronomical manuals — the 'hay'a' zij tables and treatises — described the same framework, so sphericity remained the rule by which every astronomical text opens {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. As Arabic astronomy passed into Persia, India and al-Andalus through translation and copying, this formulation travelled with it, making sphericity 'the first page' of astronomical teaching in all these centres {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}.
Its spread was not only theoretical: the makers of the astrolabe and the celestial globe built their instruments on this principle — the surface on which a maker engraves a star globe presupposes that celestial sphere being a 'sphere' {cite:commons-louvre-celestial-globe} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. When Yunus ibn al-Husayn al-Asturlabi made his bronze globe inlaid with silver in 1144 — carrying 48 constellations and 1025 star points — he was building on al-Farghani's principle and on all who preceded him {cite:commons-louvre-celestial-globe}. Thus sphericity turned from 'an idea in a book' into 'a shape in metal'.

## What it was built on
Sphericity was built on three foundations: an ancient visual observation — the circling of stars around the pole — a ready mathematical model — the geometry of the sphere and its parallel circles — and an accumulated teaching tradition, the Greek and Indian astronomical books the translators carried over {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Above that it rested on what is called 'theoretical astronomy' — the science of composing the spheres (hay'a) — which places every body in its sphere and explains its motions {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 125–126}. Al-Farghani did not invent the principle but 'restated' it in a clear Abbasid curriculum.

## What was built on it
On sphericity was built everything 'instrumental': the astrolabe — which presupposes a spherical celestial heaven to measure altitudes and times — the three-dimensional celestial globe that mimics the heavens in space, and the zij tables that compute positions on this spherical space {cite:commons-louvre-celestial-globe} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. The 'celestial sphere' on which star charts are drawn — the space on which the planets are plotted — is a direct extension of this idea {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Sphericity is therefore not 'a chapter in a book' but 'the foundation' of all the astronomy, instruments and computations that followed.

## Impact on daily life
Sphericity touched ordinary life only at its farthest horizon: the farmer knows the time from the Sun, not from the heavens' shape, and the worshipper knows the qibla from the compass or the sky, not from a model of the sphere {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. But its impact was in the 'environment of knowledge': everyone who learned astronomy — students of the houses of learning, instrument makers, astrologers — began with this principle, so the chair in the astronomy classroom was 'a seat of understanding sphericity' {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 125–126} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Teaching it was 'the test of maturity' of the whole curriculum: if a student accepted that the heavens are a sphere, he could accept all the complex descriptions that followed.

## How it changed history
Sphericity — as 'the frame' — changed the shape of astronomy itself: it made the heavens a subject that could be measured and computed, not merely a scene to behold {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 125–126} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. This frame remained the rule of Islamic astronomy for centuries: from the 'Elements' to the 'hay'a' zij tables to the planetary maps — all presuppose a spherical heaven {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. In the wider history, sphericity was one of 'the principles that crossed civilisations': from Greece to India to Baghdad to al-Andalus, it carried the name of one of the deepest scientific exchanges in history {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}.

## Who invented it?
The honest answer: there is no single inventor. The principle existed in Greek and Indian astronomy before Islam, entered Arabic with the translation movement, and al-Farghani 'presented' it in his second chapter with its observational proof {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 125–126}. His role — as his text describes it — is 'organiser', not 'inventor': he claims no priority, but gathers the ancient consensus into a single school formulation {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. The question 'who invented the sphericity of the heavens?' is a modern question that does not match the nature of ancient science: knowledge was cumulative and collective, and names are recorded for documentation, not invention {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}.

## Today
Sphericity — as 'the shape of the heavens' — is still taught in the first lesson of any introduction to astronomy: the heavens are a sphere, and the planets run on parallel circles around the poles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. But the frame has changed: modern astronomy knows that it is the Earth that turns — not the heavens — on its axis, and the stars are 'fixed' in relative space {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Even so, the 'celestial sphere' — the spherical space on which positions are plotted — remains a basic tool in star charts, navigation science and space science {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. And the bronze globe Yunus ibn al-Husayn made in 1144 — with its 1025 markers — is still displayed as witness that this principle became 'metal', not merely 'idea' {cite:commons-louvre-celestial-globe}.
Nor has the 'feeling of sphericity' vanished with the changed frame: when a person looks at the sky on a clear night he sees a 'dome' of stars turning around the pole — the same visual picture al-Farghani proved a thousand years ago {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. The principle he wrote in a school chapter remains 'the shape of the heavens' in human consciousness today — even if the language we name it with has changed {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}.

## Surviving examples
The Arabic text is the surviving witness: chapter two of the 'Elements' presents sphericity with its observational proof, and the following chapters draw the parallel and intersecting circles geometrically {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 125–126}. The Louvre celestial globe — bronze engraved and inlaid with silver, signed by Yunus ibn al-Husayn al-Asturlabi, 1144, carrying 48 constellations and 1025 star points — survives as a material witness that the principle became 'craft' {cite:commons-louvre-celestial-globe}. Copies of the 'Elements' carried the chapter through the tradition of astronomical teaching {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 125–126}.

## In culture
Sphericity did not enter popular consciousness under its name, but its image — 'the heavens are a turning sphere' — became part of the visual understanding of the cosmos in Islamic culture {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. In the books of astrology the 'mansions' and 'signs' continued to be read on this spherical space, so the principle became the background for reading the horoscope and destiny {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Thus a 'geometric understanding' turned into a 'cultural image'. And the three-dimensional celestial globe — the instrument of testing and pride in the houses of learning — remains witness that this principle did not stay 'theoretical' but became a 'craft' that artisans competed over {cite:commons-louvre-celestial-globe}.

## For young readers
Imagine you are holding a giant orange and turning it on its axis: the stars around the North Pole circle in small loops that never set, and the farther stars circle in bigger loops that rise and set — that is what al-Farghani said a thousand years ago: the heavens are a 'sphere' turning on its pole {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. He was not the first to say it: Greek and Indian astronomers knew it before Islam, but he wrote it in Arabic in the 'Elements' to teach it to students {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Today every student of astronomy still begins with the same first lesson: the heavens are a sphere, and the heavens turn {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}.
