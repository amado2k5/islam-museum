## Summary
Muslim growers and refiners carried sugar cane and its industry from Persia across three continents — Khuzistan, Basra, Egypt, Palestine, the Maghrib and Sicily — and developed the refining chain that produced the named grades: loaf sugar, stick-grown nabat, the quick-melting fanid and twice-cooked sulaymani {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it is
An agro-industry: the cultivation of the cane (qasab al-sukkar), the pressing of its juice, its decoction and crystallisation into graded sugars — the chain EI2 documents zone by zone from the geographers and the medical and culinary books {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The world before it
Cane was Indian; cultivated cane is traced to Bengal and reached Sasanian Persia, where the physicians of Jundishapur knew it before Islam. Honey, date-molasses (dibs) and fruit sugars were the sweeteners of the older world {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The story
After the conquest of Persia, the crop spread wherever heat and water allowed — papyrus attests it in Egypt by the mid-eighth century, and the tenth-century geographers map its fields from Tustar to the Jordan valley. In Egypt and Sicily it became a state-scale industry; al-Mas'udi noted Egypt's wealth in sugar, and by the tenth century Sicilian sugar was being eaten — and debated — in Qayrawan {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The maker(s)
No inventor: growers, millers, boilers and merchants across many lands. The named witnesses are the geographers — al-Istakhri, Ibn Hawqal, al-Muqaddasi, Nasir-i Khusraw — and the medical writers who classified the sugars {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it works
The cane is pressed for its juice; the juice is boiled down — decocted — and cleared; then it is crystallised: set hard in moulds as loaf sugar (tabarzad), grown on palm sticks as nabat, cooked with milk or almond oil into the fine fanid, or twice-refined into sulaymani {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The spread
East: Khuzistan, Basra, Fars, Makran, Kirman, Balkh, Sind, Oman. West: the whole Nile, the Jordan valley, Tyre and Beirut, North Africa, Iberia, Sicily, Cyprus, Rhodes, Crete — until Crusader plantations bought and planted the same model {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it was built on
Indian cane and Persian husbandry; the irrigation works of the conquest lands; the kitchen and pharmacy traditions that gave sugar its uses and its grades {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What was built on it
The Crusader and Mediterranean sugar economy — Venetian and Hospitaller plantations at Tyre and Colossi — and, far down the same agro-industrial road, the Atlantic sugar industry the standard histories trace {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:deerr-history-of-sugar-1949}.

## Impact on daily life
It made a luxury into a commodity: sugar entered cookery (the Kanz al-fawa'id uses it more than honey), medicine — al-Tamimi called it 'the full brother of honey' — and the confectioner's art, while cane-juice chewing was a village pastime at Tiberias {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it changed history
It was one of the great crop transfers of Islamic history: a whole industry moved with the faith's expansion, and the same crop later redrew the economy of the Mediterranean and, far darker, the Atlantic {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:deerr-history-of-sugar-1949}.

## Who invented it?
Nobody, and India first: the Islamic world's documented role is transmission and industrial development — carrying the crop west and refining it into a graded product {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Today
Sugar is a world commodity whose names remember the road it travelled: 'sugar' through Arabic sukkar, 'candy' from qand and nabat {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Surviving examples
The grade names in the medieval texts; the geographers' maps of the cane lands; and Sicily's memory of the industry the Normans continued after the Muslims {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## In culture
Sugar entered the poetry of hospitality and the pharmacopoeia alike — 'the full brother of honey', as a tenth-century physician put it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
A thousand years ago farmers in Muslim lands grew tall sweet grass — sugar cane — in fields from Persia to Egypt and Sicily. They squeezed its juice and boiled it until it turned into sugar, in shapes and names people still remember. That is how sugar first reached Europe.
