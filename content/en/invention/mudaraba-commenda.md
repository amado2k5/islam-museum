## Summary
The mudaraba — also called qirad or muqarada — is the Islamic contract that let money travel without its owner: an investor entrusts capital to a trading agent who returns the principal plus an agreed share of profit, while a loss falls on the capital and never on the agent's own pocket. Born of the Arabian caravan trade and codified in the earliest fiqh books, its near-twin appeared in the Italian seaports as the commenda — whether by transmission or independent invention is a question the scholarship still debates {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-qirad-en} {cite:wikipedia-commenda-en}.

## What it is
A partnership of asymmetry: the investor (sahib al-mal) risks only his capital, the agent (mudarib, amil) risks only his labour and expected share; third parties need never know the investor exists — the feature that made it ideal for long-distance trade {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The world before it
Partnership contracts were old: Babylonian tapputum, the Roman societas and foenus nauticum, Byzantine chreokoinonia, the Jewish isqa. But the qirad's likely direct origin was indigenous — the caravan trade of pre-Islamic Arabia, carried into the conquered lands by the Arabs {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-commenda-en}.

## The story
Never named in the Qur'an, the contract is nevertheless rooted in tradition: reports attribute its practice to the Prophet and his leading companions, and the earliest legal compendia — by the late eighth century — treat it at a length that speaks of long experience. Maliki and Shafi'i law called it qirad or muqarada, Hanafi law mudaraba; al-Dimashqi counts it among the three ways trade is done and al-Ghazali among the contracts every trader must know; the Cairo Geniza is replete with it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-qirad-en}.

## The maker(s)
No inventor on record. The named jurist is Shams al-A'imma al-Sarakhsi, whose al-Mabsut carries the great kitab al-mudaraba — and whose justification for it remains the classic summary of its economic function: capital alone cannot trade, skill alone has no capital, and the contract joins the two {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it works
The agent trades freely with the entrusted money or goods — even sub-contracting to further agents — returns the principal, and shares the profit at the agreed ratio; if the venture fails he owes nothing back, having lost his time and effort instead. Later Hanafi law capped his authority at the capital itself {cite:wikipedia-qirad-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The spread
From Arabia it spread with the conquests through the Near East, North Africa and Southern Europe — and in the late tenth and early eleventh centuries the commenda surfaced in the Italian seaports: Venice's collegantia and Genoa's societas, unilateral and bilateral, financing single voyages and, in EI2's judgement, germinal to the expansion of medieval European trade {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-commenda-en}.

## What it was built on
The Arabian caravan partnership, and the wider Mediterranean world of the societas and the sea-loan it resembled but did not simply repeat {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What was built on it
The commenda's line runs toward the limited company and venture capital; within Islamic law its siblings are the musharakah joint partnership and the whole profit-and-loss-sharing family of modern Islamic banking {cite:wikipedia-commenda-en} {cite:wikipedia-qirad-en}.

## Impact on daily life
It made long-distance trade open to the small investor: capital stayed home, goods moved, profits returned — the mechanism behind the Geniza merchants' letters and the seaborne wealth of both shores {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it changed history
It is a candidate for the single most consequential contract of the medieval world: the legal instrument of Islamic commerce and, by transmission or convergence, the ancestor of the European trading company {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-commenda-en}.

## Who invented it?
Contested at the point of crossing. The qirad's Islamic pedigree is uncontested — fiqh codified it from the eighth century — but whether the commenda is the qirad transplanted, or a parallel European growth from shared Mediterranean roots, Pryor's survey and Udovitch's study both leave undecided; the qirad is merely the closest in form {cite:wikipedia-qirad-en} {cite:wikipedia-commenda-en} {cite:udovitch-partnership-profit-1970}.

## Today
The contract lives twice: as mudarabah in Islamic banking — a profit-and-loss-sharing mode — and as the limited partnership written into modern commercial law {cite:wikipedia-qirad-en}.

## Surviving examples
Not objects but texts: the kitab al-mudaraba of the fiqh compendia — Sarakhsi's Mabsut, the Umm, the Mudawwana — and the hundreds of commenda-form contracts inside the Cairo Geniza {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## In culture
It entered the vocabulary of trust itself: "mudarib" the working partner, "rabb al-mal" the owner of capital — terms still on the lips of every Islamic banker, and the ancestor-word behind every silent partner {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
Imagine a man who has money but cannot trade, and another who can trade but has no money: the first hands over his capital, the second trades with it, and they split the profit as agreed beforehand; if the venture loses, the owner loses his money and the trader only his effort. This fair contract moved caravans for hundreds of years.
