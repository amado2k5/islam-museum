## Summary

In Book One of the Canon of Medicine, Ibn Sina devotes a second teaching of Fann 3 titled "the shared regimen for adults," in seventeen chapters {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.221}. It opens by stating that most of the regimen of preserving health is exercising, then regulating food, then regulating sleep, explaining that every digestion leaves residues which, if not voided, gather into diseases {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.221}. This teaching is not the invention of exercise, diet or sleep; it is a systematic ordering of the chapters of a whole day inside the older regimen-of-health tradition known to the Greek-Arabic school of medicine {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}. It is read in the 1999 Dar al-Kutub al-Ilmiyya printing at scan pages 221 to 231, where the chapter headings appear in the order the author arranged them {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}.

## What it is

Regimen here is the name of a daily plan: Chapter 1 opens by defining exercise as voluntary movement requiring consecutive deep breathing, and saying that one who masters it in moderation and at its proper time is freed from every treatment required by material diseases and the temperamental diseases that follow them {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.221}. The seventeen chapters cover the general discourse on exercise, its kinds and its timing; then massage; then bathing and the enumeration of baths; then bathing in cold water; then the regimen of food; then the regimen of water and drink; then sleep and wakefulness; then what is deferred from this place concerning coitus and purgative drugs; then strengthening weak organs, fattening them and enlarging their size; then the fatigue that follows exercise, its kinds and its treatment; then pandiculation and yawning; then other states that follow exercise; then treating fatigue arising of itself; and finally the regimen of bodies whose temperaments are not fine {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}. This is not a therapeutic prescription for a particular disease nor a behavioural protocol for the public; it is an ordered teaching presentation inside a school medical book {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}.

## The world before it

Regimen did not appear from nothing: Greek-Arabic medicine knew the idea of preserving health through diet, exercise and sleep since Hippocrates and Galen, and the Canon came as a gatherer of that heritage rather than its inventor {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. Ibn al-Nadim's Fihrist lists Ibn Sina's medical books before the Canon itself, showing that he wrote in an environment rich in medical compositions, not in an authorial vacuum {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. The invention of general preventive medicine or the first diet prescription is therefore not credited to this teaching; only its systematic ordering inside Book One is {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}.

## The story

The study places the beginning of the Canon in Jurjan, its continuation in Rayy and its completion in Hamadan, without dating the writing of this particular teaching {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. We therefore use an eleventh-century horizon, not an invented discovery day extracted from an approximate date for the whole book {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 pp.78–79}. The pages read here belong to the 1999 Dar al-Kutub al-Ilmiyya printing, and scan page 221 opens a whole teaching, not an isolated chapter {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.221}. We do not claim that all seventeen chapters were written in one sitting or that the final order was printed exactly as the author composed it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}.

## The maker(s)

**Declared Sunni frame:** Britannica explicitly calls Ibn Sina a Muslim physician; Alper discusses the claim that his father and brother prevented him from adopting their Ismailism and the philosophical doctrines attributed to him as an attributed interpretation rather than conclusive personal testimony of a specific sect {cite:britannica-avicenna opening} {cite:tdv-ibn-sina-alper affiliation-discussion}. **Reported contrary interpretation:** Alper also records Corbin's and Türker-Küyel's readings connecting his thought with Shiism or Ismailism and challenges them; these arguments are presented through the source actually read, not claimed as independently examined originals {cite:tdv-ibn-sina-alper affiliation-discussion}. The museum does not infer a son's belief from his father or his school's doctrine from a patron or teacher, and retains earlier attributed positions in the maker record {cite:tdv-ibn-sina-alper affiliation-discussion}.

The attribution here is to Ibn Sina as author of the text, not to whoever translated or commented on this presentation; the Arabic belongs to the Canon, and the institute counts these pages as a printed witness to the teaching {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}. This attribution does not imply that Ibn Sina invented exercise, diet or sleep; all of them are older materials he read and gathered {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}.

## How it works

**First: why exercise comes first.** Ibn Sina justifies putting exercise before food and sleep by arguing that every digestion leaves residue which nature's evacuation alone cannot fully expel; when this repeats, harmful materials gather: if they putrefy they cause putrefactive diseases, if their qualities intensify they cause bad temperament, if they multiply they beget congestion diseases and tumours {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.221}. Evacuating them with toxic drugs enfeebles the innate faculty and weakens the principal and servant organs {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.221}. Exercise, by contrast, is the strongest preventive against the principles of congestion: it stirs a subtle heat that dissolves each day's surplus and helps guide it to its exits, preventing the gathering of any considerable surplus over the days {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–222}. And if their vapours did not drain away they would corrupt the temperament of the spirit's substance, forcing evacuation by drugs {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.221}.

**Second: the definition of exercise.** Exercise is voluntary movement requiring consecutive deep breathing {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.221}. Some of it is exercise called for by engagement in a human occupation, and some is pure exercise undertaken for its own sake {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.222}. It has chapters of quantity and strength and speed: the little and the much, the strong and the weak, the fast and the slow, the hasty and the moderate, with a mean existing between every two extremes {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.222}.

**Third: kinds of exercise.** The text enumerates many kinds: wrestling, the mubasha grapple, the mulakaza hold, the ihdar, fast walking, archery, dancing, leaping to hang upon something, hopping on one leg, fencing with sword and spear, horse riding and arm-flapping {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.222}. Among the gentle and soft are swinging in swings and hammocks and riding boats; stronger still are riding horses, camels, litters and carts {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.222}. Among the field sports are shadow-fighting, clapping, leaping, playing with the large and small ball, playing with the polo-stick and the tabtab, wrestling, stone-throwing, horse-running and its pursuit, and the kinds of the mubasha: chest-to-chest pushing, neck-grasping and the wrestling holds {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.222}. He requires varying the exercises used and not settling on one, for each organ has an exercise specific to it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.222}.

**Fourth: exercise by voice.** The text mentions that among the exercises of the chest and the organs of breathing is producing the heavy great voice and the sharp voice and a mixture of the two, which is also exercise for the mouth, uvula, tongue and eye, improves the colour, cleanses the chest {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.223}. It warns against drawing out the great voice for too long a time with excessive daring, and requires beginning with a gentle reading and raising the voice gradually {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.223}.

**Fifth: from massage to sleep.** After exercise come chapters on the timing of its beginning and cessation, then massage, then bathing and the enumeration of baths, then bathing in cold water {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.223–225}. Then the regimen of food and the regimen of water and drink, among the longest chapters of the teaching {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.226–228}. Then sleep and wakefulness {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.228–229}.

**Sixth: the deferred, the weak organs and fatigue.** Chapter 10 defers the matter of coitus and purgative drugs to the detailed books {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.230}. Chapter 11 requires whoever keeps his health to attend to the easy kinds of expectoration, sweating, urination and evacuation, and details the strengthening of weak organs, fattening them and enlarging their size: those in the period of growth are nourished, and the aged are massaged and given moderate constant exercise and painted with bitumen {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.230–231}. He gives the example of one with thin calves, ordering slight breath-holding, moderate massage and painting the limb with the bituminous paint while increasing the exercise day by day {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.230–231}. He warns that if signs of dilated vessels and a share of swelling appear in an organ, one must reduce the exercise and massage done, stop and rest, and reverse the massage on that organ {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.231}.

**Seventh: fatigue and its states.** Chapter 12 divides the fatigue that follows exercise into kinds: the ulcerous, the tensile and the inflammatory, with a fourth added, the fatigue called the desiccatory, resembling the touch of ulcers on the skin or the enopthalmos of the skin {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.231}. It then follows its treatment, the chapters on pandiculation and yawning, other states that follow exercise, and the treatment of fatigue arising of itself {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.231–233}. The seventeenth chapter concludes with the regimen of bodies whose temperaments are not fine {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.233}.

## The spread

The study discusses Book One of the Canon as the basis of medical teaching for about seven centuries in the school and the Latin translation {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. But the circulation of a book does not prove that every reader implemented these seventeen chapters or recorded comparable results {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. The specific evidence here is that this teaching survives in a printed edition and is classified within the structure of Book One, not a complete map of health-regimen practice across the centuries {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}. We therefore do not fill reception gaps with an invented chain of institutions or pupils {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}.

## What it was built on

The teaching builds on temperamental theory: every chapter presupposes that bodies differ in their humours, and that what suits one may harm another {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}. The daily ordering itself rests on the idea that health is not the absence of disease but a balance of components, and that imbalance begins with poor regimen {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.221}. Read outside this humoral frame it loses its historical meaning {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}.

## What was built on it

Sources discuss that the Canon remained a medical reference for centuries in the school and the Latin translation, but that does not prove these chapters became a universally followed protocol {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. Similar lifestyle-medicine questions do not by themselves prove direct descent from this teaching {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. For the same context the question is similarity of problems, not proof of lineage {cite:britannica-avicenna final-evidence-discussion}.

## Impact on daily life

The directly showable impact is organizational: it made health-keeping a plan for a whole day with ordered chapters, not scattered advice {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}. That ordering can make a reader more aware of the alternation of movement, food and sleep, but it is not by itself proof of improved health in a particular population; the teaching supplies no mortality or recovery figures attributable to applying it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}. The everyday contribution described is an organized questioning, not a therapeutic guarantee or a promise about every patient {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}.

## How it changed history

The teaching shows that reflection on prevention belongs to written medical history: the author did not content himself with describing disease and its treatment, but arranged chapters for preserving health before disease occurs {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}. That ordering inside Book One, which remained a foundation for seven centuries, gives it real historical weight without turning medical history into a contest for the title of first practitioner of prevention {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. Accurate appreciation retains the humoral heritage he used and the distance between a plausible historical argument and verification tools he did not describe {cite:britannica-avicenna final-evidence-discussion}.

## Who invented it?

What is established is that Ibn Sina presented these chapters in the Canon, not that a survey of every earlier text proves him the first human to formulate any organized health regimen {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. The record therefore treats the contribution as systematization and improvement inside an older heritage, not the sole origin of all preventive science {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. Nor is this the invention of a new diet nor the first exercise protocol; they are materials Ibn Sina read and gathered into a teaching order {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}.

## Today

Visitors can read this teaching as a history of the idea that health needs daily planning, remembering that heat and cold here are not readings on a modern thermometer {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.221}. Comparison with today's medicine requires its own standards, not using an old text as instructions for practice on oneself or others {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. The safe museum example below concerns thinking and ordering only; this page supplies no prescription, no substance to take and no therapeutic procedure.

## Surviving examples

The direct witness is scan pages 221 to 231 of the Dar al-Kutub al-Ilmiyya printing, where the seventeen chapter headings appear in order {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}. The accompanying image is the opening leaf of Book One of the Strasbourg printed edition ante 1473, under a public-domain mark, showing a page of the same book to which this teaching belongs {cite:commons-canon-strasbourg-book1 file-page}. This record contains no personal manuscripts of Ibn Sina proving that he applied the chapters to documented patients {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}.

## In culture

An ordered daily list can tempt readers to imagine a direct ancestor of a modern health timetable, while explaining each chapter reveals historical meanings broader than, or different from, that analogy {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}. It is possible to value the questions of prevention and organization without concealing the humoral frame in which they were formulated {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. History here is an encounter with a document, not a modern image projected onto its author {cite:britannica-avicenna final-evidence-discussion}.

## For young readers

**A modern museum example, not an experiment from the Canon:** imagine one day in a school week: you breakfast with your family, then walk to school, then play in the break, then sleep at night. Ibn Sina described a similar system for grown-ups: first exercise, then food, then sleep. We understand that health in the view of the old physicians was something that repeated every day and did not happen once. We do not use his medical prescriptions today; we read them as a history of what physicians thought {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.221–231}.
