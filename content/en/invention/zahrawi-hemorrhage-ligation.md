## Summary

Where major bleeding threatened, al-Tasrif recommends tying off the bleeding vessel with thread — arterial ligation — instead of relying on cautery alone. The instruction is the earliest documented recommendation of vessel ligation in the surgical texts studied, and the technique it names remains the foundation of surgical bleeding control {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## What it is

The practice of isolating a cut blood vessel and securing it with a ligature — a loop of thread drawn tight — so haemorrhage stops without burning surrounding tissue. Al-Tasrif gives it for operations where cautery cannot reach or is too dangerous {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## The world before it

Bleeding was controlled by compression, by styptics, and above all by the cautery iron — a blunt instrument that sealed by burning. Effective but imprecise, cautery destroyed tissue and could not be applied deep or selectively.

## The story

In the surgical treatise compiled in Cordoba around 1000 CE, al-Zahrawi describes the management of haemorrhage with the same methodical care as his instruments: the vessel identified, lifted or held, and tied. The Latin translations carried the instruction into European surgery, where it joined cautery as the two classical options {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861}.

## The maker(s)

Al-Zahrawi (d. c. 1013 CE), author of al-Tasrif and court physician at Cordoba. The instruction is recorded in his treatise; his Muslim identity is on the person record.

## How it works

A ligature — silk or linen thread — is passed under the bleeding vessel, drawn tight and knotted, closing the lumen. The vessel stops bleeding instantly, the tissue around it is untouched, and the body later seals the tied segment permanently. The same act underlies every modern haemostat and clip {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## The spread

The Latin Chirurgia carried it to Europe; the technique appears in later medieval surgery, was revived famously by Ambroise Paré in the sixteenth century, and has been standard ever since — now with synthetic ligatures and titanium clips.

## What it was built on

On the ancient management of haemorrhage by compression and cautery — the goal was old — and on the suture craft already documented for wounds; applying thread to the vessel itself is the documented step.

## What was built on it

Every technique that occludes a vessel before or instead of cautery: Paré's amputation ligatures, the hemostatic forceps with ligature, modern clip appliers — a lineage running through operating rooms today.

## Impact on daily life

Ligation replaced indiscriminate burning with selective closure — less tissue destroyed, less shock, more survivable operations. It is the difference between sealing a wound and sealing a pipe.

## How it changed history

It is the earliest documented textual witness to arterial ligation — five centuries before Paré's famous revival — placing the technique inside Islamic surgical heritage {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## Who invented it?

Al-Zahrawi states the technique in al-Tasrif; no earlier surgical text read contains the recommendation in this explicit form. The claim is of documented firstness, not absolute invention.

## Today

Every operating room uses it — by name in open surgery, as clips in laparoscopy, as embolisation in radiology. The act of tying off a vessel is one of surgery's oldest continuously used techniques.

## Surviving examples

The instruction in al-Tasrif manuscripts and the published editions; the technique itself survives in every ligature tied today {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## In culture

Ligation is the emblem of al-Zahrawi's surgical method — precision over brute force — cited wherever the history of haemorrhage control is told.

## For young readers

When a blood vessel bleeds in a big operation, you need to close it fast — without hurting the flesh around it. A thousand years ago al-Zahrawi had the idea: tie the vessel with thread, like tying a rope, and the bleeding stops. Surgeons still do exactly that today.
