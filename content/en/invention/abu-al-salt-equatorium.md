## Summary
Abu al-Salt's universal plate is the instrument described in his treatise Sifat 'amal al-safiha al-jami'a, written by the Andalusi polymath Abu al-Salt Umayya ibn Abd al-Aziz al-Dani (d. 529 AH / 1134 CE) after his years of captivity in Alexandria and his settling at Zirid Mahdiyya {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea works} {cite:wafayat-ayan}. The plate computes the motions of all seven planets on a single described surface; the BEA lists it as the last of the three known Andalusian equatoria and notes that the equatorium family already existed in tenth-century Khurasan through Abu Ja'far al-Khazin {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea works}. The museum records the contribution as an improvement within an inherited instrument family, not the invention of the equatorium.

## What it is
An equatorium is a brass astronomical computer: a flat or slightly curved plate carrying engraved scales and rulers with which a user reads planetary positions and computes their motions without the repeated trigonometric steps an astrolabe demands {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea works}. Abu al-Salt's "universal plate" is his own described design of this kind — one plate on which the motions of the seven planets can be computed, per the title of his treatise {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea works}. It belongs to the same practical-astronomy world as the astrolabe but serves a narrower, computational purpose: getting the planets' positions for a given date quickly and accurately.

## The world before it
The equatorium was not new in Abu al-Salt's day. The BEA biography notes that Abu Ja'far al-Khazin had already described an equatorium in tenth-century Khurasan, and that two earlier Andalusian equatoria — associated with Ibn al-Samh and al-Zarqali — preceded his {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea works}. The astrolabe itself was centuries old and served a broader range of problems: prayer times, qibla, astrology and basic astronomy all had their astrolabe solutions {cite:wafayat-ayan}. What changed in the equatorium tradition was specialization: instead of one general instrument, makers produced dedicated plates for the specific task of computing planetary motions.

## The story
Abu al-Salt was born in Denia around 480 AH, studied under scholars of al-Andalus including Abu al-Walid al-Baji, reached Alexandria in 489 AH, and was exiled by the Fatimid commander al-Afdal Shahanshah in 505 AH {cite:wafayat-ayan}. In captivity he wrote prolifically: the treatise on constructing and using the astrolabe, the Wajiz on astronomy, the book of simple drugs, a book of logic he called Mut'at al-dhihn, and a refutation of Ibn Ridwan called al-Intisar {cite:wafayat-ayan}. Released after al-Afdal's death, he moved to Mahdiyya at the start of 529 AH and died there a few months later {cite:wafayat-ayan}.

His treatise on the universal plate belongs to this captive and post-captive body of work. The BEA biography lists it among his astronomical writings and describes it as the construction and use of a single plate by which the motions of the seven planets are computed {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea works}. Whether he composed it in Alexandria or at Mahdiyya is not fixed by the sources read; the treatise's existence and title are what the BEA documents. What is clear is that the work belongs to the same encyclopedic project as his astrolabe treatise: a scholar describing instruments completely enough that a reader could build them.

## The maker(s)
Abu al-Salt Umayya ibn Abd al-Aziz ibn Abi al-Salt was an Andalusi physician, astronomer, logician and poet, born in Denia and active in Egypt and North Africa {cite:wafayat-ayan}. His biography in Ibn Khallikan's Wafayat al-a'yan traces a life of movement: study in al-Andalus, years in Alexandria, captivity under al-Afdal Shahanshah, then Mahdiyya at the court of the Zirid ruler {cite:wafayat-ayan}. His Muslim identity is documented by his Arabic scientific works composed for Fatimid, Zirid and Hammadid courts; the museum's Sunni frame records the default for an Andalusi scholar of Sunni courts, with no personal madhhab separately attested {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea biography} {cite:wafayat-ayan}. The BEA judges him accomplished in medicine and astronomy though not broadly innovative — a judgement that frames his equatorium as a competent variant within an established family rather than a breakthrough {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea biography}.

The breadth of his output matters for understanding the plate. In the same captivity that produced the astrolabe treatise he also wrote the Wajiz on the configuration of the heavens, a book of simple drugs, a book of logic he called Mut'at al-dhihn, and a refutation of the Egyptian physician Ibn Ridwan {cite:wafayat-ayan}. A scholar working across medicine, astronomy and logic was exactly the kind of encyclopedist the Islamic courts valued, and his instrument treatise should be read as one panel of a much larger intellectual project.

## How it works
The treatise describes both construction and use: how the plate is laid out, what scales and rulers it carries, and how the user obtains the seven planets' positions for a given date {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea works}. In the equatorium family the plate replaces the astrolabe's repeated projection steps with a direct reading: the user aligns a rule or rider with the date and reads the planet's longitude and latitude from engraved curves {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea works}. The "universal" in the title indicates that one plate suffices for all seven planets — the design goal that distinguishes it from plates dedicated to a single body or a single computation.

## The spread
Abu al-Salt's works travelled through translation. The BEA biography notes that his astronomy and medicine were carried to Europe through Latin and Hebrew translations, and his name appears in the transmission history of Andalusi science {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea biography}. The equatorium family itself spread from Khurasan through the Islamic world and into Latin Europe, where equatoria appear in fifteenth- and sixteenth-century instruments {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea works}. Whether his specific plate design had a direct descendant is not established by the sources read.

## What it was built on
The plate rests on the equatorium tradition that preceded it: al-Khazin's Khurasani design, the two earlier Andalusian equatoria, and the astrolabe's projection mathematics that all these instruments compress {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea works}. Abu al-Salt's own astrolabe treatise — written in the same captivity — supplied the spherical background his plate assumes {cite:wafayat-ayan}. His medical and logical works show the same encyclopedic range, but the plate's lineage is specifically astronomical.

## What was built on it
The BEA suggests his plate may represent a link between the Andalusian and eastern equatorium traditions — the "last" of the three known Andalusian examples and possibly connected to the Khurasani line {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea works}. Later equatoria in the Islamic world and in Europe continued the family; whether they descend from his design specifically or from the shared tradition is not traced in the sources read. ## Impact on daily life
Its impact was scholarly rather than domestic. The equatorium served astronomers computing planetary positions for astrology, calendar work and theoretical astronomy — tasks that underpinned the era's timekeeping and its astrological medicine {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea works}. For the ordinary Muslim the plate mattered indirectly: accurate planetary positions fed the tables that determined prayer times, festival dates and medical-astrological judgements.

## How it changed history
Abu al-Salt's plate is a data point in the history of astronomical instruments: the third and last documented Andalusian equatorium, produced by a scholar working at the intersection of Fatimid, Zirid and Hammadid courts {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea works}. Its historical significance is bounded — it documents that the equatorium tradition reached Andalusi North Africa and that a single author could describe a complete instrument in a dedicated treatise. It does not mark an invention of the instrument class.

## Who invented it?
The equatorium was not invented by Abu al-Salt. Al-Khazin described one in tenth-century Khurasan, and two earlier Andalusian equatoria precede his {cite:isi-abu-al-salt-bea works}. His documented contribution is a specific plate design described in his own treatise — a competent variant within the family, possibly a link between Andalusian and eastern traditions. The museum records improvement, not invention.

## In culture
Abu al-Salt's range — medicine, astronomy, logic, poetry — places him in the Andalusi polymath tradition that the Islamic world prized {cite:wafayat-ayan}. His captivity and productivity under Fatimid patronage illustrate how political upheaval could produce scholarship rather than silence it. The equatorium itself belongs to the culture of practical astronomy that served both court astrology and the scientific infrastructure of the Islamic world.

## For young readers
Before computers, astronomers used brass instruments to calculate where planets would be. One kind, called an equatorium, was like a special ruler that gave answers quickly. Abu al-Salt was a scholar who, while a prisoner, wrote a book describing his own equatorium — a flat plate that could track all seven planets at once. His book was one of the last of its kind from Islamic Spain, and his ideas travelled to Europe through translations.
