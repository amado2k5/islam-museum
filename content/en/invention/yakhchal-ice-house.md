## Summary
The yakhchal — literally "ice pit" — is the desert refrigerator of old Iran: a conical dome of sarooj-sealed brick over an underground chamber, fed by night-freezing pools behind tall shade walls, keeping winter's ice through the summer heat. Ancient in Persia, it served a wide Islamic-era economy — the caliphal ice sellers of Baghdad, the documented freezing pits of Sawa and Aba, and finally the baraf khana of the Mughal court {cite:wikipedia-yakhchal-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it is
A passive ice house: typically a domed, often conical structure on the surface, a subterranean storage space of up to five thousand cubic metres, shade walls up to ten or fifteen metres high, and icy pools or channels where water froze by radiative cooling on clear desert nights {cite:wikipedia-yakhchal-en}.

## The world before it
Records indicate ice houses in Persia as far back as 400 BCE, part of an Iranian engineering family with the qanat and the windcatcher — technologies built around the desert's physics of evaporation, radiation and night-cold {cite:wikipedia-yakhchal-en}.

## The story
In the caliphate ice became a city commodity. The EI2's thalladj entry runs through the trade: the Baghdad ice-seller who made a fortune selling to the Tahirid governor (d. 913); Hilal al-Sabi's forty thousand pounds of ice for Ibn al-Furat's celebration; the six thousand ratls found in Ibn Bakiyya's wine store. The mountains of western Persia, Kurdistan and Hermon — "Jabal al-Thalj" — supplied it, and Hamd Allah Mustawfi records the people of Sawa and Aba letting water freeze in winter pits for summer use — the simplest form of the yakhchal's own physics {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The maker(s)
No inventor on record; the named witness is the Ilkhanid geographer Hamdallah Mustawfi, whose Nuzhat al-Qulub preserves the Jibal pit-ice practice at the heart of the technology's economy {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it works
Three tricks of passive cooling: a conical dome venting warm air at the crown like a solar chimney; thick sarooj walls — sand, clay, egg white, lime, goat hair and ash — insulating the ice chamber two metres thick at the base; and east-west shade walls guarding shallow pools whose water radiates its heat to the dry night sky and freezes {cite:wikipedia-yakhchal-en}.

## The spread
From the plateau of Yazd and Kerman the ice economy travelled west into the caliphal cities — Baghdad, Damascus and Cairo buying mountain snow at high prices — and east into India, where Humayun, Akbar, Jahangir and Shah Jahan turned it into the baraf khana: insulated pits, relay ice routes from Kashmir, sherbets and kulfi at court {cite:wikipedia-yakhchal-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it was built on
The ancient Persian ice house, the qanat that fed its water, and the whole desert architecture of passive cooling it shares with the badgir wind towers sometimes built onto it {cite:wikipedia-yakhchal-en}.

## What was built on it
The Mughal baraf khana and ab-dar khana — underground ice cellars, sandstone cisterns and cold-water fountains at Fatehpur Sikri and beyond — and the modern low-energy design school that studies it today {cite:wikipedia-yakhchal-en}.

## Impact on daily life
Cold as a service: preserved food, chilled drinks sold by the thalladj, frozen sweets like faloodeh, and medicine for the sick — the girl Shaji treated with ice bought at ten thousand dirhams a ratl {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-yakhchal-en}.

## How it changed history
It proved the desert could be made to refrigerate itself: a civilisation-scale answer to heat that ran on nothing but geometry, shade and night sky — and carried a named trade, the thalladj, into the written record {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Who invented it?
Not contested as to origin — a Persian invention the Muslim world adopted. The record stands as transmission: Persian engineering serving caliphal, Timurid-age and Mughal societies across a millennium {cite:wikipedia-yakhchal-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Today
Commercial refrigeration replaced it — and inherited its name, since "yakhchal" is simply the word for fridge in Iran, Afghanistan and Tajikistan. Standing cones, like Kerman's eighteen-metre example, are now studied as models for sustainable building {cite:wikipedia-yakhchal-en}.

## Surviving examples
The domes of Yazd province, Kerman, Meybod and the twin structures of Sirjan; the shade walls and ice pools beside them; and in the written record, the pits of Sawa and Aba and the baraf khanas of Mughal India {cite:wikipedia-yakhchal-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## In culture
Ice in the desert became a mark of civilisation itself: a vizier's feast counted in ratls of ice, a governor's gift, a traveller's marvel — and finally a proverb built into the language of the cold {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
Imagine a great clay dome like an upside-down beehive in the desert: under it a cold cellar, beside it a tall wall shading pools of water that freeze at night. That is how the people of Iran stored winter's ice, to sell it and eat iced sweets in the summer heat, without electricity.
