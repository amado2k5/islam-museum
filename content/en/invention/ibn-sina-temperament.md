## Summary

In Book One of the Canon, the first chapter of the third teaching in the first section defines temperament as a quality arising from the reaction of opposed qualities when they stand at a mean, and counts the primary forces in the four elements {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}. It then rules true equilibrium impossible in the human temperament and derives from it a practical equilibrium measured by eight considerations {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.19–21}. This is not a modern medical system nor a personality description; it is an old conceptual frame for understanding the body's structure and its disease, revised inside a school text of the Greek humour theory {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}.

## What it is

The teaching here is the third of the first teaching in Book One of the Canon, and its first chapter is the chapter on temperaments {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}. The chapter says temperament is a quality arising from the reaction of opposed qualities — heat, cold, moisture and dryness — when these qualities stand at a certain balanced mean {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}. The primary forces in the mentioned elements are four: they are the same four qualities whose interaction generates temperament {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}. The chapter then moves to a precise ruling: no true, complete equilibrium is possible in human temperament; every body inclines toward one of the four directions, and what is called equilibrium is a practical, relative equilibrium that is measured, not claimed {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.19–21}.

## The world before it

The humoral temperament theory is much older than the Canon: Hippocrates, Galen and those before them built on it the understanding of health and disease as a balanced or disturbed mixture of humours and temperaments {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}. The Canon did not invent this frame; it chose to make it an independent chapter inside the first section — the section on theoretical medicine — after defining the elements and qualities {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}. It is therefore not credited with inventing temperament theory nor the first description of it; only its conceptual organisation inside a coherent school text is attributed to it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.19–21}.

## The story

This chapter's place in Book One's architecture is foundational: after the elements, qualities and bodily humours, before the diseases of structure and their symptoms {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.19–107}. The study places the Canon's completion at Hamadan without dating this chapter specifically {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. The pages read are the Dar al-Kutub al-Ilmiyya 1999 edition, and scan 19 opens the whole chapter on temperaments {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}. We do not claim Ibn Sina composed this chapter in a single sitting or that it was printed exactly as he wrote it; we establish that the text reached us in this order {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.19–21}.

## How it works

**First: defining temperament.** The chapter says temperament is a quality arising from the reaction of opposed qualities when they stand at a mean {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}. The four qualities — heat, cold, moisture and dryness — are the primary forces in the elements, and the temperament of every member and every body is the product of their relative balance {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}.

**Second: the impossibility of true equilibrium.** The chapter rules that human temperament cannot reach a true, complete equilibrium; if one member were equilibrated, all members would have to be, and that is not the case {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.19–20}. This is not mysticism but a structural argument: complete equilibrium is a condition that cannot be realised in the living body {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.19–20}.

**Third: practical equilibrium through eight considerations.** Since true equilibrium is impossible, the chapter derives a practical equilibrium measured by eight considerations — the dimensions of increase and decrease in each of the four qualities {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.20–21}. This practical equilibrium is what the physician uses in reading a state, not a perfect mathematical ideal {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.20–21}.

**Fourth: the signs of temperament.** The chapter continues with reading the signs of temperament in the body: ten signs are counted on the commentator's method — among them touch, flesh and fat — so the temperament of each part may be known {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.20–21}. These signs are not modern diagnosis; they are reading tools inside the same humoral frame {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.20–21}.

## The spread

The Canon itself spread through schools and the Latin edition for about seven centuries {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}, but a book's spread does not prove that every reader applied this chapter or recorded its results {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. The specific evidence here is that this chapter survives in a printed edition and is codified inside Book One's architecture — not a complete map of temperament-reading practice across the centuries {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.19–21}. We therefore do not fill the gaps of reception with an invented chain of institutions or pupils {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}.

## What it was built on

The chapter builds on the theory of the humours: every body is a mixture of interacting humours and qualities, and disease is a disturbance in their balance {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}. The definition itself rests on the idea that opposed qualities, when they stand at a balanced mean, generate a temperament, and when their balance is disturbed they generate disease {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}. Read outside this frame it loses its historical meaning {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.19–21}.

## What was built on it

Modern sources discuss that the Canon remained a medical reference for centuries, but that does not prove this chapter became a universally followed protocol {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. The similarity of temperament-concept questions to psychology or modern medicine does not prove direct descent from this chapter {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. For the same context, the question of similar problems is not proof of lineage {cite:britannica-avicenna final-evidence-discussion}.

## Impact on daily life

The directly demonstrable impact is organisational: it made understanding temperament and equilibrium the first step before any description of disease {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}. This ordering can make the reader more aware that every body has its own balance, but it is not by itself evidence of more accurate diagnosis in any given population; the chapter offers no accuracy figures or clinical results attributed to its application {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.19–21}. The daily contribution described is the organisation of questioning, not a diagnostic guarantee and not a personality description {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}.

## How it changed history

The chapter shows that understanding temperament is part of the history of written medicine: the author did not content himself with describing disease and its cure, but arranged a foundational concept that explains the body's structure before everything else {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.19–21}. This ordering inside Book One — which remained a foundation for seven centuries — gives it real historical weight without turning medical history into a race for the title of first to describe temperaments {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. The precise estimate keeps the humoral heritage it used and the distance between a reasonable historical argument and verification tools it does not describe {cite:britannica-avicenna final-evidence-discussion}.

## Who invented it?

What is proven is that Ibn Sina presented this chapter in the Canon, not that a survey of every earlier text proves he was the first person to frame any system of temperaments {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.19–21} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. The record therefore treats the contribution as systematic and an improvement inside an older heritage, not as the sole origin of humour theory {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. This is not the invention of psychology nor a modern medical classification; it is material Ibn Sina read and gathered in a teaching order {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}.

## For young readers

**A modern museum example, not an experiment from the Canon:** imagine your body is like a cup holding four things: heat, cold, moisture, dryness. If the balance breaks, sickness comes; if it holds, health holds. Ibn Sina was teaching physicians to understand this balance before describing any disease; it was an old medical frame we study as history. We do not use his method today, and we do not say it was right or wrong by our standards {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}.
