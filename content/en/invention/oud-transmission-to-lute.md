## Summary
The lute that filled European courts from the thirteenth to the seventeenth century is the Arabic oud under a borrowed name — and the name itself confesses it: al-ʿūd, Arabic article and all, became Spanish alaude, Italian liuto, French luth, German Laute and English lute. Farmer calls the derivation 'an established fact' {cite:farmer-historical-facts-arabian-musical-influence-1930; encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it is
The oud is a short-necked, pear-shaped lute played with a plectrum — the most important instrument of Islamic peoples, in EI2's phrase, from the Atlantic to Persia. Its transmission to Europe was a double event: an instrument and a word travelling together {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The world before it
Europe of the early Middle Ages had the cithara and the harp — open strings, no frets, tuned by ear alone. The deeper lute family was already old in the East: undoubted lutes appear in Indian sculpture of the second century BCE, and Sasanian Persia played the barbat, the skin-or-wood-bellied ancestor EI2 distinguishes from the Arab oud proper {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The story
The refined wooden-bellied oud emerged in early Islamic Iraq — a two-stringed lute is already named in seventh-century sources, and the classic four-and-five-stringed instrument was perfected in the Abbasid capitals. The decisive westward step came through al-Andalus: Ziryab, the celebrated musician who left Baghdad for Cordoba in 822, carried the Baghdad school, its tunings and its technique into the Islamic west {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2; farmer-historical-facts-arabian-musical-influence-1930}. From there, and later through Crusade-era contact, the instrument and its name moved north with the wandering minstrels Farmer credits as the real disseminators of medieval music {cite:farmer-historical-facts-arabian-musical-influence-1930}.

## The maker(s)
No maker — a transmission has carriers, not inventors. Ziryab is the documented figurehead of the Andalusi channel; behind him stand the Abbasid lutenists — Ishaq al-Mawsili and his school — who made the oud the master instrument of Baghdad, and behind them the anonymous players who turned the Persian barbat into the Arab oud {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it works
A vaulted wooden body separate from its neck, a pegbox bent back, gut or silk strings stopped against a fingerboard — with frets (dasatin) fixing the notes, where the European cithara had none. The player strikes with a quill plectrum; the tuning (the accordatura) was a science of its own, recorded by al-Kindi, Ishaq al-Mawsili and al-Farabi {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2; farmer-oriental-musical-instruments-1931}.

## The spread
Through Muslim Spain and the minstrel circuits into France, Italy, Germany and England; through Sicily and the Crusading contact-zone as a second door. By the thirteenth century the lute is painted in the Cantigas de Santa Maria; by the fifteenth it is the court instrument of Europe; by the sixteenth it fills printed lute-books — Farmer notes the printed tutor for the luth (1557) identical in method to the guiterne's {cite:farmer-historical-facts-arabian-musical-influence-1930}.

## What it was built on
The Sasanian barbat — its name folk-etymologised as 'duck-breast' — and the older Arab skin-bellied lutes the poets called mizhar, kiran and muwattar. The wooden belly, the separate neck and the fret-map were the Islamic refinement {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2; farmer-oriental-musical-instruments-1931}.

## What was built on it
The whole European lute family: the Renaissance lute with its added bass courses, the theorbo and archlute, the mandolin; and by cousinly descent the guitar (Farmer keeps the guitar's route an open question) and the rebec from the rabab {cite:farmer-historical-facts-arabian-musical-influence-1930}.

## Impact on daily life
In the Muslim world the oud was the sound of courts, salons and taverns — and of music theory, since its frets made intervals visible and tunings teachable. In Europe the lute did the same work: it became the household instrument, the accompanist of song, the tool every educated amateur was expected to hold {cite:farmer-historical-facts-arabian-musical-influence-1930}.

## How it changed history
It is one of the clearest cases where a whole word-and-object package crossed a civilisational border. Europe did not merely adopt an instrument; it adopted the fretted, mode-tuned way of making music that came with it — and kept the Arabic name as a permanent receipt {cite:farmer-historical-facts-arabian-musical-influence-1930}.

## Who invented it?
Not one people. India shows early lutes; Sasanian Persia perfected the barbat; the Arabs refined the wooden oud and theorised its tunings; Europe received the finished instrument through al-Andalus and the Crusade-era East. Farmer's verdict is precise: we owe the Arabs 'the instruments themselves' for lute, rebec and (perhaps) guitar — and the words beyond question {cite:farmer-historical-facts-arabian-musical-influence-1930}.

## Today
The oud never left home: it remains the central instrument of Arab, Turkish, Persian and Armenian music. The lute returned in Europe's early-music revival, and its modern descendants — guitar and mandolin — are the world's commonest instruments {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Surviving examples
Historical ouds survive in museum collections — the record's image is a late West Asian oud photographed by the Cleveland Museum of Art — and lutes fill European instrument museums; medieval depictions in the Cantigas manuscripts show the instrument mid-journey, played by Muslim and Christian musicians side by side {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## In culture
The oud is the instrument of the legendary singers of the Kitab al-Aghani and of the modern masters — the 'sultan of instruments'. In Europe it became the lutenist of Shakespeare and the painted companion of Vermeer: two cultures sharing one instrument and one name {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
Say the word slowly: al-oud. Now say it the way Spanish heard it — alaude — and the way English ended up saying it: lute. The instrument in the old paintings of European kings is the same instrument still played in Cairo and Baghdad today. A word and a lute travelled together from Cordoba, and neither ever went back.
