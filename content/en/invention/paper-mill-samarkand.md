## Summary
Transmitted accounts say Chinese artisans captured after the battle of Atlakh near the Talas in ⁦(133–134/751)⁩ taught the people of [[place/samarqand|Samarkand]] to make paper from linen and hemp rags, and 'Samarkand paper' became the most famous writing material of the Islamic world {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV p. 440 KAGHAD} {cite:carter-invention-printing-1925 pp. 97–98}. This is a record of craft transmission, not of paper's invention, and the prisoner story is a report transmitted by al-Tha'alibi two centuries later, not a contemporary document.

## What it is
Paper is a sheet formed from fibres suspended in water, lifted on a mould, dried and polished; the word kaghad is Persian, perhaps of Chinese origin {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV p. 440 KAGHAD}. The documented recipe uses rotten linen, flax or hemp rags cut small, cleaned, bleached and pounded in mortars, stampers or paper-mills, then dressed with wheat-starch size and drawn through a deckle {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 440–441 KAGHAD}.

## The world before it
Before paper, Islamic administration wrote on Egyptian papyrus (kirtas) and on parchment {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV p. 440 KAGHAD}. China had known paper for centuries before Islam, so the story here is the reception of a mature craft, not the creation of a new material {cite:carter-invention-printing-1925 pp. 97–98}.

## The story
Al-Tha'alibi ⁦(d. 429/1038)⁩ reports from the author of 'Journeys and Kingdoms' that [[person/zayyad-ibn-salih|Ziyad ibn Salih]] took Chinese prisoners among whom were papermakers who taught their craft at Samarkand, until it became a major article of commerce {cite:carter-invention-printing-1925 p. 98} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. VIII pp. 1105–1106 SAMARKAND}. The Tang annals confirm the battle's date, and Carter notes that surviving early papers are rag paper even though the Arabic report speaks of 'grasses and plants' {cite:carter-invention-printing-1925 pp. 97–98}.

## The maker(s)
No papermaker's name survives. Credit attaches to transmitters: Ziyad ibn Salih in the account, and the unnamed Chinese artisans in fact {cite:carter-invention-printing-1925 p. 98}. Carter also records that tenth-century scholars were already disputing whether the Umayyads or the Abbasids deserved the honour of the introduction — the narrative was contested from the start {cite:carter-invention-printing-1925 p. 98}.

## How it works
Rag fibres are separated in water and pounded to pulp, dressed with starch, lifted on a deckle and shaken until the fibres interlace; the sheet is laid flat, dried on a wall, rubbed with flour-and-starch and polished smooth {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 440–441 KAGHAD}.

## The spread
From Samarkand the craft moved to Baghdad, where al-Fadl ibn Yahya probably founded the Dar al-Kazz mill about ⁦(178/794)⁩ — see [[invention/baghdad-papermaking|papermaking in Baghdad]] — then to the Tihama, Yemen, Egypt, Damascus, Tripoli, Hama, Manbij, Tiberias, the Maghrib, Jativa in Spain, Persia and India {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV p. 440 KAGHAD} {cite:carter-invention-printing-1925 pp. 98–99}.

## What it was built on
It was built on the Chinese craft and displaced papyrus and parchment; the 'Pharaonic' paper variety was made to compete with papyrus even in Egypt {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV p. 440 KAGHAD}.

## What was built on it
The paper industries of Baghdad, Damascus and Jativa, Europe's 'charta damascena', and the entire book culture of the Islamic world that grew on cheap writing material {cite:carter-invention-printing-1925 pp. 98–99}.

## Impact on daily life
Writing material became cheaper, so registers, letters and then books multiplied; al-Jahiz reports Harun al-Rashid ordered paper for government offices because writing on it cannot be erased or scratched out unnoticed {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV p. 440 KAGHAD}.

## How it changed history
It shifted civilisation's writing material to an abundant medium and prepared the age of the paper book — without making every later mill a direct branch of Samarkand {cite:carter-invention-printing-1925 pp. 98–99}.

## Who invented it?
The Chinese invented paper; the Islamic world's role was to receive, adapt and spread the craft, and the prisoner story is a transmitted account rather than a proved foundation act {cite:carter-invention-printing-1925 pp. 97–98} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV p. 440 KAGHAD}.

## Today
The world paper industry still lifts suspended fibre onto a mould and dries the sheet, at industrial scale; the Meros workshop at Konigil near Samarkand keeps the handcraft visible {cite:commons-konigil-paper-enric Description}.

## Surviving examples
The oldest paper bearing Arabic writing found in Egypt dates from ⁦(180–200/796–815)⁩, and masses of early papers have been recovered from Egyptian town rubbish heaps {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV p. 440 KAGHAD}.

## In culture
'Samarkand paper' became a proverb in itself: al-Jahiz paired it with Egyptian papyrus as the emblematic writing material of east and west {cite:carter-invention-printing-1925 pp. 98–99}. The patron-named varieties — fir'awni, sulaymani, ja'fari, talhi, tahiri, nuhi — preserve the craft's memory {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV p. 440 KAGHAD}.

## For young readers
People once wrote on costly animal skins and on Egyptian plant rolls. An old report says Chinese craftsmen captured in a battle in 751 taught the people of Samarkand to make paper from worn-out rags. Their paper became the most famous of all, and the craft travelled to Baghdad, Damascus, Egypt and Spain.
