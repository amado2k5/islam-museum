## Summary
The kamal — pilots called it simply khashaba, "the piece of wood" — is a small wooden rectangle with a knotted string through its middle, and it is one of the most consequential two ounces of timber in the history of the sea. With it an Arab pilot measured the pole star's height above the horizon, and therefore his latitude, by sliding the board along the string held in his teeth. Invented by Arab navigators of the late ninth century, it made the earliest known latitude sailing possible — the first step towards quantitative navigation {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en} {cite:mcgrail-boats-of-world-2004}.

## What it is
A card of wood about five by two-and-a-half centimetres, pierced in the centre by a string carrying equally spaced knots. Each knot marks one isba' — a "finger" of altitude, about a degree and a half — the same finger-width unit pilots had always used to gauge a star's height at arm's length {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en}.

## The world before it
Indian Ocean pilots read the heavens by eye: the pole star at "so many fingers" over the horizon, the southern stars appearing and setting in sequence. The eye is a fine instrument but the arm is a poor ruler — a hand's width at arm's length varies, and on a dark rolling sea nobody could lay a mark where "ten fingers" was {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en}.

## The story
McGrail dates the kamal's origin to Arab navigators of the late ninth century and its Indian Ocean employment to the tenth. For centuries it was the pilot's own instrument, carried in the seabag, recalibrated by each man against his own arm. Indian navigators adopted it soon after the Arabs; Chinese navigators followed some time before the sixteenth century. In Ibn Majid's age — the great fifteenth-century pilot of the Indian Ocean — the kamal and its knot-system were the normal language of a ship's position {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en} {cite:mcgrail-boats-of-world-2004}.

## The maker(s)
Anonymous pilots. No treatise names an inventor; the kamal belongs to the unwritten workshop of the monsoon routes — the Arab shipmasters of the Gulf and the western Indian Ocean who needed a number, not an estimate, for their latitude {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en} {cite:mcgrail-boats-of-world-2004}.

## How it works
Hold the string's end in your teeth — the mouth is a fixed point that never shakes. Slide the board along the taut string until its lower edge sits exactly on the horizon and its upper edge covers the pole star. Count the knots between teeth and board: that is the star's altitude in fingers, and the altitude of the pole is your latitude. Heading for a known port, you tie a special knot for its parallel and sail until the board tells you that you have arrived {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en}.

## The spread
Indian pilots took it up early — the same monsoon routes, the same sky. Chinese navigators adopted it before the sixteenth century, developing their own finger-and-jiao units. Europe never took it up widely: the board is too small to read Polaris accurately in northern latitudes where the star rides high, and the cross-staff and backstaff filled that gap instead {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en}.

## What it was built on
The pilot's naked eye and the isba' unit — finger-widths at arm's length, roughly a degree and a half each, an angle that stays surprisingly constant from person to person. The kamal simply fixed that variable arm to a constant string {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en}.

## What was built on it
The cross-staff and backstaff — the same sliding-altitude principle scaled up for higher latitudes — and ultimately the sextant, which still measures the same angle the kamal measured, only with mirrors {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en}.

## Impact on daily life
For the sailor it changed everything: a voyage plan could now be "run down the parallel" — sail to the known latitude of your destination, then turn east or west for the coast — instead of a coastwise creep or a lucky guess. Latitude became cargo: a number you could carry, sell a route by and trust a ship to {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en} {cite:mcgrail-boats-of-world-2004}.

## How it changed history
It is the earliest known instrument of latitude sailing — the moment navigation became quantitative. Every later altitude-measuring navigator's tool, from cross-staff to sextant to GPS's satellite geometry, stands in its line {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en} {cite:mcgrail-boats-of-world-2004}.

## Who invented it?
The Arab navigators of the Indian Ocean, to McGrail's dating — the attribution is a craft's, not a person's. Indian adoption followed quickly enough that some writers describe a shared ocean tradition; the record's honest answer is: invented by Arab pilots, borrowed by the whole ocean {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en} {cite:mcgrail-boats-of-world-2004}.

## Today
The kamal is still made and taught — in traditional-seamanship schools, in sea-kayak navigation courses where it serves as the no-battery fallback, and among Gulf seafarers who keep it as living heritage {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en}.

## Surviving examples
Working kamals survive in maritime collections and in the hands of traditional pilots; being a consumable board of plain wood, medieval examples do not survive as museum pieces — the tradition is the specimen {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en}.

## In culture
Among the Arabs of the Gulf the kamal is remembered as the emblem of the old pilotage — the little board that stood for a whole science of stars, seasons and monsoon winds {cite:wikipedia-kamal-en}.

## For young readers
How can a piece of wood and a string tell you where you are on the ocean? Hold the string in your teeth, slide the little board along it until its bottom edge rests on the horizon and its top edge touches the pole star, then count the knots. The number of knots tells you how far north or south you are — sailors did this for a thousand years before anyone had a compass or a map.
