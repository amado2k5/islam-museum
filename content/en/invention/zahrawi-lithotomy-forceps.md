## Summary

In the surgical treatise of al-Tasrif, al-Zahrawi describes specialised instruments for extracting bladder and urethral stones — long forceps to grip stones in the bladder, and a diamond-tipped drill he calls al-Mishab for stones lodged in the urethra. The instruments are drawn in the manuscript, making this the earliest documented specialised toolkit for stone surgery in the surgical literature studied {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003}.

## What it is

A set of purpose-designed surgical tools for lithotomy: slender forceps introduced through the urethra or a perineal incision to seize bladder stones, and the al-Mishab — a fine drill worked by hand to pierce and break up a stone blocking the urinary passage {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003}.

## The world before it

Stone-cutting was ancient — the Hippocratic oath already names specialists in "cutting for the stone" — but it was a blind operation done with knife and finger. No specialised extraction instruments are documented in the earlier texts al-Zahrawi's record was measured against.

## The story

In Cordoba in the decades around 1000 CE, al-Zahrawi compiled al-Tasrif's surgical volume — the part "for him who is not skilled in anatomy" — describing each operation with the instrument it needs, drawn so a workshop could copy it. The stone chapter details both the forceps method and, for stones that will not pass, the al-Mishab drill {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## The maker(s)

Al-Zahrawi (Abu al-Qasim Khalaf ibn al-Abbas al-Zahrawi, d. c. 1013), court physician at Cordoba. The instruments are recorded in his own treatise; his Muslim identity is on the person record.

## How it works

For a urethral stone, the Mishab's slender shaft is passed down the passage until its point meets the stone; gentle turning drills a hole through which the stone can be broken or flushed free — a minimally invasive alternative to cutting. For bladder stones, the forceps grasp and extract through incision or the natural channel {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003}.

## The spread

Al-Tasrif's surgical chapters were translated into Latin by Gerard of Cremona and remained a surgical reference in Europe for five centuries; its instrument drawings were copied into later manuscripts and printed editions {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861}.

## What it was built on

On the ancient lithotomy operation described in the Hippocratic corpus — the procedure was old — and on the instrument tradition al-Zahrawi systematised; the specialised tooling is his documented contribution.

## What was built on it

The principle of instrument-per-condition in urological surgery: later forceps, scoops and scopes, down to modern endoscopic and laser lithotripsy, follow the same idea of a tool designed to reach and break a stone.

## Impact on daily life

Stone surgery was among the most feared operations; specialised instruments that could reach, grip or drill a stone lowered its danger — the beginning of urology as an instrument craft.

## How it changed history

It is the earliest documented specialised instrument set for stone removal — a landmark in the history of urological surgery {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003}.

## Who invented it?

Al-Zahrawi describes the instruments as his own in al-Tasrif and draws them; earlier hints of drilling tools are unverified. The claim here is textual — the earliest documented specialised toolkit read — not an absolute priority claim.

## Today

The Mishab's job is done by ultrasonic and laser lithotripters; the idea — a slender instrument that reaches the stone and breaks it in place — is unchanged.

## Surviving examples

The instrument drawings in al-Tasrif manuscripts — the Topkapi copy is the famous witness — reproduced in the Spink & Lewis edition {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## In culture

The Mishab appears in histories of surgery as the emblem of al-Zahrawi's inventiveness — a named tool a thousand years old.

## For young readers

A stone stuck inside the body is agony — and a thousand years ago, getting it out was dangerous. Al-Zahrawi invented special tools: tongs to grab the stone, and a tiny drill to break it where it sat. Instead of guessing, the surgeon now had the right tool for the job.
