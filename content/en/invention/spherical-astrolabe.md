## Summary
The spherical astrolabe shows the heavens and the earth as a real globe — no flattening, no projection — rotating under a pierced shell that carries the ecliptic and the stars. It was developed inside Islamic astronomy through a chain of treatises running from Qusta ibn Luqa in the early tenth century to al-Marrakushi in the thirteenth, and it reached the Latin world in the star-books of Alfonso X of Castile {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it is
An astronomical instrument: a metal globe engraved with horizon, meridian and altitude circles, over which turns an openwork "spider" holding the ecliptic, the equator and pointers for the fixed stars, with an alidade for taking readings {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. Where the ordinary flat astrolabe squashes the sphere onto a plate by stereographic projection, this one keeps the sphere itself.

## The world before it
Astronomers already had two ways of modelling the sky: the celestial globe, which is a solid star-map, and the armillary sphere, a skeleton of rings. EI2 connects the spherical astrolabe's idea — through Tannery's research — to the Greek hemispherical sundial, the scaphe or arachne, a bowl whose shadow traced the hours {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. The flat astrolabe, the workhorse of medieval astronomy, was the rival it never beat.

## The story
The instrument enters the written record in Abbasid treatises. The Fihrist of Ibn al-Nadim even names Ptolemy as its first maker — EI2 corrects this as a confusion with the armillary instrument of Almagest V.1. The real documented chain is Muslim: Qusta ibn Luqa (d. c. 912), al-Nayrizi (d. c. 922), al-Biruni's comprehensive work on astrolabe construction, and finally the Moroccan al-Marrakushi (d. c. 1262), whose version replaced the alidade with a turning rule and gnomon {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. In thirteenth-century Castile the Libros del Saber of Alfonso X described the astrolabio redondo, compiled by the Jewish astronomer Isaac ibn Sid — the design crossing languages without losing its parts.

## The maker(s)
No single maker. The instrument is a collaboration across three centuries of treatise-writers: Qusta the Baalbek translator, al-Nayrizi the mathematician of the Baghdad tradition, al-Biruni the encyclopedist, and al-Marrakushi the Moroccan instrument-writer — each adding a chapter rather than a prototype {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it works
The globe carries the horizon and the altitude circles (almucantars) on its upper half and the lines of the unequal hours below; holes bored through the meridian let the axis be set for the observer's latitude. The openwork spider, holding ecliptic, equator and star-pointers, rotates around that axis to imitate the turning sky; the alidade — or al-Marrakushi's rule with its little gnomon — reads off positions {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The spread
The idea travelled on paper, not in brass. From Abbasid Iraq it passed to the Maghreb with al-Marrakushi and to Castile in the Alphonsine corpus, and it lived on in Ottoman instrument-making — the sole surviving specimen, dated 885/1480-81, is an Ottoman work now in Oxford's Museum of the History of Science {cite:king-synchrony-heavens-2004}. It never spread widely as a working tool: EI2 notes it did the same jobs as the flat astrolabe while being far less handy {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it was built on
The celestial globe for its star-map; the armillary sphere for its rings; the scaphe sundial for the idea of a bowl-shaped sky; and the mature spherical geometry of the Islamic astronomy books, which treated the sphere as confidently as the plane {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What was built on it
The Alphonsine astrolabio redondo, which added a shadow-square and a calendar plate to the Islamic design; the Ottoman specimen of 1480-81; and, at a distance, every teaching globe and armillary display that lets a student turn the sky with a finger {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Impact on daily life
Very little directly — this was always an instrument of scholars' tables, not markets or mosques. Its importance is what it proves about the workshop culture that produced it: metal-workers who could engrave a sphere and fit a pierced shell to it, and a readership for books that assumed such craft {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it changed history
It marks a ceiling of astronomical craftsmanship: the moment when Islamic instrument-makers tried to carry the whole sky in a single ball of metal. The planispheric astrolabe won the practical contest and dominated for a thousand years; the spherical one survives as the rarer, harder proof of what the craft could attempt {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Who invented it?
Honestly contested. The medieval Fihrist credited Ptolemy — a confusion with his armillary instrument, says EI2. Tannery derived the idea from the Greek bowl-sundial; the documented development is Islamic, from Qusta to al-Marrakushi. The truthful answer is: an old Mediterranean idea grown into an instrument inside Arabic astronomy {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Today
The instrument is extinct. One Ottoman example survives in Oxford, studied by David King in his 1962 paper on the fifteenth-century spherical astrolabe; everything else lives in the treatise diagrams {cite:king-synchrony-heavens-2004}. Its descendants are conceptual: star-globes, orreries and planetarium projectors that show the sphere itself.

## Surviving examples
A single dated specimen: the Ottoman spherical astrolabe of 885/1480-81 in the Museum of the History of Science, Oxford — EI2's article, written before the specimen's standing was settled, states that no spherical astrolabes seem to have been preserved; King's bibliography records the study that made the Oxford piece the standard witness {cite:king-synchrony-heavens-2004}. A fragment of a West-Islamic example was published by Canobbio in 1976 {cite:king-synchrony-heavens-2004}.

## In culture
The astrolabe is the emblem of Islamic astronomy — Chaucer wrote the first English technical treatise on its flat cousin — and the spherical type is its most elegant branch: the sky held in the hand as a ball of pierced brass {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
Imagine holding the whole sky in your hands as a hollow metal ball, with a second shell full of holes spinning over it to show where the stars are. That is a spherical astrolabe. Astronomers in the Muslim world wrote about it a thousand years ago, and today only one is left — in a museum in Oxford.
