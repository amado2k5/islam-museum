## Summary

In Book One of the Canon, the second teaching opens by defining disease, cause, symptom and sign, then divides the diseases of structure into four general kinds and three genera {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–107}. This division is not a modern clinical taxonomy and not an entry to practice; it is a load-bearing element of medicine itself inside the theory of the humours {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–107}. It is read in the Dar al-Kutub al-Ilmiyya 1999 edition on scans 103 to 107, where the definitions, kinds and genera appear in the order the author arranged {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–107}.

## What it is

The teaching here is the second of the second teaching in Book One, and it contains eight chapters in the text's own order {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.103}. Chapter one defines disease as a disturbance of the body's natural structure, the cause as what corrupts that structure, the symptom as what appears of the disease's effect, and the sign as what indicates an absent state {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.103}. Chapter two divides the diseases of structure into four general kinds: what happens by increase or decrease in the quantity of a component, what happens by change in its kind, what happens by corruption of its unity, and what happens by delay or hastening of its term {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.104–105}. Chapter three gathers these kinds into three genera: diseases of diversity, diseases of composition, and diseases of expansion {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.105–107}.

## The world before it

The divisions and definitions of disease are not new in Greek-Arabic medicine: Hippocrates, Galen and those before them built on the idea that disease is a disturbance of the body's temperament {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–107}. The Canon did not invent this frame; it chose to make it an independent teaching inside the architecture of Book One, after the definitions of the basic concepts {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.103}. It is therefore not credited with inventing modern disease classification nor the first system of disease names; only its methodical ordering is attributed to it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–107}.

## The story

The place of Book One of the Canon in the first book of medicine is a general teaching place: it defines concepts first, then divides diseases, then teaches the reading of signs {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–154}. The study places the completion of the Canon at Hamadan without dating this particular teaching {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. The pages read are the Dar al-Kutub al-Ilmiyya 1999 edition, and scan 103 opens a complete chapter rather than an isolated paragraph {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.103}. We do not claim Ibn Sina composed this ordering in a single sitting or that it was printed exactly as he wrote it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–107}.

## How it works

**First: defining disease, cause, symptom and sign.** Chapter one opens by defining disease as a disturbance of the body's natural structure, the cause as what corrupts the structure, the symptom as what appears of the disease's effect, and the sign as what indicates an absent state {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.103}. These definitions are not mere names; they are the conditions for understanding everything that follows {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.103}.

**Second: the diseases of structure in four kinds.** Chapter two divides the diseases of structure into four general kinds: what happens by increase or decrease in the quantity of a component, what happens by change in its kind, what happens by corruption of its unity, and what happens by delay or hastening of its term {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.104–105}. Each kind has examples of known diseases, so the classification becomes applicable rather than merely theoretical {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.104–105}.

**Third: three genera of diseases.** Chapter three gathers these kinds into three genera: diseases of diversity, diseases of composition, and diseases of expansion {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.105–107}. Diseases of diversity afflict the body's own structure; diseases of composition afflict the connection of the members; diseases of expansion afflict the balance of the whole body {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.105–107}. This division makes every disease readable on two levels: its kind and its seat {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.105–107}.

**Fourth: diseases of diversity.** Diseases of diversity afflict the body's own structure: a member may weaken or be corrupted, or its shape or size may change {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.106}. These diseases are read at the level of the single member, so the question asked is: is the member sound or defective? {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.106}.

**Fifth: diseases of composition.** Diseases of composition afflict the connection of the members: the joining of one member to another may be delayed, hastened or corrupted {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.106}. These diseases are read at the level of the relation between members, so the question asked is: is the connection sound or defective? {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.106}.

**Sixth: diseases of expansion.** Diseases of expansion afflict the balance of the whole body: the temperament may be disturbed or the surrounding air may change {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.107}. These diseases are read at the level of the whole body, so the question asked is: is the balance sound or defective? {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.107}.

## The spread

The study discusses Book One of the Canon as the foundation of medical teaching for about seven centuries in the school and in the Latin edition {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. But a book's spread does not prove that every reader applied these chapters or recorded comparative results {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. The specific evidence here is that this teaching survives in a printed edition and is codified inside Book One's architecture — not a complete map of disease-classification practice across the centuries {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–107}. We therefore do not fill the gaps of reception with an invented chain of institutions or pupils {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}.

## What it was built on

The teaching builds on the theory of the temperaments: every chapter assumes that bodies differ in their humours and that disease is a disturbance in their balance {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.19}. The division itself rests on the idea that disease is not one thing but multiple states requiring different tools to understand {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–107}. Read outside this frame it loses its historical meaning {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–107}.

## What was built on it

Modern sources discuss that the Canon remained a medical reference for centuries, but that does not prove these chapters became a universally followed protocol {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. The similarity of disease-classification questions to modern medicine does not prove direct descent from this teaching {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. For the same context, the question of similar problems is not proof of lineage {cite:britannica-avicenna final-evidence-discussion}.

## Impact on daily life

The directly demonstrable impact is organisational: it made the definition of disease, cause, symptom and sign the first step of everything after it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.103}. This ordering can make the reader more aware that every disease has a kind and a seat, but it is not by itself evidence of more accurate diagnosis in any given population; the teaching offers no accuracy figures or clinical results attributed to its application {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–107}. The daily contribution described is the organisation of questioning, not a diagnostic guarantee and not a promise for every patient {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}.

## How it changed history

The teaching shows that classifying disease is part of the history of written medicine: the author did not content himself with describing disease and its cure, but arranged chapters to train the reader in understanding the kinds and genera of disease {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–107}. This ordering inside Book One — which remained a foundation for seven centuries — gives it real historical weight without turning medical history into a race for the title of first classifier of diseases {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. The precise estimate keeps the humoral heritage it used and the distance between a reasonable historical argument and verification tools it does not describe {cite:britannica-avicenna final-evidence-discussion}.

## Who invented it?

What is proven is that Ibn Sina presented these chapters in the Canon, not that a survey of every earlier text proves he was the first person to frame any system for classifying disease {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–107} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. The record therefore treats the contribution as systematic and an improvement inside an older heritage, not as the sole origin of all disease-classification science {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. This is not the invention of modern classification nor the first system of disease names; it is material Ibn Sina read and gathered in a teaching order {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–107}.

## For young readers

**A modern museum example, not an experiment from the Canon:** imagine you are in an old library and want to find a book about the stars. You do not open it in one place; you first look in the subject index, then in the name index, then in the date index. Ibn Sina was teaching physicians to do something similar with disease: first know what disease is, then divide it into kinds, then read it. We understand that classification for the ancients was an ordering of knowledge, not a medical prescription; we do not use his method today {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.103}.
