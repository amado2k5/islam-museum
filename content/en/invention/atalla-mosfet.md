## Summary
The MOSFET — the metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor — is the little insulated-gate switch at the heart of every modern chip. Mohamed Atalla and Dawon Kahng built the first working one at Bell Labs in 1959, on the back of Atalla's surface-passivation work; it is now the most common transistor ever made and, by count of manufactured units, the most-fabricated device in human history {cite:wikipedia-mosfet-en} {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-atalla-en}.

## What it is
A field-effect transistor: a tiny device in which a voltage on an insulated gate controls how much current flows through a silicon channel, letting it switch or amplify a signal. It needs almost no input current to hold a state, can be made in complementary pairs ⁦(CMOS)⁩ that sip power, and packs down to sizes where billions sit on a single chip {cite:wikipedia-mosfet-en}.

## The world before it
Physicist Julius Edgar Lilienfeld had proposed the field-effect idea in 1925, but nobody could build one: the silicon surface trapped electrons at unstable "surface states", so the signal never reached the semiconductor. Germanium devices ruled, and the field-effect transistor remained a concept {cite:wikipedia-mosfet-en}.

## The story
At Bell Labs in Murray Hill, the Egyptian engineer Mohamed Atalla — trained in mechanical engineering, self-taught in physical chemistry — led a small team of new recruits, including Dawon Kahng, that senior management barely took seriously. Between 1956 and 1960 Atalla solved the surface-state problem by growing a protective silicon-dioxide layer — thermal passivation — and in 1959 he and Kahng demonstrated the first working MOSFET with both PMOS and NMOS processes. Bell overlooked it; Atalla left for Hewlett-Packard in 1962. Recognition came later: a 1975 Franklin medal, the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 2009, and an IEEE milestone in 2014 {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-atalla-en} {cite:wikipedia-dawon-kahng-en}.

## The maker(s)
The sources credit the pair jointly. Mohamed Atalla ⁦(1924–2009)⁩, born in Port Said, supplied the passivation breakthrough and the team leadership; Dawon Kahng ⁦(1931–1992)⁩, born in what is now Seoul, fabricated the PMOS and NMOS devices — and went on to co-invent the floating-gate memory cell. ## How it works
Atalla's oxide layer quieted the silicon surface; a metal gate laid over that insulation then pulls or repels charge carriers in the channel beneath by pure voltage — a switch that uses almost no standing power, which is exactly why it could shrink and multiply for decades {cite:wikipedia-mosfet-en}.

## The spread
From one Bell Labs device it became the industry's default switch: MOS integrated circuits were proposed by the same pair in 1960, CMOS logic made low-power computing routine, and the transistor shrank through every generation of microprocessor and memory until today it is the working element of essentially all electronics {cite:wikipedia-mosfet-en} {cite:wikipedia-dawon-kahng-en}.

## What it was built on
Lilienfeld's 1925 concept, the transistor era at Bell Labs, and above all Atalla's own thermal-oxidation passivation — without which the field-effect device stayed theoretical {cite:wikipedia-mosfet-en} {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-atalla-en}.

## What was built on it
The whole digital stack: MOS integrated circuits, CMOS logic, microprocessors, memory chips, the floating-gate cell that became flash storage, and the FinFET generations that still carry the design forward {cite:wikipedia-mosfet-en} {cite:wikipedia-dawon-kahng-en}.

## Impact on daily life
Every phone call, text, photo and payment today passes through MOSFETs by the billion; the device also freed computing from rooms full of hot, power-hungry components and put it on the desk, then in the pocket {cite:wikipedia-mosfet-en} {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-atalla-en}.

## How it changed history
It launched the silicon revolution: Moore's Law was, in practice, the MOSFET shrinking without end for sixty years. The recorded article calls it the most common transistor by far — billions on each chip — and counts the 1959 device among the inventions that changed life and culture worldwide {cite:wikipedia-mosfet-en} {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-atalla-en}.

## Who invented it?
Not contested: the sources name Atalla and Kahng together at Bell Labs in 1959, and both later entered the National Inventors Hall of Fame. Lilienfeld had the idea first but never a working device — the record keeps concept and construction distinct {cite:wikipedia-mosfet-en} {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-atalla-en} {cite:wikipedia-dawon-kahng-en}.

## Today
MOSFETs in CMOS form still build every processor and memory; the 2014 IEEE milestone marks the 1959 invention officially, and the same insulated-gate geometry persists inside the newest FinFETs {cite:wikipedia-mosfet-en}.

## Surviving examples
No single museum object anchors it — the record is a documented device-type; its "survivors" are the fabrication lines still printing its descendants and the standards record (IEEE milestone, Inventors Hall of Fame entries) {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-atalla-en} {cite:wikipedia-dawon-kahng-en}.

## In culture
Atalla's later fame as the "Father of the PIN" and a "Sultan of Silicon" made him a figure in Egyptian popular memory too — Arabic media mark him as the Port Said engineer who changed the world's electronics {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-atalla-en}.

## For young readers
In 1959 an Egyptian engineer named Mohamed Atalla and his Korean colleague Dawon Kahng built an electronic switch too small to see. That little switch is called the MOSFET, and today it is inside every phone, computer, car and game — by the billion. It is the most-made thing in all of history.
