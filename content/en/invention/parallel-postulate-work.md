## Summary
A four-century programme around Euclid's fifth postulate: Ibn al-Haytham's (965-1039) attempted proof by contradiction, which introduced motion into geometry; Umar Khayyam's (d. 1123) explicit substitute axiom on convergent lines with the first study of the quadrilateral later named for him and Saccheri; Nasir al-Din al-Tusi's 1250 Discussion Which Removes Doubt; and Sadr al-Din's 1298 recasting, printed at Rome in 1594, which became Saccheri's starting point toward non-Euclidean geometry {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en} {cite:rosenfeld-youschkevitch-geometry-1996} {cite:katz-history-mathematics-1998}.

## What it is
A sequence of treatises criticising Euclid's fifth postulate: attempted proofs, explicit substitute axioms, and the analysis of three summit-angle cases of a quadrilateral (right, acute, obtuse) as testable hypotheses {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en}.

## The world before it
The fifth postulate was a known problem since Proclus (410-485), who recorded Ptolemy's failed proof and offered a false one of his own; the Arabic commentators inherited an open question, not a finished rule {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en}.

## The story
Ibn al-Haytham attempted a proof by contradiction, introducing motion and transformation into geometry and building his quadrilateral; a century later Khayyam substituted an explicit axiom on convergent lines and studied the three cases; in 1250 al-Tusi criticised Khayyam's attempt and tried his own contradiction proof; in 1298 Sadr al-Din recast the work, and the text printed at Rome in 1594 landed on Saccheri's desk {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en} {cite:katz-history-mathematics-1998}.

## The maker(s)
Three named figures, each with a treatise on the subject: Ibn al-Haytham, who opened the Islamic critique; Umar Khayyam, author of the substitute axiom and the quadrilateral; Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, author of the Discussion Which Removes Doubt — completed by his son Sadr al-Din's final recasting {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en}.

## How it works
Rather than accept the postulate, assume its contrary: take a quadrilateral with two right base angles and test the summit angles — right returns Euclid, acute or obtuse generates two candidate geometries; the same dissection Saccheri and Lambert would use {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en}.

## The spread
From Baghdad and Basra to Khurasan and Maragha, then via the 1594 Rome text to European geometers: Saccheri, Vitale (1680) and Lambert (1766) {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en}.

## What it was built on
The Arabic translations and commentaries of the Elements, the inherited record of failed proofs from Proclus, and the period's own tools of reductio and explicit axiomatics {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en}.

## What was built on it
A direct line to Saccheri (the 1594 Rome text on his desk) and Lambert, then Lobachevsky's (1829) and Bolyai's (1831) geometries and Beltrami's independence proof (1868) {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en} {cite:katz-history-mathematics-1998}.

## Impact on daily life
Theoretical work, not daily practice; its methodological impact is that mathematics learned not to accept the self-evident unexamined, and to study alternative hypotheses rather than dismiss them {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en}.

## How it changed history
It turned postulate-criticism into a research programme; it handed Europeans the tools (the two quadrilaterals, the three cases) with which non-Euclidean geometry was discovered {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en} {cite:katz-history-mathematics-1998}.

## Who invented it?
No single inventor; it is cumulative work carrying the names of Ibn al-Haytham, Khayyam, al-Tusi and Sadr al-Din, each with a documented treatise {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en}.

## Today
It is read as a foundational chapter in the prehistory of non-Euclidean geometry, presented in histories of mathematics before the European names {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en} {cite:katz-history-mathematics-1998}.

## Surviving examples
The treatises survive: Ibn al-Haytham's work, Khayyam's Explanations of the Difficulties in the Postulates of Euclid, al-Tusi's Discussion Which Removes Doubt, and Sadr al-Din's 1298 text printed at Rome in 1594 {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en}.

## In culture
The parallel postulate became the standard example of the contest between self-evidence and proof in the philosophy of mathematics {cite:wikipedia-parallel-postulate-en}.

## For young readers
Euclid's rule says parallel lines never meet. Ibn al-Haytham, Khayyam and al-Tusi spent centuries trying to prove it, and the shapes they drew were used by Europeans hundreds of years later to discover whole new geometries.
