## Summary
The torquetum — the Latin texts spell it turketum — is a medieval observing instrument that reads the sky in three coordinate systems at once: horizon, equatorial and ecliptic. An astronomer sighted a star through it and could read off, mechanically, the numbers another astronomer would need in whichever system he worked — in effect an analogue computer for the heavens. Where it was born is genuinely unsettled: the earliest surviving descriptions are Latin texts of the 1280s, while Renaissance tradition credited the twelfth-century Seville astronomer Jabir ibn Aflah {cite:wikipedia-torquetum-en} {cite:lorch-jabir-torquetum-1976}.

## What it is
A set of graduated plates and rings mounted on three levels. The base plate is levelled to the horizon; above it a second assembly turns in the equatorial plane; above that a third works in the ecliptic plane of the zodiac. A sighting rule and scales let the observer take a star's position in one system and convert it into the others without computation {cite:wikipedia-torquetum-en} {cite:lorch-jabir-torquetum-1976}.

## The world before it
Astronomers measured the sky in three different coordinate languages — altitude and azimuth above the local horizon, right ascension and declination on the celestial equator, longitude and latitude along the ecliptic — and converting between them meant spherical trigonometry or the astrolabe's graphical projection. No single device did the conversion mechanically {cite:lorch-jabir-torquetum-1976}.

## The story
The first witnesses are two Latin treatises of the 1280s, probably written near Paris: Franco of Poland's dated text of 1284 and the undated work of Bernard of Verdun. Neither names an inventor nor explains the strange word turketum. The attribution story came later: Regiomontanus in 1469 credited "Geber the Spaniard" — Jabir ibn Aflah of Seville, first half of the twelfth century — and Tycho Brahe in 1598 guessed "Arabians or Chaldeans" of the twelfth century. Modern scholarship under Lorch examined Jabir's own instrument in the Islah al-Majisti and found it similar in function — likely the torquetum's inspiration rather than the torquetum itself. On the Islamic side, the fifteenth-century Cairo muwaqqit 'Izz al-Din al-Wafa'i built an equatorial armilla that the historian Tekeli studied in direct comparison with the torquetum {cite:wikipedia-torquetum-en} {cite:lorch-jabir-torquetum-1976} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The maker(s)
No secure name. Jabir ibn Aflah carries the oldest attribution — made two and a half centuries after his death — while the dated texts are anonymous. The Muslim instrument-makers documented beside the type are al-Wafa'i of Mamluk Cairo, whose equatorial armilla Tekeli compared with it, and Jamal al-Din, credited with carrying a simplified version to the Yuan observatory in 1267 {cite:wikipedia-torquetum-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it works
Level the base to the horizon. The middle assembly, tilted to the pole, measures equatorial angles directly. The top assembly, tilted further to the ecliptic, measures along the zodiac. Because each level is graduated, a single sighting fixes the body in all three frames — the instrument computes by its geometry what the astrolabe computed by its lines {cite:lorch-jabir-torquetum-1976} {cite:wikipedia-torquetum-en}.

## The spread
From the anonymous Latin texts of the 1280s the instrument spread through European astronomy: Peter of Limoges observed Halley's comet with it at the turn of the fourteenth century, John of Murs cited it in defence of observational astronomy, and a simplified torquetum-type instrument stood in the Chinese imperial observatory of 1276-1279 — Needham credits Jamal al-Din as its 1267 transmitter {cite:wikipedia-torquetum-en}.

## What it was built on
Lorch characterised it as the combination of Ptolemy's astrolabon — an observing instrument of rings — with the coordinate conversion the plane astrolabe performs graphically. On the Arabic side it stands beside Jabir ibn Aflah's described device and the equatorial armillae of the muwaqqit tradition {cite:lorch-jabir-torquetum-1976} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What was built on it
Richard of Wallingford's fourteenth-century rectangulus performed the same task with linear scales read by plumb lines — a simplification born of the torquetum. The surviving specimens, all sixteenth-century, show Erasmus Habermel's redesign letting one mounting serve all three scales; the most famous is Praetorius's 1568 Nuremberg torquetum {cite:wikipedia-torquetum-en}.

## Impact on daily life
A specialist's tool, not a household one: it lived in observatories and princely collections, and its public life was in demonstrations, comet observations and the learned imagery of books like Apian's Astronomicum Caesareum {cite:wikipedia-torquetum-en}.

## How it changed history
It proved that coordinate conversion — the hardest routine work of medieval astronomy — could be mechanised. That idea, the analogue computer, is the thread running from the turketum of 1284 to the equatorially mounted telescope {cite:lorch-jabir-torquetum-1976} {cite:wikipedia-torquetum-en}.

## Who invented it?
Honestly contested. The museum records three competing witnesses: Regiomontanus's attribution to Jabir ibn Aflah of Seville; the anonymous Latin texts of the 1280s that name no one; and the documented Islamic-side parallel in al-Wafa'i's equatorial armilla. Lorch's judgement — Jabir's instrument inspired the type — is the most careful available {cite:wikipedia-torquetum-en} {cite:lorch-jabir-torquetum-1976} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Today
All surviving torqueta are sixteenth-century instruments in museum collections — the Praetorius example of 1568 at Nuremberg chief among them — and the instrument lives on as an icon of pre-telescopic observing: it is the device on the table in Holbein's Ambassadors of 1533 {cite:wikipedia-torquetum-en}.

## Surviving examples
Sixteenth-century specimens only, including Johannes Praetorius's torquetum of 1568; al-Wafa'i's equatorial armilla belongs to the same family on the Islamic side {cite:wikipedia-torquetum-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## In culture
The torquetum became the emblematic astronomer's instrument — the object a painter chose to say "this man measures the heavens" — in Holbein's Ambassadors and on the title page of Apian's imperial astronomy {cite:wikipedia-torquetum-en}.

## For young readers
Imagine a machine like three stacked rings on a table. Point it at a star, and the rings give you the star's address in three different sky-languages at once — no maths needed. Astronomers used it for more than two hundred years, from a time when nobody had a telescope. Nobody is quite sure who invented it — which makes it a mystery machine as well as a clever one.
