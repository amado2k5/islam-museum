## Summary
Writing about the year 985, the geographer al-Muqaddasi counted among the wonders of Khurasan "the windmills at Sistan and Bushanj" — mills whose stones were turned not by water but by the wind. It is the earliest written attestation of working windmills in any literature. EI2 confirms that wind-powered mills first appeared in Islamic Persia, exactly where the geography demanded them: a land of fierce seasonal wind and scarce running water {cite:muqaddasi-ahsan-taqasim-bga-1877} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it is
A vertical-axis mill — the panemone type. A bundle of sails on an upright shaft sits inside a slotted wall; the wall admits the prevailing wind onto half the rotor only, so the sails are pushed around on one side and sheltered on the return side. The shaft drops straight down onto the millstones — the same direct drive as the old horizontal waterwheel, with the river replaced by the wind {cite:wikipedia-windmill-en}.

## The world before it
Milling power meant water or muscle. The horizontal watermill and the animal quern had ground the region's grain for centuries; Roman engineers knew the vertical-wheel watermill too. Nobody, as far as the written record shows, had yet used wind as an industrial power source. In most of the world that made sense — winds are fickle. In Sistan it did not {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The story
Sistan, the basin of the Helmand river on today's Iran-Afghanistan border, has a famous climate feature: the bad-i sad-o-bist roz, the "wind of a hundred and twenty days" that blows relentlessly through the summer. When al-Muqaddasi travelled Khurasan in the later tenth century he listed the region's marvels — and among the tombs and fortresses he put "windmills at Sistan and Bushanj" (the Persian town of the same flat, windy country) {cite:muqaddasi-ahsan-taqasim-bga-1877}. Later geographers repeated the picture, and scholarship (Hassan and Hill; Lohrmann's study of the sources) dates the clear evidence for the vertical-axle type to the ninth century {cite:wikipedia-windmill-en}.

## The maker(s)
Anonymous Sistanian millwrights. No text names an inventor; the first written witness is the travelling geographer al-Muqaddasi, whose book was compiled at Shiraz about 375/985 {cite:muqaddasi-ahsan-taqasim-bga-1877}. A much later story makes the Iranian captive Abu Lu'lu'a promise the caliph Umar "a mill the world will talk of" — the literature itself flags it as a possible later embellishment, and the museum records it as legend, not document {cite:wikipedia-windmill-en}.

## How it works
The trick is shielding. A vertical rotor cannot steer into the wind like a European post mill, so the Sistan builders wrapped the sails in a high wall with slots facing the prevailing wind. Air funnels in, drives the exposed half of the vane-wheel, and the sheltered half swings back round unresisted. It is inefficient — only half the rotor works at a time — but the wind in Sistan is so constant that efficiency hardly mattered {cite:wikipedia-windmill-en}.

## The spread
The mill stayed regional for centuries: Sistan, Khurasan, and down the wind corridor toward the Indus. EI2 places the type's first use in Islamic Persia and notes that mills of wind and water both ran across the medieval Islamic world wherever each was cheaper {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. The windmill idea travelled to India and China, and — by a route scholars still debate — northwest Europe saw its first recorded windmill at Weedley, Yorkshire, in 1185, of the quite different horizontal-axis type {cite:wikipedia-windmill-en}.

## What it was built on
The horizontal watermill's layout: a wheel driving a shaft that turns the upper stone directly, no gearing. The Sistanian contribution was to rotate the whole concept ninety degrees and feed it air instead of water — plus the wall-and-slot intake that made a drag rotor workable {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-windmill-en}.

## What was built on it
Windmills across the eastern Islamic world: the type is documented through the medieval period and survived in daily use at Nashtifan in Iranian Khorasan into the modern era — the longest-lived descendant line. Whether Europe's windmill descends from it is an open question the record honestly leaves open; what is certain is that the idea of the windmill itself is documented in the Islamic east first {cite:wikipedia-windmill-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Impact on daily life
In its homeland, everything: flour where there was no river strong enough to grind it, and water lifted where the stream ran too weak to turn a wheel. EI2's mill article stresses that mediaeval enterprises chose water or wind by simple economics — whichever was more constantly available and cheaper {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it changed history
It is the first documented harnessing of wind for industrial power — a proof that a civilisation could run machinery on the weather. The principle the Sistanian millwrights established — collect a moving fluid, turn a shaft, do work — is the same one every wind turbine embodies today {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-windmill-en}.

## Who invented it?
Unknown hands in Sistan — the honest answer is "the millwrights of the Helmand basin, sometime before the written record begins". The first name attached is not an inventor's but a witness's: al-Muqaddasi, who saw them as wonders worth recording {cite:muqaddasi-ahsan-taqasim-bga-1877}.

## Today
The descendant mills at Nashtifan still stand — some turning — as one of Iran's celebrated heritage sites, and the panemone design appears in every history of wind power as humanity's earliest practical wind machine {cite:wikipedia-windmill-en}.

## Surviving examples
No ninth-century mill survives; mills are rebuilt generation after generation. The living examples are the asbads of Nashtifan and related structures across Sistan and Khorasan — same principle, same orientation to the same wind, endlessly renewed timber {cite:wikipedia-windmill-en}.

## In culture
In Persian the word is asbad or asb-i bad — "wind horse" — a name that captures the poetry of the machine better than any diagram: the wind itself, harnessed like an animal {cite:wikipedia-windmill-en}.

## For young readers
In a land called Sistan the wind blows hard all summer, but the rivers are too weak to run mills. So people there built mills that run on air: tall walls with openings that catch the wind and push big sails around, turning the grinding stones below. A traveller wrote about them over a thousand years ago — the first time anyone wrote about a working windmill anywhere.
