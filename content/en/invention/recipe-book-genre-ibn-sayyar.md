## Summary
The Kitab al-Tabikh — "The Book of Dishes" — is the oldest surviving Arabic cookbook: compiled in tenth-century Baghdad by Abu Muhammad al-Muzaffar ibn Nasr ibn Sayyar al-Warraq, it gathers more than six hundred recipes from the kitchens of the eighth- and ninth-century Abbasid court into 132 chapters, ending with the manners of the table. With it the recipe book entered Arabic letters as a genre {cite:wikipedia-kitab-al-tabikh-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it is
A chaptered cookery book: over 600 recipes arranged in 132 sections, recording the dishes of the Abbasid elite — and closing, as EI2 notes, with chapters on table manners rather than food alone {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sayyar-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The world before it
Cooking was old, writing it down was newer. The Fihrist already knew many treatises on cookery — now lost — and the kitchen itself was an inherited Near Eastern technology, from the palace cook-houses the caliph al-Mutadid kept separate from the bakeries to the single-room ovens of the peasant house {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The story
In the tenth century a Baghdadi compiler gathered the court's memory: recipes of the eighth- and ninth-century Abbasid kitchens, set down as Baghdad's own prestige began to fade. Scholars suggest the book may have been prepared for the Hamdanid prince Sayf al-Dawla, who was raising his Aleppo court's cultural standing against the declining capital; one surviving manuscript, now in Istanbul, later belonged to the Ayyubid sultan al-Salih Najm al-Din Ayyub and his wife Shajar al-Durr {cite:wikipedia-kitab-al-tabikh-en}.

## The maker(s)
Ibn Sayyar al-Warraq — Abu Muhammad al-Muzaffar ibn Nasr — an Iraqi author of Baghdad about whom almost nothing is recorded beyond the book itself; the Sayf al-Dawla patronage is a scholarly suggestion, not a certainty {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sayyar-en} {cite:wikipedia-kitab-al-tabikh-en}.

## How it works
Like a modern cookbook, by structure: named dishes grouped into chapters, ingredients and methods recorded, the whole table culture codified — and capped by adab chapters on how to eat, turning a manual into a mirror of courtly life {cite:wikipedia-kitab-al-tabikh-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The spread
The genre it opened continued under its very name: Muhammad ibn Hasan al-Baghdadi's cookbook of 1226 and an anonymous thirteenth-century Andalusi Kitab al-Tabih followed it, while the text itself travelled west in translation — Nasrallah's annotated Brill edition of 2007, and selections by Zaouali, Favaro and Waines {cite:wikipedia-kitab-al-tabikh-en}.

## What it was built on
The Abbasid kitchen itself: the matbakh complexes of palace and prosperous house, the lost cookery treatises the Fihrist lists, and the older culinary inheritance of the Near East {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What was built on it
The Islamic cookbook as a genre — the later Baghdadi and Andalusi namesakes — and the modern scholarly kitchen-history built on Nasrallah's translation {cite:wikipedia-kitab-al-tabikh-en} {cite:nasrallah-annals-caliphs-kitchens-2007}.

## Impact on daily life
It let private craft become public knowledge: the dishes of a court — porridges, Persian-named stews, Iraqi nabatiyyat — set down for any household that could read or hire a reader {cite:wikipedia-kitab-al-tabikh-en}.

## How it changed history
It preserved the palate of a civilisation at its height: without it the Abbasid table would be anecdote; with it, six hundred documented dishes anchor the history of everyday life in the caliphate {cite:wikipedia-kitab-al-tabikh-en}.

## Who invented it?
Ibn Sayyar al-Warraq, as compiler — the book is original as the earliest surviving Arabic cookbook, though it drew on the older, now-lost treatises of the same Baghdad tradition {cite:wikipedia-kitab-al-tabikh-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Today
It lives three ways: a manuscript in Istanbul, an edited Arabic text (Oehrnberg and Mroueh, Helsinki 1987), and translations that have made its dishes cookable again — revival chefs work directly from it {cite:wikipedia-kitab-al-tabikh-en} {cite:wikipedia-ibn-sayyar-en}.

## Surviving examples
The Istanbul manuscript once owned by al-Salih Najm al-Din Ayyub and Shajar al-Durr — a book that passed through the hands of a sultan and a sultana {cite:wikipedia-kitab-al-tabikh-en}.

## In culture
The cookbook turned cuisine into heritage: dishes carry poetry and names of caliphs and courtiers, so that the book is read as much for the court it remembers as for the food it teaches {cite:wikipedia-kitab-al-tabikh-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
Imagine a huge notebook a man in Baghdad wrote a thousand years ago, with more than six hundred recipes for the caliphs' food: stews and stuffings and sweets, and even how to sit down to eat. Because of it we know today what the people of Baghdad ate in their city's finest age.
