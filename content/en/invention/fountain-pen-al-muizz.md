## Summary
Eight centuries before the modern fountain pen, a caliph in Egypt reportedly asked for a pen that would carry its own ink — one that 'writes without an inkpot' and does not stain the hand or the sleeve. The story survives through al-Qalqashandi's great chancery handbook, and EI2 notes plainly that 'a kind of fountain-pen was invented in Egypt' in the Fatimid period. No specimen exists; it is history's earliest documented reservoir pen — by account, not by artefact {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2; bosworth-mediaeval-fountain-pen-1981}.

## What it is
A pen with an internal ink reservoir: the caliph al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah (r. 953-975) wanted to write for a long stretch without dipping, and without ink soiling his fingers or clothes. The account describes a pen that met both demands — the defining feature of every fountain pen since {cite:bosworth-mediaeval-fountain-pen-1981}.

## The world before it
Every scribe worked the same kit: the qalam — a reed tube cut between two knots, sliced obliquely and slit at the point — dipped constantly into the dawat inkpot. EI2 describes the craft in detail: Wasit reeds from the marshes of Iraq were best, cut on the ivory mikatta board with a long penknife, the nib's two halves deliberately unequal for each script {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The story
The Fatimid court — first in Ifriqiya, then in Cairo after al-Mu'izz's entry in 973 — ran one of the world's great writing bureaucracies. In that milieu, the report says, the caliph asked for a pen that would free the hand from the inkpot, and one was made for him. The account reached posterity through the Mamluk chancery writer al-Qalqashandi's Subh al-A'sha — Bosworth's 1981 article brought it to modern attention as a plausible prototype of the fountain pen {cite:bosworth-mediaeval-fountain-pen-1981}.

## The maker(s)
The caliph is named; the craftsman is not. Al-Mu'izz stands as commissioner in the report; the pen's maker — presumably a court artisan — is anonymous, and al-Qalqashandi is the describer who carried the story into the chancery literature {cite:bosworth-mediaeval-fountain-pen-1981}.

## How it works
The account does not give engineering. The principle is unmistakable: a reservoir holding ink inside or alongside the pen, feeding the nib in a controlled way so that writing continues without dipping — the same problem modern pens solve with cartridges, feeds and gravity-or-capillary channels {cite:bosworth-mediaeval-fountain-pen-1981}.

## The spread
It did not spread — that is the honest answer. One caliph, one report, no surviving object and no line of descendants. The idea slept for eight and a half centuries inside a chancery handbook while the world wrote with quills {cite:bosworth-mediaeval-fountain-pen-1981}.

## What it was built on
Everything the Islamic pen-craft already knew: the best reed stock, the perfected nib-cut, the miklama and kalamdan cases that already married pen and inkpot into one portable kit. The reservoir pen was the logical next step of a mature writing technology {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What was built on it
Nothing directly — the modern fountain pen is a parallel invention. Petrache Poenaru's 1827 French patent opened the nineteenth-century line that produced the cartridge pen; the connection to Cairo is the problem posed, not the design transmitted {cite:bosworth-mediaeval-fountain-pen-1981}.

## Impact on daily life
In its own day, almost none — one courtly commission. Its value to daily life is retrospective: proof that the wish to write without an inkpot, which every fountain-pen user shares, is nearly a thousand years older than the object on the desk {cite:bosworth-mediaeval-fountain-pen-1981}.

## How it changed history
It changed the history of an idea rather than of a technology: it rewrites the fountain pen's origin story from an 1827 patent back to a tenth-century caliph's request, and it testifies to the engineering ambition of the Fatimid court workshop {cite:bosworth-mediaeval-fountain-pen-1981}.

## Who invented it?
The report says an unnamed craftsman made it for al-Mu'izz — so 'the Fatimid court' is the safest attribution. Bosworth treats the account as a genuine early prototype; what cannot be claimed is priority of an object, since none survives and no second technical description exists {cite:bosworth-mediaeval-fountain-pen-1981}.

## Today
Every cartridge and piston pen in every pocket descends conceptually from the same wish. The al-Mu'izz pen itself is known only through the transmitted account — and through records like this one, which preserve the report as report {cite:bosworth-mediaeval-fountain-pen-1981}.

## Surviving examples
None. No Islamic reservoir pen has been found. The closest material witnesses are the pen-boxes and inkpots of the era — the qalamdan sets, like the Qajar pen-and-ink box pictured in this record, that kept pen and inkpot together {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## In culture
The pen has always been honoured in Islamic civilisation — the crafts of calligraphy and the chancery produced a whole literature of ink, reed and nib, and the al-Mu'izz story is its most delightful entry: a king asking the impossible of a pen, and reportedly getting it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
When you write with a pen that has ink inside it, you are using an idea that a caliph in Cairo asked for more than a thousand years ago: 'make me a pen that carries its ink and doesn't dirty my hands.' The books say his craftsmen did it. We have never found that pen — but the wish was real, and it finally came true for everyone.
