## Summary
The numerals the world writes today — nine figures plus zero, each weighted by place — were invented in India. The Islamic world adopted them in Abbasid Baghdad, taught them in its reckoning books and carried them to Europe, where a Latin translation of al-Khwarizmi's arithmetic and Fibonacci's Liber Abbaci of 1202 made them known {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}. The record is a transmission, not an invention.

## What it is
A decimal place-value system of numerals — the digits and the way of writing number with them — that the Islamic world received from India and passed on, in two regional forms (eastern and the ghubar or 'dust' figures of the Maghrib), to Latin Europe {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}.

## The world before it
Europe and the ancient Near East reckoned with the abacus and wrote numbers in letters — Roman figures, Greek alphabetic numerals, the Arabic abjad values. Written reckoning as we know it did not exist; the Indian system reached Baghdad with the astronomical books called the Sindhind under al-Mansur {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}.

## The story
The numerals travelled the same road as Indian astronomy. In al-Ma'mun's Baghdad [[person/al-khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]] wrote his book on reckoning with the nine figures; al-Kindi had written on the Indian method before him, and Sanad ibn Ali is credited with another {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}. The Arabic writers called the science 'ilm hindi — Indian science — openly recording where it came from. About 1130 al-Khwarizmi's lost Arabic book surfaced in Latin as the Algoritmi de numero Indorum; in 1202 Leonardo of Pisa's Liber Abbaci spread the system through merchant Europe {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}.

## The maker(s)
No single maker: the numerals are Indian, and the Islamic-world carriers were a succession of teachers — al-Kindi, Sanad ibn Ali, al-Khwarizmi, the later reckoning writers — and then translators and merchants. [[person/fibonacci|Fibonacci]], who learned the figures at Bugia, is the best-documented single transmitter into Europe {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}.

## How it works
Each of the nine figures takes its value from its position: units, tens, hundreds. The empty position is held by zero. With place value, addition, subtraction, multiplication and division become written procedures anyone can follow — the method the record [[invention/algorism|algorism]] describes {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}.

## The spread
From India to Baghdad with the Sindhind; through the Islamic world's schools and merchant practice in two forms — the eastern digits still written in Arabic (٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩) and the Maghribi ghubar forms that fed the European line; then through the twelfth-century translations and Fibonacci's book into Latin Christendom {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}.

## What it was built on
The Indian decimal system itself, carried on the astronomical tables and the merchant routes; the Arabic treatises did not disguise the debt, and al-Biruni's India book documented the numerals in their own land {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}.

## What was built on it
Written arithmetic, the dust-board reckoning of the schools, al-Uqlidisi's decimal fractions ([[invention/decimal-fractions-al-uqlidisi|a separate record]]), algebra's practical arithmetic — and in Europe, algorism, mercantile bookkeeping and eventually the digit that computes {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}.

## Impact on daily life
It made large calculation writable and checkable by anyone with pen and dust-board — tax registers, market accounts, inheritance shares and astronomical tables all ran on it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it changed history
It carried the world's most successful notation. The name the West gave the method — algorism — was al-Khwarizmi's own Latinised name, and from it comes 'algorithm', the word of the computing age {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}.

## Who invented it?
India invented it. The Islamic world's role — adoption, teaching, refinement and carriage to Europe — is the documented claim, and the Arabic sources said the same {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}.

## Today
Both modern digit families — the Western 0-9 and the Eastern Arabic ٠-٩ — descend from the same Indian-Islamic system; the museum records them as one journey {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}.

## Surviving examples
The Cambridge manuscript of the Latin Algoritmi (pictured at media/dixit-algorizmi-cambridge); the Eastern Arabic digits still in daily use across the Islamic world; the ghubar ancestors of the printed European figures {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}.

## In culture
'Algorism' and 'algorithm' keep al-Khwarizmi's name alive wherever code runs — a rare case where a scholar's name became the thing itself {cite:smith-karpinski-hindu-arabic-1911}.

## For young readers
Long ago, people in India invented a clever way to write numbers with ten shapes including a little circle for zero. Scholars in Baghdad learned it and wrote books about it, and a merchant's son from Pisa learned it in North Africa and wrote a big book in 1202 that taught Europe. That is why the numerals on your phone are called 'Arabic numerals' — and why 'algorithm' is named after a scholar from Khwarizm.
