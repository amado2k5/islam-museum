## Summary
The Ommaya reservoir is a small silicone dome planted under the scalp, joined to a catheter reaching a ventricle of the brain: through it doctors deliver chemotherapy or sample cerebrospinal fluid without a single lumbar puncture. Ayub Khan Ommaya, a Pakistani-American neurosurgeon at the NIH, invented it in 1963 — the first subcutaneous port for repeated intrathecal access, and the first medical port made of self-sealing inert silicone {cite:wikipedia-ommaya-reservoir-en} {cite:wikipedia-ayub-khan-ommaya-en}.

## What it is
An intraventricular catheter system: a thin tube ends in one lateral ventricle, and its other end joins a reservoir under the skin of the scalp. A needle through the skin into the dome injects drugs that reach the brain's fluid directly, or draws cerebrospinal fluid out for testing {cite:wikipedia-ommaya-reservoir-en}.

## The world before it
Intrathecal therapy meant repeated lumbar punctures — painful needles in the lower back, repeated as often as the treatment demanded. Ventricular catheters drained fluid but offered no simple, repeatable, self-sealing port {cite:wikipedia-ayub-khan-ommaya-en}.

## The story
Ommaya took his medical degree at King Edward Medical College in 1953, studied at Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar and trained under Joseph Pennybacker, then emigrated to America in 1961 and rose to Chief of Neurosurgery at the National Institutes of Health. Studying malignant brain tumors there, he devised his reservoir in 1963. The same restless career produced the centripetal theory of traumatic brain injury, the first coma score, work on CT-scanner resolution with Hounsfield — and, through a congressman's friendship, the CDC's National Center for Injury Prevention and Control {cite:wikipedia-ayub-khan-ommaya-en}.

## The maker(s)
Ayub Khan Ommaya ⁦(1930–2008)⁩ is the documented sole inventor; the device has carried his name in the medical literature since its creation, and Pakistan's Dawn called him a "trailblazing Pakistani surgeon" at his death {cite:wikipedia-ayub-khan-ommaya-en}.

## How it works
Silicone is biologically inert and self-sealing: a needle punctures the dome, deposits the drug, and the material reseals as it leaves. Pressing the reservoir pushes the dose along the catheter into the ventricle, spreading it through the cerebrospinal fluid — a repeatable port in place of a repeated wound {cite:wikipedia-ayub-khan-ommaya-en}.

## The spread
It became standard equipment in neuro-oncology for brain tumors, leukemia and lymphoma affecting the coverings of the brain, and for leptomeningeal disease — then for intracerebroventricular morphine in terminal-cancer palliative care; its implanted-port pattern spread across medicine into venous access ports everywhere {cite:wikipedia-ommaya-reservoir-en}.

## What it was built on
Ventricular catheter technique, CSF-shunt practice and the new medical silicones of the era — Ommaya's contribution was the implantable self-sealing dome that turned drainage into repeatable two-way access {cite:wikipedia-ayub-khan-ommaya-en}.

## What was built on it
Every modern subcutaneous drug port descends from the concept; and its own uses widened — most recently, in January 2017, UT Southwestern researchers used an Ommaya reservoir to measure astronauts' intracranial pressure in weightlessness {cite:wikipedia-ommaya-reservoir-en}.

## Impact on daily life
For a patient with brain cancer it means treatment arrives through the skin of the head instead of a needle in the spine: less pain, less infection risk, and chemotherapy delivered where it must act {cite:wikipedia-ommaya-reservoir-en} {cite:wikipedia-ayub-khan-ommaya-en}.

## How it changed history
Sixty years on, it is still the standard subcutaneous route into the brain's fluid system, and it founded the entire implanted-port concept that oncology now takes for granted {cite:wikipedia-ayub-khan-ommaya-en}.

## Who invented it?
Uncontested: the reservoir bears Ommaya's own name, and both sources date the invention to 1963 at the NIH {cite:wikipedia-ommaya-reservoir-en} {cite:wikipedia-ayub-khan-ommaya-en}.

## Today
Still implanted under its original name — for intrathecal chemotherapy, CSF sampling and palliative morphine — with modern descendants reaching far beyond the skull {cite:wikipedia-ommaya-reservoir-en}.

## Surviving examples
The living record is clinical, not archaeological: the device in today's operating theatres, the name in every neurosurgery textbook, and the implanted-port family it founded {cite:wikipedia-ommaya-reservoir-en}.

## In culture
Ommaya appears in "The Muslims who shaped America", won Pakistan's Sitara-i-Imtiaz in 1982, and even appears as himself beside Susan Sarandon in the 1992 film Lorenzo's Oil — a surgeon whose name patients know from the device itself {cite:wikipedia-ayub-khan-ommaya-en}.

## For young readers
Imagine a doctor who must send medicine into a patient's brain every week, and each time has to push a big painful needle into the patient's back. In 1963 Dr Ayub Khan Ommaya made a little button planted under the hair of the head: the doctor touches it, puts the medicine in, and the medicine travels straight to the brain — no needles, no repeated pain.
