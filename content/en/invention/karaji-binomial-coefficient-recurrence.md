## Summary
How can we find the numbers appearing when a sum of two quantities is multiplied by itself repeatedly? The sources attribute to [[person/al-karaji|Abu Bakr al-Karaji]] a rule generating these coefficients by adding adjacent entries in a table; [[person/al-samawal-al-maghribi|al-Samawal al-Maghribi]] transmits the account of an original computation that no longer survives {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography} {cite:samawal-dsb algebra}. Its setting is early-eleventh-century Baghdad algebra, but this account does not establish the particular lost text's exact composition date or writing location {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography} {cite:samawal-dsb algebra}. We describe a bounded improvement in coefficient generation, not the invention of all algebra or the world's first arithmetic triangle {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography} {cite:jaina-mathematics-mactutor combinations}.

## What it is
A binomial here is a sum of two parts; a coefficient records how often a particular product contributes after like terms are collected, illustrated in the transmitted account by the square's sequence of one, two and one {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. In modern teaching notation we write(a+b), whose square is a²+2ab+b²; these letters and superscripts are not a transcription of Karaji's manuscript {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. This page's particular contribution is a method for finding the next power's coefficient list, rather than another account of all the [[invention/karaji-polynomial-arithmetic|operations on composite expressions]] already described separately {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. The accompanying modern image displays six rows, whereas the historical witness describes a table that can be pictured as a triangle lying on its side {cite:commons-pascal-triangle-five description} {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}.

## The story
MacTutor reports that Karaji calculated the cube of a sum in al-Fakhri, and the cube of a difference and fourth power of a sum in al-Badi; the general table construction is preserved in al-Samawal's later writing {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. Adel Anbouba's Dictionary of Scientific Biography entry places Karaji's coefficient calculation after1007, referring to an edition of al-Badi that we have not independently read {cite:samawal-dsb algebra}. That reference is not treated here as the exact composition date of a complete surviving original {cite:samawal-dsb algebra}. Al-Samawal admired Karaji but also identified shortcomings and developed improvements; the biographies describe [[work/al-bahir-fi-al-jabr|al-Bahir in algebra]] as a youthful work that may subsequently have been revised {cite:samawal-mactutor Biography} {cite:samawal-dsb algebra}. The preservation story is therefore a later author naming a predecessor within a larger algebraic project, not a documented personal meeting.

## How it works
The translated testimony reproduced by MacTutor begins with ones and constructs1,2,1 for the square; the extremes represent each part's square, while the middle records the product of the two parts twice {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. To obtain the next list, retain a one at each extreme, add one and two to obtain three, and then two and one to obtain another three, producing1,3,3,1 {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. Generating this list does not require dividing a large factorial or independently searching for every coefficient; adjacent addition is the procedure actually described by the witness {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}.

In a modern reconstruction we can name the coefficient of power n at position k as C(n,k), giving the internal recurrence C(n+1,k)=C(n,k−1)+C(n,k), with one at each boundary {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. This indexing and symbolism explain the rule; they are not the author's words or evidence that he supplied the modern definition of a zeroth-power row {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. When(a+b)³ is multiplied by(a+b), each interior term receives contributions in two ways: a preceding term multiplied by a and its neighbour multiplied by b, whose coefficients must consequently be added {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. From1,3,3,1 we therefore obtain1,4,6,4,1 for the fourth power, followed by1,5,10,10,5,1 for the fifth, the last row of the modern illustration {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography} {cite:commons-pascal-triangle-five description}.

Take a=2 and b=3 as numbers selected by the museum for explanation, not a historical example attributed to the author: the square gives4+12+9=25, and the cube8+36+54+27=125 {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. The fourth-power list gives16+96+216+216+81=625, agreeing with5⁴ {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. This numerical check illustrates what coefficients do, but does not alone prove the rule for every power; MacTutor distinguishes the early successive reasoning from a rigorous exposition of mathematical induction {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. The scope here is finite integer powers, not an infinite series for a negative or fractional exponent {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography} {cite:pascal-mactutor Biography}.

Symmetry supplies another check on the witness's lists:1,3,3,1 reads identically from either end, as do the displayed fourth- and fifth-power rows {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography} {cite:commons-pascal-triangle-five description}. In the reconstruction, exchanging the names of the two parts reverses term order without changing their sum, making symmetry a teaching check rather than an attribution of a further undocumented theorem {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. The number of coefficients exceeds the exponent by one because the two boundary terms remain alongside the interior contributions {cite:commons-pascal-triangle-five description}.
