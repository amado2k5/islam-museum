## Summary
From the eleventh century onward the Islamic lands were stitched together by an architecture of hospitality: the khan, a fortified inn planted at every day's march along the caravan roads — courtyard, rooms, mosque, stables and a guarded well. Erdmann counts 119 of them for Iran, Syria and Anatolia in the thirteenth century alone; the network was the medieval world's greatest system of road infrastructure {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it is
The khan — what Europeans, following Pierre Belon's 'Carbaschara', came to call the caravanserai — is a square fortress-lodging: an outer wall with corner towers and buttresses, a single gate, an inner courtyard ringed by travellers' rooms, an oratory, sometimes a bathhouse, and covered stables and stalls sorted by animal {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The world before it
Long-distance roads always needed stopping points. EI2 cites Herzfeld's link to the Achaemenid relay stations (avahana) on the Sardis-Susa highway — Herodotus's posts fifty parasangs apart — and the similar Byzantine stations. The idea was old; what was missing was density, security and a common architectural standard {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The story
In lands where nomads and mountain bandits made the open road dangerous, and where wells and provisions were sparse, the fortified khan became the indispensable companion of commerce. It flowered with particular vigour in the Islamic world from the seventh/thirteenth century: Seljuk and later patrons endowed khans one day's journey — about twenty miles — apart along the routes of Anatolia, Syria and Iran. The word first appears in Arabic epigraphy on the Khan al-'Aqaba foundation of 610/1213 in Syria {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The maker(s)
Sultans, viziers, merchants and waqf endowments — the builders are legion and mostly anonymous in EI2's account. The witnesses are the travellers who slept in them and wrote them into geographies and itineraries, Nasir Khusraw foremost among them {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it works
Arrive at dusk: the gate closes behind the caravan. Travellers take rooms off the courtyard; horses go to covered stables, mules and camels to their own stalls — 'horses cannot endure the smell of dromedaries', EI2's builders knew. There is a well, often an oratory, sometimes a bath; goods are stored under the same walls that keep bandits out {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The spread
The 'isthmus region' between Asia Minor and the Iranian plateau was its heartland, but the type ran the whole caravan world — Syria's 1213 epigraph, the Seljuk stone khans of Anatolia, the brick ribats of the Khorasan roads, and onward to India and the Maghreb. Around some khans, towns grew up: Khan Shaykhun and Jisr al-Shughur in Syria began as conurbations around a khan {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it was built on
The ancient way-station; the frontier ribat's fortress plan; the urban funduq — the warehouse-hostelry for merchants inside cities, which EI2 carefully distinguishes from the highway khan {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What was built on it
The urban khan type — the covered merchants' khans of Cairo, Aleppo and Tyre — and, in the long view, every roadside hotel, motel and service station. The waqf endowment that kept many khans free or cheap for travellers became a model of public infrastructure {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Impact on daily life
For the traveller it meant the difference between a night's lodging and a night of risk; for the merchant, safe warehousing mid-route; for the pilgrim, a staged path to the holy cities. EI2 notes the khans were also 'a centre for the exchange of ideas' — strangers from three continents sharing one courtyard {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it changed history
The khan network is the circulatory system of the medieval Islamic economy: it is what made Silk-Road trade, the overland hajj and the travelling-scholar culture physically possible at continental scale. No single khan is famous; the network itself is the achievement {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Who invented it?
Not the Islamic world — the way-station is as old as trade. The documented Islamic achievement is the network: turning an ancient convenience into a standardised, endowed, continent-wide chain of fortified guest-houses at a density the older empires never reached {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Today
The caravan economy is gone, but hundreds of khans survive — restored as monuments, still working as souks and warehouses (Khan al-Khalili in Cairo is the famous urban heir), and studied as the fossil record of the roads themselves {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Surviving examples
Standing examples in this record's own collection include the twelfth-century Sharaf caravanserai at Sarakhs, the Bisotun caravanserai, the Ganjali Khan caravanserai of Kerman, the Rüstem Pasha caravansaray of Edirne, and the urban Khan al-Khalili and Khan As'ad Pasha tradition of Egypt and Syria {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## In culture
The caravanserai is the road-movie set of the medieval east: the place where the geographer, the spy, the Sufi and the spice merchant all spent the same night. Persian and Turkish travel writing made it a literary figure — the saray that is never home {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
Imagine a hotel that is also a castle, planted alone in the desert every day's walk, where anyone — kings, pilgrims, camel drivers — could sleep safe behind one big gate. Muslims built thousands of these desert hotels, and thanks to them a merchant could travel from China to Spain sleeping safely every single night.
