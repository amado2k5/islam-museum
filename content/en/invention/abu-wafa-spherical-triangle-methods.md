## Summary

An astronomer calculating on a sphere cannot simply use a straight-sided triangle drawn on paper: the boundaries are great-circle arcs, and the problem connects their lengths with the angles at which they meet. The contribution presented here is [[person/abu-al-wafa-al-buzjani|Abu al-Wafa al-Buzjani]]'s simplification of these relationships, including a proof of the general sine relation and methods for right spherical triangles—not the invention of all trigonometry {cite:abu-wafa-youschkevitch-dsb trigonometry}. The French publication of1892 places the proof beside an explicit priority dispute; both remain in our story {cite:abu-wafa-carra-almageste-1892 pp.423–424}.

## What it is

The spherical triangle considered here has three great-circle arcs for sides; a great circle lies in a plane through the sphere's center. A side is measured by its arc's central angle, not a straight chord through the sphere; a vertex angle is between the directions of the two arcs meeting there. This distinction is essential to reading the translated proof, and explains why we cannot take the plane sine law and retain its ordinary side lengths unchanged on a sphere {cite:abu-wafa-carra-almageste-1892 pp.423–424}.

The method is not a brass instrument or a new observatory, but a set of relationships, proofs and calculations that helps recover an unknown from suitable data. Youschkevitch describes simpler methods replacing the six-quantity apparatus; the encyclopedia records sine and tangent rules for right triangles {cite:abu-wafa-youschkevitch-dsb trigonometry} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.1 p.178}. We do not inflate the count by treating every relationship as a separate invention: this record represents one bounded methodological contribution.

## The world before it

The sky was not beyond calculation before Abu al-Wafa. In the translated preface, the author names inherited work and Greek predecessors, then explains his intention to simplify procedures and add proofs and examples {cite:abu-wafa-carra-almageste-1892 pp.413–414}. The scientific biography explains the cumbersome use of Menelaus's six-quantity configuration in spherical problems. That background makes the simplification intelligible; it is not an opponent to erase in pursuit of an absolute beginning {cite:abu-wafa-youschkevitch-dsb trigonometry}.

Nor should improved solution methods be confused with introducing every trigonometric function. The Encyclopaedia of Islam rejects attributing the introduction of tangent, cotangent, secant and cosecant to Abu al-Wafa because Habash al-Hasib had used them earlier {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.1 p.178}. Aydin likewise mentions Habash's earlier tables, although reference works differ in wording about definitions and particular first uses {cite:tdv-ebul-vefa-buzcani pp.348–349}. A precedent does not cancel a later improvement, but it changes the label we should give that improvement.

## The story

Born in Buzjan, Abu al-Wafa moved to Iraq and lived in [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]]; Aydin records the move in348/959, teachers from his family, and contacts with contemporary scholars {cite:tdv-ebul-vefa-buzcani pp.348–349}. Britannica describes observing work and cooperation with [[person/al-biruni-muhammad-ibn-ahmad|al-Biruni]], placing calculation among questions about sky, position and time rather than in an isolated biography {cite:britannica-abu-al-wafa biography}. These reports do not identify the desk where he wrote his proof or the day he completed it.

The translated preface distinguishes geometrical proof from calculation, emphasizes numerical problems, and discusses corrected tables: a broader scientific and teaching program than the slogan “discovered a law” {cite:abu-wafa-carra-almageste-1892 pp.413–414}. The record's tenth-century date is a broad activity frame, not an invented year of composition. Death reports differ between997 and998; we turn neither into an exact proof date or a fabricated Hijri conversion {cite:britannica-abu-al-wafa biography} {cite:tdv-ebul-vefa-buzcani pp.348–349}.

## How it works

In our modern teaching notation, vertices are `A,B,C` and opposite sides `a,b,c`, each side an angular arc rather than a chord length. The spherical sine law is then `sin(a)/sin(A)=sin(b)/sin(B)=sin(c)/sin(C)`. This corresponds to the translated text's proportionality between sines of angles and sines of opposite sides—not the plane ratio of a side itself to an angle's sine {cite:abu-wafa-carra-almageste-1892 pp.423–424}. Modern letters are not asserted to be the author's manuscript labels, or our vertex order the translator's figure.

For `C=90°`, the relations in this convention include `sin(a)=sin(c)sin(A)`, `tan(a)=tan(A)sin(b)`, and `cos(c)=cos(a)cos(b)`. This is a normalized formulation of the simpler right-triangle apparatus described in the encyclopedia {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.1 p.178}. The first retrieves an unknown when suitable data are available; the second connects tangent and sine; the third relates the three arcs. They are connected relationships, not three unrelated inventions, and do not imply that any three arbitrary numbers always determine exactly one valid triangle.

Try a modern example created for this explanation, not historical numbers from Abu al-Wafa's book: `a=30°`, `b=45°`, `C=90°`. The cosine relation gives `c≈52.238756°`; the tangent relations give `A≈39.231520°` and `B≈63.434949°`. Substitution makes all three sine ratios approximately`0.790569415`, providing a numerical check. This verifies our example in the stated notation, not an independent reconstruction of every step of the translated proof {cite:abu-wafa-carra-almageste-1892 pp.423–424}.

Notice that our example's angles total more than180 degrees: we do not force a spherical triangle to obey the plane triangle's rule. For surface distance on a sphere of radius`R`, multiply the arc angle in radians by`R`; a sine value alone is not kilometers. Inverting sine also requires attention to angle choice and context, so the formula is not a promise of a unique solution for arbitrary input. These are modern geometrical explanations of the relationships read, not a claim that the author used calculator language {cite:abu-wafa-carra-almageste-1892 pp.423–424}.

The translated proof does not merely announce a ready-made ratio: it begins with great-circle arcs bounding a triangle, introduces arcs associated with poles, then perpendicular projections to establish the proportionality {cite:abu-wafa-carra-almageste-1892 pp.423–424}. This distinguishes checking a particular number from proving a general relationship. Our numerical example can reveal the mistake of using a side angle instead of its sine, but cannot replace the geometrical construction. Agreement among three numbers in one example does not by itself prove a law for every triangle.

## Impact on daily life

The passage summarized by Carra names applications including Qibla azimuth and numerical examples: a documented window onto the usefulness of astronomical relationships, not a count of beneficiaries or an estimate of computation speeds everywhere {cite:abu-wafa-carra-almageste-1892 p.425}. ## How it changed history

The point is not that astronomy changed overnight on a known date, but that a calculating apparatus could be simplified and extended. Youschkevitch places Abu al-Wafa among early proofs of the general relation and describes the tangent method {cite:abu-wafa-youschkevitch-dsb trigonometry}. This is more precise than a contest over “the first person to know sine.” It reveals accumulation: inherited problems, simpler formulation, a demonstrable proof, and later historical debate over priority for a shared result.
