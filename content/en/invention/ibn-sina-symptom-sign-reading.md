## Summary

In Book One of the Canon, Ibn Sina devotes a third teaching of Fann 2 titled "symptoms and indications," in eleven chapters and two summaries {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}. It opens by stating that the symptoms and signs indicating one of the three states — present, past and coming — indicate either a present matter, a past matter or a coming matter {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}. It then distinguishes what indicates the health of the organs from what indicates disease, lays down the rules of inferring from the apparent to the hidden, and closes with chapters on the signs of temperaments, congestion, humoural dominance, obstructions, winds, swellings and solution of continuity {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.154–170}. This system is not the invention of diagnosis nor the first clinical reading; it is a teaching order inside the older science of signs and evidences in Greek-Arabic medicine {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.154–170}.

## What it is

The teaching here is Ta'lim 3 of Ta'lim 2 in Book One, containing eleven chapters and two summaries according to the text's arrangement {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}. Chapter 1 is a general discourse on symptoms and indications: it describes the health signs and the signs indicating diseases, the kinds of symptoms by their time — temporary, untimed, and the signs of the end of affairs — then the signs taken from the special and common sensibles {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.154–155}. Chapter 2 is the signs distinguishing local from sympathetic diseases, containing anatomy and the six laws of inference from actions, what is voided and retained, pain, swelling, posture and apparent symptoms {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.156–157}. Chapter 3 is the signs of temperaments, and the remaining chapters are the signs of the equable temperament, of those not well-formed in constitution, of congestion, of humoural dominance, of obstructions, of winds, of swellings and of solution of continuity {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.158–170}.

## The world before it

The science of signs and evidences is old in Greek medicine: the text itself attributes to Galen the sayings that a sign may indicate a present, a past or a coming matter {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}. The Canon did not invent this logic; it chose to make it a separate teaching inside the structure of Book One after the definitions of disease, cause and symptom {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.103}. The invention of clinical diagnosis or the first method of examining a patient is therefore not credited to it; only its systematic ordering is {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.154–170}.

## The story

The place of Book One in the Canon is a general teaching place: it defines concepts first, then divides diseases, then teaches the reading of signs {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.103–154}. The study places the Canon's completion in Hamadan without dating this chapter {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. The pages read are the 1999 Dar al-Kutub al-Ilmiyya printing, and scan page 154 opens a whole section, not an isolated paragraph {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}. We do not claim that Ibn Sina composed this ordering in one sitting or that it was printed exactly as he wrote it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.154–170}.

## How it works

**First: the sign indicates three states.** The chapter opens by stating that the symptoms and signs indicating one of the three states — present, past and coming — indicate either a present matter, a past matter or a coming matter {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}. It attributes to Galen that the present matter benefits the patient alone in what he should do, the past matter benefits the physician alone, who infers his own progress in the art and so gains confidence in his counsel, and the coming matter benefits both {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}. This threefold division is the key to everything that follows {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}.

**Second: signs of health.** The health signs include what indicates equilibrium of temperament and what indicates equilibrium of composition: essential signs such as creation, position, quantity and number being as they should be; accidental signs like sense and beauty; and completeness signs, from the perfection of actions and their continuance in perfection {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}. The inference from actions to the principal organs runs: to the brain through the states of voluntary actions, sense and imagination; to the heart through pulse and breath; and to the liver through stool and urine, whose weakness is followed by stool and urine resembling the washing of fresh meat {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}.

**Third: disease symptoms and their time.** The symptoms indicating diseases include those indicating the disease itself, such as the variation of the pulse in fever; those indicating its location, such as the variation of the pulse over the sites of swelling; and those indicating its cause, such as the signs of congestion in their differing states {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}. Among the temporary symptoms are those that begin and end with the disease, like cough and the serrated pulse with pleurisy; among the untimed are those that sometimes follow the disease and sometimes not, like the headache of fever; and among the last-comers are the signs of crisis, of ripening and of failure, mostly in acute diseases {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.154–155}.

**Fourth: signs from the sensibles.** The signs taken from the special sensibles are like the states of colour and the states of touch in hardness, softness, heat and cold; and from the common sensibles taken from the creation, positions, movements and rests of the organs {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.155}. Among these are what indicates hidden states: lip tremor indicating vomiting and its measures, short fingers indicating a small liver, belching indicating flatulence and bad digestion, the convexity of the nail indicating consumption, and inference from smells and tastes in the mouth {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.155}. The visible sensible may indicate a hidden matter: the redness of the cheek indicating pneumonia, the convexity of the nail indicating a lung ulcer {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.155}. Among the symptoms taken from stillness are apoplexy, epilepsy, syncope and palsy; and from movement, shivering, tremor, hiccough, sneeze, yawn, stretching, cough, convulsion and spasm {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.155}.

**Fifth: anatomy of the interior and the six laws.** Chapter 2 lays down the rule that the physician attempting to manage diseases of the inner organs must know anatomy, to know whether pain and swelling are on the organ or at a distance from it, whether it participates, whether the separated matter is of its substance or a passage through which another's separates, whether what is voided may itself be voided, and whether a defect has befallen the organ's action {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.156}. He then relies in inferring internal diseases on six laws: from the harms of actions, which is primary and permanent; from what is voided and retained, which is permanent but not primary; from pain; from swelling; from posture; and from the appropriate apparent symptoms, which are neither primary nor permanent {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.156–157}.

**Sixth: inference from actions and from what is voided.** The harms of actions have three faces: decrease, like weakened sight and harder slower digestion; change, like seeing what is not or the stomach spoiling food; and cessation, like the eye not seeing at all and the stomach not digesting at all {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.156}. The indications of what is voided and retained include unnatural retention of what should be voided, or unnatural voiding: either of the organs' own substance, like a sore throat indicating erosion in the trachea and the crust in an abrasion indicating the thick or fine intestines; or not of their substance, like decomposed blood, stones, the too little and too much dregs and urine, black stool and urine, and stool exiting upward in ileus {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.157}.

**Seventh: pain, swelling and posture.** The indications of pain are confined to two genera: by location, the right side for the liver and the left for the spleen; and by kind, the heavy for swelling in an insensitive organ, the distending for much matter, and the burning for acrid matter {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.157}. The indications of swelling have three faces: from its substance, redness over yellowness and hardness over blackness; from its location; and from its shape, the crescent at the right indicating the liver itself and the elongated one the muscle above it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.157}. The indications of posture come from positions and participations, as pain in a finger is inferred from a preceding lesion in the sixth pair of the neck's nerves {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.157}.

**Eighth: the local and the sympathetic.** The physician must observe which appeared first and guess it is the original and the other sympathetic, and which remains after the second's end and guess the original; the sympathetic appears last and rests with the first's rest {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.157}. But error may occur because the original lesion may be imperceptible and painless at its beginning, its harm felt only after the sympathetic disease appears, which is in truth a sequel to it; the precaution is that the physician know the participating organs from anatomy and the lesions occurring in each organ, question the patient about signs of diseases that may be in the participating organs, and observe the harms of actions — if found preceding, he judges the disease sympathetic {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.157–158}. Among the organs, some organs' diseases mostly lag behind others': the head's diseases in most cases are sympathetic to the stomach's, and the reverse is rarer {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.158}.

**Ninth: signs of temperaments.** Chapter 3 makes the genera of indications by which temperament states are known ten, beginning with touch: he observes whether it equals the correct touch in moderate lands and moderate air — if it equals it that indicates equilibrium, and if the correct-tempered toucher feels cold or heat, or over-softness or over-hardness, without cause from air or bathing, then it is not of moderate temperament {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.158}. The second genus of indications is taken from flesh, fat and suet: much red flesh indicates moisture and heat with sliminess, suet and fat always indicate cold and flabbiness, and little suet and fat indicates heat {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.159}. The following chapters — the signs of the equable temperament, of those not well-formed in constitution, of congestion, of humoural dominance, of obstructions, of winds, of swellings and of solution of continuity — complete the ten genera of indications {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.159–170}.

## The spread

The study discusses Book One of the Canon as the basis of medical teaching for about seven centuries in the school and the Latin translation {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. But the circulation of a book does not prove that every reader implemented these chapters or recorded comparable results {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. The specific evidence here is that this teaching survives in a printed edition and is classified within the structure of Book One, not a complete map of sign-reading practice across the centuries {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.154–170}. We therefore do not fill reception gaps with an invented chain of institutions or pupils {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}.

## What it was built on

The teaching builds on the earlier chapters of definitions: disease, cause, symptom and sign were defined before they were read {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.103}. The sign here is not mere information; it is inference from visible to hidden inside humour theory {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}. Read outside this frame it loses its historical meaning {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.154–170}.

## What was built on it

Sources discuss that the Canon remained a medical reference for centuries, but that does not prove these chapters became a universally followed protocol {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. Similar clinical-reading questions do not by themselves prove direct descent from this teaching {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. For the same context the question is similarity of problems, not proof of lineage {cite:britannica-avicenna final-evidence-discussion}.

## Impact on daily life

The directly showable impact is organizational: it made the sign from visible information into a tool of inference for an unseen state {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}. That ordering can make a reader more aware that every observation may be evidence, but it is not by itself proof of more accurate diagnosis in a particular population; the teaching supplies no accuracy figures or clinical results attributable to applying it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.154–170}. The everyday contribution described is an organized questioning, not a diagnostic guarantee or a promise about every patient {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}.

## How it changed history

The teaching shows that reading signs belongs to written medical history: the author did not content himself with describing disease and its treatment, but arranged chapters to train the reader in inference from visible to hidden {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.154–170}. That ordering inside Book One, which remained a foundation for seven centuries, gives it real historical weight without turning medical history into a contest for the title of first clinical reader {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. Accurate appreciation retains the humoral heritage he used and the distance between a plausible historical argument and verification tools he did not describe {cite:britannica-avicenna final-evidence-discussion}.

## Who invented it?

What is established is that Ibn Sina presented these chapters in the Canon, not that a survey of every earlier text proves him the first human to formulate any system for reading signs {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.154–170} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. The record therefore treats the contribution as systematization and improvement inside an older heritage, not the sole origin of all sign science {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. Nor is this the invention of a new diagnosis nor the first clinical method; they are materials Ibn Sina read and gathered into a teaching order {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.154–170}.

## Today

Visitors can read this teaching as a history of the idea that the body leaves signs to be read, remembering that pulse and heat here are not readings on a modern instrument {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}. Comparison with today's medicine requires its own standards, not using an old text as instructions for diagnosis on oneself or others {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. The safe museum example below concerns thinking and ordering only; this page supplies no prescription, no substance to take and no diagnostic procedure.

## In culture

The idea that the body leaves signs can tempt readers to imagine a direct ancestor of today's diagnosis, while explaining each chapter reveals historical meanings broader than, or different from, that analogy {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.154–170}. It is possible to value the questions of inference from visible to hidden without concealing the humoral frame in which they were formulated {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol.3 pp.974–977}. History here is an encounter with a document, not a modern image projected onto its author {cite:britannica-avicenna final-evidence-discussion}.

## For young readers

**A modern museum example, not an experiment from the Canon:** imagine you are playing in the yard and hear your friend's cough. The cough is a small sign, but it may indicate a simple cold or something else. Ibn Sina taught physicians that every visible sign may be evidence of something unseen, but the sign alone is not enough for a judgement. We understand that diagnosis in the view of the old physicians was a reading, not a single guess; we do not use his method today {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.154}.
