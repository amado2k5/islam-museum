## Summary

At Rayy near Tehran, [[person/al-khujandi-abu-mahmud-hamid-ibn-al-khidr|Abu Mahmud al-Khujandi]] built a monumental instrument to observe the Sun's altitude at meridian transit. Named the Fakhri sextant after his patron Fakhr al-Dawla, it included a sixty-degree arc of a circle approximately43 metres in diameter, inside a building admitting sunlight through an aperture. This record concerns that particular project, not the invention of angular measurement or everything subsequently called a sextant {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea sextant}.

Its attraction lies in ambition and limitation together. A larger scale makes small angular differences easier to read, but the building carrying the instrument can itself move. Modern biographies report [[person/al-biruni-muhammad-ibn-ahmad|al-Biruni]]'s criticism that the aperture settled between observations; al-Khujandi's own text, by contrast, argues at one point against attributing the discrepancy to the instrument. Both witnesses matter. Numerous numerical places are not a certificate of achieved accuracy {cite:khujandi-mactutor settlement} {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 p67}.

## What it is

Here a sextant is a sixth of a circle, not six hands or six hours. The meridian arc lies in the local north–south vertical plane. Its purpose was to read the Sun's altitude at transit and combine observations near the solstices to determine the obliquity of the ecliptic and the locality's latitude. The Arabic text connects an observing instrument with a calculation; this was not simply a graduated decoration on a wall {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 pp62-66}.

Nor should the name suggest a small seaman's instrument. Two pictures at the bottom of printed page66 in the1908 edition are editorial comparisons with contemporary sextants, not original Fakhri construction plans. Rayy's device was a large fixed installation receiving sunlight. A later drawing with mirrors and a movable arm cannot establish its precise arrangement. A shared name or arc size does not demonstrate identical engineering or a direct transmission route {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 pp66-69}.

## The world before it

Obliquity was already a problem of observation. Al-Khujandi compares his result with values he attributes to Indian astronomers and Ptolemy, making their disagreement a subject for explanation. His account distinguishes degrees, minutes and seconds and considers what older instruments could show. We report his comparison without independently endorsing every ancient observation or date embedded in it {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 p67}.

Van Brummelen's biography places him among makers of astrolabes, quadrants and armillary instruments. His contribution did not replace a world without geometry with one possessing it. It changed scale, integration and the means of locating the solar image's centre. His other instruments remain separate research questions rather than automatic additions to this record or to the invention count {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea instruments}.

## The story

The Arabic account names Fakhr al-Dawla's support and skilled participants who helped establish readings. Printed page62 dates observations to384AH, with994–995 beside it; the modern biographies identify the observing programme as994. We use994 as a documented observation horizon, not the exact date of invention, excavation or completion. No newly guessed Hijri conversion is supplied. The date of seeing the Sun is not necessarily the date every component was manufactured {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 p62} {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea observations}.

Al-Khujandi records consecutive days, cloudy conditions interrupting observations, and the problem that a reading need not coincide exactly with the solstice. He uses calculations of solar motion to bridge observation and the desired position. The result therefore depended on weather, timing, correction and arithmetic, not on a number emerging automatically from masonry as soon as the arc was finished {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 pp62-64}.

## The maker(s)

MacTutor explicitly identifies al-Khujandi as an Islamic astronomer and mathematician. That individual biographical description supplies the Muslim identity basis, not his name or residence. The museum retains its inherited Sunni classification under its declared default frame, separately from a documented personal school not found in these readings. The patron's affiliation and dynasty do not establish the instrument maker's own denomination {cite:khujandi-mactutor biography}.

The biographies give different approximate birth dates, around940 in MacTutor and around945 in Van Brummelen. This instrument record need not resolve that difference or silently alter the existing person's death record. We have an identifiable designer, patron and observing programme, not a complete list of builders or wages. Construction and graduation required skill, but unknown collaborators should not receive invented biographies {cite:khujandi-mactutor biography} {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea biography}.

## How it works

Van Brummelen describes sunlight entering a roof aperture and reaching the scale, with a cross-marked circle used to identify the image's centre. The centre matters because the image has area rather than being a dimensionless point. Positions on the known arc become angular altitudes. This is historical explanation, not advice to look at the Sun or an invitation to construct a solar-viewing experiment {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea sextant}.

For the calculation alone, take an independently derived modern idealization: a northern latitude of35degrees and obliquity23.5degrees, with the Sun south of the zenith at both noon observations. Summer altitude is78.5 and winter altitude31.5. Their mean is55; latitude is90−55=35. Half their difference is23.5, the obliquity. This illustrates the text's comparison of two altitudes, not Rayy data or a verbatim medieval formula. Its branch assumptions matter: it cannot be used unchanged across zenith passages or in the other hemisphere {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 pp64-66}.

Now add one degree to both hypothetical readings. Their mean becomes56, giving inferred latitude34, while half the difference remains23.5. Add one degree to the summer reading alone instead: inferred latitude becomes34.5 and obliquity24. A shared offset and a seasonally changing offset have different effects. This arithmetic is not an estimate of historical settlement, nor a complete model of refraction, site elevation or the apparent solar disc {cite:khujandi-mactutor settlement}.

## The spread

Van Brummelen calls the Rayy installation a model for similar instruments at Maragha and Samarkand in the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries, describing different construction materials as a way to avoid settlement. We attribute that relationship to his account. We have not examined the construction records of all three observatories or proved a continuous chain of craftsmen. Similarity of principle is not documentation of every transferred detail {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea later-observatories}.

He also suggests that observations probably continued with other instruments and that al-Khujandi eventually produced the Fakhri astronomical handbook. “Probably” remains a qualification, not a firm replacement date or a calculated service life for the sextant. A partially surviving later Persian handbook possibly drawing on his work does not establish that every number in it came from this particular arc {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea zij}.

## What it was built on

The project depended on a meridian line, a controlled vertical plane, a graduated arc, a means of locating projected light and calculations relating the observation to latitude and obliquity. The appended account attributed to Biruni describes walls, aperture, arc and centring arrangements, with manuscript alternatives in the notes. We do not select an old cubit figure and convert it into falsely exact modern metres as though an uncontested archaeological survey had been made {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 pp68-69}.

Van Brummelen's approximate43metres describes the circle's diameter, not radius. Confusing the two would double the intended size. The accompanying modern teaching triangle provides angular geometry context only: it is not the building's elevation, aperture position or original arc layout. Its side labels do not decode the lettered diagram in the Arabic text {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea sextant} {cite:commons-trigonometry-triangle description}.

## What was built on it

An important outcome was criticism of the observations. MacTutor reports Biruni's diagnosis that the aperture sank about a span during the work, whereas al-Khujandi's own text treats differing results as evidence against constant obliquity. We have not repeated an experiment or established that al-Khujandi accepted Biruni's explanation. These are separately attributed interpretations, with sources a reader can inspect {cite:khujandi-mactutor settlement} {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 p67}.

The case distinguishes slow real change in a phenomenon from instrumental bias. A discrepancy pointing in the expected direction does not by itself establish the amount of real change. Stability, graduation and corrections still matter. This is a modern methodological reading of the documented problem, not a claim that every earlier argument was wrong or that large instruments were inherently worthless {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea observations}.

## Impact on daily life

Its immediate work concerned the sky and geographical position, not a household device regulating every resident's day. Latitude and obliquity belonged to astronomical knowledge, but the readings examined here provide no user totals, financial savings or measured improvement in navigation. We leave those numbers absent instead of manufacturing a commercial success story the sources do not tell {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 pp62-67}.

A more visible human consequence is organized work: a patron supports, a maker designs, skilled participants establish readings, and observation awaits clear sky before entering a calculation. The instrument should not erase those people into the story of an isolated genius. Equally, collective execution does not remove al-Khujandi's documented design role. Patronage, manufacture and authorship are connected without being interchangeable {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 p62}.

## How it changed history

The installation embodies the ambition of increasing size to make small angular differences easier to read. Its instability belongs to that history, not to an embarrassing footnote that must be omitted. Van Brummelen calls the immediate result a failure yet still connects it with later instruments. Failure and learning coexist in one account; there is no need to invent uninterrupted triumph leading to modern technology {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea sextant}.

Nor does the sextant inherit all its maker's mathematics. Priority in the spherical sine theorem has different claimants and evidence, discussed by MacTutor and in the museum's [[invention/abu-wafa-spherical-triangle-methods|Abu al-Wafa spherical-triangle record]]. His appearance in that dispute is not a reason to count this installation, a theorem and a surviving astrolabe as either one invention or three without separate research {cite:khujandi-mactutor sine-priority}.

## Who invented it?

MacTutor attributes the monumental mural sextant's design to al-Khujandi, and his printed observing account supports the particular project. We record the maker of this arrangement while classifying its contribution as an improvement within an older measuring tradition. That does not make it the first solar-altitude measurement or first opening admitting sunlight. Any modern “first” must retain the narrow object it describes {cite:khujandi-mactutor sextant} {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 p62}.

Likewise, “quadrant” in Van Brummelen's introduction does not establish a second invention to add to the total: his detailed paragraph specifies a sextant and sixty-degree arc. The separate quadrant lead remains uncounted pending independent distinction. His shamila, described elsewhere in the biography with its own function and latitude range, is another object, not a convenient way to multiply this record {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea instruments}.

## Today

Reading the documents now makes resolution and accuracy distinct. MacTutor speaks of seconds; Van Brummelen's text prints10' for the scale marking; the Arabic author explicitly discusses degrees, minutes and seconds. We neither silently repair the printed mark nor certify an exact smallest graduation from this disagreement. Recovering a correct scale would still leave the question of an aperture changing position {cite:khujandi-mactutor sextant} {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea sextant} {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 p67}.

A reader can follow the modern numerical example and test what happens when one input changes. The point is sensitivity of inference, not an instrument ready for observation or a complete manufacturing revival from incomplete descriptions. Keeping the example bounded preserves the source's actual achievement: joining construction, observation and scientific interpretation in one documentary case {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 pp62-69}.

## Surviving examples

The directly read witness is Louis Cheikho's1908 edition in al-Mashriq, printed pages60–69, available as a scan on Jan Hogendijk's website. We examined introduction, text, appended narrative and notes, not an autograph manuscript. Printed page61 warns of damaged or uncertain numerical readings in the exemplar. ## For young readers

Take two paper numbers:78.5 and31.5. Add them and divide by two to get55; subtract55 from90 to get35. Subtract the smaller number from the larger and halve the answer to get23.5. This is a calculation game using our idealized example, not instructions for watching the Sun. No light, lens, climbing or looking at a bright object is needed {cite:khujandi-cheikho-1908 pp64-66}.

Change only the first reading to79.5: both calculated answers change. Then instead add one to both original readings: only inferred latitude changes in this model. The comparison explains a historian's question: did the sky change, or did part of the instrument move? It cannot decide the Rayy episode, but it shows why criticism of a measurement can matter as much as the ambition to make it {cite:khujandi-mactutor settlement}.
