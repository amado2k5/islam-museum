## Summary
Al-Mizzi's astrolabe with mixed stereographic projections is the instrument variant attributed to the Damascene muwaqqit Shams al-Din Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Mizzi (c. 690–750 AH / 1291–1349 CE), who the BEA reports — through the fifteenth-century scholar Ibn al-Attar — as having made astrolabes combining projections about both poles {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. His documented craft includes unusual astrolabes that sold for ten dinars, signed Damascus quadrants preserved in the British Museum, and treatises on the astrolabe and the sine quadrant {cite:isi-mizzi-bea} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 20}. The museum records the contribution as an improvement inside the astrolabe tradition, not the invention of the astrolabe or of mixed projection.

## What it is
The astrolabe is the medieval Islamic astronomical computer: a brass rete of star-pointers rotating over a plate engraved with horizon, meridian and almucantar coordinates, from which a user reads altitudes, times and qibla directions {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 20}. Al-Mizzi's variant used mixed stereographic projections — combining projections about both celestial poles in one instrument — as Ibn al-Attar reported of his work {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. The result was an astrolabe whose coordinate grid was drawn by a non-standard projection scheme, producing plates that differed from the standard Damascus or Maghrebi patterns.

## The world before it
The astrolabe was already a mature instrument by al-Mizzi's century. Planispheric astrolabes — projecting the celestial sphere stereographically onto a plane — had been made in the Islamic world for five centuries, with established schools in Baghdad, Córdoba, Cairo and Damascus {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 20}. The Damascene school in particular had produced sophisticated instruments under scholars like Ibn al-Shatir and Ibn al-Mutruh. What al-Mizzi added was not the instrument class but a variant within it: astrolabes whose plates were computed by mixing projections about both poles, a scheme Ibn al-Attar found noteworthy enough to record {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}.

## The story
Shams al-Din al-Mizzi was a Hanafi astronomer and timekeeper (muwaqqit) at the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus, active in the first half of the eighth century AH {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina catalog describes him as famous for making unusual astrolabes, one of which sold for ten dinars, and notes a sundial bearing his signature preserved in the British Museum {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 20}. The BEA biography, drawing on Ibn al-Attar's notice, adds that he made astrolabes with mixed projections about both poles and that he wrote treatises on the astrolabe and the quadrants {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. Among his works, the treatises al-Rawdhat al-muzhirat on the astrolabic quadrant and Kashf al-rayb on the sine quadrant were popular with students of timekeeping {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}; he had studied in Cairo in his youth under the physician and encyclopedist Ibn al-Akfani {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}.

His career sits inside the Mamluk period, when Damascus was a major centre of instrument-making and astronomical education. The muwaqqit's practical need — accurate prayer times for the congregations at the Umayyad Mosque — drove the precision his instruments achieved. His signed quadrants, dated between 1326/1327 and 1333/1334, are the physical evidence of that environment {cite:isi-mizzi-bea} {cite:commons-british-museum-quadrants}.

What distinguishes al-Mizzi in the record is identifiability. Most instrument-makers of the period are anonymous; his name survives because he signed his work, because a later scholar found his astrolabes worth describing, and because the Bibliotheca Alexandrina catalog preserved his biographical details. The ten-dinar price recorded for his astrolabe places him among the elite makers of his city — not a routine workshop hand but a craftsman whose name had market value.

## The maker(s)
Al-Mizzi's Muslim identity is documented explicitly: the BEA biography names him al-Mizzi al-Hanafi and places him as muwaqqit of the Umayyad Mosque, an institution of Sunni Damascus {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. His Arabic treatises on instruments are the scholarly work; his maker's mark is the signature on quadrants and the market reputation that sold one astrolabe for ten dinars {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 20}. He was a craftsman-scholar: the person who both designed the instrument and, presumably, directed or performed its construction.

The Damascus of his day was a city of workshops and madrasas where instrument-making was a recognised craft with its own literature. A muwaqqit attached to the Umayyad Mosque had both the mathematical training and the institutional reason to produce precise instruments — the prayer timetable demanded them {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. Al-Mizzi's signed quadrants and named treatises place him at the top of that craft culture, not at its margins. His biographer al-Safadi reports that he also excelled in oiling bowstrings and in constructing mechanical devices in the manner of the Banu Musa — a wider picture of the craft than his name on a quadrant suggests {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}.

## How it works
A standard planispheric astrolabe projects the celestial sphere from the south pole onto a plane; the horizon, meridian and almucantar circles become computable curves on the plate {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 20}. Mixed projection means combining projection schemes about both poles — the resulting plate carries coordinate curves that cannot be produced by either pole's projection alone, yielding a hybrid grid {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. The user still rotates the rete against the plate to read positions, but the non-standard grid gives different — and for some latitudes, more convenient — readings than the standard patterns.

## The spread
Al-Mizzi's instruments stayed within the Damascus workshop tradition; there is no documented export of his specific plates beyond the city {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. His treatises on the astrolabe and sine quadrant circulated as texts, contributing to the pedagogical literature of instrument-making. The mixed-projection variant itself is documented only through Ibn al-Attar's fifteenth-century report — whether it became a school or remained a personal style is unknown {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. What can be said is that his reputation survived at least a century after his death, since Ibn al-Attar found his astrolabes distinctive enough to describe their projection scheme by name.

## What it was built on
The variant rests on the mature planispheric astrolabe tradition and on the mathematical theory of stereographic projection developed by Muslim astronomers from al-Khwarizmi through al-Battani {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 20}. The Damascene school had already produced astrolabes of high sophistication; al-Mizzi's innovation was applying a mixed scheme to that inherited instrument {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. His treatises show he understood the theory, not merely the craft.

## What was built on it
Nothing is documented as built directly on al-Mizzi's mixed-projection astrolabes {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. His quadrant-making fed the continuing Damascene tradition of observational instruments, and his treatises joined the literature used by later instrument-makers. The British Museum's Damascus quadrants — signed instruments from his milieu — are the tangible survivors of the workshop culture he belonged to {cite:commons-british-museum-quadrants}.

## Impact on daily life
The astrolabe served daily religious and practical needs: prayer times, qibla direction, and the astrological judgements that permeated educated life {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 20}. A more accurate or more convenient instrument meant faster, more reliable answers for the muwaqqit and for anyone consulting him. The ten-dinar price of his astrolabe placed it among luxury scientific instruments — affordable to scholars and courts, not to ordinary people. For the congregation at the Umayyad Mosque, the instrument's value was indirect but real: the timekeeper's precision determined when the call to prayer was called.

## How it changed history
Al-Mizzi's contribution is documentary rather than revolutionary: he is evidence that the astrolabe tradition in Mamluk Damascus remained alive and inventive at the workshop level {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. The mixed-projection variant shows that instrument-makers were still experimenting with projection mathematics five centuries after the astrolabe's invention. His signed quadrants and named treatises make him one of the few instrument-makers of his period identifiable by name — a rarity that gives his record historical weight beyond the instrument itself.

## Who invented it?
The astrolabe was not invented by al-Mizzi, and mixed stereographic projection is not documented as his invention either {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. His documented role is maker of astrolabes employing the mixed-projection variant — production within a tradition, possibly personal experimentation, possibly workshop practice transmitted to him. The museum records improvement: a documented specialist whose instruments were notable enough for a later scholar to record their projection scheme.

## Today
No surviving astrolabe is securely identified as al-Mizzi's mixed-projection work; the dated evidence is the quadrant tradition he belonged to {cite:isi-mizzi-bea} {cite:commons-british-museum-quadrants}. The astrolabe survives as a museum object and as a teaching tool for spherical astronomy; modern makers reproduce historical plates for education. The displayed photograph shows Damascus quadrants of his era — context for the workshop, not a copy of his astrolabe.

## Surviving examples
Signed Damascus quadrants attributed to his milieu survive in the British Museum {cite:commons-british-museum-quadrants}. His treatises survive in manuscript; no mixed-projection astrolabe bearing his name is known {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. The generic diagram in this record is a modern educational illustration of the mixed-projection principle, not a reconstruction of his instrument.

## In culture
Al-Mizzi belongs to the culture of the muwaqqit — the timekeeper whose craft joined astronomy, mathematics and religious practice in the mosque's service {cite:isi-mizzi-bea}. His instruments and treatises show that this craft culture produced not just routine copies but named innovators whose work later scholars found worth recording. The astrolabe itself became an emblem of Islamic science in the popular imagination, and makers like al-Mizzi are its human face.

## For young readers
The astrolabe is an ancient brass computer that tells time and finds directions using the stars. In Damascus, a scholar named al-Mizzi became famous for making special versions of it — ones that used a clever mixed way of drawing the star maps. His instruments were so good that people paid ten gold coins for them, and some of his signed tools are still in museums today.

And so al-Mizzi's record stands as a witness that the Damascene astrolabe tradition did not die in the eighth century AH — it was still producing named makers who reconsidered the mathematics behind the instrument.
