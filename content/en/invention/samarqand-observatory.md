## Summary

Around 1420 CE, on the hill of Kuhak outside Samarqand, the Timurid ruler Ulugh Beg raised the largest observatory of the medieval world: a three-storey circular building housing a sextant of forty metres' radius — the Fakhri sextant — where a resident team of astronomers measured the heavens nightly for three decades. Out of it came the era's most accurate star catalogue, and a model of state-sponsored observational science unmatched until Tycho Brahe {cite:mactutor-ulugh-beg}.

## What it is

A permanent research institution, not merely an instrument: the observatory held a teaching circle, a computing staff, a library and giant fixed instruments sunk into the hill for stability — architecture, personnel and programme in one {cite:mactutor-ulugh-beg}.

## The world before it

Observatories existed — Maragha under Nasir al-Din al-Tusi two centuries earlier, the Abbasid stations before that — but they were ruler-dependent projects that faded with their patrons. None matched the planned scale of Samarqand's instruments or staff.

## The story

Ulugh Beg (1394–1449), grandson of Timur, governed Transoxiana from Samarqand and was himself a trained mathematician and astronomer. About 1420 he founded the observatory, appointed Qadi-zada al-Rumi and Jamshid al-Kashi to lead its work, and presided over its observing programme for thirty years — until his murder in 1449 ended both reign and institution {cite:mactutor-ulugh-beg}.

## The maker(s)

Ulugh Beg (Mirza Muhammad Taraghay), Timurid ruler of Transoxiana and working astronomer — commissioner and participant; al-Kashi and al-Rumi led computation under him. His Muslim identity is recorded on his person record.

## How it works

Precision came from size: the marble Fakhri sextant, a meridian arc some forty metres in radius set in a trench, let observers read solar and stellar altitudes to arc-seconds. Repeated measures over years averaged out error — the tables improved as the decades accumulated {cite:mactutor-ulugh-beg}.

## The spread

The institution died with its patron — the building was razed within a generation — but its output travelled: the Zij circulated through Ottoman, Indian and European collections, and the observatory entered legend as the summit of pre-telescopic astronomy.

## What it was built on

On the Maragha observatory model — instruments, staff, library, patron — refined by two centuries of Islamic positional astronomy and by al-Kashi's computational programme.

## What was built on it

The very idea of the state observatory: Tycho's Uraniborg (1576) stands in the same institutional lineage — a permanent facility, precision instruments, cumulative measurement — as does every national observatory since.

## Impact on daily life

It made astronomy a public enterprise: salaries, a building, a curriculum — science as institution rather than a scholar's private work.

## How it changed history

It produced the most accurate pre-telescopic star data and proved that long-duration institutional observation — not individual genius alone — advances precision astronomy {cite:mactutor-ulugh-beg}.

## Who invented it?

Ulugh Beg, in the patron's sense — he founded, funded and worked it — but the observatory was also a collective: al-Kashi and al-Rumi's names stand inside its tables.

## Today

Its excavated sextant trench is preserved at Samarqand as a museum; its star positions are still compared to modern values to measure the drift of centuries.

## Surviving examples

The excavated Fakhri sextant arc and the Zij manuscripts in world libraries {cite:mactutor-ulugh-beg}.

## In culture

Samarqand's observatory is Uzbekistan's emblem of scientific heritage — on currency, stamps and museum fronts.

## For young readers

Six hundred years ago a king who loved the stars dug a giant marble arc into a hill, big as a building, and used it to measure the sky more precisely than anyone before. His observatory ran for thirty years — until it became the model for every observatory since.
