## Summary
The Riyasi is a pen invented by al-Fadl ibn Sahl — Dhu al-Riyasatayn, vizier of al-Ma'mun — which Ibn al-Nadim calls "the finest of pens", branching into several pens whose names the Fihrist lists: the great Riyasi, half-Riyasi, the third, small-half, light-third, the muhaqqiq, the munthar, the washi, the raqa'iq, the mukatabat, ghubar al-halba, the narcissus and the bayad {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. The museum records this pen as a chancery innovation attributed to the Abbasid caliphate's vizier in al-Ma'mun's reign — not as the invention of the Arabic script or of calligraphy in general.

## What it is
The Riyasi in the Fihrist is not a bare script but a pen — a weighted writing instrument with sizes and names — inside an ordered chancery system of al-Ma'mun's age {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18-19}. Ibn al-Nadim describes the ranked weighted pens: he begins with the heavy pens used for sound documents — among them qalam al-tumar, written on a Syrian roll with a palm-leaf, and qalam al-thulthayn, qalam al-sijillat, qalam al-'uhud, qalam al-mu'amarat, qalam al-amanat, qalam al-dibaj, qalam al-mudmaj, qalam al-muraqqa' and qalam al-nusakh — then says that al-Fadl ibn Sahl "invented a pen that is the finest of pens, known as al-Riyasi, and it branches into several pens" {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. The branch names show it was not a single pen but a family of pens of different sizes for different chancery purposes, from the great to the small {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. Ibn al-Nadim describes neither the letterforms nor a written specimen; his material is the pen's name, its branches and its office in the diwan {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}.

## The world before it
Before the Abbasid state, Ibn al-Nadim records that people wrote in the manner of the old script; when the Abbasid state appeared, the mushafs were reserved for those scripts and a script called al-Iraqi arose — the perfected one named warraqi — improving until the time of al-Ma'mun, when his companions and secretaries beautified their scripts and people boasted of it {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18}. Just before ranking the pens, Ibn al-Nadim mentions a man who edited the sultan's documents to the kings of the regions in scrolls, known as al-Ahwal al-Muharrir of the Barmakid works, versed in the meanings and shapes of script, who made it into kinds — a man who, in the text's phrase, was at the utmost in clumsiness and filth yet fluent beyond compare: he had codified the scripts and named their pens {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18}. The Abbasid chancery in al-Ma'mun's age was thus an arena of calligraphic rivalry: writers boasting of beautified hands, and weighted pens ranked for different uses, from scrolls to registers {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18-19}. That rivalry had a political edge: the scrolls carrying the sultan's orders to kings were documents of authority, and the finer their script the stronger the diwan's standing before kings and enemies alike {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18}. It is in exactly this setting that the Riyasi pen "arose", in Ibn al-Nadim's words {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}.

## The story
Al-Fadl ibn Sahl — to whom Ibn al-Nadim gives the title "Dhu al-Riyasatayn" — was al-Ma'mun's vizier {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. The TDV Islamic Encyclopedia traces his career: he was born at Sarakhs in Khurasan; his father Sehl ibn Zadanfarukh was an Iranian Zoroastrian who converted under the influence of Yahya ibn Khalid al-Barmaki {cite:tdv-fazl-b-sehl pp. 275-276}. Al-Fadl entered the service of Ja'far ibn Yahya al-Barmaki, and when the Barmakids fell he became al-Ma'mun's tutor; he accepted Islam in 190 AH / 806 CE {cite:tdv-fazl-b-sehl pp. 275-276}. In the struggle between al-Ma'mun and his brother al-Amin, al-Fadl led, with his brother al-Hasan, the campaign of empowerment; al-Ma'mun appointed him over the East and named him "Dhu al-Riyasatayn" — the first to combine military and civil authority in a single office, a title that appeared on the dinars struck in his reign {cite:tdv-fazl-b-sehl pp. 275-276}. TDV closes his biography with two notes that reach our pen: that al-Ma'mun's support of scholarly activity rested largely on the cultured vizier al-Fadl ibn Sahl, and that al-Fadl was killed in a bath at Sarakhs in 202 AH / 818 CE, most probably by the caliph's order {cite:tdv-fazl-b-sehl pp. 275-276}. So the pen attributed to him was born — if Ibn al-Nadim's attribution holds — inside a viceregal diwan at the height of its power {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19} {cite:tdv-fazl-b-sehl pp. 275-276}.

## The maker(s)
The attributed maker is al-Fadl ibn Sahl, al-Ma'mun's vizier titled "Dhu al-Riyasatayn" {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. He is a Muslim in the sources read: TDV records that he accepted Islam in 190 AH / 806 CE, after his father had converted at the Barmakids' instigation {cite:tdv-fazl-b-sehl pp. 275-276}; no source names his legal school, so he is kept within the museum's Sunni frame. His role here is "inventor" because Ibn al-Nadim explicitly attributes the invention to him ("he invented a pen"), with the caveat that this attribution stands in a single bibliographic source of the tenth century CE, not in a contemporary written or drawn witness {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}.

## How it works
The pen is not a machine but a convention of writing: the name "riyasi" — echoing its owner's title "Dhu al-Riyasatayn" — suggests it issued from the diwan of the supreme vizier who managed military and civil affairs together {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19} {cite:tdv-fazl-b-sehl pp. 275-276}. Its office in the diwan is shown by its branch list: a great Riyasi pen, a half, a third, a small-half, a light-third, a muhaqqiq, a munthar, a washi, a raqa'iq, a mukatabat, ghubar al-halba, a narcissus and a bayad {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. These names measure the pen by sizes and uses: great and formal for the weightiest documents, half and third for smaller work, and functional names — correspondence, raqa'iq, ghubar al-halba — for letters and missives {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. What the script looked like is unknown today; Ibn al-Nadim contented himself with names and ranking, and no manuscript in the Riyasi hand has been identified in the sources read {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}.

## The spread
The name Riyasi does not spread in the sources read beyond the Fihrist; its only known testimony is what Ibn al-Nadim recorded around 987 CE {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. Ibn al-Nadim mentions neither a copying school nor pupils of al-Fadl ibn Sahl in script; he places the invention inside the ranked chancery pens {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. The documented spread is the spread of the Fihrist itself — among the most widely transmitted bibliographic books in the tradition — not the spread of a script specimen {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}.

## What it was built on
The pen rests on earlier chancery accumulation: the heavy pens ranked before him by al-Ahwal al-Muharrir — the Barmakid man who "made the scripts into kinds" and knew their meanings and shapes — which form the basis of Ibn al-Nadim's whole ranking {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18}. And on the improvement of the Iraqi script — the perfected one named warraqi — which reached its height in al-Ma'mun's age until his secretaries' script was boasted of {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18}. The attribution to al-Fadl ibn Sahl in the Fihrist comes after all this ranking: not an invention from nothing but a new pen "the finest of pens" inside an existing system {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}.

## What was built on it
The sources read establish that no later script descended directly from the Riyasi pen attributed to al-Fadl ibn Sahl {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. The later Abbasid chancery hands — muhaqqaq, naskh, thuluth — form a wider family, and some branch names of the Riyasi (such as muhaqqiq and raqa'iq) overlap with older pen names, so their relation is unresolved in the text {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18-19}. The record proves the pen's existence in Ibn al-Nadim's description, not a chain of script descent.

## Impact on daily life
In the caliphal diwan, writing was authority: the scrolls carrying the sultan's orders to kings needed a uniform official hand that surpassed what the market and rivals could produce {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18}. The "beautifying of scripts" among al-Ma'mun's secretaries was a matter of pride — "and people boasted of it", in Ibn al-Nadim's phrase — meaning script was professional and cultural capital for the diwan {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18}. The "finest of pens" attributed to the caliphate's sole vizier carried that capital to a symbolic summit: a script in the name of the man who joined the two powers, suggesting that the state's official writing was Abbasid in every sense {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19} {cite:tdv-fazl-b-sehl pp. 275-276}. But its documented practical effect stops at Ibn al-Nadim's description; there is no evidence it changed people's everyday papers.

## How it changed history
If the attribution holds, the record changed our view of the Abbasid diwan: writing there was not merely inherited technique but a field of innovation, where the vizier could invent titles and pens {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19} {cite:tdv-fazl-b-sehl pp. 275-276}. The name "Dhu al-Riyasatayn" itself — borne by the man who invented the pen — became the title of the dual state al-Ma'mun built after the brothers' war, so pen and title are two faces of one diwan {cite:tdv-fazl-b-sehl pp. 275-276}. And Ibn al-Nadim's testimony survives as proof that script for them was "a science with measures", weighed and ranked, not merely pretty letters {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18-19}.

## Who invented it?
Ibn al-Nadim explicitly attributes the invention: "he invented a pen that is the finest of pens, known as al-Riyasi" {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. Precision requires three bounds: first, that the attribution stands in the Fihrist — a bibliographic book of about 987 CE — not in a contemporary written or drawn witness {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. Second, that the Riyasi was one of several ranked chancery pens, from which many names branched, so the claim here covers the attributed pen and its branches, not all chancery script {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. Third, that the early chancery pens preceded al-Fadl ibn Sahl in Ibn al-Nadim's ranking — including what al-Ahwal al-Muharrir the Barmakid ordered — so the innovation is new inside an existing system {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18-19}. The museum records this as an attributed "original" — the documented source makes al-Fadl ibn Sahl the inventor — while preserving that it is transmitted by a bibliographer, not a script witness.

## Today
No manuscript or inscription in the Riyasi hand is identified in the sources read {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. Today Arabic scripts — naskh, thuluth and digital faces — are designed and printed, but no single chancery pen can be tied to a modern product {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. The displayed image is a later Abbasid-period Qur'an folio in broken cursive — context for the Abbasid chancery-script tradition after al-Fadl ibn Sahl's time, not a specimen of the Riyasi pen {cite:commons-met-abbasid-quran-folio}. The closest approach to this record today is appreciating the pen-names and branches in the Fihrist, not viewing a lost script.

## Surviving examples
The only documented witness is the Fihrist text at pages 18–19 of the al-Furqan edition: the ranking of the weighted pens, then the sentence "he invented a pen that is the finest of pens, known as al-Riyasi and branching into several pens" with the branch names {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18-19}. No manuscript specimen in the Riyasi hand is documented in the sources read; the testimony is descriptive, not formal {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. The displayed Qur'an folio — Met 62.152.6 — witnesses the later Abbasid naskh tradition, not the pen itself {cite:commons-met-abbasid-quran-folio}.

## In culture
The Fihrist is an early "museum" of bibliography: Ibn al-Nadim preserves pen-names and their ranks in it as if preserving artifacts {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18-19}. The Riyasi — a name derived from the title of the vizier who made it, as if script became a diwan by privilege — compresses al-Ma'mun's age: a script rivalling the power al-Fadl ibn Sahl joined in his own person {cite:tdv-fazl-b-sehl pp. 275-276} {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 19}. In the study of Arabic script today, this record shows that script was not "pretty letters" but an institution: a pen for every station, a name for every pen, an owner for every name {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 18-19}.

## For young readers
In the time of the Abbasid caliphs, scripts had many names and weighted pens for every station: a pen for sultanic letters to kings, a pen for registers, a pen for contracts. It is said that a great minister named al-Fadl ibn Sahl invented a new pen he called the Riyasi because it was the finest of pens, and it branched into many pens used by the chancery's writers. No specimen of this pen survives, but the names of its branches remain in the book al-Fihrist — telling us that script was a measured science for them, not just pretty letters.

And so the Riyasi remains a witness that script among Muslims was no mere ornament but the language of power: the pen's name carries the name of the man who joined army and diwan, and its branches preserve that it was a complete system of pens for every function. Perhaps this is why its name survived in the Fihrist after the script itself vanished — names outlive specimens.
