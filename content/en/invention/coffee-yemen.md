## Summary
The name 'kahwa' was transferred from wine to the drink of the coffee-bean in [[place/yemen|Yemen]] towards the end of the eighth century AH / fourteenth century CE, and drinking spread from Sufi circles because it aided wakefulness for devotional nights {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV p. 482 KAHWA}. Traditions credit its introduction at Aden to the jurist [[person/muhammad-ibn-said-al-dhabhani|Muhammad ibn Sa'id al-Dhabhani]] and at Mocha to 'Ali ibn 'Umar al-Shadhili; from Yemen it passed to Mecca, Cairo and Istanbul {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 482–486 KAHWA}.

## What it is
Coffee is a drink brewed from the beans of the coffee plant, native to Ethiopia, or from the dried husk of the bean (qishr) as Yemenis preferred {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV p. 486 KAHWA}. The word kahwa is old — used in poetry for wine — and was most plausibly transferred to the new drink, though a derivation from 'Kaffa' remains unproven {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV p. 482 KAHWA}.

## The world before it
Arabia had no comparable stimulant drink before coffee; the tree grew wild in Ethiopia, and its arrival in Yemen is not attested before Islam {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV p. 482 KAHWA}. Chewing the berries themselves (nakl) is described as an older practice, before the husks and beans came to be brewed {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 483–484 KAHWA}.

## The story
Al-Jaziri, quoting Ibn 'Abd al-Ghaffar, says coffee-drinking was known at Cairo at the start of the tenth century AH, brought by Sufis from Yemen; they say the jurist [[person/muhammad-ibn-said-al-dhabhani|Muhammad ibn Sa'id al-Dhabhani]] ⁦(d. 875/1470)⁩ learned of it during a forced stay on the African coast and carried it back to Aden {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 482–483 KAHWA}. A treatise by 'Abd al-Qadir al-'Aidarus instead calls 'Ali ibn 'Umar al-Shadhili ⁦(d. 821/1418)⁩, the patron saint of Mocha, the 'innovator of coffee' — the traditions compete rather than agree {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 483–484 KAHWA}. At Mecca coffee was known about ⁦(817/1414)⁩ or late in the ninth century, coffee-houses opened, and Kha'ir Beg's attempt to ban it in ⁦(917/1511)⁩ failed to stop it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 484–485 KAHWA}.

## The maker(s)
Three names contest the honour of the introduction: al-Dhabhani, al-Shadhili and al-'Aidarus, and their plurality points to local traditions rather than one documented event {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 483–484 KAHWA}. Ukers likewise treats the Omar-and-the-goats-type legends as unhistorical {cite:ukers-all-about-coffee-1922 pp. 16–17}.

## How it works
The berries are dried and husked in mills, producing bean and husk; in Yemen the favoured drink was an infusion of the husk (qishr). The tree bears from its fourth year and fruits almost year-round in terraced gardens between about 1100 and 2200 metres {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 485–486 KAHWA}.

## The spread
From Yemen to Mecca (about ⁦(817/1414)⁩), then to Cairo in the early sixteenth century through the Sufis of al-Azhar, then Damascus and Aleppo where Rauwolff found it common in ⁦(1573)⁩, and Istanbul under Suleyman, where the first coffee-house opened in ⁦(962/1554)⁩ {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 484–485 KAHWA}.

## What it was built on
It was built on a wild Ethiopian tree, a poetic word that meant wine, and Yemen's Red Sea trading networks — not on the discovery of a plant {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 482, 486 KAHWA}.

## What was built on it
Coffee-houses (buyut al-kahwa) and the kahwekhane institution, nicknamed 'school of the wise', the jurists' fatwas for and against it, and the world Mocha trade {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 484–488 KAHWA}.

## Impact on daily life
It gave devotional and working life a non-alcoholic stimulant: Sufis drank it with pious intention, classed it among acts of obedience, and assigned it a litany repeating 'ya kawi' 116 times {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 483–484 KAHWA}.

## How it changed history
A Yemenite order's beverage became a principal commodity of Middle Eastern commerce; Yemen held the supply monopoly until the English and Dutch East India companies broke it, carrying cultivation to Java, the Caribbean and South America {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 487–488 KAHWA}.

## Who invented it?
No inventor is established; traditions name al-Dhabhani, al-Shadhili or al-'Aidarus, and scholars treat these as concurrent local accounts rather than a single discovery {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 483–484 KAHWA} {cite:ukers-all-about-coffee-1922 pp. 16–17}.

## Today
Coffee is among the most-traded beverages on earth, from Yemeni qishr to the world's espresso and filter traditions {cite:ukers-all-about-coffee-1922 pp. 16–18}.

## Surviving examples
Al-Shadhili's tomb and mosque at Mocha, with his well and gate, preserve his memory for ordinary people; the earliest written mentions of the drink appear in tenth-century AH / sixteenth-century books {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 482–484 KAHWA}.

## In culture
'Spiritual coffee' and 'Sufi coffee' are phrases writers used for inner delight, linking the drink to the dhikr nights of the orders {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IV pp. 483–484 KAHWA}.

## For young readers
Coffee is the fruit of a tree whose seeds are called beans. The tree lived wild in Ethiopia, then Yemenis planted it and made a drink from it. Sufi worshippers drank it to stay awake at prayer nights, and from Aden, Mecca and Mocha it travelled to the whole world. There are several stories about who found it first, and none is certain.
