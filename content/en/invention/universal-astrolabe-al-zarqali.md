## Summary

In 11th-century [[place/tulaytula|Toledo]], [[person/al-zarqali|Ibrahim ibn Yahya al-Zarqali]] developed an astronomical plate that did not require a replacement horizon plate whenever the observer changed latitude; it became known as the zarqaliyya and, in European usage, the saphaea {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. This was an improvement within the astrolabe tradition, not invention of the astrolabe from nothing or proof that every later universal instrument descended from it {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 41, 47–49}. Universal means applicable across latitudes, not a machine that discovers location and time without knowledge or interpretation {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 47–53}. The photograph shows a display copy in Cordoba, not an object authenticated as al-Zarqali's own work {cite:commons-calahorra-azafea-codex description}.

## What it is

The plate is a geometrical representation of the sky on a disc, with lines for moving between coordinate systems and a ruler representing the horizon {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. An ordinary planispheric astrolabe carries a rotating star-and-zodiac network above a plate appropriate to a particular latitude; the zarqaliyya instead combines coordinate grids on its surface, without that rotating network on the front {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 47–48}.

The disc is not a photograph of the sky: it represents angular relationships and circles projected from an imagined sphere onto a plane {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 49–53}. Coordinates are different addresses for the same position, expressed relative to the celestial equator, the ecliptic or the observer's horizon {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 51–53}. The instrument organizes these relationships for calculation, but a person still chooses the inputs and reads the marks {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 47–53}.

## The world before it

The difficulty was not absence of earlier astronomical instruments: an ordinary astrolabe's horizon was drawn on a plate associated with a particular latitude {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 40, 49}. Moving north or south changes the horizon's relationship to the celestial sphere, requiring a suitable plate {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 49–53}. The universal plate addresses a design question: could a different representation allow one disc to serve multiple latitudes? {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 47–53}

The zarqaliyya remains part of astrolabe construction and spherical geometry; the study compares the projections by their viewpoints and coordinate representation {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 49–52}. Its story does not begin with a sky previously beyond calculation, nor erase earlier work because a new solution relaxed one instrument's constraint {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 41, 49–53}.

## The story

Roser Puig describes al-Zarqali as a Toledan instrument maker who taught himself astronomy and worked with Said al-Andalusi under the city's ruler al-Mamun {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. This was the 11th-century ruler of Toledo, not the Abbasid caliph who lived centuries earlier {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Bir and colleagues connect his prominence with precision craftsmanship and participation in observation {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 p. 46}.

Puig reports a 14th-century Egyptian witness to a lost work by Said: al-Zarqali constructed an instrument called al-zarqala for al-Mamun in 1048/1049 and wrote 100 chapters on its use {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. This is a report transmitted through a later witness, not a foundation document independently read by this museum or a signature dating every plate variant; the record therefore dates the design to the 11th century rather than applying that year indiscriminately {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. The different treatises also show an instrument accompanied by explanation and teaching, not an isolated disc {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 p. 47}.

## The maker(s)

Al-Zarqali is the improver of this particular design and author of treatises about it; Puig distinguishes his observations, tables and other instruments from the universal plate {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. The equatorium representing planetary circles, for example, is another instrument: its treatment of Mercury must not become an explanation of the plate's mechanism {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

The Bibliotheca Alexandrina Antiquities Museum explicitly names him in its section on Muslim astronomers: a cited biographical classification, not an inference from name or country {cite:bibalex-astrolabe-949 The Role of Muslim Astronomers}. The browser's Sunni default is a declared presentation rule, not a quotation asserting that he personally professed a particular legal school; neither the institutional account nor Puig's biography supplies such a personal declaration {cite:bibalex-astrolabe-949 The Role of Muslim Astronomers} {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

## How it works

The change begins with projection: Puig describes replacing the standard astrolabe's equatorial stereographic projection with a meridian projection onto the solstitial-colure plane, viewing from the equinox points and combining the representations {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Put simply, a different geometrical arrangement maps the celestial sphere to the disc; the sky itself has not changed {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 49–52}.

The front combines equatorial and ecliptic grids: the first represents right ascension and declination, the second longitude and latitude relative to the ecliptic {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 51–53}. A horizon ruler allows representation of the third system, altitude and azimuth associated with the observer's location {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 48, 51–53}. Lines and marks thus support conversion between celestial addresses without engraving a separate horizon plate for each latitude {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 49–53}.

The study explains that circles on the sphere become circles or straight lines in this projection and that angles are preserved, making the drawing useful for calculation {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 p. 49}. Reading precision is not unlimited: its construction discussion recommends a size that makes marks distinguishable, with different graduation marks for different intervals {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 53–54}. Fine engraving does not mean all surviving instruments have identical dimensions {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 52–54}.

Puig distinguishes functions on the back, including a trigonometric quadrant and a circle associated with the Moon's distance; Bir and colleagues discuss altitude, calendar, zodiac and sine scales there {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260} {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 p. 48}. Without examining the photographed copy, every visible curve should not be assigned a specific function: photographs show particular objects, whereas treatises describe designs that can differ {cite:commons-calahorra-azafea-codex description} {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

## The spread

The evidence here concerns circulation of texts and knowledge, not sales: Puig identifies a Castilian translation of the zarqaliyya treatise and Latin and Hebrew translations of the shakkaziyya treatise {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Bir and colleagues describe its presence in Marrakushi's work on timekeeping, with construction and operating guides in separate sections {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 41–42}. This article relies on scholarly identification of those translations, not independent readings of their manuscripts {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

That distinction prevents drawing one European route for everything: transmission of a zarqaliyya book is not automatically transmission of identical shakkaziyya details or every other “universal” instrument {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. The pages read do not count workshops and users or date acquisition by every port {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 41, 46–54}.

## What it was built on

The technical comparison starts with the planispheric astrolabe, its local horizon plate and rotating star network, alongside knowledge of projections and spherical relationships {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 47–52}. The improvement retains the astronomical problem while rearranging coordinate representation and the ruler so that a single latitude no longer constrains the plate {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 47–53}. This is an explainable design relationship, not identification of one physical prototype al-Zarqali copied {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 46–53}.

The record does not list every earlier astrolabe inventor: the technical pages read do not establish an uninterrupted chain of workshops with every link identifiable {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 41, 47–54}. The particular contribution remains clear without suppressing the older instrument's history {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 47–53}.

## What was built on it

Puig presents the shakkaziyya as a simplified form in Marrakushi's account, reducing parts of the ecliptic grid and differing from the more elaborate zarqaliyya on the back {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. This distinguishes named variants in that reference, rather than declaring every later universal astrolabe a direct descendant of one object {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

Further evidence includes preservation of Marrakushi's construction account and its modern study and drawing, the subject of the 2020 article {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 40–41, 47–54}. These witnesses do not authorize an undocumented family tree extending to every modern navigation instrument {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 41, 47–54}.

## Impact on daily life

The identifiable practical change is reduced need for different horizon plates when observing latitude changes; it matters to someone moving a calculation between locations {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 40, 49–53}. It does not remove the need for coordinate information, a ruler and scale reading: the device organizes relationships for a human user {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 47–53}.

The study connects these instruments with calculation of time and place rather than metal ornament alone {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 p. 41}. The sources read do not establish money or minutes saved, or numbers of sailors carrying one; a design advantage cannot be turned into unsupported statistics {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 41, 46–54}.

## How it changed history

The plate brings craft, geometry and explanation together: sources preserve long treatises, translations and a later construction account, not only a remembered name {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260} {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 41, 47}. These document teachable knowledge moving between languages and scholarly settings, a narrower claim than one instrument creating all European navigation {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

The comparison also shows improvement through representing a known problem differently: celestial angles remain the subject, but their arrangement on a portable readable surface changes {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 49–54}. This describes a documented technical advance, not civilizational superiority or erasure of translators and copyists {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

## Who invented it?

The claim is specific: Puig and Bir's team attribute the zarqaliyya and treatises to al-Zarqali and explain the departure from ordinary astrolabes {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260} {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 41, 47–53}. The browser therefore records an improver, not inventor of every astrolabe or every latitude-independent solution {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 47–53}.

Four dates must remain distinct: design, composition of a treatise, copying a manuscript and manufacture of a surviving object; one does not automatically date the others {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260} {cite:louvre-abu-dhabi-saphea catalogue fields}. Nor does the 2012 photograph date manufacture of its display copy or authenticate it as the Andalusian original {cite:commons-calahorra-azafea-codex description and date}. The design attribution can remain documented while those limits stay visible {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

## Today

Louvre Abu Dhabi lists a brass saphea, LAD2019.001, ca. 1224, diameter 28.3cm, possibly produced in Andalusia, and described as on display when consulted {cite:louvre-abu-dhabi-saphea catalogue fields}. That catalogue does not establish that al-Zarqali made it or that its manufacture dates the invention {cite:louvre-abu-dhabi-saphea catalogue fields}.

The photograph selected here shows a display copy at Torre de la Calahorra in Cordoba according to its photographer's description; the museum has not adopted that description's claim about an original elsewhere as an up-to-date provenance record {cite:commons-calahorra-azafea-codex description}. An idea can be encountered in a metal exhibit and studied in a book without equating a teaching copy with a historical original {cite:commons-calahorra-azafea-codex description} {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 47–54}.

## Surviving examples

Textual witnesses include the treatises and translations Puig distinguishes and Marrakushi's account discussed in the modern study; the original manuscripts were not independently examined for this record {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260} {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 40–41}. Note 34 on p. 52 of the study identifies an instrument in the Bibliotheque nationale de France used for one drawing, but publication does not automatically license its modern photograph {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 p. 52 n.34}.

The Louvre object and licensed Cordoba-copy photograph are different witnesses, not authenticated views of the same object {cite:louvre-abu-dhabi-saphea catalogue fields} {cite:commons-calahorra-azafea-codex description}. Keeping them separate prevents an attractive image from standing in for missing ownership or dating evidence {cite:commons-calahorra-azafea-codex description}.

## In culture

European names retain traces of transmission: Puig discusses variants of al-Zarqali's name and the plate treatises, while the photograph's caption calls the copy an Azafea {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260} {cite:commons-calahorra-azafea-codex description}. A name is a linguistic and scientific trace, not sufficient evidence for dating each object bearing it {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. The sources used do not document a particular film or novel featuring this plate, so none is invented here {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 40–41, 46–54}.

## For young readers

Imagine a sky map whose horizon changes as you travel north or south; an ordinary plate suits a particular latitude {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 49–53}. Instead of carrying a plate for every location, al-Zarqali used grids and rulers adjustable across latitudes {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Universal does not mean it knows your location by itself: a person still reads and calculates, while the lines connect different ways of describing the sky {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 47–53}.
