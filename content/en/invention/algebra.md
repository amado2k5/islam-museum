## Summary
Algebra, al-jabr wal-muqabala, is the art of finding an unknown number from known ones through an equation. [[person/al-khwarizmi|Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi]] set it out in Baghdad under the caliph [[person/abd-allah-al-mamun|al-Ma'mun]] in a concise book, [[work/al-jabr-wal-muqabala|al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala]], for people's needs in inheritance, bequests, trade and land measurement {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 pp. 29–30}. He reduced every problem to three kinds of term and six types of equation, each with a rule and a geometric proof {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 pp. 30–43}. [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]] names him the first to write in this art {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun p. 495}, and the science still carries the name of his book in the world's languages {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 25}.

## What it is
Ibn Khaldun counts algebra among the branches of the science of number: a craft by which the unknown is drawn out of the known when there is a relation between them that requires it. Its terms are number, the "thing" (the root) and "wealth" (the square), and the equations between them end in six problems {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun p. 495}.

Al-Khwarizmi begins in the same way. The numbers needed in reckoning by al-jabr and al-muqabala, he says, are of three kinds: roots, squares and simple number that belongs to neither {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 30}. Two operations give the art its name. Al-jabr, "restoring", removes a subtracted quantity from one side by adding it to both; al-muqabala, "balancing", cancels like terms that appear on both sides. To these TDV adds radd, dividing by the coefficient of the square, and ikmal, completing it to a whole square {cite:tdv-cebir pp. 195–201}. In al-Khwarizmi's worked problems the restoring step appears again and again: a quantity "less ten things" is restored and the ten things are added to the other side {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 pp. 51–53}.

## The world before it
People had solved equations long before al-Khwarizmi. The Cairo editors of his book open with that history. The Egyptian Ahmes papyrus of about 1700 BCE, the oldest surviving school book, already has a first-degree equation in one unknown with a special sign for the unknown {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 14}. The Babylonians made tables of squares and cubes, and Euclid has problems that amount to geometric solutions of quadratic equations {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 16}. Heron of Alexandria gives the first analytic solution one can be sure of {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 pp. 16–17}, and Diophantus knew how to solve quadratics with positive coefficients but did not arrange their types in order as al-Khwarizmi did {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 pp. 17–19}.

In India, Aryabhata must have known the solution of quadratics, Brahmagupta in the seventh century gave a rule in words for solving them, and Mahavira, al-Khwarizmi's contemporary, also gave rules {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 pp. 19–20}. TDV adds that the methods of double false position and of working backwards, and some types of word problem, entered Islamic algebra from Indian books, and that [[person/qusta-ibn-luqa|Qusta ibn Luqa]] translated Diophantus's Arithmetica into Arabic under the title Sina'at al-jabr, "the art of algebra" {cite:tdv-cebir pp. 195–201}.

## The story
In the early ninth century al-Ma'mun gathered scholars in Baghdad. The Cairo editors quote [[person/ibn-al-nadim|Ibn al-Nadim]]'s [[work/al-fihrist|Fihrist]]: al-Khwarizmi devoted himself to al-Ma'mun's treasury of wisdom, was one of the astronomers, and made the two astronomical tables known as the Sindhind {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 22}.

In his preface al-Khwarizmi praises God and asks blessings on the Prophet Muhammad, then says what moved him to write. The imam al-Ma'mun, commander of the faithful, loved learning, kept the learned close and helped them make clear what had been obscure, and this encouraged him to compose a concise book on reckoning by al-jabr and al-muqabala {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 29}. He limited it to what is subtle and noble in reckoning and what people need constantly: inheritance, bequests, the division of estates, judgements, trade and all their dealings with one another, land measurement, digging canals, engineering, and other matters {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 30}. The book has no year, but al-Ma'mun reigned from 813 to 833 CE {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 22}, so it was written in those years.

A copy survives in the Bodleian Library at Oxford, written in Cairo in Muharram 743 AH. Frederic Rosen published it with an English translation in London in 1831, and the Cairo edition of 1939 was prepared from that manuscript {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 26}.

## The maker(s)
[[person/al-khwarizmi|Abu Abd Allah (or Abu Ja'far) Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi]] was an astronomer and mathematician at al-Ma'mun's court in Baghdad. The Cairo editors describe him as learned in arithmetic, geography and astronomy, and as rightly regarded as the founder of algebra {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 24}. In the preface to his algebra he writes as a Muslim: he praises God, asks blessings on the Prophet and on the prophets before him, and calls al-Ma'mun the imam and commander of the faithful {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 29}.

## How it works
Al-Khwarizmi first gives three simple types, in which one kind of term equals another: squares equal roots, squares equal a number, and roots equal a number. He then gives three compound types: squares and roots equal a number, squares and a number equal roots, and roots and a number equal squares {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 pp. 31–32}.

His best-known example is "a square and ten roots equal thirty-nine dirhams". The rule: halve the roots, which gives five; multiply it by itself, twenty-five; add this to thirty-nine, sixty-four; take its root, eight; and subtract half the roots, five, which leaves three. That is the root, and the square is nine {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 pp. 32–33}. He then proves the rule with a drawing. A square stands for the unknown square, and rectangles on its sides stand for the ten roots; the corner left to complete the large square is five by five, or twenty-five, and the whole square is sixty-four with a side of eight {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 pp. 35–37}.

He also notes the case with no solution: when the square of half the roots is smaller than the number joined to the square, "the problem is impossible". The editors add that the name lasted among mathematicians until the study of imaginary quantities began at the end of the eighteenth century {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 35}. His algebra was wholly in words, with no symbols, and it accepted only positive roots {cite:tdv-cebir pp. 195–201}. He ends the theory with the rule of the whole book: every problem worked by al-jabr and al-muqabala must lead to one of the six types {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 43}.

## The spread
The book was studied, commented on and extended across the Islamic world. The Fihrist names at least three men who wrote commentaries on it {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 23}. Ibn Khaldun says that many Andalusians wrote fine commentaries on it, the best of them al-Qurashi's {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun pp. 495–496}. Robert of Chester translated it into Latin, and L. C. Karpinski published an English version of that Latin translation in 1915 {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 26}.

## What it was built on
Al-Khwarizmi's algebra drew on a long history of equation-solving: first-degree equations in the Ahmes papyrus, Babylonian tables, Greek geometric and analytic solutions, and Indian rules in words {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 pp. 13–21}. TDV also notes the Indian influence on its methods and word problems {cite:tdv-cebir pp. 195–201}. What he added was order: three kinds of term, six types, a rule for each, and a proof for each.

## What was built on it
[[person/abu-kamil-shuja-ibn-aslam|Abu Kamil Shuja' ibn Aslam]], about 925 CE, names al-Khwarizmi plainly as one of his sources {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 24}. Ibn Khaldun puts him next after al-Khwarizmi {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun p. 495}, and TDV says he grounded algebra in Euclidean geometry, worked with irrational numbers and solved geometric problems by algebra. [[person/al-karaji|Al-Karaji]] then made algebra arithmetical, freeing it from geometry, and worked on the expansion of (x + 1) to the power n. His follower [[person/al-samawal-al-maghribi|al-Samaw'al al-Maghribi]] wrote numbers and quantities with letters in an abstract style. [[person/umar-khayyam|Umar al-Khayyam]] did for third-degree equations what al-Khwarizmi had done for the first and second degrees: he sorted them into thirteen types and solved them with conic sections {cite:tdv-cebir pp. 195–201}. The Cairo editors add that al-Khayyam quotes Ibn Musa as a known authority without needing to name him {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 24}. Ibn Khaldun had heard that a mathematician of the East took the equations past the six types to more than twenty, with geometric proofs {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun p. 496}.

## Impact on daily life
The book was written for daily needs. Al-Khwarizmi says it was meant for inheritance, bequests, the division of estates, trade, land measurement and digging canals {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 30}. A long Book of Bequests in the same work, beginning at scan p. 83, applies its rules to shares of estates and bequests {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 83}.

## How it changed history
Solving number problems became a science, with its own kinds of term, its chapters and its proofs: every problem is brought by al-jabr and al-muqabala to one of the six types {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 43}. The Cairo editors write that his equations, such as "a square and ten roots equal thirty-nine", were repeated in every algebra book from his time to the start of the modern age, and that some are still found in algebra books today {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 24}.

## Who invented it?
The museum records al-Khwarizmi's work as an improvement. He did not invent the solving of equations, which is older than him, but he was the first known writer to make it an independent science.

**Sunni view (Ibn Khaldun).** Ibn Khaldun says the first to write in this art was Abu Abd Allah al-Khwarizmi, and after him Abu Kamil Shuja' ibn Aslam. He calls al-Khwarizmi's book on the six problems one of the best written on the subject, and says many Andalusians wrote commentaries on it {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun pp. 495–496}.

**Academic view (TDV).** TDV reports the general agreement of historians that algebra became an independent science with al-Khwarizmi, and that later mathematicians such as Sinan ibn al-Fath acknowledged him as first. The one dissenter in his own era was Abu Barza, who claimed the honour for his grandfather Abd al-Hamid ibn Wasi' ibn Turk; Abu Kamil firmly rebutted him. TDV also sets out why some modern historians doubt that the book was the first: it calls itself a summary, al-Khwarizmi does not claim to be first, its terms are already mature, and there was a book by Ibn Turk and a lost book by Sanad ibn Ali on the subject {cite:tdv-cebir pp. 195–201}.

Both views agree that his book is the oldest surviving work to set out algebra as a science of its own.

## Today
Algebra is today a basic branch of mathematics taught in schools, and it writes equations in symbols. Al-Khwarizmi's algebra was wholly in words and accepted only positive roots; al-Samaw'al later began to write numbers and quantities with letters {cite:tdv-cebir pp. 195–201}. The name of the science in all the world's languages comes from the word al-jabr that al-Khwarizmi used as the title of his book, and from his own name come the Latin "algorismus" and the English word "algorithm" {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 25}.

## Surviving examples
The book survives in a manuscript in the Bodleian Library at Oxford, copied in Cairo in Muharram 743 AH {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 26}. The image on this page shows two pages of a Bodleian manuscript of the book, with its proofs drawn as squares in red ink {cite:wikimedia-commons}.

## In culture
The names of al-Khwarizmi and of his book passed into the languages of the world. Among Muslims, Ibn Khaldun names him the first to write on algebra, and Zakariyya al-Qazwini says he was the first to bring algebra to the Muslims. In English his name became "algorithm", a set way of solving problems, and Chaucer used "augrim" for zero; until the early eighteenth century the numerals were called "algorismus" in Latin, and the Spanish word for a digit is "guarismo". His book's title became "algebra" {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 25}.

## For young readers
About twelve hundred years ago a scholar in Baghdad named al-Khwarizmi wrote a book teaching people how to find a number they did not know, so they could share out inheritances, measure land and work out trade. He sorted the problems into six kinds and explained each with drawings of squares. From his book's name, al-Jabr, comes the word algebra that pupils learn all over the world {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 pp. 29–32} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 25}.
