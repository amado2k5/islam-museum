## Summary
Nucleotide excision repair is the mechanism by which a living cell cuts out ultraviolet-damaged DNA and rebuilds it — and Aziz Sancar, a Turkish-American biochemist, mapped it enzyme by enzyme. The Nobel committee honoured his map, with Tomas Lindahl's and Paul Modrich's parallel mechanisms, by the 2015 Chemistry prize "for mechanistic studies of DNA repair" {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-2015-press-release} {cite:wikipedia-aziz-sancar-en}.

## What it is
One of the cell's repair toolkits: when UV light welds neighbouring bases of DNA into damaging kinks, the UvrABC machinery cuts the damaged strand on both sides of the lesion, lifts out a stretch of twelve to thirteen nucleotides, and lets the cell rebuild the gap with fresh code {cite:wikipedia-aziz-sancar-en}.

## The world before it
By the early 1970s scientists thought DNA was extremely stable; Tomas Lindahl then showed it actually decays so fast that life should be impossible — proving repair systems must exist. Photoreactivation was known too: bacteria dead from UV recovered under blue light, the puzzle that first drew Sancar in {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-2015-press-release} {cite:wikipedia-aziz-sancar-en}.

## The story
Born in Savur, Mardin, in 1946 to a family with no schooling, Sancar took a medical degree at Istanbul University, reached the University of Texas at Dallas and finished a doctorate on photolyase in 1977. Rejected for postdocs, he spent five years at Yale as a technician in Dean Rupp's lab — and there changed his field: identifying the UvrABC endonuclease and showing it makes two cuts on the damaged strand, lifting out the lesion with twelve to thirteen nucleotides. At Chapel Hill he traced the mechanism's later steps in bacteria and its harder human version, won the 2015 Nobel, and extended the same programme to the circadian clock {cite:wikipedia-aziz-sancar-en} {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-2015-press-release}.

## The maker(s)
Aziz Sancar ⁦(born 1946)⁩, practising Muslim and first Turkish member of the US National Academy of Sciences, is the Nobel-credited mapper of nucleotide excision repair — a documented original contribution to knowledge, not the claim of having invented the cell's machinery itself {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-2015-press-release} {cite:wikipedia-aziz-sancar-en}.

## How it works
The repair crew works in the dark: UvrABC endonuclease recognises the UV lesion, makes a double incision on the damaged strand, and removes a twelve-to-thirteen-nucleotide stretch carrying the damage; the healthy strand then guides resynthesis, restoring the code {cite:wikipedia-aziz-sancar-en}.

## The spread
The mechanism generalised beyond bacteria: Sancar himself worked out the more complex human version at Chapel Hill, and the whole understanding — that cells patrol and repair genomes constantly — became a pillar of molecular medicine {cite:wikipedia-aziz-sancar-en} {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-2015-press-release}.

## What it was built on
Photolyase and photoreactivation — his own doctoral territory — and Lindahl's demonstration that DNA needs repair at all; Rupp's laboratory gave him the experimental system to dissect the dark pathway {cite:wikipedia-aziz-sancar-en} {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-2015-press-release}.

## What was built on it
New cancer treatments built on repair biology, the understanding of inherited sunlight-sensitive skin-cancer diseases, and Sancar's own circadian work on the Period and Cryptochrome genes that may tune the timing of chemotherapy {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-2015-press-release} {cite:wikipedia-aziz-sancar-en}.

## Impact on daily life
It explains why skin cancers follow sun damage in people born with repair defects — and it underpins therapies designed to attack the repair systems of tumor cells {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-2015-press-release}.

## How it changed history
With Lindahl and Modrich it completed the first molecular map of genome maintenance — thousands of daily injuries met by a dedicated repair crew — reframing the living cell as a self-repairing machine {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-2015-press-release}.

## Who invented it?
The mechanism is nature's; the documented credit for mapping it molecularly is Sancar's alone for nucleotide excision repair, beside Lindahl's base excision and Modrich's mismatch repair — the Nobel text divides the credit explicitly {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-2015-press-release}.

## Today
Still a live programme at UNC Chapel Hill — the human steps of the pathway, photolyase's photocycle and the circadian genes — and a foundation of oncology's design logic {cite:wikipedia-aziz-sancar-en}.

A son of Savur who spoke no English on arrival — and a self-described practising Muslim proud of his faith — became the first Turkish member of the National Academy of Sciences and a Nobel laureate whose foundation hosts Turkish students in America {cite:wikipedia-aziz-sancar-en}.

## For young readers
Every day, sunlight and other things break little pieces of the DNA inside our cells, but we have tiny invisible workers — enzymes — that snip out the broken piece and fix it. Aziz Sancar, a Muslim scientist from Turkey, discovered exactly how these workers do their job step by step, and he won the Nobel Prize in 2015.
