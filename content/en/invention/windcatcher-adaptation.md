## Summary
The windcatcher — Persian badgir, Arabic badahandj, Egyptian malqaf — is the tower that air-conditions a house without fuel: it catches the breeze above the roof and drives it down into rooms, cellars and cisterns. Older than Islam, it was renamed, studied and perfected in the Islamic cities: Ibn Yunus analysed its orientation in Cairo, Abd al-Latif al-Baghdadi priced its grand shafts at five hundred dinars, and its earliest Cairene survivor crowns the al-Salih Talai mosque {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-windcatcher-en}.

## What it is
A ventilating tower projecting high above the roofs of houses — square-sectioned and four-sided in Persia, up to thirty-four metres tall and closable in winter; short, wide and bidirectional as the Egyptian malqaf — channelled with baffles so that wind is forced down on one side and warm air drawn out the other {cite:wikipedia-windcatcher-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The world before it
Such contrivances were already known in the Ancient Near East — Pharaonic Egyptian house models show roof windcatchers, and Babylon knew the like — while in Persia the badgir ran back to Achaemenid architecture. The word Islam used, badahandj, is itself an arabised Persian term {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-windcatcher-en}.

## The story
Iraq was the bridge west. The early Abbasid palace of Ukaydir already holds square ventilation shafts; by early Fatimid times the badahandj marked the Egyptian skyline, where the astronomer Ibn Yunus (d. 1008-9) discussed the correct orientation of its single aperture to the prevailing north wind. The device entered literature — the Thousand and One Nights names it, and the litterateur al-Ghuzuli (d. 1412-3) devoted a chapter of his anthology to it — while the grand Cairene shafts of Abd al-Latif al-Baghdadi's day (d. 1231-2) cost up to five hundred dinars {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The maker(s)
No inventor on record. The named analyst is Ibn Yunus, the Fatimid astronomer who studied how to aim the shaft at Egypt's cooling wind — an early written treatment of a vernacular device {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it works
Two engines: wind pressure driving outside air down baffled shafts into the rooms below, and the stack effect — a solar chimney — pulling hot air up and out when the air is still. Paired with water, it cools further by evaporation: fountains (salsabil) and wetted matting chill the incoming draft in dry climates {cite:wikipedia-windcatcher-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The spread
From the central plateau around Yazd and the Gulf coastlands — where Marco Polo called the badgirs of Hormuz the only things making summer life bearable — it went west through Iraq to Syria and Egypt as the badahandj and malqaf, and east into South Asia as the metre-square mungh of Sind; it also ventilated qanats, mine shafts and ice houses {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-windcatcher-en}.

## What it was built on
The Persian badgir and the older Near Eastern wind-shaft — Achaemenid towers, Pharaonic house scoops — plus the courtyard, qanat and salsabil ensembles it joined {cite:wikipedia-windcatcher-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What was built on it
The whole passive-cooling grammar of the Islamic city: malqaf plus salsabil plus shuksheika roof vent over the qaa hall, badgir over the cistern and the yakhchal — and, today, the ventilation towers of green architecture {cite:wikipedia-windcatcher-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Impact on daily life
It made summer habitable: cool air in the reception hall, a ventilated cellar, a bearable afternoon in Hormuz — the difference between fleeing the heat and living through it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it changed history
It is one of the clearest cases of a pre-Islamic device carried and completed under Islam: a Persian tower renamed into Arabic, analysed by an astronomer, priced like a luxury, sung in an anthology — and still answering the question of cooling without energy {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Who invented it?
Not contested — inherited. Pharaonic Egypt, Babylon and Achaemenid Persia hold the older witnesses; the Islamic contribution is adaptation: the badahandj-malqaf line, the four-sided Yazd tower, the scientific and literary record {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-windcatcher-en}.

## Today
It retreated before electric air conditioning — which now consumes about sixty percent of Egypt's peak power — but it is returning as a model of passive, zero-energy design, its Yazd towers and Cairene survivors conserved as heritage {cite:wikipedia-windcatcher-en}.

## Surviving examples
The badgir skyline of Yazd, the shaft in the qibla wall of the al-Salih Talai mosque (555/1160) — Cairo's earliest survivor — the malqafs of Cairene houses, and the wind towers of the Gulf and Sind {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-windcatcher-en}.

## In culture
It entered belles-lettres: the badahandj named in the Nights, a whole chapter of al-Ghuzuli's Matali al-Budur gathering its poetry — a building part famous enough to write poems about {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
Imagine a tower on the roof of a house in a hot city, open on every side: it catches the breeze up high and pushes it down cool into the rooms, and when no wind blows it works as a chimney pulling the heat upward. The houses of Yazd and Cairo lived with this air conditioning for centuries without electricity.
