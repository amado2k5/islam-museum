## Summary
The two equinox points - where night and day are equal - drift slowly through the zodiac because Earth's axis is tilted and moves in a long cycle. Al-Battani, the Abbasid astronomer, measured this motion and found the equinox regressing about one degree per 66 years, from which he derived a tropical year of 365 days, 5 hours, 46 minutes and 24 seconds. This is not the discovery of the phenomenon itself - the advance of the equinoxes was already known - but a more precise measurement of its rate, a number that entered calculations of the year and the seasons. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

The modern rate of regression is about 50 arcseconds per year, roughly one degree every 71 years, so al-Battani's measurement is close to it but not identical. The date is recorded by century because the sources give no single year for his derivation; his observing career spanned decades in the ninth and early tenth centuries. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

## What it is
This is a record of a "measurement method" for the rate of equinox regression. The idea is that the equinoxes are not fixed in the zodiac; they slide slowly because of the precession of Earth's axis. Al-Battani measured this sliding repeatedly and found an approximately constant rate: one degree every 66 years. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

The result is tied to deriving the tropical year: if you know how much the equinox lags behind the previous year, you can compute the year's length more accurately than the simple sidereal year. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

## The world before it
The phenomenon itself - the regression or advance of the equinoxes - was known before al-Battani; ancient astronomers had noticed that the equinox positions change slowly. Hipparchus in the second century BCE measured a similar advance, and the sources read do not give his figure here so we do not quote it. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

Ptolemy transmitted the phenomenon in his Almagest, so equinox regression became part of inherited astronomy. Al-Battani came to measure it, not to invent it. {cite:battani-mactutor} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

To understand the importance of this measurement, we must know what the regression controls. The equinoxes are not merely geometric points; they are the reference from which the Sun is measured in the zodiac, and from which the dates of the seasons and astronomical calendars are computed. If they slide slowly without anyone measuring their rate, error accumulates over centuries until the season dates become inaccurate. Al-Battani's measurement stopped that accumulation with a number. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

## The story
Al-Battani observed at Raqqa and the surrounding region over decades. He measured risings and culminations of the Sun and stars repeatedly, compared the equinox positions across years, and found the regression to be about one degree per 66 years. From the same observations he derived the tropical year of 365 days, 5 hours, 46 minutes and 24 seconds. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

The biography says he "found" the equinox regression at one degree per 66 years, but does not detail his instruments or observation days. We invent no coordinates; we keep to what the sources document. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

The story is therefore not one of "discovery" but of "measurement": a man who gathered repeated observations over years, compared equinox positions across time, and produced a rate. This kind of patient work is what characterises tenth-century Islamic astronomy: not a lone genius but a documented methodology. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

## How it works
The astronomical idea: Earth spins on its axis and orbits the Sun, and its axis is tilted relative to its orbital plane. This tilt makes the equinoxes move slowly through the zodiac over thousands of years. Al-Battani measured star and equinox positions over time and found the rate. {cite:battani-mactutor} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

The figure of 66 years per degree is more accurate than Ptolemy's value, but less than the modern figure of about 50 arcseconds per year. The difference could come from instrument precision or the length of the observation series; the sources do not explain. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

## The spread
Al-Battani's values entered later zij tables, and his book was translated into Latin, carrying his results westward. What travelled was a number and the method that produced it: a table that records the equinoxes as "one degree per 66 years" rather than a fixed date. ## What was built on it
The regression rate entered the calculation of the tropical year, and thence the fixing of equinox and season dates in zijs. Later astronomers used it without re-measuring until someone measured again. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

Every computation that needed to know when the equinoxes cross the equator relied on a number like this. Error in the rate accumulates each year; so correcting it - even by a fraction of a degree - improved all the derived tables. This is the effect of a single number across the whole chain of calculations. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

## Impact on daily life

## How it changed history

## Who invented it?
Equinox regression was not invented in Islam; the documented contribution is measuring its rate at 1° per 66 years and deriving the tropical year. Al-Battani improved the measurement, and earlier observers had noticed the phenomenon. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

This means "who invented the regression" is not a precise question; the more precise question is "who measured its rate with documentation?" and the answer is al-Battani, after those who noticed it before him. This distinction preserves the chain of making. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

## Today
Today the regression is measured at about 50.3 arcseconds per year. Al-Battani's figure of 66 years per degree is more accurate than Ptolemy's but less than the modern value; it remains a witness to his method, not a match to the present. {cite:battani-mactutor} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

Modern measurement reaches far greater precision through instruments and long observation series al-Battani could not have had. His achievement is not that his number survives unchanged but that a ninth-century observer produced a value close to the modern one with the means of his time - a reminder that patient method can approach truth long before instruments make it easy. {cite:battani-mactutor} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}
