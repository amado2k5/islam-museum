## Summary
In Muharram 919 — March or April 1513 — an Ottoman admiral named Piri Reis signed a world map drawn on gazelle skin at the naval base of Gallipoli. The surviving fragment, the western third, shows the Atlantic: the coasts of Europe and Africa and the New World as he could piece it together from up to five sources, including, by his own account, a map made by Columbus that has since been lost. It is the most famous map ever produced in the Islamic world {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it is
A hand-drawn world map on parchment, 90 by 63 centimetres in its surviving piece, in the Topkapı Palace library (Revan 1633 mükerrer). It is anchored in a double tradition, says EI2: the Mediterranean portolan chart and the world map of the Great Discoveries — with something of a third, the lavishly coloured "presentation specimen" meant to impress a patron {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The world before it
Mediterranean seamen navigated by portolan charts — wind-rose diagrams of harbours and headlands, practical and precise for that sea. Meanwhile the voyages of Columbus and the Portuguese were drawing a wider world that no Islamic map yet showed. Ottoman sailors of Piri Reis's generation collected European charts the way they collected prizes: as trophies, tools and intelligence {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The story
Piri Reis learned the sea as a corsair beside his uncle Kemal Reis, then in Ottoman service — and after Kemal Reis's death about 1510-11, he settled at Gallipoli, the fleet's home port, and turned to cartography. In 1513 he composed the map and signed it in the colophon: "the poor Pir son of Hadjji Mehmed, known as paternal nephew of Kemal Reis." He records that Sultan Selim I graciously accepted it in 1517 at Cairo, after the conquest of Egypt. The map was then torn — how, no one knows — and the surviving western part lay forgotten until 1929, when it was found in the Topkapı library and became an international sensation {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The maker(s)
One maker, signed and dated: Piri Reis b. Hadjji Mehmed (d. Cairo, 961/1553-4). EI2's portrait is of a self-made scholar of the sea — an ear trained in the corsair schools of the western Mediterranean, an eye schooled on Italian and Catalan charts, and an original mind that fused them {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it works
As a cartographic object it is compilation, not survey: he fitted together what the sources said — portolan coastlines for the old world, the Columbus-derived and Portuguese material for the new — drawing, colouring and annotating in the Ottoman way, with legends noting wonders and recent discoveries. The scholarship identifies up to five source charts behind the surviving fragment {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The spread
Oddly, almost nowhere in its own day: EI2 notes the contrast between the world's later fascination and the apparent indifference of the sixteenth century — it was Piri Reis's manual, the Kitab-i Bahriye (1521, reworked 1526), that was copied and read for two centuries. The map waited three hundred and sixteen years for its fame. Its 1935 facsimile, published by the Turkish Historical Society on Ataturk's instructions, broadcast it worldwide {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it was built on
The portolan tradition of the medieval Mediterranean; the new cartography of the Discoveries — a Columbus map and between one and four Portuguese charts by the author's own statement; and the Ottoman presentation-piece tradition, a map meant to be admired as well as used {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What was built on it
His own second world map of 1528 (a smaller surviving fragment, the Atlantic's north); generations of Turkish historical scholarship since 1929; and a place in every world history of cartography, where it stands as the earliest known witness to a lost Columbus original {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-piri-reis-map-en}.

## Impact on daily life
Little — a map in a palace drawer changes no one's breakfast. Its meaning was symbolic even in its making: proof that an Ottoman admiral could gather the world's new geography into a single image fit for a sultan {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it changed history
It changed the history of cartography, not of voyages: it is the earliest surviving map to show any part of the Americas drawing on Columbus himself, and the single most famous Ottoman map. Its 1929 discovery gave the young Turkish republic a scientific icon and gave world scholarship a unique document {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Who invented it?
Piri Reis — the question is not who drew it (his colophon says) but what he drew from. The debate is over the lost sources: how much came from Columbus's own chart, what the vanished eastern two-thirds contained, and whether his "Oriental sources" added anything Europe did not have {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Today
The fragment is one of the Topkapı Palace's most visited treasures. Facsimiles, editions and a vast modern literature keep it alive — along with fringe theories EI2 mentions politely and does not endorse {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Surviving examples
Two pieces of Piri Reis's hand survive: the 1513 western fragment (Revan 1633 mükerrer) and a smaller part of the 1528 map. The lost two-thirds of 1513 — the bulk of Europe, Africa and all of Asia — are gone {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-piri-reis-map-en}.

## In culture
In Turkey the map is a national symbol — printed, filmed, celebrated since 1929; in world culture it is the standing riddle of the Columbus connection, endlessly reproduced and debated {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
Five hundred years ago an Ottoman sailor named Piri Reis drew a map of the whole world on soft leather. He copied it from sailors' charts — including a map drawn by Columbus that no longer exists. Only a third of his map survives, in a palace museum in Istanbul, but that piece is so famous that people come from all over the world just to look at it.
