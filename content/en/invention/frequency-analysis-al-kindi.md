## Summary
Letters in a concealed message may change their names while repetition remains a clue. [[person/al-kindi|Al-Kindi]] describes counting letters in a sample of a known language, ranking them by use and comparing that ranking with the message's symbols. Numbers are not his final verdict: a short message may depart from ordinary usage, and a guess needs checking against letter combinations and context {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 216–218}. TDV calls his the earliest surviving treatise on concealing and recovering messages while acknowledging earlier concealment {cite:tdv-muamma pp. 319–320}. This exhibit concerns that documented method, not invention of secrecy or a supposed ability to open every cipher.

## What it is
*Istikhraj al-muamma* means recovering what writing has concealed, as distinct from *ta'miya*, making a message unclear to someone unfamiliar with its method. TDV distinguishes this technical use from a literary riddle hiding a name in verse {cite:tdv-muamma pp. 319–320}. Frequency means how often a letter appears in a body of text, not its numerical value or alphabetical place.

Al-Kindi combines quantitative and qualitative clues: how often does a shape occur, which letters can precede and follow it, and does the proposed reading fit elsewhere? {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 216–218}. The instrument is not a metal machine or merely a memorised codebook but an explainable, repeatable procedure. A reader can see where a guess began, what strengthened it and where it needs changing.

## The world before it
Concealment did not begin with al-Kindi. TDV describes older traditions and reports a *Book of Muamma* attributed to al-Khalil ibn Ahmad before listing later authors {cite:tdv-muamma pp. 319–320}. That earlier book's text was not read here; an ordered list of names is not evidence that a specific procedure passed between them.

Al-Kindi's own introduction says earlier philosophers and thinkers used unfamiliar signs to restrict access to their books, and that recovery can help readers understand them {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 213–214}. The context is wider than a battlefield: concealed writing might carry knowledge or correspondence. The opening read names no intercepted enemy message and supplies no intelligence victory to invent for a more dramatic story.

## The story
The treatise belongs to al-Kindi's Abbasid world, whose biography describes court connections and scholarly work in [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] {cite:tdv-kindi-kaya biography}. It addresses Abu al-Abbas and says it answers a request for an accessible explanation of recovering what concealed books record {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 213–214}. The museum preserves that address; a modern footnote identifying the addressee is not the author's dated signature.

The record therefore uses the ninth century rather than manufacturing an exact invention year from a modern edition or a possible caliph's reign. The text read appears in volume one of *Ilm al-ta'miya wa-istikhraj al-muamma ind al-Arab*, the Damascus 1987 edition identified by TDV {cite:tdv-muamma bibliography}. Composition, manuscript copying and modern editing are different dates and different historical questions.

## The maker(s)
Al-Kindi is the author named in the text, and TDV confirms the attribution and its status as the earliest surviving treatise of this kind {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 213} {cite:tdv-muamma pp. 319–320}. His record role is “describer”. That does not turn a manuscript copyist or modern editor into the method's inventor, or prove that no earlier knowledge existed and was lost.

Affiliation is displayed on his person page. Mahmut Kaya describes al-Kindi's defence of Islam and rejects shared questions as sufficient proof of Mu'tazili membership, while reporting and challenging Walzer's and de Boer's contrary classifications {cite:tdv-kindi-kaya Mu'tezile section}. The museum uses its Sunni frame with the disagreement marked, not as a documented personal declaration of a particular school. Those contrary positions are reported through Kaya, not from reading the scholars' original books.

## How it works
First, identify the message's language. The text calls for a sample in that language, approximately a skin's worth or similar, counting each letter type and ranking the types from most frequent downward {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 216}. It does not compare every language in the world with one universal Arabic table, but known usage with a message in the same language.

Next, classify the concealed message's shapes and count them. Match the most frequent shape to the highest-ranked sample letter, the next to the second, and continue {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 216}. Changing a shape need not remove the linguistic regularity carried by the text. Numbers become evidence where the strange appearance alone supplies little.

The author immediately qualifies this for short messages: they may not contain every letter, and a letter unusually common in one passage may be balanced by another only in a longer text {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 216}. This is not a guaranteed minimum word count. The approximate reference sample must not become a fixed success threshold for every message.

Qualitative testing follows. If two letters are guessed, ask whether they can combine in that language; examine their neighbours and test them elsewhere. Contradictions prevent establishing a letter's identity merely because its frequency rank seemed suitable {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 217}. Short Arabic combinations such as *min*, *an* and *lam* supply further clues {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 217–218}.

Conventional message openings can help, but should not override stronger evidence: discard an opening-based guess if it conflicts; agreement strengthens it {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 218}. Verse has another route, using rhyme, letter counts and metre, testing possible letters against metrical feet until sense fits {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 219}. The method is a sequence of checks and revisions, not a magical button producing correct plaintext every time.

## The spread
TDV lists works by Ibn Adlan, Ibn al-Durayhim, Ibn Dunaynir and others, relating official correspondence and expanding administrative departments to the need for concealment and recovery {cite:tdv-muamma pp. 319–320}. That documents a field of writing and instruction, not a census of officials applying every step in al-Kindi's text.

The Damascus edition compares later treatises when correcting letter counts, allowing study of textual traditions and their interpretation {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 236}. No arrow is drawn from Baghdad to every European capital: the references read identify no translator, copy or chain of quotation establishing such a route here.

## What it was built on
The procedure uses knowledge of language: letter repetition, possible combinations and distinctions between prose, verse, speech and writing {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 215–219, 237–241}. Explaining Arabic frequency, the text distinguishes pronounced sounds from short vowels not independently represented in ordinary writing {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 236–237}. What is counted is a written trace within a writing system, not every sound a person makes.

The treatise also divides concealment into simple and compound forms, discussing substituted shapes, changed positions, added null signs and combinations of operations {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 220–226}. These families cannot all be reduced to single substitution. Their variety helps explain why the analyst must diagnose the kind of concealment before extending one procedure to it.

## What was built on it
The treatise became part of the corpus studied by the editors of *Ilm al-ta'miya*, alongside Ibn Adlan and Ibn al-Durayhim, as the volume description and TDV bibliography show {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 title page} {cite:tdv-muamma bibliography}. This is a documented link in editing and historical scholarship, not proof that every later writer borrowed every statement from al-Kindi.

The text leaves room for development: it abbreviates compound ciphers after discussing simple forms; an editorial note reports Ibn Dunaynir's criticism of that limited treatment {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 224}. Reading an innovation alongside what successors found incomplete is more accurate than turning early survival into perfection in every branch.

## Impact on daily life
TDV connects the art with administrative correspondence and discusses modern expansion of concealment and recovery into diplomacy, trade and computing {cite:tdv-muamma pp. 319–320}. The distance between these settings cannot be collapsed into crediting every modern procedure to one book. The immediate lesson is that the written language of work can be examined systematically.

A reader sees that renaming signs need not erase every trace: relationships may still reveal part of the message {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 216–218}. This page is neither contemporary security advice nor a promise to compromise data. It gives no number of solved messages, financial saving or battle outcome, because the references used establish none.

## How it changed history
The treatise combines counting with linguistic knowledge, checking several places and revising a guess when evidence conflicts {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 216–219}. Its value as a surviving witness is that the reasoning can be followed, rather than accepting a story that someone solved a puzzle. TDV places its author at the beginning of surviving writing in this field {cite:tdv-muamma pp. 319–320}.

Survival is not absolute priority: an earlier text may be lost, and TDV does report a book attributed to al-Khalil {cite:tdv-muamma pp. 319–320}. The record preserves that gap instead of filling it with a civilisation's victory over another. We can describe the evidence before us; the fate of all lost knowledge does not become known because this text survives.

## Who invented it?
**The Islamic-history reference:** Ismail Durmus in TDV credits al-Kindi with clearly distinguishing substitution from changed positions and setting out frequency, adjacency and conventional openings, calling his the earliest surviving work {cite:tdv-muamma pp. 319–320}. That is a specific attribution to compare with the text, not a claim that he first concealed information.

**The primary text and its limits:** Counting, comparison and the short-message warning are present, but no exact invention day or proof that all earlier centuries lacked the idea {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 213–219}. The maker's disputed affiliation is separately displayed through labelled readings; it does not prove or disprove a mathematical procedure.

## Today
The critical edition makes the Arabic procedure readable with notes marking additions and corrections. Its editors explicitly identify bracketed headings as their own {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 213 note}. Readers can distinguish the author's language from twentieth-century editorial aids.

The method is historical knowledge suitable for teaching, not a universal recipe for every encryption system. Its textual conditions include language, cipher type, enough material and cross-checks {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 216–219, 225–226}. The accompanying diagram is the site's relationship chain, not a reproduction of an executable plan written by al-Kindi.

## In culture
*Muamma* covers two fields that should not be confused: secret correspondence and hiding a name in poetry. TDV distinguishes them and describes the latter's literary clues and methods {cite:tdv-muamma pp. 319–320}. Both may offer the pleasure of uncovering meaning, but finding a name in a couplet is not automatically statistical analysis of a message.

Al-Kindi's introduction places usefulness in recovering meanings hidden by unfamiliar signs, not in an illustrated individual hero story {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 213–214}. The museum shows writing itself. Its author remains a person within a scholarly setting, with disputed classifications and a limited surviving text, rather than an imagined face or a legend replacing evidence.

## For young readers
Imagine a game replacing every letter with a sign while keeping the same sign whenever that letter returns. First ask which sign appeared most. This resembles al-Kindi's counting; it is an invented teaching game, not a historical example from his table. Remember that a short message may repeat one unusual word and mislead you.

Al-Kindi warns about short texts and asks readers to test combinations and neighbouring letters {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 216–218}. A clue is not a final answer: collect more evidence and reconsider what does not fit. This is a language-and-counting game, not encouragement to read someone else's private messages.
