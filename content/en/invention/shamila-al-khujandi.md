## Summary

In the tenth century CE, [[person/al-khujandi-abu-mahmud-hamid-ibn-al-khidr|Abu Mahmud Hamid ibn al-Khidr al-Khujandi]] described in a treatise titled "On the Construction of the Comprehensive Instrument" an observing instrument he called the "shamila", said to have been designed to replace the astrolabe or the quadrant within a limited range of latitudes {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea comprehensive-instrument} {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works treatise}. His design confined it to a single terrestrial latitude; its later commentator, [[person/al-badi-al-asturlabi|Hibatallah ibn al-Husayn al-Asturlabi]], then found a way to make it serve all latitudes {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works commentary}.

This is a record of a documented design, not of a surviving tool: the treatise has never been printed or translated, and no physical example of the shamila itself has been found. Knowledge of it persists through five scattered manuscripts in Oxford, Birmingham, Bursa, Cairo and Tehran, and through an abridgment inserted by [[person/abu-ali-al-hasan-al-marrakushi|Abu Ali al-Marrakushi]] in his compendium on timekeeping {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works manuscripts} {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works transmission} {cite:ismi-khujandi-ala-shamila witnesses}. The accompanying image is not the shamila but another instrument made by al-Khujandi that survives bearing his signature {cite:commons-khujandi-astrolabe-bini metadata}.

## What it is

The shamila — also called the "general instrument" in some catalogues — is an astronomical observing instrument, not a calculating device or a nautical tool. Glen Van Brummelen describes it as an "invention" recorded in the treatise "The Comprehensive Instrument", an instrument "designed to replace the astrolabe or a quadrant", though it was not universal in the absolute sense because it was confined to a particular range of terrestrial latitudes {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea comprehensive-instrument}. The bibliographer Katib Celebi — known in translation as Hajji Khalifa — calls it "the horizontal plate named the comprehensive among the astrolabes", in one version of sixty chapters and in another a preliminary and fifteen chapters {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works hajji-khalifa}.

The names "comprehensive" and "universal" promise a gathering of functions; they do not prove that the gathering succeeded in a single design. The Arabic record says al-Khujandi's design served only one latitude, and that his Asturlabi commentator was the one who made it serve many {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works commentary}. Nor can we describe its working mechanism in detail today — how many disks, how many rings — because its text has never been printed or translated; all we hold are catalogues, manuscripts and later summaries {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works treatise}.

## The world before it

The tenth-century astronomer had a fleet of instruments at hand: the planispheric astrolabe with its swappable plates for each latitude, the quadrant for measuring angles, and the armillary rings of the great observatory. The shamila is not a claim to have invented this whole tradition, but an offer of one design said to replace two instruments within a defined circumstance {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea comprehensive-instrument}.

In the same milieu al-Khujandi was pursuing a parallel project: the Fakhri sextant, the monumental meridian arc near Rayy on which he recorded observations in 994, a separate record in [[invention/fakhri-sextant-rayy|the Fakhri sextant entry]]. The shamila is a portable instrument described in a treatise; the sextant is a fixed building on a hill. Sharing a maker does not make the two instruments one thing {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea sextant} {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works treatise}.

## The story

We do not know al-Khujandi's full biography. MacTutor says he was born about 940 at Khujand on the Jaxartes and died in 1000, that he was probably one of the city's notables, and that the Buyid dynasty — especially Fakhr al-Dawla, who ruled from 976 to 997 — supported him. The biography describes him as an Islamic astronomer and mathematician, the classification on which this record relies for counting him Muslim, while his being Sunni rests on the museum's declared frame rather than on evidence of a personal school {cite:khujandi-mactutor biography} {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea biography}.

In that setting he wrote his treatise on constructing the comprehensive instrument. The text has never reached print or translation; Hogendijk notes that Richard Lorch worked on editing and translating it and did not finish before his death. The treatise survives in manuscripts: Oxford Huntington 566; Birmingham 560, which also contains Hibatallah's commentary; Bursa Haraççıoğlu 1217, from which Sezgin published leaves; Cairo Dār al-Kutub miqat 970, described in David King's catalogue; and Tehran's Nasiri private library, a copy in Hibatallah's own hand {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works treatise} {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works manuscripts}.

## The maker(s)

The recorded maker is [[person/al-khujandi-abu-mahmud-hamid-ibn-al-khidr|Abu Mahmud al-Khujandi]] in the role of inventor. His Muslim identity is fixed on the person record on the basis of MacTutor's explicit description of him as an "Islamic astronomer", and the Sunni label is preserved from the museum's declared frame, not inferred from a school {cite:khujandi-mactutor biography}. Neither Buyid patronage nor noble descent establishes the maker's school; this separation is deliberate in the Fakhri sextant record itself {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea biography}.

No rival claimant to the shamila appears in the sources read. The attribution is stable in Van Brummelen's biography and in the bibliographic tradition of the catalogues. [[person/al-badi-al-asturlabi|Hibatallah ibn al-Husayn al-Asturlabi]] — a Baghdad instrument maker of the early twelfth century — is not the origin's inventor but its developer: Hogendijk describes his commentary as having "modified the instrument and made it suitable for all latitudes, whereas al-Khujandi had designed the instrument for an individual latitude" {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works commentary}. His role in this invention's record is therefore commentator and extender, not a second maker.

## How it works

We cannot explain the shamila's mechanism in detail, because its text has never been printed or translated and its manuscript has not been read here {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works treatise}. What the sources establish is three points: that it is an astronomical observing instrument placed among the astrolabes in Katib Celebi's description; that it was designed to replace the astrolabe or the quadrant within a limited latitude; and that the original version serves only one terrestrial latitude {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea comprehensive-instrument} {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works commentary}.

The description "horizontal plate" in Katib Celebi suggests it was probably a horizontal plate or mechanism within the astrolabe family rather than a full planispheric astrolabe by definition. But the name "comprehensive" and the description "universal" carry the ambition of gathering several functions into one instrument; the sources read do not tell us which functions were actually combined or how they were regulated. Sezgin reconstructed the original version in his instrument catalogue, but we have found no detailed structure for it in this research, so we do not describe one {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works hajji-khalifa} {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works sezgin-reconstruction}.

## The spread

The shamila's spread was not the spread of an instrument but of a text. The five manuscripts are distributed across Oxford, Birmingham, Bursa, Cairo and Tehran, and the Tehran manuscript carries Hibatallah's commentary in his own hand alongside the original in its mixed copy. [[person/abu-ali-al-hasan-al-marrakushi|Abu Ali al-Marrakushi]] inserted the instrument into a section of his "Jami' al-mabadi' wa-l-ghayat" — his compendium on timekeeping and the construction of observational instruments — apparently in Hibatallah's version, without naming either craftsman {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works transmission} {cite:ismi-khujandi-ala-shamila witnesses}.

Al-Marrakushi's summary carried the instrument from a personal device into a chapter of a general encyclopedia. An anonymous Berlin treatise, "A Summary on the Construction of Some Observational Instruments and Working with Them", was derived from al-Marrakushi; the German scholar Oskar Schirmer was able to attribute the instrument to al-Khujandi through a note in [[person/al-qifti|Ibn al-Qifti]]'s "Ta'rikh al-Hukama" about Hibatallah {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works transmission}. The sources read contain no evidence that the shamila itself travelled between cities or was copied by craftsmen; what travelled was its description {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works manuscripts}.

## What it was built on

The shamila was built on the astrolabe tradition and the instruments of angular measurement. The planispheric astrolabe was the astronomer's daily standard: plates swapped for each latitude, celestial grids, and rings for time and direction. The quadrant was the direct alternative for large angular measurement. The idea of the "comprehensive instrument" is not the invention of astronomy itself but an attempt to compress the functions of several tools into one design {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea comprehensive-instrument}.

In the same milieu, al-Khujandi was building the Fakhri sextant: a sixty-degree arc about 43 metres in diameter inside a building near Rayy, designed for readings finer than minutes. The same ambition — making the instrument larger and more precise — joins the two projects, but the means differ: one is a fixed stone building with an observatory, the other a portable instrument described in a treatise {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea sextant} {cite:khujandi-mactutor sextant}.

## What was built on it

The first to "build on" the shamila was its commentator. Hibatallah ibn al-Husayn al-Asturlabi found a way to make it serve all latitudes, whereas al-Khujandi had shown — as the catalogues transmit — that it could serve no more than one. This commentary survives with the original in Birmingham manuscript 560 and in the Tehran autograph in Hibatallah's hand, and a similar text may stand in Oxford Marsh 663 {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works commentary} {cite:ismi-khujandi-ala-shamila complement}.

Then came al-Marrakushi's abridgment, which reduced the instrument to a section of an encyclopedia. After it came the anonymous Berlin summary, which rested on al-Marrakushi rather than on the original. Thus knowledge of the shamila passed through three channels: manuscripts of the original, Hibatallah's commentary, and later summaries. The sources read establish no direct descent to later astrolabes or to [[invention/universal-astrolabe-al-zarqali|al-Zarqali's universal plate]]; the name "comprehensive" remained a name in the catalogues, not a documented transmission tree {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works hajji-khalifa} {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea comprehensive-instrument}.

## Impact on daily life

No documented impact of the shamila on everyday life exists. It is a specialist astronomical observing instrument, not a household tool or a new profession. The records read give no numbers of its users, no places where it was employed, no count of uses. Even the commentary that widened it did not leave the circle of the craft; it remained in manuscripts and scholars {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works transmission}.

The firmer impact is scientific-bibliographic: a single instrument design stayed alive through a chain of manuscripts, summaries and commentaries. That is an impact on the history of knowledge, not on the history of daily life {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works transmission}.

## How it changed history

The shamila did not change the history of astronomy in any directly provable way, because we hold neither a model nor a read text. But its history illustrates an important point: how the design of an instrument lives in the collective memory of scholars even after its material trace is lost. The instrument itself vanished, but its name survived in Hajji Khalifa's catalogues, its function in Hibatallah's commentaries, and its description in al-Marrakushi's encyclopedia {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works transmission} {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works hajji-khalifa}.

And the story of its single-latitude restriction and its commentator's later widening is an example that "universal" in a name does not mean a complete first design; the instrument itself proved its limits at one latitude and needed correction into a comprehensive version. This is a lesson in instrument design: breadth is not a magic key but an engineering decision with a price {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works commentary}.

## Who invented it?

The museum records [[person/al-khujandi-abu-mahmud-hamid-ibn-al-khidr|al-Khujandi]] as the inventor of the comprehensive instrument. The astronomical biography agrees, calling it an "invention" in his treatise, and the catalogue traditions attribute the treatise and its title to him. No rival claimant appears in the sources read {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea comprehensive-instrument} {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works treatise}.

But the attribution rests on secondary literature and bibliography, not on a reading of the original text. The treatise has never been printed or translated, and its manuscripts have not been examined here. If we want to verify the details of the design, its functions or its precision, we need a manuscript edition — work Lorch did not finish before his death and no one else has completed {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works treatise}. So we say: the attribution is documented; the details are unread.

## Today

Today no known physical example of the shamila exists. There is no critical edition and no translation. But the work is not forgotten: Sezgin included a reconstruction of the original version in his "Science and Technology in Islam" catalogue (vol. 2, pp. 151-153), and republished leaves from the Bursa manuscript {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works sezgin-reconstruction}. The name stays alive in the history of instruments as an example of the "comprehensive instrument" ambition that many later astronomers pursued {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works treatise}.

The image accompanying this record is not the shamila but an astrolabe signed by Hamid ibn al-Khidr al-Khujandi and dated 374 AH (984-985), held by the Museum of Islamic Art in Doha. It is another instrument by the same maker, from the same century, but it is not the shamila, of which no example survives {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works astrolabe} {cite:commons-khujandi-astrolabe-bini metadata}.

## Surviving examples

No material witness of the shamila survives. The textual witnesses are: five manuscripts — Oxford Huntington 566, Birmingham 560, Bursa Haraççıoğlu 1217, Cairo Dār al-Kutub miqat 970, and Tehran's Nasiri library. The ISMI database records four witnesses; Hogendijk adds the fifth {cite:ismi-khujandi-ala-shamila witnesses} {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works manuscripts}.

The commentary that widened it to all latitudes survives in Birmingham manuscript 560 and in the Tehran autograph in Hibatallah's own hand. Al-Marrakushi's summary survives in his book. These are all the remains: texts, not instruments {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works commentary} {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works transmission}.

## In culture

The shamila did not enter popular culture; there are no tales, proverbs or mentions in non-scholarly books read here. It entered only the history of instruments: Hajji Khalifa's catalogues mention it, Hibatallah's commentaries widen it, and al-Marrakushi's encyclopedia summarizes it. Its name "the comprehensive" carries a clear ambition: one instrument that does everything {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works hajji-khalifa}.

The signed astrolabe that survives from al-Khujandi — the accompanying image — testifies that he was not merely a designer on paper; he made actual instruments bearing dates and names. But the shamila itself remained within the bounds of the treatise and the manuscripts {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works astrolabe}.

## For young readers

A thousand years ago, a scientist named al-Khujandi imagined an instrument that would say: "Instead of carrying an astrolabe and a quadrant, take this comprehensive tool." He wrote a treatise explaining how to make it. But it worked only at one latitude on Earth — for example, in one particular city. Hundreds of years later, another scientist named Hibatallah found a way to make it work everywhere. Today no example of it exists, but we know the story from old manuscripts {cite:van-brummelen-khujandi-bea comprehensive-instrument} {cite:jphogendijk-khujandi-works commentary}.
