## Summary

Maqqari attributes an additional oud string to [[person/ziryab|Ziryab]] in al-Andalus, following an inherited four-string arrangement: a refinement, not invention of the oud itself {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 p.126}. This is a qualified historical attribution because Dwight Reynolds shows how Maqqari assembled older voices into an idealized portrait of one artist {cite:reynolds-ziryab-2008 pp.158–166}. The photograph shows a much later Cleveland instrument, not an oud surviving from his court {cite:cleveland-oud-1918-350 catalogue}.

## What it is

The subject is an extra string within the oud's arrangement, not a separate new instrument. The account calls it red, places it below the mathlath and above the mathna, and associates it with the soul in an instrument-as-body analogy {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 p.126}. This specifies the attributed contribution: changing the equipment of a familiar instrument, expressed through an old combination of music and humoral ideas, rather than a surviving product with complete dimensions and tuning specifications {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 p.126}.

## The world before it

Maqqari begins with a four-string oud corresponding to four bodily temperaments; the addition therefore presupposes an existing instrument and performing craft {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 p.126}. His Baghdad training story places Ishaq al-Mawsili's oud beside Ziryab's, with competition over singing and instrument refinement, not discovery of the plucked string from nothing {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 pp.122–123}. Naming that inheritance preserves other people's work without inventing an unknown first maker to complete a diagram.

## The story

The TDV biography connects Ziryab with Baghdad, Kairouan and [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]], dating his arrival at Abd al-Rahman II's court to822. It does not establish that as the year the string was added {cite:tdv-ziryab-arslan-erkocoglu pp.464–465}. Maqqari recounts a meeting with Harun al-Rashid, refusal to use the teacher's oud, preference for an instrument made by his own hands, westward departure and generous Andalusi patronage {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 pp.122–125}.

The attractive narrative needs a distinction between storyteller and witness. Maqqari wrote much later; Reynolds's comparison reveals multiple, sometimes contradictory sources behind the Ibn Hayyan material he used {cite:reynolds-ziryab-2008 pp.155–158}. The record consequently gives a broad ninth-century setting, not an invented year, day or court session for the modification {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 p.126}.

## The maker(s)

The attributed improver is Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Nafi, Ziryab. TDV describes training under Ibrahim al-Mawsili and his son Ishaq, placing him within an educational tradition {cite:tdv-ziryab-arslan-erkocoglu pp.464–465}. The Muslim identity basis here is Al Jazeera's explicit presentation of his biography in its Muslim Scholars series: a named biographical classification, not a personal profession of belief. Sunni is separately the museum's declared frame, not a personal school proved by his name or patron {cite:aljazeera-ziryab-2022 introductory paragraphs}. Reynolds's suggestion of a convert father is not substituted for that basis {cite:reynolds-ziryab-2008 p.166}.

## How it works

The account relates string order, colour and thickness: a yellow zir, red mathna, white mathlath and black bamm, with the additional red string between the middle ones {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 p.126}. The colours are not a modern key from which frequency can be calculated. They participate in an analogy of humours and soul, not medical evidence or an acoustic law {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 p.126}.

A separate improvement replaces a wooden plectrum with an eagle quill, praised for lightness and gentleness on the strings. Changing the plucking tool is not the same act as adding a string {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 pp.126–127}. The passage does not supply a fully specified fifth-string pitch or measurements from which to reconstruct the instrument faithfully {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 p.126}.

## The spread

The biography attributes organized singing instruction in Cordoba and schools on its model in other Andalusi cities. It describes stages moving from rhythm and words to melody and then performance subtleties {cite:tdv-ziryab-arslan-erkocoglu pp.464–465}. These are accounts of teaching, not an inventory of five-string ouds or a map recording each instrument's arrival {cite:tdv-ziryab-arslan-erkocoglu pp.464–465}. The spread of a reputation and teaching model cannot automatically become a documented material route for every string.

## What it was built on

The clear inheritance is the account's four-string oud and the singing craft learned from earlier teachers, not an empty field created by one person {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 pp.122–123,126}. The instrument/body comparison is itself part of the historical explanation: useful for understanding that thinking, not for describing human physiology today {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 p.126}. The teaching diagram shows this limited relationship rather than attaching it to an undocumented invention.

## What was built on it

Cleveland discusses the oud's lute-family connections and wider influence, but a family description does not establish the route from Ziryab's particular string to a particular guitar {cite:cleveland-oud-1918-350 catalogue}. Reynolds observes modern performers claiming descent from Ziryab's music; that is meaningful cultural memory, not a handover record for every melody or wooden component across the centuries {cite:reynolds-ziryab-2008 p.155}. Similar names therefore do not create a confirmed technical dependency.

## Impact on daily life

Maqqari locates singing and the oud in court gatherings and instruction, and names sons and daughters who practiced the craft: a social and professional setting rather than simply a slogan about solitary invention {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 pp.125,128–129}. Reynolds restores family music-making and differing children's abilities to a scene obscured by the singular-genius portrait {cite:reynolds-ziryab-2008 p.161}. These accounts cannot quantify use of the additional string beyond the circles described.

## How it changed history

Ziryab became central to the story of Andalusi music. Reynolds describes a remembered figure combining musical genius, elegance and courtly accomplishment, enlarged by texts separated in time {cite:reynolds-ziryab-2008 pp.155–156,166}. This matters historically even where priority cannot be proved: it shows how a culture chooses one personality to embody artistic transmission and changing taste {cite:reynolds-ziryab-2008 p.166}. It does not mean every detail is fabricated; it means the portrait needs its sources and voices kept visible.

## Who invented it?

:::perspectives
**Sunni frame — attributed account:** Maqqari's added string, position and symbolism are presented as his report, not a ruling on music or established universal priority {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 p.126}.

**Academic reading — biography criticism:** Reynolds compares Ibn Hayyan and Maqqari, finding omissions of rivals, children's achievements and multiple voices. That qualifies confidence in the composite portrait; it is not an experiment proving the string itself false {cite:reynolds-ziryab-2008 pp.158–166}.

TDV phrases priority more categorically. Its authors' attribution remains visible without treating a later encyclopedia as an independent ancient artifact confirming the claim {cite:tdv-ziryab-arslan-erkocoglu pp.464–465}.

## Today

Cleveland describes the oud as still prominent, recognizable by its pear-shaped body, fretless fingerboard and angled pegbox {cite:cleveland-oud-1918-350 catalogue}. Continuity of the name does not make every present-day oud preserve Ziryab's reported arrangement. Nor can the visible string count in a modern photograph settle a ninth-century question {cite:cleveland-oud-1918-350 catalogue} {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 p.126}.

## Surviving examples

The photographed [[object/ud-shami|Cleveland oud1918.350]] is West Asian, late1800s–early1900s, made of wood, gut and metal. Its image is CC0 {cite:cleveland-oud-1918-350 catalogue and API}. Its wooden body, decorated soundhole, strings and pegs are clearly visible: a much later comparison, not Ziryab's instrument or a documented reconstruction {cite:cleveland-oud-1918-350 inspected photograph}. The evidence for the attribution here is textual, not ownership of a surviving original.

## In culture

TDV places stories about dress, hair, food and hygiene beside music. This records the breadth of Ziryab's cultural image, not patents granted for every custom mentioned {cite:tdv-ziryab-arslan-erkocoglu pp.464–465}. Reynolds reads that breadth against nostalgia for a lost Andalusi golden age and the making of memory: studying a cultural symbol differs from proving the first maker of every detail {cite:reynolds-ziryab-2008 pp.165–166}.

## For young readers

Imagine someone telling you that a small part was added to an old instrument. Ask what existed before it, who tells the story and whether the same object survives. Ziryab's account has earlier strings and an extra one attributed to him, while the photographed oud is much later {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 p.126} {cite:cleveland-oud-1918-350 catalogue}. Compare description and photograph without treating them as one witness: a museum visit becomes practice in asking questions, not a competition to memorize inventors.
