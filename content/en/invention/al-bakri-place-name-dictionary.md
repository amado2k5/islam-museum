## Summary

Mu'jam ma Ista'jam is Abu Ubayd al-Bakri of al-Andalus's dictionary of geographical names: in it he ordered settlements, abodes, villages, cities, mountains, waters and wells on the mu'jam letters in the Maghribi manner, gathered the names from hadith, reports, histories and poetry, and resolved variant readings of places {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18} {cite:tdv-ebu-ubeyd-el-bekri} {cite:tdv-cografya}. The sources read classify this work as the first geographical dictionary ordered alphabetically as far as is known, and Europe's first alphabetical geographical encyclopaedia did not appear until the sixteenth century {cite:tdv-cografya}. The composition date is unsettled; the known bounds are al-Bakri's death in Shawwal 487 AH ⁦(October 1094 CE⁩ {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan p. 457} above and the earliest surviving copy, manuscript L, completed in 709 AH ⁦(1309 CE⁩ {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 9–10} below.

## What it is

The dictionary is a comprehensive geographical book in dictionary form: chapters ordered on the language's mu'jam letters, and under each chapter place-names from the lands of Islam and their surroundings, with al-Bakri recording each name's location and its variant readings together with the hadith, reports and poetry in which it occurs {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}. Its very title announces its programme: 'Mu'jam ma Ista'jam' — a book gathering what had grown obscure to people among the names, because al-Bakri saw these names had become unclear in everyday use {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–16}. No other work before this date in the sources read gathers place-names in this alphabetically ordered form {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 7–12}.

## The world before it

Before al-Bakri, geographical names were dispersed: they occur in books of hadith, reports, histories, poetry, geography and genealogy, each name in its own context with no single reference gathering them {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}. The Arabic alphabetical dictionary was already old — ordering on the mu'jam letters was established in works of language — but that system had not been transferred to the field of place-names {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–16}. The Arabic dictionary was not new; the novelty was applying it to geography {cite:tdv-cografya}.

## The story

Al-Bakri composed his dictionary in Córdoba, where he gathered fiqh, hadith and letters and collected his licences; the edition's title page styles him the jurist, hafiz, man of letters and vizier, and encyclopaedias record his Cordoban studies and ijazas {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 13–16} {cite:tdv-ebu-ubeyd-el-bekri}. In his introduction he states that he gathered the names from hadith, reports, histories and poetry because he saw them grow obscure to people, and ordered the book's chapters on the mu'jam letters {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}. The introduction gives no precise completion date; the earliest surviving copy is dated 709 AH and the death is 487 AH, so composition is bounded between the two limits {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 9–10} {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan p. 457}.

## The maker(s)

**The declared Sunni frame:** the person record presents al-Bakri as a jurist, hafiz, man of letters and vizier in al-Andalus, resting on the scholarly biography and ijaza records {cite:tdv-ebu-ubeyd-el-bekri} {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 13–16}. **The historical reading:** the TDV geography article records that al-Bakri applied the Arabic alphabetical system to a geographical book for the first time as far as is known, and that Europe's first alphabetical geographical encyclopaedia did not appear until the sixteenth century {cite:tdv-cografya}. The sources read show no doctrinal dispute or contested identity; al-Bakri's person is documented through ijaza chains and a fiqh heritage {cite:tdv-ebu-ubeyd-el-bekri}.

Attribution here is to al-Bakri as the compiler of the dictionary in Córdoba in his later years; the work is not credited to a translator or commentator {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 13–16}.

## How it works

**First: gathering the names.** Al-Bakri collects place-names from two main sources: the heritage corpus — hadith, reports, histories and poetry — and the works of geography and genealogy {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}. He states that this gathering was driven by his sight that the names had grown obscure to people {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–16}.

**Second: alphabetical ordering.** He orders the book's chapters on the mu'jam letters in the Maghribi manner — the Andalusian way of ordering dictionaries — so the reader finds every name under its first letter {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18} {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–16}. The letter-order table survives at the front of the edition {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–16}.

**Third: resolving the readings.** In each entry al-Bakri records the name's location and its variant readings and resolves differences of transmission about places, so the entries become a unified reference rather than a mere list of names {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}. This is the editorial work: not mechanical gathering but criticism of the readings {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}.

**Fourth: geographical explanation.** Al-Bakri explains each name — where it lies, its distance from known cities, and what distinguishes its terrain — so the dictionary becomes a geographical guide, not only a linguistic one {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}.

## The spread

The dictionary spread through a wide manuscript tradition: the 709 AH copy is the earliest surviving {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 9–10}, and it kept being read in the 1876 Wüstenfeld and 1945–51 Cairo editions {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 7–12}. Wüstenfeld notes in his preface that Yaqut al-Hamawi — author of the larger Mu'jam al-Buldan — searched for al-Bakri's book and could not find it, showing the work stayed rare even among the greats {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 7–12}. Its actual spread across the centuries needs wider citation studies than the sources read provide {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 7–12}.

## What it was built on

The dictionary builds on two endowments: the linguistic system — the ordering of dictionaries on the mu'jam letters — and the dispersed geographical corpus in the tradition {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}. Every name in the book has an origin in a hadith, report, history, poem or earlier geographical work; the dictionary is the point of gathering, not the origin of the names {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}. Without these inherited endowments there is no dictionary {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}.

## What was built on it

Al-Bakri's dictionary is followed in the history of Arabic geographical dictionaries by Yaqut al-Hamawi's Mu'jam al-Buldan — the largest work of this kind in the seventh century AH — and the later dictionaries {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 7–12} {cite:tdv-cografya}. In Europe, the TDV geography article says the first alphabetical geographical encyclopaedia did not come until the sixteenth century, more than two centuries after al-Bakri {cite:tdv-cografya}. This does not mean al-Bakri 'preceded' Europeans in geography as a whole — it means alphabetical ordering in Arabic geography preceded its European counterpart {cite:tdv-cografya}.

## Impact on daily life

The impact described in the sources is organisational: it turned geographical names scattered through the tradition into one ordered reference, so the traveller, hadith scholar and genealogist could find a place and its variant readings under a single alphabetical entry {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}. This means that whoever asked about a place-name no longer had to trace the dispersed heritage books {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}. The sources read provide no statistics on users or documented practical results of applying the dictionary {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 7–12}.

## How it changed history

The dictionary proved that dictionary ordering suited the science of places, not only language {cite:tdv-cografya} {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}. This opening is what makes the dictionary an epistemic event: it transferred a known tool to a new field, so Arabic geography gained a search system of its own {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–16}. The book kept being read in the Wüstenfeld and Cairo editions {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 7–12}, and represented the way later generations organised their knowledge of places {cite:tdv-cografya}.

## Who invented it?

What is proven is that al-Bakri gathered the dictionary and ordered it on letters in Córdoba before his death in 487 AH, and that the sources read describe him as the first to apply this system to a comprehensive geography as far as is known {cite:tdv-cografya} {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 13–18}. The invention is not the linguistic dictionary itself — that craft is older — but its transfer to the field of place-names {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–16}. We do not claim al-Bakri gathered every geographical name existing in his age; the sources provide no complete census of the dictionary {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 7–12}.

## Today

The dictionary is not used for everyday travel today, but it remains a reference for scholars of Arabic place-names; it survives in the 1876 Wüstenfeld edition ⁦(two volumes⁩ and the 1945–51 al-Saqqa Cairo edition ⁦(four volumes⁩ {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 7–12}. The modern reader finds in it a picture of how the Arabic heritage preserved ancient place-names — their linguistic course and geographical history — in one referable system {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}.

## In culture

The dictionary is an example that 'geography' in the Arabic heritage was not only a map but knowledge of place-names and their readings {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}. Its idea — that names need an ordering that makes them referable — is a thoroughly modern idea in the world of databases and search {cite:tdv-cografya}. The reader can appreciate this similarity without claiming al-Bakri invented computer search; the essential difference is that the dictionary is a manual system on paper, not a machine {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}.

## For young readers

**A modern museum example, not from the dictionary itself:** imagine you collected the names of every street in your old town from old stories, songs and books, then ordered them alphabetically like a dictionary, and wrote under each name where it is and what it means. That is how al-Bakri made his dictionary a thousand years ago: he gathered place-names from hadith, reports and poetry and ordered them on letters. He called it 'Mu'jam ma Ista'jam' because the names had become obscure to people, so he explained them for them {cite:mujam-ma-istaajam-bakri scan pp. 14–18}.
