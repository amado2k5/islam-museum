## Summary
In the reign of the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun a measurement project without precedent in Islamic science was carried out: he sent two parties of scholars to the plain of Sinjar in the Jazira, some seventy miles west of Mosul; one marched north and the other south from a single point, each watching the sun's altitude above the meridian until it changed by one degree after correction for the sun's declination, measuring the ground covered and re-surveying on the way back until the two parties met at their starting point {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 316-317}. The survey produced two figures for the length of a meridian degree: fifty-six miles, and then fifty-six and two-thirds miles — the value al-Biruni later used in his own computations {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates}. The museum records this project as an improvement on an old Greek reading of the meridian degree as five hundred stadia, not as the invention of arc measurement itself.

## What it is
The "meridian-degree survey" is an applied scientific programme for measuring the length of one degree of the meridian on the earth's surface, then multiplying it by three hundred and sixty to obtain the earth's circumference or derive its radius {cite:etymological-dictionary-mamun-method}. The idea is old — the ancients knew it from measuring a terrestrial arc between two cities or from differences of latitude — but what was new in al-Ma'mun's day was that it was executed as a large-scale organised field survey: two parties moving in opposite directions on a single meridian, each tracking the sun's apparent tilt to know when they had covered the distance corresponding to a full degree, then measuring it in local cubits, fixing it with markers on the ground and re-verifying it in a second survey {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates}.

## The world before it
Before the Sinjar survey, knowledge of the earth's circumference rested on inherited Greek readings. Al-Biruni reports in his book Determining the Coordinates of Cities that al-Ma'mun read in Greek books that a degree of the meridian equalled five hundred stadia, but found that the length of the Hellenistic stade was not sufficiently known to the translators to let them tie it to local measures, so no one could say with certainty how many Arab cubits that degree was {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates}. Older geographical practice did know how to measure meridian arcs between different latitudes, but the gap between an obscure Greek unit and a familiar local one is what drove the project: what was needed was not a new idea but a reliable number on which to compute the earth {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates}. Al-Ma'mun's own age was an age of systematic translation; it is enough to recall that his reign was marked by encouraging the transfer of Greek books and the running of the House of Wisdom, which explains how the project came to rest on an old Greek reading rather than starting from nothing {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl}.

## The story
The direct transmitter of what we know about the survey is al-Biruni, who reports from Habash al-Hasib that Khalid dictated the figure to him, and that Habash himself heard it from Khalid — Khalid having dictated the number to the judge Yahya b. Aktham, then chief justice of Basra — a detail al-Biruni himself notes as an Islamic side to the project, since a man of the law, not merely an astronomer, took part in it {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates}. Al-Farghani, for his part, transmits from Abu'l-Fida a different account: a company of scholars gathered in the Sinjar desert in al-Ma'mun's reign, split into two parties, one walking north and the other south until the pole rose one degree for each, and the distance came out at fifty-six and two-thirds miles for some parties and fifty-six without fraction for others, the larger figure being adopted {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 316-317}. The astronomical etymological dictionary adds that the measurement was repeated a second time and that the result came out "somewhat between fifty-six and fifty-seven Arabic miles," then multiplied by three hundred and sixty to yield the circumference from which the radius was drawn {cite:etymological-dictionary-mamun-method}.

## The maker(s)
Al-Ma'mun ibn Harun al-Rashid is the patron: he is the one who read the old Greek figure, recognised the obscurity of the stade unit, and ordered a new survey {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates}. A documented Muslim figure: al-Tabari's history and the Minhaj chronicle preserve his biography as the Abbasid caliph who fought his brother al-Amin, designated Ali al-Rida as heir and then took Baghdad {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl}, and the museum records his affiliation within the era's default Sunni frame {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434}. Al-Khwarizmi — author of the Algebra and the Sindhind tables — is among the scholars whom Britannica names as having joined al-Ma'mun's project to fix the earth's circumference by measuring one degree of meridian across the plain of Sinjar {cite:britannica-al-khwarizmi}; the primary accounts speak of "a company of scholars" without listing every member, so the museum records his participation as shared execution of the programme, not sole authorship of the arc-measurement principle.

## How it works
The reported method rests on three steps. First, astronomical observation: each party watches the sun's angular altitude at the meridian — its highest point in the daily arc — and continues until that altitude changes by one degree, subtracting the natural change caused by the sun's declination as it moves through the year {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates} {cite:etymological-dictionary-mamun-method}. Second, ground measurement: the distance covered is measured in cubits, and arrows or markers are planted at stages along the route to fix the course {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates}. Third, verification: on the way back the survey is repeated so that each party re-checks its earlier estimates until the two meet again at the point of departure; the result is then multiplied by three hundred and sixty degrees to obtain the circumference, from which the radius is derived {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates} {cite:etymological-dictionary-mamun-method}. Al-Farghani also names the units used: a Hashimite cubit of eight palms or thirty-two fingers, and the black cubit adopted by al-Ma'mun reckoned at six palms and three fingers — twenty-seven fingers — though the OCR readings of the Arabic text need checking against the page image {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 317-319}. The method's rigour lies in the fact that human error in walking or measuring was absorbed by a second survey before the figure was adopted, a step that distinguishes it from earlier single-effort measurements.

## The spread
The survey was not an isolated event but part of the earth-measurement movement in the Islamic world. The value it produced — fifty-six and two-thirds miles per degree — is the one al-Farghani explicitly gives in his book on the principles of astronomy, calling it "as tested in al-Ma'mun's days, when several scholars joined in its measurement," multiplying it by three hundred and sixty to yield an earth's circumference of twenty thousand four hundred miles, then dividing the circumference by three and seven-sevenths to obtain a diameter of about six thousand five hundred miles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 99-100}. Al-Biruni himself uses the same value — fifty-six and two-thirds — in his later computations, as MacTutor notes {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates}.

## What it was built on
The survey rested on two inherited pillars. The first is the principle of measuring an arc of the meridian and multiplying it out — a principle the ancients knew from the time of Eratosthenes onward, the same principle al-Biruni later contrasts with his mountain-and-horizon method as two ways of measuring the terrestrial arc {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation pp. 183-189}. The second is al-Ma'mun's intellectual apparatus of translation and book collection: the project itself arose from reading a translated Greek book, which means the problem was not the absence of the idea but the absence of the unit {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates} {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl}. The parallel time-branch — Sanad ibn Ali's measurement by horizon dip from a mountain, also ordered by al-Ma'mun — shows that the caliph supported more than one method for solving the question of the earth's size {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation pp. 185-189}.

## What was built on it
It is the result, not the construction, that later generations inherited: fifty-six and two-thirds miles per degree became the adopted value in al-Farghani's computations of circumference and diameter {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 99-100}, was used by al-Biruni in his calculations {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates}, and was carried forward by later astronomical dictionaries as the entry "Mamun's method" {cite:etymological-dictionary-mamun-method}. The connection, however, does not imply direct dependence: al-Biruni's Nandana mountain measurement is an independent method from the Sinjar survey even though the two share goal and patron {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation pp. 183-189}.

## Impact on daily life
The survey's impact was scholarly before it was everyday. It changed nothing in the streets of Baghdad or in the farmers' seasons, but it changed a single number that determined every later geographical computation: the earth's circumference. And since Islamic geography served calendar, navigation and astrology together, a reliable degree value was an indirect input to many sciences {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 99-100} {cite:etymological-dictionary-mamun-method}. The deeper significance is that the project displayed an institutional pattern: a caliph who funds, scholars who execute, a judge who takes part, and translators who prepare the books — organised scientific thinking, not an individual adventure {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates}.

## How it changed history
The survey changed the history of measurement in three ways. First, it is the earliest large-scale documented Muslim meridian survey, with declination correction and an out-and-back re-survey {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 316-317}. Second, it settled the Greek stade question practically: the geographer no longer needed to know the length of an obscure Hellenistic stade but knew the length of a local Arab degree {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates}. Third, it produced a number that stayed in geographical use for centuries: al-Farghani multiplied it into a 20,400-mile circumference, al-Biruni used it, and dictionaries transmitted it {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 99-100} {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates} {cite:etymological-dictionary-mamun-method}. The record claims no universal priority: meridian-arc measurement was known before al-Ma'mun, and the project is an improvement on an old reading, not an invention of the principle.

## Who invented it?
No one "invented" arc measurement at Sinjar; the principle is inherited from ancient Greek measurement, and al-Ma'mun himself had read its result in a translated book {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates} {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation pp. 183-189}. What the project did was an executive improvement: replacing an obscure unit with a documented local measurement, under a caliph and an assembled body of scholars. The museum records the attribution as improvement, not invention: the real credit belongs to the Abbasid system of translation and patronage, and the number itself reached later books {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 316-317}.

## Today
No material trace of the survey survives — no boundary marker, no engraved route. The plain itself today is the Jebel Sinjar plain in northern Iraq, and the photograph displayed in this record is a modern landscape of that plain, not a historical image {cite:commons-sinjar-balistan}. The value the survey produced — about 56.7 Arab miles per degree — can be compared with a modern measure: MacTutor notes that al-Biruni derived a 6339.6 km radius from his computations, while an academic summary cautions about the figure's accuracy and the unresolved cubit unit, so the medieval record is not converted into a modern measurement here {cite:biruni-mactutor}. What remains worth noting is the method: two parties, meridian observation, declination correction, and a verifying second pass.

## Surviving examples
There is no written observational log from the day of the survey itself. What remains is convergent later testimony: al-Biruni's text relayed through MacTutor on Habash, Khalid and Yahya b. Aktham {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates}, al-Farghani's account from Abu'l-Fida of the two parties and the two figures {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 316-317}, and the dictionary entry that organises the method {cite:etymological-dictionary-mamun-method}. These witnesses are not a contemporary record, but they agree on method and result — and that is the limit of what history offers: a documented programme with results, not a field diary {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates}.

## In culture
The survey carries a double cultural symbolism. On one side it is an example of the Abbasid "scientific court": a caliph who did not merely patronise books but funded a field measurement to solve a practical astronomical question {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates} {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl}. On the other, a judge took part — judge Yahya b. Aktham, chief justice of Basra — a detail al-Biruni himself noted as a sign that science in that age was not the monopoly of astronomers but extended to men of law and jurisprudence {cite:mactutor-biruni-coordinates}. The site of Sinjar itself is a loaded historical place: a broad plain in the Jazira that ancient travellers crossed, among them Ibn Battuta on his way from Nasibin to Mosul {cite:mujam-al-buldan-sader-1995}.

## For young readers
More than twelve hundred years ago the caliph al-Ma'mun wanted to know the size of the earth more exactly than people then did. He gathered scholars in the desert of Sinjar; one team walked north and one south, and each time the sun's height at noon rose by one degree they measured the distance they had covered. The answer came out near fifty-six and two-thirds miles per degree — a number later scholars used for centuries in computations of the earth.
