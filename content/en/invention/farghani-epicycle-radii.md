## Summary
How did the old Muslim astronomer know the size of a planet's small circle? In chapter sixteen of "Elements of Astronomy" al-Farghani gives a unified table: the epicycle radii of each planet on a single scale where the eccentric deferent's radius is sixty parts. He gives the Moon six and a half parts, Mercury twenty-two and a half, Saturn six and a third, and Jupiter eleven and a half ⁦(OCR readings needing visual check before the figures are relied on)⁩. This is not the invention of the small orb — it is an inherited structure — but an organised transmission of its parameters into Arabic in an influential book. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}

The record documents a transmitted computational method: the values themselves belong to the earlier astronomical tradition, and al-Farghani's contribution is their selection and arrangement on a unified scale for Arabic readers. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}{cite:iranica-fargani-ahmad chapter plan}

## What it is
It is a parameter table: for each of the five planets and the two luminaries — the Sun and the Moon — the radius of its epicycle is expressed in parts out of sixty, the sixty being the eccentric deferent's radius. This unified scale means the astronomer needs no conversions: every value sits on one scale and can be compared directly with the others. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}

Alongside the radii, al-Farghani in the following chapters gives the planets' periods in their orbs — the Moon in about twenty-seven and a third days, Mercury in three months and twenty-six days, and Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn in various longer periods — and the retrograde arcs for each planet, all within the same two consecutive chapters of the Jawami'. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 67}

## The world before it
Inherited circular astronomy had known since Ptolemy that the planets do not move at a uniform pace; each planet has anomalies in its speed and in its apparent position. The common treatment was to add a "small orb" — the epicycle — whose centre circles the rim of the deferent, explaining the swing between slowness and speed, together with an eccentric orb explaining the changing distance. The computational problem every old astronomer faced was fixing the size of these circles: what radius for the epicycle and what offset for the centre? {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}

The answer in the earlier traditions was scattered figures in different units, whose reader needed conversion tables and memory. al-Farghani's table's value is that it gathered these values on one clear scale, putting the figures he worked with within the Arabic reader's reach without the labour of conversion. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}

## The story
al-Farghani wrote this chapter within the setting of his book "Elements of Astronomy", composed in the milieu of the Abbasid caliphate, where he was among the leading mathematicians and astronomers of his age, serving caliphs from al-Ma'mun to al-Mutawakkil. The TDV encyclopaedia records that the book condensed Ptolemy's Almagest into thirty chapters, and that chapters twelve to eighteen cover the description of the orbs and the motions of the luminaries and planets. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya biography}{cite:iranica-fargani-ahmad chapter plan}

Chapter sixteen in particular — "On the dimensions of the planets' orbs called epicycles at the eccentric deferents and the distances of the eccentric centres from the centre of the Earth" — is where al-Farghani sets out the dimensions and centres of the deferents, beginning with the Sun's single eccentric orb, then the five planets each having two centres off the Earth's centre on a straight line. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}

## The maker(s)
The name attached to the record is al-Farghani, in the role of "transmitter" not "inventor". The epicycle itself — the small orb — is an inherited structure from the astronomy of Ptolemy and his predecessors, and the epicycle-radii values he gives belong to the earlier tradition, not invented by him. What he actually did was gather these values into a unified table and place them in clear Arabic. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}{cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}

There are no other partners: the book is a single-author work attributed to al-Farghani, and verifying its attribution to the circulating Arabic manuscripts is the work of historians, not scientists. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}

## How it works
The computational idea is simple: each wandering planet moves on its deferent in a non-uniform path; if we add to it a small orb — the epicycle — on which the planet circles, and turn this small orb's centre around the rim of the deferent, the result is a composite giving a varying speed and an oscillating position matching what is seen. The epicycle's radius determines the extent of this oscillation: the larger it is, the greater the planet's speed variations and deviations. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}

And the table al-Farghani gives assigns these small orbs defined radii: the Moon — the nearest body — has a relatively small epicycle (six and a half parts of sixty), Mercury — the fastest planet — has the largest (twenty-two and a half parts), while Saturn — the slowest — has nearly the smallest (six and a third parts). This ordering is logical: the faster and nearer bodies need a larger orb to explain their oscillation. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}

## The spread
The table did not remain in Arabic: the book's Latin translations carried it into European astronomy. TDV records two medieval Latin translations attributed to Johannes Hispalensis in 1134 and Gerard of Cremona in 1175, then a Hebrew translation by Judah Anatoli in the mid-thirteenth century, then Golius's edition in 1669 which joined the Arabic text and the Latin translation. Through them the book became a reference in European astronomy until the end of the fifteenth century. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya transmission}

At every stage of this chain the parameter table travelled with the chapters' structure, though its figures sometimes appeared in the Latin translations with values slightly different from their Arabic readings. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya transmission}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}

## What it was built on
The table rests on an inherited orb structure: the eccentric deferent, the small epicycle, and their relative centres. al-Farghani does not invent this structure but fills it with numbers. The internal evidence in the text points the same way: phrases such as "we have shown" and "we have mentioned" indicate the values are taken from earlier foundations, and his role in the chapter is presentation, not creation. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}

At a deeper level it rests on the principle of "the single measure": choosing sixty parts as the unified radius of the deferent is a convenient computational choice that makes comparison between planets possible — an improvement in the method of presentation, not in the orbital values themselves. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}

## What was built on it
Built on this table is a history of its use: later Arabic astronomical books that relied on the Jawami' in computing planetary positions, the zij literature that inherited its parameters, and the Latin translations that fed European astronomy. The epicycle parameters in particular were among the materials needed by every planetary-position computation in the Ptolemaic tradition. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

This does not mean every figure in the table was used verbatim everywhere; later astronomers adjusted the values to their own observations and doctrines, but the structure — a unified scale and clear dimensions — remained a reference. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

## Impact on daily life
The table was not merely abstract theory; it was a practical tool for anyone wanting to know a planet's position at a given moment: the astronomer computing for navigation, the judge fixing the calendar, the observer predicting a planet's appearance. Every computation of a planet's position began from these parameters: knowing the epicycle's radius lets the astronomer compute the planet's apparent position on its path. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 67}

In the Islamic calendar, knowing the planets' positions meant knowing the seasons of the year, the seasons of sowing, and the worships tied to them; the table that carried these parameters was in essence part of the structure organising public time. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy index}

## How it changed history
The table changed history by its position in the chain of transmission: it carried the epicycle parameters into Arabic in an organised text, then into Latin through two translations, becoming a reference in European astronomy for centuries. The encyclopaedic evidence is that the book was described as "indispensable" for astronomical study from the twelfth century to the end of the fifteenth. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

This does not mean the values themselves were new or accurate by modern standards; they belong to the earlier astronomical tradition and are limited by the observation accuracy of that time. The record's value is that it documents how these parameters lived in Muslim and European scientific consciousness for a long time. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

## Who invented it?
The honest answer: al-Farghani did not invent the epicycle or its radii values; the structure and values are inherited from the astronomy of Ptolemy and his predecessors. What he actually did was gather them into a unified table and place them in clear Arabic — a work of transmission and organisation, not creation. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}{cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}

Nor do we know today, with certainty, who first fixed these values; ancient astronomy accumulated on itself, and the numerical parameters were adjusted across generations of observation. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}

## Today
Today no astronomer uses al-Farghani's table to compute real positions; modern planetary computation rests on precise celestial mechanics and modern astronomical observation. But the table has not died: it is still taught in the history of astronomy as a picture of the parameters of the Earth-centred model, and displayed in science museums as a document of a particular astronomical way of thinking at a particular time. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

The image accompanying this record — the deferent, epicycle and three-centres diagram — shows the structure these parameters measure, not the table itself; its purpose is to connect the visitor between the figures in the book and the structure they describe. {cite:commons-ptolemaic-deferent-epicycle description}

## Surviving examples
The direct surviving witness is the printed text itself: Golius's 1669 edition, which preserved the Arabic text and the Latin translation together, and in which chapter sixteen holds its table. Among indirect witnesses are the Latin translations that made the book a reference in European astronomy until the fifteenth century, and Sezgin's facsimile edition of 1986. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy preface}{cite:tdv-fergani-kaya transmission}

## In culture
The table entered Muslim scientific consciousness through the Jawami' as "al-Farghani's book", which became synonymous with teaching astronomy; even those who never read the Almagest read its summary. In contemporary heritage al-Farghani is named in lists of great Islamic astronomers alongside al-Battani and al-Khwarizmi, though he lacks the popular fame some gained because his role was "transmitter" rather than "originator". {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 519}{cite:tdv-fergani-kaya biography}

## For young readers
Imagine each planet plays on the rim of a big wheel (the deferent), and inside it a small wheel (the epicycle) on which the planet rolls. The table al-Farghani wrote tells us: the Moon's little wheel has a radius of about six parts, Mercury's about twenty-two parts, and so on. These numbers are carried over from older astronomy, but al-Farghani's book gathered them in one clear place. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}

And why did we need these numbers? Because old astronomers had no computer; they calculated by hand and with tables, and every number in the table saved them a long computation. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 65}
