## Summary
What is the "eccentric orb" in al-Farghani's account? It is a circular orb whose centre is not the centre of the Earth, so that a body's distance from it varies between the farthest point — apogee — and the nearest — perigee. In chapter sixteen of "Elements of Astronomy" al-Farghani explains that the Sun has one eccentric orb whose centre lies two and a half parts off the Earth's centre when the orb's radius is sixty parts ⁦(an OCR reading)⁩, that the five wandering planets each have two centres off the Earth's centre on a straight line through it, that Mercury has a third moving centre, and that the Moon has two centres, one fixed and one moving. This is an organised transmission of an inherited astronomical structure, not the invention of a new structure. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 84}

The record documents a transmitted computational method: the structure itself belongs to the earlier astronomical tradition, and al-Farghani's contribution is presenting it with clear figures in an influential Arabic book. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}{cite:iranica-fargani-ahmad chapter plan}

## What it is
It is a geometrical pattern: its circle is not centred on the Earth but on another centre that displaces it. A body moving on this circle is sometimes farther from the Earth and sometimes nearer, and this periodic change in distance is exactly what explains certain anomalies that the Earth-centred circle alone cannot. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 84}

In al-Farghani's account there is no single unified structure for all bodies: the Sun has one eccentric orb with a defined offset, the wandering planets have more complex patterns with two or three centres, and the Moon has its own special composition. The table is not "one recipe" but a "map of structures", each for a different body. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}

## The world before it
Ancient astronomy had known for a long time that the apparent paths were not perfect circles centred on the Earth; observations showed that some bodies varied in speed and distance in patterns that simple circular motion could not explain. The common treatment — in the astronomy of Ptolemy and his predecessors — was to introduce a "second centre" for the circle: instead of the circle's centre being the Earth, its centre is another point, and this offset produces the distance variations that explain what is seen. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}

The computational problem every old astronomer faced was fixing the size of this offset: what distance between the circle's centre and the Earth's centre? In which direction? And to what extent does the distance change? {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}

## The story
al-Farghani wrote this chapter within the setting of his book "Elements of Astronomy", composed in the milieu of the Abbasid caliphate, where he was among the leading mathematicians and astronomers of his age, serving caliphs from al-Ma'mun to al-Mutawakkil. The TDV encyclopaedia records that the book condensed Ptolemy's Almagest into thirty chapters, and that chapters twelve to eighteen cover the description of the orbs and the motions of the luminaries and planets. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya biography}{cite:iranica-fargani-ahmad chapter plan}

Chapter sixteen in particular — "On the dimensions of the planets' orbs called epicycles at the eccentric deferents and the distances of the eccentric centres from the centre of the Earth" — is where al-Farghani sets out the dimensions and centres of the deferents, beginning with the Sun's single eccentric orb, then the five wandering planets each having two centres off the Earth's centre on a straight line, then noting Mercury's exception with a third moving centre, and the Moon's exception with two centres, one fixed and one moving. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}

## The maker(s)
The name attached to the record is al-Farghani, in the role of "transmitter" not "inventor". The eccentric orb itself is an inherited structure from the astronomy of Ptolemy and his predecessors, and the values he gives — such as the Sun's orb centre being two and a half parts of sixty off the Earth's centre — belong to the earlier tradition, not invented by him. What he actually did was present this structure with clear figures in organised Arabic. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}{cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}

There are no other partners: the book is a single-author work attributed to al-Farghani, and verifying its attribution to the circulating Arabic manuscripts is the work of historians, not scientists. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}

## How it works
The geometrical idea is that the circle's centre is not the Earth's centre, so a body on this circle has two opposite positions: the "apogee" position, farthest from the Earth, and the "perigee" position, nearest to it. Between them the body travels along its circular path, its distance from the Earth changing continuously — which is exactly what explains a body's varying speed: it is faster when near and slower when far in the comparable astronomical traditions. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 84}

And al-Farghani explains that this structure is not uniform: the Sun — the simplest body — has one eccentric orb whose centre is two and a half parts of sixty off the Earth's centre. The five wandering planets — Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn — have a more complex structure: two centres off the Earth's centre on a straight line, with Mercury having a third moving centre on one of the centres, and the Moon having two centres, one fixed and one moving. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}

## The spread
The structure did not remain in Arabic alone: the book's Latin translations carried it into European astronomy. TDV records two medieval Latin translations attributed to Johannes Hispalensis in 1134 and Gerard of Cremona in 1175, then a Hebrew translation by Judah Anatoli in the mid-thirteenth century, then Golius's edition in 1669 which joined the Arabic text and the Latin translation. Through them the book became a reference in European astronomy until the end of the fifteenth century. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya transmission}

At every stage of this chain the eccentric-orb structure travelled with the chapters' structure as a whole; the chapters concerned with it crossed with the structure of all the chapters. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya transmission}

## What it was built on
The whole record rests on an inherited orb structure: the eccentric deferent, its multiple centres, and its distance from the Earth's centre. al-Farghani does not invent this structure but presents it with figures. The internal evidence in the text points the same way: phrases such as "we have shown" and "we have mentioned" indicate the values are taken from earlier foundations, and his role in the chapter is presentation, not creation. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 84}

At a deeper level it rests on the principle of "the circle centred on something other than the Earth": this structure assumes a body's path can be represented by a circle whose centre is not necessarily the Earth's, an assumption inherited by all ancient astronomy and untouched by al-Farghani. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}

## What was built on it
Built on this record is a history of its use: later Arabic astronomical books that relied on the Jawami' in computing planetary positions, the zij literature that inherited its parameters, and the Latin translations that fed European astronomy. The eccentric-orb structure in particular was among the materials needed by every planetary-position computation in the Ptolemaic tradition. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

This does not mean every later book took the structure in the same wording; the influence operated at the level of the general system, not line by line. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

## Impact on daily life
This structure was not dry theory alone; it was the computational structure on which people's knowledge of planetary positions was built: the seasons of the year, the times of worship tied to the seasons, predictions of eclipses. Every computation of a planet's position began from its orbs' structure: knowing the circle's centre and its offset lets the astronomer compute the planet's apparent position on its path. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 84}

In the Islamic calendar, knowing the planets' positions meant knowing the seasons of the year, the seasons of sowing, and the worships tied to them; the book that carried this structure was in essence an instrument for organising public time before precise clocks existed. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy index}

## How it changed history
The record changed history by its position in the chain of transmission: it carried the eccentric-orb structure into Arabic in an organised text, then into Latin through two translations, becoming a reference in European astronomy for centuries. The encyclopaedic evidence is that the book was described as "indispensable" for astronomical study from the twelfth century to the end of the fifteenth. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

This does not mean the structure itself was new or accurate by modern standards; it belongs to the earlier astronomical tradition and is limited by the observation accuracy of that time. The record's value is that it documents how this structure lived in Muslim and European scientific consciousness for a long time. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

## Who invented it?
The honest answer in this record: al-Farghani did not invent the eccentric orb, and no one in the Islamic world did; it is a circular structure inherited from earlier Greek-Indian astronomical traditions, which al-Farghani carried into Arabic in a more orderly system. What he actually did was present the structure with clear figures and explain its centres — a work of transmission and organisation, not creation. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}{cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}

Nor do we know today, with certainty, who first fixed this structure; ancient astronomy accumulated on itself, and the circle centred on something other than the Earth was the language of all astronomy before anyone claimed it. That is why the record is classed "transmission", not "creation". {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}

## Today
Today no astronomer uses al-Farghani's structure to compute real positions; modern planetary computation rests on precise celestial mechanics and modern astronomical observation. But the structure has not died: it is still taught in the history of astronomy as an Earth-centred model, and displayed in science museums as a picture of a particular astronomical way of thinking at a particular time. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

## Surviving examples
The direct surviving witness is the printed text itself: Golius's 1669 edition, which preserved the Arabic text and the Latin translation together, and in which chapter sixteen holds its structure. Among indirect witnesses are the Latin translations that made the book a reference in European astronomy until the fifteenth century, and Sezgin's facsimile edition of 1986. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy preface}{cite:tdv-fergani-kaya transmission}

## In culture
The structure entered Muslim scientific consciousness through the Jawami' as "al-Farghani's book", which became synonymous with teaching astronomy; even those who never read the Almagest read its summary. In contemporary heritage al-Farghani is named in lists of great Islamic astronomers alongside al-Battani and al-Khwarizmi, though he lacks the popular fame some gained because his role was "transmitter" rather than "originator". {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 519}{cite:tdv-fergani-kaya biography}

## For young readers
Imagine you draw a circle on paper, then place its centre not in the middle of the circle but a little way off it — that is how old astronomers "drew" a planet's path! The circle itself stays circular, but its displaced centre makes the planet farther from the Earth sometimes and nearer sometimes. al-Farghani did not invent this idea, but he wrote it clearly in an Arabic book that became very famous, until it was translated into Latin and stayed a reference in Europe for five hundred years. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 66}
