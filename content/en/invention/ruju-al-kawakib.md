## Summary
Retrograde motion is the motion by which an outer planet — Mars, Jupiter, Venus, Mercury — sometimes appears to walk backward along the zodiac, moving westward for weeks before going forward again {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. Al-Farghani explained it in his chapter on the five wandering stars by composing two motions: the planet's motion on the small 'epicycle' and the motion of its centre on the large 'deferent', so the planet looks retrograde when its own motion outweighs the centre's — greatest at perigee {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. The model itself was inherited from Ptolemaic astronomy, so al-Farghani is a transmitter-explainer, not an inventor {cite:commons-ptolemy-epicycles} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}.

## What it is
'Retrogression' in ancient astronomy is the appearance of a planet occupying a place in the zodiac it has not reached before, then continuing its course — an ancient phenomenon astronomers had observed long before al-Farghani {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. The retrogression does not affect the five planets alone: the Sun and Moon have their own motions, but the visible backward loop belongs to the planets that travel farther from the Sun {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. The observation that this explanation needs is that a planet does not move in a single circle but in two composed circles: a small circle on which the planet runs, and a large circle on which the small circle's centre travels {cite:commons-ptolemy-epicycles}. With this composition, the simple motion explains what seems complex in the sky.

## The world before it
Before al-Farghani wrote, Greek astronomers had observed the planets' retrogression and spent centuries trying to explain it {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. In the Almagest, Ptolemy gathered the observations of ages and set out his model: a planet turning on an epicycle whose centre travels on a deferent, the two motions together carrying the planet through the zodiac {cite:commons-ptolemy-epicycles}. Retrogression was not alone in the list of problems: the same mechanism troubled the five planets, the Sun and the Moon, each treated in its own chapter of the book {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. This model reached Arabic with the translation movement of the second century AH and became part of the curriculum taught to students of astronomy in the houses of learning {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. The world before al-Farghani was not empty: it held an ancient observation spanning centuries and a ready Ptolemaic model awaiting a clear Arabic exposition suited to the teaching curriculum.

## The story
In his 'Elements of Astronomy' (al-Jawami' fi 'ilm al-nujum), al-Farghani devoted a whole chapter — 'On the retrogression of the five wandering stars in the zodiac' — to explaining why these planets appear to go backward {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. He opens the chapter with the mechanics: a planet at the top of its epicycle moves eastward just as its centre does, so it looks faster; when it descends to the western side of the epicycle, its visible motion turns westward while its centre pushes it eastward, and when the planet's motion outweighs the centre's the planet appears retrograde in the zodiac {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. Then he fixes the limits: each of the five planets has a precise position on its epicycle where retrogression begins and ends, and he gives the degree figures for each planet separately — Mars and Jupiter's figures differing from Mercury's and Venus's {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 195}. He closes with a precise question: why does the Moon not retrogress, though it too runs on an epicycle? The answer is that its retrograde arc in the zodiac is tiny beside its centre's speed, so no retrogression worth noting is seen {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 195}. Thus al-Farghani delivers in a single chapter: the description, the mechanism, the numerical limits and the exception — the very style of the 'Elements' in every chapter.

## The maker(s)
No single inventor of the retrogression explanation exists in the record. The model — epicycle and deferent — is a Greek astronomical device, and Ptolemy's account in the Almagest is the source on which Islamic astronomy built {cite:commons-ptolemy-epicycles} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Al-Farghani's role is documented precisely in his book: he is the 'explainer' who carried the model into clear Arabic for students, fixed the limits of retrogression for each planet, and explained the Moon's exceptional case {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. This is not a defect of his ability but a description of his role: the author of the 'Elements' was a schoolbook that gathered the inherited astronomical curriculum in one text, not the author of a new theory.

## How it works
The idea at its core is simple: the planet is not a point travelling on one line, but a point turning on a small circle whose centre travels on a large circle {cite:commons-ptolemy-epicycles}. If the planet's motion on the small circle is in the same direction as the centre's motion on the large one, the observer adds the two motions and sees the planet faster in its course; if the planet's motion is opposite to its centre's and outweighs it, the observer sees the planet walking backward in the zodiac {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. Retrogression is greatest when the planet reaches 'perigee' — the nearest point of its epicycle to the Earth — because its apparent speed there is at its peak, then it stops retrogressing at fixed limits and resumes its direct course {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 194–195}. The mechanism thus explains everything the observation saw: slowing, standing still, going backward, then returning — a cycle that repeats for each planet to a different degree {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. The modern diagram 'How epicycles explain retrograde motion' re-enacts this same sequence in three panels: the planet in front, then in the middle, then behind on its small circle {cite:commons-ptolemy-epicycles}.

## The spread
Al-Farghani's explanation spread with his book itself: the 'Elements of Astronomy' became a school reference in astronomy, read by students in Baghdad and beyond who carried its chapters {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Later astronomical manuals — zij tables and hay'a treatises — described the same epicycle mechanism, so al-Farghani's model remained the standard Arabic formulation of the retrogression explanation for centuries {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. As Arabic astronomy passed into Persia, India and al-Andalus through translations and copies, this chapter travelled with it as the rest of the book did {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Retrogression did not remain a 'puzzle' in the Islamic curriculum: it became a studied chapter with preserved numerical limits.
Nor was its spread confined to theory: the 'Elements' teaches all the 'heavenly motions' — from the daily to the annual — and the retrogression explanation is the culmination of the planetary geometry the student needs {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Everyone who read the 'Elements' whole also read, with the retrogression chapter, the whole rules of astronomical computation: how the planet is placed in its sphere, how its position is calculated, and why it sometimes looks retrograde {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}.

## What it was built on
The retrogression explanation rests on two pillars: an ancient astronomical observation — that planets sometimes return in their course — and a ready mathematical model, the epicycle mechanism from the Almagest {cite:commons-ptolemy-epicycles} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. Above that it stood on a mature computational science: the calculations of distances, sizes and relative speeds that made it possible to fix the limits of retrogression for each planet instead of merely describing the phenomenon {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. The model was not al-Farghani's invention but the 'mould' into which he poured the simplified Arabic content.

## What was built on it
On this explanation rose the following: the 'zij' tables that use epicycle parameters to compute planetary positions for astrology and calendars {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. Later discussions on the precision of the model and its match to observation, among them what eventually prepared the Copernican revision of the orbits in Europe {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. In astronomical teaching, al-Farghani's retrogression chapter remained 'the Arabic text' that explains to the beginner what had been complex in the Greek original.
Its effect was not confined to theoretical astronomy: the computations that come out of this model — times of conjunctions and the planets' standings — fed the astronomical calendars needed by those working in astrology and computation {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. Understanding the retrogression mechanism was a gateway to understanding the rest of the 'hay'a' — the science of composing the spheres — on which the whole Islamic astronomical system was built.

## Impact on daily life
Retrogression touched ordinary life only at its farthest horizon: the calendars of agriculture, taxes and seasons were built on solar and lunar computations, not on detailed planetary positions {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. But its impact was in the environment of science: the students of the House of Wisdom and those after them learned that the sky is 'computable' — that every strange motion in it has a geometric mechanism that explains it {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Retrogression was thus an early test of the viability of the whole Abbasid astronomical programme: if the model succeeded in explaining the oldest of phenomena, the whole curriculum stood firm.
Nor was its effect limited to books: the 'Elements' became one of the texts copied and taught in the schools of astronomy for centuries, and every new copy carried the retrogression explanation to a new generation of students {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. This chapter — though it changed no one's life directly — was part of the educational structure that formed astronomers in the Islamic world for centuries.

## How it changed history
Al-Farghani's explanation changed not external history but 'the history of knowledge': it turned a complex Greek mechanism into a clear Arabic chapter a student could hold in his class {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. The epicycle model remained the computational frame for the planets until the European Renaissance, when it was replaced by heliocentric ellipses {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Al-Farghani's book preserves today the Arabic witness to this stage: the text by which generations of Muslim astronomers learned how the planets 'walk'.

## Who invented it?
The honest answer: there is no single inventor. The phenomenon was observed in antiquity, the model was set in Greek astronomy, and al-Farghani carried it into Arabic and explained it for the Abbasid curriculum {cite:commons-ptolemy-epicycles} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. His role — as his own text describes it — is the role of 'explainer', not 'inventor': his chapter explains and claims no priority {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. This is the correct way to read the 'Elements': a book that gathers inherited science and presents it in the language of its age, not an encyclopedia of inventions.

## Today
Retrogression is no longer taught as a living celestial phenomenon — modern astronomy knows the planets travel the Sun in elliptical orbits — but it is still taught in histories of astronomy and models of ancient planetary motion {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Al-Farghani's text is still read in studies of Islamic astronomical history as 'the Arabic formulation' of the epicycle explanation {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. The diagram 'How epicycles explain retrograde motion' — the modern drawing that re-enacts the same mechanism — is still used in teaching books {cite:commons-ptolemy-epicycles}. In museums and exhibitions of the history of science, physical models of the epicycles are displayed to remind students of the 'composite-sphere' stage that preceded modern celestial mechanics {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}.

## Surviving examples
The Arabic text itself is the surviving witness: chapter fifteen of the 'Elements' explains retrogression by the epicycle mechanism and fixes each planet's limits {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. Copies of the 'Elements' carried the chapter through the tradition of astronomical teaching {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. There is no 'device' for retrogression because the subject is computational, not instrumental: the witness is the text and the table, not the machine. The modern editions of the book — such as Golius's 1669 edition — show that the chapter remained readable even in ages when the mechanism was no longer used in living astronomy {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}.

## In culture
Retrogression did not enter popular consciousness under its name, but the mechanism of 'the planet walks backward' became a figurative image of understanding: in poetry and prose the rebellious phenomenon is compared to one who 'returns in his course' in the heavens {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195}. In the books of astrology, the retrograde positions of the planets — especially Mars's retrogression — remained an interpretive measure of the traits of 'return' in persons and events {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Thus the astronomical puzzle became a cultural metaphor. The very term 'ruju'' — return — stayed alive in the Arabic language, and when a poet says someone 'returned on his way', the old celestial image still stands behind it.

## For young readers
Imagine you are riding a bicycle that travels in a small circle, while the circle's centre travels in a big circle around a field. Sometimes your bicycle turns the same way as the centre, so you look faster; sometimes it turns against it, so you look like you are walking backward — that is exactly what a planet does in the sky! Al-Farghani explained a thousand years ago that Mars and its sisters 'go back' because they circle on small circles whose centres circle on big ones. Because this explanation comes from Ptolemaic astronomy, the credit for the understanding belongs to the ancients — and al-Farghani was the Arabic explainer who made it clear to students {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 193–195} {cite:commons-ptolemy-epicycles}.
