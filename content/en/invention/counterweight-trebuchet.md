## Summary
The counterweight trebuchet — the heaviest stone-thrower of the pre-cannon age — appears in the Islamic record at the siege of Hims in 1248 as the 'mandjanik maghribi', was used in batteries at Acre in 1291, and was carried by Muslim engineers to Yuan China in 1272. Its origin is one of the great unsettled questions of technology: China, Byzantium, western Islam and Latin Christendom all have their advocates {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:chevedden-counterweight-trebuchet-2000}.

## What it is
A beam about twenty metres long turning on a fulcrum: the short arm carries a box of lead, iron or stone weighing ten to thirty tonnes; the long arm carries a sling. Released by a trigger, the falling counterweight whips the sling around and throws a heavy stone roughly three hundred metres {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The world before it
Muslim armies had used the traction trebuchet — the same beam thrown by teams of men pulling ropes — since the seventh century, from the machine called 'Mother of the hair' at Mecca in 683 to the batteries at Baghdad in 865-66; before that stood the torsion artillery of the Greeks and Romans {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The story
The first report of the counterweight machine in Islam is Abu'l-Fida's 'western trebuchet' at the siege of Hims in 646/1248. A generation earlier, an arms treatise written for Saladin by [[person/mardi-ibn-ali-al-tarsusi|Mardi ibn Ali]] described trebuchets, all of the traction kind save one 'Persian' machine that may have been a counterweight type — a possible earlier appearance, unproven. By 1291 the machines stood in numbers at the fall of Acre {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The maker(s)
Military engineers, mostly nameless; Mardi ibn Ali is the earliest documented writer on the type, and two Muslim engineers — Ala' al-Din and Isma'il — built the machines for Qubilay Khan at Fancheng in 1272 and earned biographies in the Yuan official history {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it works
A massive timber tower carries the fulcrum; the long beam is divided in a ratio of about five or six to one. A winch draws the long arm down; a trigger releases it. The counterweight box falls, the beam swings, the sling — nearly as long as the beam — accelerates the stone past the beam's own speed and lets it fly when one rope slips its hook {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The spread
From western Islam or Christendom into Syria by 1248; through the Mamluk armies to Acre and Tlemcen — where marble balls of 230 kg have been recovered — and by Muslim engineers into Yuan China, where the type was long called 'Muslim phao' {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it was built on
The traction trebuchet, documented in China by the fourth century BCE and carried west through the Turkish lands — the family from which the counterweight type grew {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What was built on it
The last generation of great stone-throwers before gunpowder artillery; the 'Muslim phao' tradition in China; and a lasting place in the history of technology as the textbook case of diffusion {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:chevedden-counterweight-trebuchet-2000}.

## Impact on daily life
It belonged to war, not the household: its daily reality was the labour of hundreds — timber yards, foundries, the crews who winched and served it — and the fear it taught cities' defenders {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it changed history
It deepened the siege until cannon ended both trebuchet and many walls; its journey — into Syria under a 'western' name, then east to China under a 'Muslim' one — is the diffusion of technology written in a single machine {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Who invented it?
Unresolved. EI2 leaves the origin 'not yet resolved' between Christendom and western Islam toward the end of the twelfth century; Chevedden argues the Islamic world, the Italian record of 1199 argues Latin Europe, and China's priority belongs to the traction family {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:chevedden-counterweight-trebuchet-2000}.

## Today
The machine survives as reconstructions and a favourite of engineering demonstrations; the scholarly argument over its birthplace continues {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:chevedden-counterweight-trebuchet-2000}.

## Surviving examples
No complete medieval machine survives; the evidence is the texts — Abu'l-Fida, al-Maqrizi, Mardi's treatise, the Yuan biographies — and recovered stone shot such as the Tlemcen marble balls {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## In culture
The mandjanik entered Arabic from Greek through Aramaic and passed into Spanish as almajaneque — a word that crossed as many frontiers as the machine {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
Long ago engineers built a giant seesaw with a heavy box on one end and a sling on the other. When the box dropped, the arm flew up and threw a stone as far as three football fields. Muslim armies used it at Hims and Acre, and Muslim engineers even built it for the Khan in China — but nobody is sure who invented it first.
