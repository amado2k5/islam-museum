## Summary

[[person/al-jazari-engineer|Al-Jazari]]'s castle clock opens doors, drops balls from two falcons' beaks and lights roundels at night, multiplying time signals within one machine {cite:mfa-castle-clock-14533 catalogue}. Behind the spectacle are water, a float and an operator resetting the apparatus, not magic or perpetual motion {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.20–22,39–41}. Its contribution is a calibrated seasonal-hour clock within an older tradition, not invention of time or the first computer.

## What it is

The first chapter of the book's first category describes a water clock indicating solar hours, with ten sections within the chapter {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.17}. A display faces the observer while interconnected equipment operates behind it; Hill distinguishes the falling reservoir float from the outflowing water, two sources driving different parts {cite:jazari-hill-1974 editor-notes-I.1}.

It is not the [[invention/elephant-clock|elephant clock]], [[invention/jazari-double-piston-pump|double-piston pump]] or [[invention/jazari-peacock-washer|peacock washer]]. The book separates clocks, water raising and service vessels, so features cannot simply migrate between designs {cite:tdv-cezeri pp.505–506}. “Castle clock” names the known design, not an excavated stone clock assigned to a particular fortress.

## How it works

First, reconsider “hour.” The design divides daylight into twelve portions and darkness into twelve, although their lengths change seasonally; a seasonal hour is not always sixty minutes {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.17–18,editor-notes-I.1}. In our invented example, a15-hour day gives75-minute portions and a9-hour night45-minute portions. These are not claimed as a historical date or location.

Behind the screen, a reservoir float descends with the water and transmits movement through ropes and pulleys {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.20–22,editor-notes-I.1}. But falling water level in an unregulated tank changes the outflow conditions. A separate chamber has another float and conical plug: rising water closes the inlet, falling water permits replenishment, maintaining its level approximately {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.21–22,24}.

These are not one float performing every task. One provides motion through descent; the other regulates the feeding chamber. A further regulator changes outlet position for the required duration {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.20–22,24–25}. The second float can be understood as hydraulic feedback, but modern control terminology does not make the machine a computer program or the first regulator in history.

Seasonal hours have a numerical consequence. Emptying the same hypothetical total volume in15 hours and then9 requires the second average flow to be15/9 times the first. In our paper example,600 volume units divided by15 gives40 per hour, while division by9 gives about66.67. Those quantities and units are ours, not apparatus specifications or measured historical performance.

A circular scale alone does not ensure the required ratios. The author reports failed geometric divisions, then measures water discharged in a fixed hour and establishes scale positions through calculation and experiment before engraving them {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.17–18,24–25}. Geometry serves calibration rather than replacing it. The operator chooses the relevant setting; the machine does not independently sense tomorrow's daylight duration.

The mechanism moves a cart carrying a crescent indicator, sequentially releases doors and sends balls towards the falcons. A ball changes a falcon's balance, making it tip and then recover after release {cite:jazari-hill-1974 editor-notes-I.1}. A separate water route drives a wheel and pegs moving percussionists' arms; Hill explains wind sound through an air vessel and flute, not a free-choice robotic musician {cite:jazari-hill-1974 editor-notes-I.1}.

At night, illuminated roundels indicate the passing divisions, while the operator sets the moon's position and an opening representing its phase {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.39–41}. Displaying the sky is not independently computing its motions. The observer sees the result of mechanics and human-supplied initial information, not continuous astronomical observation.

## Impact on daily life

The museum describes doors, falcons, balls, illuminated roundels and musicians repeatedly signalling time {cite:mfa-castle-clock-14533 catalogue}. Multiple interfaces make a moment visible and audible, but do not prove who attended or that every person could interpret or hear the signals equally.

The instructions expose human work: returning water, resetting parts and establishing positions for the next cycle {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.39–41}. “Automatic” must not erase the operator. Automation is not absence of care; these instructions do not establish the attendant's wages or measured water losses over months.
