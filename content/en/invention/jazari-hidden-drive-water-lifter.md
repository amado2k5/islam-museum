## Summary

A cow appears to circle and turn a water lifter, but this cow is hollow wood, carried by a lever with its hooves clear of the platform. Water drives the machine {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.182–183}. In the third water-raising chapter, [[person/al-jazari-engineer|al-Jazari]] describes a pool concealing a wheel and drive below, with jars lifting water to a channel above {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.182–183}. The arrangement combines lifting and visual display, not animal power and perpetual motion. Understanding it requires two questions: what moves before the eye, and what supplies the energy? The first answer alone cannot establish the second.

## What it is

A hidden hydraulic drive operates a jar-chain lifter. Above the pool stand a pillar, disc, model cow and wheels; a two-cord loop carries vessels into the water and up to an elevated channel {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.182}. Beneath the floor, discharged water passes over a wheel in a chamber, transmitting motion upward {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.182–183}. This is a composite machine, not a living cow pulling the rest of the equipment.

The entry covers the completeV.3 arrangement. It differs from the [[invention/jazari-double-piston-pump|double-piston pump]] ofV.5: this display does not employ that pump's suction valves and opposing pistons {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.182–183,265}. Shaft, gears and jars are not counted as separate inventions. Their significance here lies in their relationships within a particular assembly, not multiplying the count by its components.

## How it works

Water enters the pool, with part discharged along a route to a scoop wheel in the lower chamber. Flow turns the wheel and axle; gears transmit rotation to a vertical shaft {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.182–183}. This is the hidden drive, not the cow visible above. Supply, drainage and a downward water path permitting work are necessary. This is not a closed pool creating its own power.

The shaft passes through the hollow pillar to the upper assembly, where another pair of gears drives the jar wheel {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.182–183}. The loop carries vessels into the water to fill, then raises them to discharge into a channel and onward to the chosen destination {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.183}. A movement description alone gives neither measured flow nor the area actually irrigated.

A lever carries the lightweight wooden cow with its hooves above the disc. The shaft turns the cow, not the reverse {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.182}. This detail changes the reading of the whole scene. The familiar appearance of an animal drive does not produce power in the described device. Not every moving component is a prime mover; the most conspicuous component can be a load carried by the drive.

Hill explains discharge at the wheel's centre and the space for a channel between its rims, including his interpretation of its necessary support {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.265}. Relationships are presented, not a construction plan. An unclear fraction in extracted text is not adopted as the water split, nor are historical spans and capacities converted into unverified modern fabrication specifications.

## What it was built on

The machine combines the jar wheel called Sindi with a lower water wheel, gears and vertical shaft, adding a cow figure's movement to the display {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.182–183}. Combining parts does not make each newly invented. Their functions can be distinguished: receive energy, transmit rotation, carry water or create an appearance. Components moving together may nevertheless perform quite different tasks.

Hill explains the Sindi name geographically and presents the wheel's construction, but the earlier historical source he cites was not independently read for this entry {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.265}. That association remains attributed to him, not developed into an original maker's biography, religion or certain transmission chain. ## What was built on it

Hill's modern explanation separates the wheel and channel construction, exaggerating the jars' size and showing only two for clarity {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.265}. Measuring their proportions from that drawing would misread his intention. He explicitly explains that apparent spilling outside the channel results from explanatory enlargement {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.265}. Clear relationships do not guarantee scale.

These pages do not establish the origin of turbines or every modern saqiya. Hill calls this the first of only two devices in al-Jazari's book using a water wheel as prime mover, not the first hydraulic prime mover in world history {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.265}. Removing the book-specific qualification would create a claim larger than its evidence, so it remains explicit.

## Impact on daily life

The assembly raises some water from pool to channel, connecting useful transport with deliberate beauty: silk, copper, marble and colour {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.182–183}. Hill infers from materials and ornament that its purpose was at least partly decorative, rather than solely a large practical plant {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.265}. The inference is attributed, not converted into an undocumented garden name or eyewitness account.

Driving water remains in the accounting even when below the floor. The text provides incoming, outgoing and jar-lifted water routes {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.182–183}. An ingenious arrangement does not mean saving all energy or lifting the entire supply. We did not measure efficiency or verify the endurance of materials and coatings in a historical installation.

## How it changed history

The device shows lifting and display meeting in one assembly, with apparent cause differing from actual cause {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.182–183}. Understanding its specificity does not require calling it the first agricultural robot. Wooden cow and jar loop connect to the same drive while serving different functions {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.183}.

Reading text with image also reveals omissions. Hill notes a pictured overflow pipe absent from the wording and another associated machine left undescribed {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.265}. Those limits are not invitations to invent the missing material, but reasons to preserve evidence levels. A historical description can be rich without constituting a complete modern construction dossier.
