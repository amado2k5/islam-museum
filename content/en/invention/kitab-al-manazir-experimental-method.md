## Summary

The Book of Optics is not a treatise on a single act of sight nor a description of one experiment, but a methodological programme in seven books in which [[person/ibn-al-haytham|Ibn al-Haytham]] gathered the study of light's properties in its three states — rectilinear propagation, reflection and refraction — by a method that joins experimental testing, which he called i'tibar, with mathematical proofs for interpreting natural phenomena {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.9}. The editor of the modern Arabic edition counts the book as "the most remarkable product of Arab scholars in the field of physics" and the most important optics book to appear between Ptolemy's second-century treatise and the works of Kepler and Descartes in the early seventeenth century {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.9}. Ibn al-Haytham composed it in Egypt in the fifth century AH, and the record fixes no exact year of composition; it then passed into Latin, reached Europe, and remained a reference in optics until its printing in 1572 {cite:tdv-ibnul-heysem works} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.47–48}.

## What it is

The book is at root a study of the properties of light and of the sight of visible objects, divided by its author into seven books: the first on how vision works in general, the second on detailing the meanings sight perceives, their causes and how they are perceived, the third on the errors of sight in direct vision and their causes, the fourth on perception by reflection from polished bodies, the fifth on the positions of images and the pictures seen in polished bodies, the sixth on the errors of sight in reflected perception, and the seventh on perception by refraction from behind transparent bodies that differ from air {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.63}. The edition's editor sees the book as divided into two great parts: the first on the irradiation of light and the sight of objects on the straight line, which is the subject of the first three books, and the second on the reflection and refraction of light and what follows from them in the perception of objects in mirrors of different shapes and in transparent bodies, which is the subject of the last four books {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.10}.

And the Optics is not a single book only in later copies; its author had himself composed an earlier treatise on optics in which "he followed in many of its measures persuasive ways", and when he reached verified proofs for all the visual meanings he set about composing this book, saying that whoever finds the earlier treatise should know it is dispensable because its meanings are contained in this book's substance {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.64}. This means the Optics is the full methodological edition that absorbed the earlier experiment, not Ibn al-Haytham's first look at light.

## The world before it

In the Greek world the study of optics was counted as one of the branches of mathematics, like astronomy, mechanics and music, and Aristotle ranked it among the mathematical enquiries closest to the natural sciences {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.9}. Before him, mathematicians, philosophers and physicians had written on the subject: Euclid, Ptolemy and Galen among them, each composing his own theory of how sight occurs {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.9–10} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.61}. The book itself surveys the opinions of earlier investigators in the opening chapter of the first book, and finds them different and mutually distant: some hold that vision occurs by a form passing from the visible object to the sight, others that it occurs by a ray issuing from the sight to the object, extending along straight lines that together form a cone whose apex is the centre of vision and whose base is the surface of the object; and the sects among the latter differ over the form of that ray and how it comes to be {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.61–62}.

In the Islamic world, research in optics had already accumulated before Ibn al-Haytham, but no work in the sources read had gathered the states of light into one methodological structure before this book; the matter was distributed among separate treatises on mirrors, lenses and refraction, and among opinions not bound to one another {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.60–62} {cite:tdv-ibnul-heysem works}.

## The story

Ibn al-Haytham — Abu Ali al-Hasan ibn al-Hasan ibn al-Haytham — was born at Basra around 354 AH as the biographical notices report, and held a post in a diwan under the Buyids, then was invited by the Fatimid al-Hakim to Egypt over the Nile {cite:tdv-ibnul-heysem biography}. The later historians' lives — specifically Ibn al-Qifti in the History of the Philosophers and Ibn Abi Usaybi'a in the Lives of the Physicians — relate that when he could not carry out the Nile project he had proposed, he feared al-Hakim, dissimulated and feigned madness until al-Hakim's death in 411 AH, then left his house and settled in a dome by the gate of Cairo's al-Azhar mosque, devoting himself to copying, transcription and teaching, copying Euclid, the Almagest and the Middle Books and selling them for known prices by which he lived {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.22–24}.

In this Egyptian period, which falls before his death around 432 AH (1040–41 CE), he composed the Book of Optics; neither the book's text nor the record fixes its exact year of composition {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.9} {cite:tdv-ibnul-heysem biography}. The manuscripts show that the book continued to be copied for centuries after him: the earliest surviving parts were copied in 476 AH (1083 CE) at Basra by Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn Ja'far al-'Askari, ending with the note "in the hand of the author's son-in-law as a whole", and bearing a waqf for Sultan Mahmud al-Ghaznawi; and the only complete surviving copy today is the Ayasofya manuscript no. 2448, copied in 869 AH (1464–65 CE) for Sultan Mehmed II in thuluth script with gilded frames and headings and red figures {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.38–43}.

## The maker(s)

The whole book is Ibn al-Haytham's alone — Muhammad ibn al-Hasan ibn al-Haytham, known as Abu Ali al-Hasan — as the opening of the first book declares in its author's name "the shaykh Abu Ali al-Hasan ibn al-Hasan ibn al-Haytham on the Optics" after the basmala {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.60}. His Islam is documented on two grounds: that the book opens with its author's name and a basmala, and that the biographical notices record for him treatises answering those who attacked prophethood and the Mu'tazila; no source read attests a particular school of law for him, so the museum records him within the Sunni frame while noting that no text states a specific madhhab {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.60} {cite:tdv-ibnul-heysem biography} {cite:tdv-ibnul-heysem works}.

## How it works

The book works by a method it announces in its opening: it examines the existents, reviews the states of visible objects, distinguishes the properties of particulars, and gathers by induction what pertains to sight in the act of seeing — what is constant, unchanging and manifest, not doubtful — then ascends in enquiry and measure by degrees and order, with criticism of premises and restraint in conclusions, making its aim "the use of equity, not the following of desire" and the seeking of truth, not leaning with opinions {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.63}. The induction here is not mere collecting of examples but a regular examination of phenomena from which rules are graduated.

Before doing so it reviews the opinions of the ancients on how sight occurs, finding them contradictory and distant — among them the doctrine that the ray is a solid conjoined body, and that which sees it as fine straight lines diverging to the object, and that which sees air, when it touches sight, receive a quality and become a ray — then decides that the truth of this matter is "composed of the natural sciences and the mathematical sciences": natural because sight is one of the senses, and mathematical because sight perceives shape, position, magnitude, motion and rest and is specified by straight lines {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.61–62}. This formulation — the mixing of natural science with mathematics — is what the edition's editor makes the basis of Ibn al-Haytham's new manner, since the book regularly alternates between the description of logically ordered experiments and the application of mathematical meanings and principles to optical phenomena {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.9–10}.

The book gives an example of i'tibar in the second chapter of the first book: it states that every visible object is perceived by sight unless a dense barrier intervenes, and proposes to test this "with rulers and tubes" — taking a ruler of the utmost straightness and a hollow cylindrical tube of the finest roundness, neither wider than the eye's socket — to establish that perception falls along straight lines between the surface of sight and the surface of the object {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.65}. This kind of experiment controlled by instruments is what the edition's editor calls the experimental testing on which the whole study rests {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.9}.

## The spread

Not a single complete Arabic text of the book survives, but it reached many Arabic manuscripts: the Basran "military" copy of the first three books' parts, the Fatiḥ manuscript no. 3215 for the complementary volume, the only complete copy, Ayasofya 2448 of 869 AH, and the Ahmad III manuscript of 1899 copied in 915 AH (1509 CE), which records that it was transferred from the "military" copy {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.38–44}. All of them in the end go back to one original called the "military" copy, that is, the set of Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn Ja'far al-'Askari {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.43–44}.

Then came the greater transfer: the book was translated into Latin, and the edition's editor knows neither the translator nor the date nor the place of the translation for certain, supposing it was made in Spain at the beginning of the thirteenth century CE or the end of the twelfth {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.47}. The first mention of the translation in the Latin world occurs in Jordanus de Nemore's treatise On Triangles between 1220 and 1230 CE, and some twenty Latin manuscripts survive today, at least seven copied in the thirteenth century — among them the manuscript of the Royal Observatory library in Edinburgh copied in 1269 CE — and there is also a fourteenth-century Italian translation from the Latin preserved in a single Vatican manuscript {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.47–48}.

Within the Islamic world itself the book begot its most important scientific offspring: Kamal al-Din al-Farisi wrote the Tanqih al-Manazir at the direction and supervision of his teacher Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi; al-Farisi relates that he searched for the book in the libraries of Fars, then obtained it from the furthest lands and found in it "sound experiments and investigations freed by geometric and observational instruments", and finding the book too long beyond measure, his teacher ordered its abridgement {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.44–46}. The Tanqih was printed at Hyderabad in the Deccan in 1348 AH (1930 CE) in two volumes, and in it al-Farisi followed a manner distinguishing what he ascribed to Ibn al-Haytham by the word "he said" from what he ascribed to himself by "I say", and went beyond abridgement at times to completion, raising difficulties and objections {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.44–46}.

## What it was built on

The book rests on a long heritage of optics that preceded it: the first book's opening itself presents the opinions of the ancients — of the people of nature and of the people of mathematics — and their contradictions, on which Ibn al-Haytham builds a criticism, not a blind transmission {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.60–62}. The author himself declares at the end of the preface that he had before this book composed a treatise on optics in which he followed persuasive ways, and when he reached verified proofs for all the visual meanings he set about composing the Optics {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.64}. The basis on which the book was built thus includes the Greek heritage — Euclid, Ptolemy, Galen and Aristotle — and what accumulated in the Islamic world before him, and Ibn al-Haytham's own earlier experiment in the treatise that preceded it {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.9–10} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.64}.

## What was built on it

What was built on the book has two documented faces: Arabic and Latin. In Arabic, Kamal al-Din al-Farisi wrote the Tanqih al-Manazir, which became the channel carrying Ibn al-Haytham's experiments and investigations to those who came after, not the parent text alone {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.44–46}. In Latin, the Polish scholar Witelo followed the Optics' manner in his own book on optics, drawing its subjects and figures from it and even transferring its wording in many places, as the edition's editor notes {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.47}. After them came the line of European philosophers and scholars who took much from the second and third books — on the psychology of perception — since the editor calls the researches of those two books the most comprehensive and mature that has reached us in that subject from the ancient and medieval periods {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.10}.

## Impact on daily life

The Optics was not an instrument people used in their homes, but it was the foundation on which the science of optics was built in later centuries, and from it flowed into the scientific and intellectual products that changed scientific life in Europe. The book itself describes no everyday device nor instrument but a method of understanding; yet its enquiry into visible objects, instruments and experimental testing laid a basis after which it became impossible to speak of sight without an experimental reference {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.9–10} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.65}.

## How it changed history

The book changed the course of optics itself: before Ibn al-Haytham, the study of optics in the Greek world was a branch of mathematics whose aim was more philosophical than experimental; after him it became a science "composed of the natural sciences and the mathematical sciences", resting on ordered testing and mathematical proof together {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.9–10} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.61}. This transformation is what makes the edition's editor count the book as "the most remarkable product of Arab scholars in the field of physics" and as stretching historically between Ptolemy's book in the second century CE and the works of Kepler and Descartes in the early seventeenth century {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.9}. The Latin translation and Risner's printing of the book in 1572 alongside Witelo's, with comparison tables between the Arabic text and the Latin translation, completed this extension into Europe {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.10–11}.

## Who invented it?

The book is not a literal invention nor a claim of absolute priority; its author himself presents the opinions of the ancients, finds them divergent, and builds a new enquiry on them, claiming neither to be the first to say that light travels straight nor the first to use experiment in science {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.60–62}. The attribution here is sound and bounded: the author is one known figure, the book is ascribed to him in its opening and in every copy and catalogue, and the manuscript evidence confirms it was copied and read for centuries after him {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.60} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.38–44}. But the museum does not claim Ibn al-Haytham "invented the science of optics" or that the idea of incoming rays had never occurred to anyone before him; what he did was join experiment and proof in one disciplined structure across the three states of light {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.9–10}.

## Today

Today the Optics is studied in the history of scientific method and the history of optics as a model of joining controlled observation to mathematical proof, not as a text of anatomy or modern physics {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.9–11}. Its manuscripts, its Latin printings and its relation to al-Farisi's Tanqih remain subjects of research in the history of science, while this record establishes no direct line to contemporary optical instruments or today's laboratories {cite:tdv-ibnul-heysem works}.

## Surviving examples

The clearest witnesses are the Arabic copies: the three parts of the "military" copy dated 476 AH (1083 CE) at Basra, the only complete copy, Ayasofya 2448 dated 869 AH (1464–65 CE), and the Ahmad III manuscript of 1899 copied in 915 AH (1509 CE) {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.38–44}. In Latin there are some twenty manuscripts, at least seven of them from the thirteenth century, and Risner's printed edition of Basel 1572 which gathered the Latin translation with Witelo's book and a treatise on dawn and twilight {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.47–48} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.11}. In Arabic too there is Kamal al-Din al-Farisi's Tanqih al-Manazir printed at Hyderabad in 1348 AH (1930 CE), and a page of this Tanqih manuscript appears on Wikimedia Commons under an open licence {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.44}.

## In culture

The Optics did not remain a dry scientific text in memory; its manuscripts show how it was read, endowed and revised: in the Basran "military" copy there is a waqf for Sultan Mahmud al-Ghaznawi on the parts, and successive ownership notes and endowments for names across the centuries recur, meaning the book stayed in the treasuries of both power and learning {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.39–40}. In the complete Ayasofya copy the geometric figures were drawn in red and the headings in gold as the edition's editor describes, so the book was not merely research but material copied and illuminated {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.43}. In later times al-Farisi's Tanqih entered the making of Islamic optics' modernity before the Optics itself reappeared in Risner's printed editions {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.44–46}.

## For young readers

About a thousand years ago a Muslim scholar named Ibn al-Haytham gathered in the Book of Optics what the eye sees with what calculation proves, in seven books: three on vision on the straight line, and four on reflection and refraction. He did not content himself with watching what light does; he tested it with simple tools — a ruler and a tube — then proved his results with numbers and figures. The book reached Europe through a Latin translation and changed how scholars thought about light and sight, and it is still studied today as a model of science that begins with experiment {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp.9–10} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.63} {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p.65}.
