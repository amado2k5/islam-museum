## Summary
Da'irat al-mu'addil is a small astronomical instrument invented by Abd al-Aziz ibn Muhammad al-Wafa'i, the Egyptian astronomer and timekeeper of Cairo, in the ninth century AH. It combines on one brass disc a small sundial that measures time by its shadow and a magnetic compass surrounded by a wind-rose and the names of cities with lines marking their qibla directions, so the qibla of any city written on the rim can be read once the disc is aligned with north. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 25} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

The Bibliotheca Alexandrina catalogue describes his treatise on it as concerning the da'irat al-mu'addil he invented, a multi-purpose instrument that measures the hour-angle of celestial bodies and whose base carries qibla lines for various places. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 25}

The instrument was not an abstract theory; it kept being made nearly three centuries after its attribution, reached Ottoman circles where admiral Seydi Ali Reis described it, and examples like the Khalili Collection piece dated 1161 AH survive today. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description} {cite:khalili-collections}

## What it is
This is an "instrument" in the sense of a device: a flat brass disc carrying two measurements on one surface. At its centre sits a magnetic compass, around it a wind-rose with its names, and around the circumference the names of cities with lines indicating their qibla direction from that place. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

Its first function is computational astronomy: it measures the hour-angle of celestial bodies - how much of the day or night remains until a heavenly body reaches a given position - information a timekeeper needs for prayer times. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24}

Its second function is religious geography: it determines the qibla direction for any place named on its rim, by orienting the compass to north and then reading the line belonging to that city's name. Thus al-Wafa'i saved his user from carrying two separate devices. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

## The world before it
Before al-Wafa'i, a timekeeper carried separate tools: an upright or horizontal sundial for time, other direction-finding devices for the qibla, and sometimes an astrolabe or sine quadrant for harder astronomical problems. The idea of "one disc with several functions" was not entirely new, but combining exactly these functions on a single circle was a documented practical addition. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

Among the earlier instruments named in the literature is Ibn al-Shatir's "Sanduq al-Yawakit" (Jewel Box), a fourteenth-century CE Syrian astronomical timekeeping device that some researchers saw as the da'irat's inspiration. This acknowledgement of priority does not diminish al-Wafa'i's attribution; it places him in a sequence of instrument development. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

In Mamluk Cairo, timekeeping was an institutional function: whoever fixed prayer times in mosques and schools needed accuracy, reliability and speed, and carrying several instruments was a daily burden, not a cosmetic one. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

## The story
Abd al-Aziz ibn Muhammad al-Wafa'i was born in 811 AH and died in 879 AH, an Egyptian astronomer and timekeeper at Cairo's al-Mu'ayyad Mosque, with more than twenty treatises in astronomy and timekeeping. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24}

In this muwaqqit milieu he wrote his treatise on the da'irat al-mu'addil, describing the instrument he had devised: a brass disc combining compass, sundial and qibla lines, measuring the hour-angle of celestial bodies. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 25}

The technical description of surviving pieces clarifies the structure: a circular brass disc, a magnetic compass at its centre, a wind-rose with its names around it, and around the circumference the qibla names of cities. Below the south point sits a small sundial with a small gnomon, and holes on the disc mark where missing levelling screws and a graduated semi-circular arc once were; that arc was hinged along the east-west line and could be inclined at a set angle to the horizontal by a graduated scale in the meridian plane. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

One known piece is signed by its maker "Amal Hasan Baba al-Duyuwi" in 1171 AH (1757/58 CE), a name unknown to the literature, showing the instrument kept being made nearly three centuries after al-Wafa'i's death. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

## The maker(s)
Al-Wafa'i is the maker to whom the instrument is attributed in instrument literature and in the auction description. His role is "inventor": not merely an improver or describer, but the author of a new combination gathering two tasks on one disc, with a surviving treatise describing it. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24}

His Muslim identity is documented: he was born and worked in Mamluk Cairo as a mosque timekeeper, wrote in Arabic, and the single source read describes him in an Islamic scholarly context with no reference to any other tradition. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

No specific legal school is named for him in the sources read, so his Muslim identity is recorded under the default Sunni frame of Mamluk Cairo, not as a personal madhhab claim. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24}

## How it works
The disc works on two simultaneous principles. First, direction: the instrument is levelled by its levelling screws, then the compass is turned until its needle points north, so that each city's name on the rim carries a line pointing to its qibla from that place; the user reads the direction at a glance. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

Second, time: below the south point sits a small sundial with a gnomon that measures the Sun's hours by shadow, and the graduated semi-circular arc on the east-west line can be inclined at a set angle to suit the latitude, so the instrument works as a universal sundial. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24}

This structure makes the instrument a "da'irat al-mu'addil" in the technical sense: a circle that measures the equatorial hour-angle - the body's relative position on the equatorial circle - which is its name in instrument literature. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

## The spread
The instrument did not stay in Cairo; it crossed into Ottoman circles where admiral Seydi Ali Reis described it in his book, making it familiar in the maritime and administrative circles of the Ottoman world that needed timekeeping and directions. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

Surviving pieces prove the temporal spread: a Khalili Collection example dated 1161 AH/1748-49 CE, roughly three centuries after al-Wafa'i's death, and another piece signed 1171 AH/1757-58 CE by a maker unknown to the literature. {cite:khalili-collections} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

This spread was horizontal rather than revolutionary: copies did not transform into a new complex model but remained similar in structure, showing its design stabilised early and endured for centuries. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description} {cite:khalili-collections}

## What it was built on
The instrument was built on established traditions: compass science for finding north, the sundial for time, and qibla calculation lines produced by earlier timekeeping scholars, including what is attributed to Ibn al-Shatir's "Jewel Box". {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

The astrolabe and the sine quadrant were the two main astronomical instruments of al-Wafa'i's milieu; the source does not say the da'irat replaced them, but it was a dedicated instrument for timekeeping and direction, not a substitute for general astronomical computing devices. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

The documented innovation is not in any single part but in the assembly: one disc carrying compass, sundial and qibla lines, an arrangement not found in a single piece before al-Wafa'i according to the literature read. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

## What was built on it
Built directly on it: Ottoman admiral Seydi Ali Reis's description of a similar "da'ira-yi mu'addil", and its later copies in Egypt, Syria and the Ottoman world into the eighteenth century CE. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description} {cite:khalili-collections}

Built on it also: the concept of the "compound instrument" in timekeeping - the idea that one carried device performs several functions became a pattern in later timekeeping devices, down to modern electronics that gather compass, clocks and calendar in a single piece. {cite:khalili-collections} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

Its surviving treatise is what let modern literature know its structure: without al-Wafa'i's description, references could not have identified surviving pieces as "da'irat al-mu'addil" rather than anonymous sundial discs. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24}

## Impact on daily life
Its effect was immediate and daily: the timekeeper at the Mu'ayyad Mosque needed to fix prayer times and their direction, and the instrument saved him carrying a separate sundial, compass and qibla calculation papers. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

The traveller and merchant needed the direction to Mecca wherever they were, so carrying this instrument let them know their qibla without asking each city for its direction - a practical service for worship and geography together. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

In later Ottoman circles, similar pieces were used in varied contexts from timekeeping to administration, showing its design met a continuing need. {cite:khalili-collections} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

## How it changed history
The instrument did not change theoretical astronomy; what it changed was "instrument culture": it proved a timekeeper could gather several measurements into one compact device, a principle that would govern the design of later clocks and dials. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description} {cite:khalili-collections}

It is also an example of "horizontal transmission" of technology: from Cairo to Istanbul to museum collections, without becoming a central scientific instrument; it remained a daily service tool, which is itself a lesson in a kind of innovation. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description} {cite:khalili-collections}

In the history of timekeeping institutions, the instrument is recorded as one of the tools of the Mu'ayyad Mosque milieu, tying it to the history of religious education and time in Mamluk Cairo. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24}

## Who invented it?
The attributed answer in the literature: Abd al-Aziz al-Wafa'i, timekeeper of the Mu'ayyad Mosque. His attribution is "original" because the literature credits him by name, and the piece signed in 1171 AH proves the instrument was still being made nearly three centuries after his death. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24}

But precision requires noting that the documented stimulus is Ibn al-Shatir's "Jewel Box": the instrument may have been inspired by an earlier device, so al-Wafa'i did not invent the "idea of the compound disc" from nothing but improved and combined it in a distinctive design. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

His Muslim identity is fixed by his working context: a mosque timekeeper in Mamluk Cairo, writing in Arabic, with Arabic sources needing no further proof. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

## Today
Da'irat al-mu'addil is not made today; electronic watches, GPS devices and qibla apps have replaced it, gathering all these functions in a single phone. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description} {cite:khalili-collections}

But its historical value remains: examples in collections like Khalili tell how the timekeeper worked before electricity, and how compass, sundial and qibla lines were combined on one disc. {cite:khalili-collections} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

Reading it today is useful to instrument designers: its design is an early example of "combining on one disc" that would become a principle in modern timekeeping devices. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

## Surviving examples
The documented surviving copies are two: a piece in the Khalili Collection dated 1161 AH/1748-49 CE, and another piece described in the Oriental Art Auctions sale ART3004238, about 24 cm in diameter and signed by its maker "Amal Hasan Baba al-Duyuwi 1171" (1757/58 CE). {cite:khalili-collections} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

Auctioned pieces are sometimes incomplete: the graduated semi-circular arc and levelling screws are missing in the known example, making the auction's technical description important for understanding the full original structure. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

No surviving example from al-Wafa'i's own time (ninth century AH) is known in the sources read; the earliest known examples are late Ottoman, meaning the instrument lived centuries before its surviving copies were gathered. {cite:khalili-collections} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

## In culture
The da'irat did not become a popular icon, but it is an icon in the history of Islamic instruments: in catalogues and histories of timekeeping devices its name appears alongside astrolabes and quadrants. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

Its very name "da'irat al-mu'addil" tells its specialty: not merely a sundial but a circle measuring the "equatorial" hour-angle, a precise technical name reflecting its age's understanding of applied astronomy. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24}

In museum collections today these pieces are displayed as examples of "practical taste" in Islamic civilisation: small elegant instruments that cost little but performed precise daily services. {cite:khalili-collections} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

## For young readers
Imagine a brass disc the size of a plate: at its centre a needle like a compass point, and around it the names of cities like "Cairo" and "Damascus" with lines pointing to Mecca. Turn the disc until the needle agrees with north, and you instantly know which way to face the qibla. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}

And below the south point on the same disc sits a tiny sundial: a stick stands in the Sun and draws a line, telling you how many hours remain until sunset or noon. {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24}

This disc was invented by al-Wafa'i, an Egyptian scientist who fixed prayer times in a Cairo mosque. Instead of carrying two clocks, a Muslim carried one disc that knew both the time and the qibla. That is how Islamic instruments were small but ingenious. {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 24} {cite:oriental-art-auctions-dairat-muaddil technical description}
