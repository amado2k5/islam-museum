## Summary

In the thirtieth treatise of his "al-Tasrif li-man 'ajiza 'an al-ta'lif," al-Zahrawi (d. c. 1013 CE) assembled the largest known catalogue of surgical instruments of his heritage: about two hundred instruments named and many of them drawn — scalpels and hooks, cautery irons, dental forceps, arrow removers, and instruments for crushing and drilling stone. The instruments were not a bare list: he described how each was used, when to use it, and its dangers, making the book the reference of surgeons for centuries in al-Andalus and then in Latin Europe. This record documents the "surgical-instrument system" as an institutional invention in the history of surgery, not any single tool {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi} {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## What it is

The whole body of instruments al-Zahrawi described and drew in al-Tasrif: cutting and incision tools, fire cauteries, dental extraction instruments, arrow removers, crushing and drilling instruments, and surgical sutures including the catgut he specified. In the manuscript tradition the drawings are counted the first large illustrated corpus of surgical instruments in medical literature {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## The world before it

Before al-Zahrawi, surgical knowledge lay scattered: translated Greek texts (Galen, Paul of Aegina) naming tools without organised illustration, and craft practice passed down orally. No known Arabic book had put surgery's manual dimension — its instruments and their pictures — into a single dedicated treatise. Al-Zahrawi himself said surgery "had almost died out" in his time for want of practitioners, and set out to revive it through teaching {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi}.

## The story

Al-Zahrawi worked in Cordoba at the court of Abd al-Rahman III and al-Hakam II. Aiming al-Tasrif at the practitioner "unable to compile" his own material, he made its last treatise the surgical one, in three books: cautery, then operations (incision, bloodletting, extraction), then the setting of fractures and dislocations. He drew the instruments and showed the surgeon's hand position and the site of each cut — the first known illustrated surgical book in the historians' reading {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## The maker(s)

Abu al-Qasim Khalaf ibn Abbas al-Zahrawi (c. 936–1013 CE), Cordoba's physician and surgeon, recorded as Muslim on his person record. Historians credit him specifically with devising or describing some instruments — the urethral drill for stone among them — but most of the corpus is collection, improvement and illustration, not invention from nothing; this record documents the system rather than one tool.

## How it works

The method works on three levels: illustration (drawing the tool so the learner cannot mistake it), functional description (when and how each tool is used), and clinical warning (the risks of each intervention and when to abstain). The drawing itself was a teaching technology: he pictured the incision blade, its length and grip, so the book could teach surgery in the teacher's absence {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## The spread

Al-Tasrif travelled from Cordoba to Europe through the Toledo Latin translations of the twelfth century and became the basis of surgical teaching in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, influencing Guy de Chauliac and his peers. The instrument drawings recurred in Latin manuscripts {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861}.

## What was built on it

Later surgical books were built on it: the medieval European manuals that repeat its instrument drawings and descriptions, and the classification of tools into cutting, cautery and extraction that stayed a pattern in surgical writing.

## What it was built on

It was built over the translated Greek heritage — above all Paul of Aegina — and over local craft practice in surgery and cupping; al-Zahrawi gathered, organised and illustrated, and acknowledged his predecessors {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi}.

## Impact on daily life

It lowered surgery from a hidden craft to a written, teachable discipline; it specified each intervention's tools precisely for the first time, so the patient depended less on one surgeon's manual genius and teaching became a curriculum.

## How it changed history

It changed surgery's course in both civilisations: in Islam al-Tasrif remained the reference; in Europe its Latin transmission grounded university surgery. Historians of medicine call al-Zahrawi the father of surgery in the sense that his instrumental-teaching system founded the field {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973}.

## Who invented it?

The question has two levels. Who invented each instrument? Unsettled — most instruments existed earlier or were improved by him. Who invented the system — an illustrated surgical book joining tools, function and warning? Here the evidence in the sources read assigns priority to al-Zahrawi {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi}.

## Today

Modern surgical instruments — from scalpels to endoscopes — do not descend line by line from al-Zahrawi's, but his book's principle stands: define the instrument, draw it, state its use and its danger.

## Surviving examples

Later manuscripts of al-Tasrif carry the instrument drawings (as the illustrated copies in world libraries), and the printed Latin translations (Leclerc 1861) fix his text {cite:commons-zahrawi-instruments}.

## In culture

Al-Zahrawi stands in popular memory as "the father of surgery"; the manuscript pictures of his instruments recur as emblems of the Islamic scientific flowering.

## For young readers

Imagine a surgeon a thousand years ago: how did he learn his craft? Al-Zahrawi said: draw the tool as it is, write how to hold it and when to use it, and what happens if you err. His book became a school for surgeons for hundreds of years.
