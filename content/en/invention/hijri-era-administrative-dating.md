## Summary
This is not the invention of the Moon or the names of the months, but the adoption of a shared reference for numbering years in an expanding administration: historical reference accounts associate official Hijra-era dating with the caliphate of [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar ibn al-Khattab]], retaining consultation and disagreement about its adoption year {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. Sahl ibn Sad's report in Bukhari distinguishes reckoning from the Prophet's arrival in Medina from reckoning from his mission or death; it identifies a reference, not the complete minutes of an administrative meeting {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 3934}. The contribution is institutional formalization within an older history of calendars, not ownership of all knowledge about time {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}.

## What it is
A written date answers different questions: which day within a month, which month within a year, and which year within a numbered sequence? Changing the reference for the last question does not necessarily invent new answers to all the others; this teaching distinction helps explain the calendar and its era {cite:tdv-takvim-akgur lunar-calendar} {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. The lunar Hijri year has twelve months; observational traditions connect their beginnings with sighting, while year numbering refers back to the migration year {cite:britannica-islamic-calendar} {cite:tdv-takvim-akgur lunar-calendar}. Here “formalization” means the shared administrative reference, not a decree specifying every calculation used today {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}.

## The world before it
The early Islamic administration entered a world of calendars, month reckoning and eras associated with rulers or other beginnings; Arabic lunar months were not created by Umar's policy {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. The expanding state encountered different regional administrative traditions, so choosing a dating reference did not require all local practices and fiscal years to disappear immediately {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. Acknowledging these predecessors locates the contribution more precisely: institutional coordination can be important without claiming that the institution discovered the Moon, the month or the very possibility of keeping records {cite:tdv-takvim-akgur lunar-calendar} {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}.

## The story
Abdollahy reports traditional adoption years16,17 or18AH, paired with637–639CE; Britannica selects639CE {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri} {cite:britannica-islamic-calendar}. This record does not convert disagreement into an invented decision day or silently print one account as universal agreement {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. Abdollahy's account includes Ali proposing the migration as the reference and Umar preferring Muharram as the beginning of the year; these are reported historical narratives, not a contemporary consultation transcript we have independently inspected {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. The existing [[event/umar-adopts-hijri-calendar|adoption]] and [[event/hijri-calendar-decree|calendar decision]] pages provide paths into recorded traditions rather than permission to combine their different details without evidence.

## How it works
**The numbering reference.** Two groups can agree on month names but disagree on a year number if they count from different events. This is a modern explanatory example of an era, not a newly discovered ancient letter. Fixing the migration reference makes year numbers comparable within the system, while the month, day and method of establishing them still need to be known {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}.

**Year beginning is not event day.** Abdollahy distinguishes a Muharram year beginning from the actual migration in Rabi I: the system does not assert that the journey happened on1Muharram {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. Nor is622CE the year of the later administrative decision: one is a retrospective epoch and the other is adoption in the historical accounts {cite:britannica-islamic-calendar}. A community can select an earlier event for subsequent numbering without that choice proving that a founding document survives from the event's own year.

**Month and sighting.** Akgur explains a synodic period of about29.53days, which cannot make every month the same whole number of days; the described calculations include years of354 or355days {cite:tdv-takvim-akgur lunar-calendar}. An average does not establish that a crescent was seen at a particular place, nor eliminate differences of visibility and locality {cite:tdv-takvim-akgur lunar-calendar}. Deciding the reference for numbered years therefore does not automatically settle every problem of month opening.

**Later arithmetic.** Britannica describes a tabular representation with eleven leap years in thirty; Abdollahy describes variation in the placement of intercalary days among calculated schemes {cite:britannica-islamic-calendar} {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. In a modern arithmetic illustration, thirty354day years plus11days total10631days, averaging approximately354.3667; this explains that numerical representation, not an algorithm quoted from Umar's decree {cite:britannica-islamic-calendar}. A good average does not turn a particular table into an eyewitness record of every locality's practice.

## What it was built on
The institutional arrangement used older months, a remembered communal event and administrative practices of ordering information; it did not discover the month's length afresh {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri} {cite:tdv-takvim-akgur lunar-calendar}. Sahl's report is useful precisely because it distinguishes possible reference events, but it does not describe every fiscal alternative or give the complete correspondence {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 3934}. Abdollahy also reports earlier dated letters: their discussion leaves room for development rather than creation from nothing, without implying that this museum has authenticated every original letter or the histories reporting it {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}.

## What was built on it
A lasting dating tradition can retain an era while changing its means of calculating or establishing months; observational practice, astronomical tables and modern software therefore should not be collapsed into one historical stage {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri} {cite:tdv-takvim-akgur lunar-calendar}. Abdollahy also discusses Solar Hijri calendars, but sharing “Hijri” does not turn a solar year into twelve lunar months {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy}. Separating epoch from year structure preserves both the related name and the substantive difference, rather than assigning much later reforms to Umar merely because they invoke the same migration memory.

## Impact on daily life
A shared era helps order documents across changes of officeholders, an institutional benefit that explains why a history of inventions includes conventions as well as metal machines {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. The lunar year is shorter than the solar year, however, so its months move through seasons rather than maintaining a fixed harvest position; Abdollahy discusses resulting fiscal difficulties {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. Choosing the migration reference was not a magical reconciliation of devotional time, harvests and every financial obligation in one permanent season {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. This exhibition supplies neither a present-day religious ruling nor a binding month-opening date for any country.

## How it changed history
Here the innovation is a shared rule for interpreting a year number, capable of outlasting the ruler whose administration is credited with its adoption {cite:britannica-umar-i} {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. The papyrus connects institutional memory with writing materials and preservation while keeping evidence of use distinct from an account of foundation {cite:commons-perf-558-recto} {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. It does not show that Hijri dating alone caused imperial expansion, or that every later dated document copied a lost instruction; neither causal story is established by the sources actually examined.

## Who invented it?
The precise answer is that reference works attribute administrative formalization to Umar's caliphate, retaining consultation and Ali's reported contribution within older traditions of months and reckoning {cite:britannica-umar-i} {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. These witnesses do not answer who invented “the calendar” in the absolute {cite:tdv-takvim-akgur lunar-calendar}. Adoption remains variously16,17 or18AH, not a unique date derivable by averaging the year's length {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}. Britannica gives16July622Julian for the epoch, while Abdollahy gives15July: this attributed difference remains visible rather than being silently settled by an automatic conversion or an unread crescent report {cite:britannica-islamic-calendar} {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy lunar-Hejri}.

## Today
Lunar Hijri dating remains alive in religious, cultural and administrative uses with different observational and computational forms; “Islamic calendar” is not a guarantee of one identical table everywhere {cite:britannica-islamic-calendar} {cite:tdv-takvim-akgur lunar-calendar}. Reading an old date requires distinguishing the writer's wording from a modern editor's calculation, and year precision from a known day; Akgur also discusses documentary expressions for the beginning, middle and end of months {cite:tdv-takvim-akgur lunar-calendar}. Recognizing the limits of precision is part of understanding the object, not a defect to conceal beneath an unsupported exact civil date.
