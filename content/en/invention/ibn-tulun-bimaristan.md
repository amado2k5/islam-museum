## Summary
[[person/ahmad-ibn-tulun|Ahmad ibn Tulun]] established a hospital in the [[place/al-fustat|Fustat]] area, endowed it with properties and set conditions for admission, care and discharge: this is the account [[person/al-maqrizi|al-Maqrizi]] quotes from the compiler of the Tulunid biography {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. The administration kept a patient's clothes and money safe, supplied hospital dress, bedding, medicines and food until recovery, and returned belongings on discharge {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. Dates differ: al-Maqrizi reports 259 AH from al-Kindi and 261 AH from the biography, while TDV gives 872 CE {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337} {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. The browser records institutional development in a particular example, not the invention of hospitals or priority for every procedure.

## What it is
This was a hospital with conditions, endowed properties and an administration, not a room in the ruler's palace. The account says Ibn Tulun endowed it with the diwan house, houses in the shoemakers' quarter, a qaysariyya and the slave market, and excluded soldiers and mamluks from treatment {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. “Civilian” distinguishes its admission remit from the army, rather than claiming unrestricted access for every person.

He also built two bathhouses, one for men and one for women, endowing them for the hospital and other beneficiaries {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. Two bathhouses do not establish the number or arrangement of hospital wards: the passage read gives no such plan.

## The world before it
Hospitals predated Ibn Tulun. TDV's institutional history describes [[invention/rufayda-medical-tent|Rufayda's tent]], [[invention/walid-bimaristan|al-Walid's foundation]] and [[invention/harun-bimaristan|Harun's hospital]] on the Jundishapur model {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. In Fustat itself it names a foundation by Fath ibn Khaqan in 861, before the date it gives for Ibn Tulun {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}.

The compiler of the Tulunid biography, as quoted by al-Maqrizi, nevertheless says Egypt had no earlier maristan {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. Neither account is deleted to manufacture a “first”: the priority claim remains attributed, while this record follows the specifically documented organization.

## The story
Ibn Tulun arrived in Egypt in 868 as Bayakbak's deputy and later established al-Qatai northeast of Fustat as a new centre, with a mosque, palace and hospital among its buildings, according to his TDV biography {cite:tdv-ahmed-b-tolun pp. 141–143}. The hospital account belongs to this programme of urban building.

Al-Maqrizi's page begins by quoting Abu Umar al-Kindi's Book of Governors: Ibn Tulun ordered a hospital for the sick, built in 259 AH. He then quotes the biographical compiler's date of 261 AH and the endowment of properties after completion {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. The record does not automatically turn the difference into construction-start and opening dates: that is not what the text says.

Al-Maqrizi also mentions 262 AH in an account of endowments for the hospital, a water installation and a mosque on the hill. He gives expenditure on the hospital and its income-producing property as sixty thousand dinars {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. This is expenditure on an institution and its endowment estate in the account, not an annual budget or a sum paid to each patient.

## The maker(s)
Ibn Tulun is the commissioner, endower and setter of the reported conditions. His TDV biography describes his study of hadith and Hanafi jurisprudence at Samarra, further learning and military experience at Tarsus, and arrival in Egypt and foundation of al-Qatai {cite:tdv-ahmed-b-tolun pp. 141–143}. His religious affiliation is recorded on that biographical basis, with the museum's Sunni default frame explicitly stated, not inferred from his name or Turkish origin.

Al-Maqrizi refers to physicians, the custodian and attendants by their functions, without naming individual staff in this account {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. The ruler is therefore not credited with diagnosis, preparing medicines or the builders' work, and unnamed physicians are not supplied with invented names.

## How it works
Al-Maqrizi's quotation sets out a clear patient journey {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}:

1. **Admission and safekeeping.** The patient's clothes were removed and kept, with their money, by the hospital custodian.
2. **The stay.** The patient received clothing and bedding, rather than having their family alone arrange them.
3. **Continuing care.** Medicines, food and physicians attended the patient morning and evening until recovery.
4. **Discharge.** When the patient could eat a chicken and a loaf, discharge was ordered and money and clothes returned.

These are historical conditions reported by a source, not present-day treatment instructions. The account combines household and financial organization with treatment, but does not identify medicines or methods for measuring their effectiveness {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. The diagram links [[invention/waqf|waqf]] to the institution; it does not reconstruct its rooms.

## The spread
The passage does not trace these procedures to a particular successor institution. After this account al-Maqrizi names Kafur's hospital in Egypt in 346 AH; TDV gives that foundation a date of 957 CE {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337} {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. A succession of hospitals in one city does not itself prove the copying of admission and discharge rules, so the data does not draw a documented transfer arrow between them.

The article keeps the example within the wider bimaristan history TDV describes, in Egypt, Baghdad, Damascus and elsewhere, without making Ibn Tulun the originator of every detail in that history {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}.

## What it was built on
The explicit financial basis is endowment: properties dedicated to the hospital's service and two bathhouses whose benefits were assigned to it and others {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. The inventions chain therefore links this example to [[invention/waqf|waqf]]. This concerns use of a legal and financial form, not a claim that Umar's land at Khaybar financed an Egyptian hospital.

Earlier hospitals remain historical context, not a particular plan proven to have been copied. Neither al-Maqrizi's two pages nor the encyclopedia passage read names a drawing brought from Baghdad to construct this institution {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan pp. 2336–2337} {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}.

## What was built on it
The sources read do not name a modern hospital receiving every procedure from this maristan along a documented chain. TDV compares its patient care and services, apart from technical capabilities, to those of modern hospitals {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. This is the encyclopedia author's comparison of features, not proof of direct transmission for each procedure.

The direct dependents field therefore remains empty, while the story presents what can be verified: the endowed property, the patient's journey and administration {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}.

## Impact on daily life
The most immediate patient benefit in the account is safekeeping of clothes and money during a stay, with dress, bedding, food and medicine becoming part of the institution's provision until recovery {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. TDV says rich and poor were accepted, except for slaves and soldiers {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. The exception is not hidden behind “care for everyone”.

Al-Maqrizi includes people with mental illness among those the ruler inspected and describes one patient as restrained in an anecdote {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. This is an account of care and confinement in its period, not a claim that the institution's practices met today's medical ethics.

## How it changed history
TDV treats the hospital as an important example in hospital and psychiatric history because its description reveals organized care beyond sheltering the sick {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. Al-Maqrizi adds oversight: Ibn Tulun rode out every Friday to inspect the stores and their contents, physicians and patients {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}.

The same narrative ends those visits after an incident with a patient: weekly inspection is not turned into a practice that lasted for the institution's entire life {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. The pages give no mortality or length-of-stay statistics with which to measure treatment outcomes.

## Who invented it?
**The Sunni historical source.** Al-Maqrizi preserves al-Kindi's and the biographical compiler's differing dates of 259 and 261 AH, and attributes the claim that Egypt had no earlier hospital to the latter {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}.

**The academic encyclopedia.** TDV gives 872 CE and compares its services to modern hospitals, but also mentions Fath ibn Khaqan's Fustat institution of 861 {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}.

The museum records institutional development in this example, without deciding “Egypt's first hospital”. The uncertainty concerns dates and priority, not the commissioner's name. Attribution therefore remains “improvement”, with those limits visible in its basis and article rather than adopting the disputed priority claim.

## Today
Al-Maqrizi says the hospital had disappeared without a surviving trace. He locates it in al-Askar by reference to Ibn Tulun's mosque, Kom al-Jarih, the Sadd bridge and the wall separating the cemetery {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2336}. Those landmarks alone do not supply precise modern coordinates.

The surviving [[building/masjid-ibn-tulun|Mosque of Ibn Tulun]] is another building from the same patron's programme; TDV's biography lists it and the hospital among the foundations of al-Qatai {cite:tdv-ahmed-b-tolun pp. 141–143}. This page's photograph shows the mosque, not hospital remains.

## Surviving examples
The witness directly read is the pair of pages in the Madbuli edition of al-Khitat: scan pp. 2336–2337, corresponding to printed pp. 545–546 in this part. Both images were checked for dates, endowments, admission conditions and expenditure {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan pp. 2336–2337}.

The surviving mosque's photograph is not presented as the hospital plan: it illustrates Ibn Tulun's building context, not treatment rooms that al-Maqrizi says had vanished {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2336}.

## In culture
Al-Maqrizi relates that a patient told the ruler he was not mentally ill but had contrived to obtain a pomegranate from Arish. The ruler ordered one brought; the patient then threw it at his chest, and the account says his inspections stopped afterwards {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. It remains a source's anecdote, not a clinical record from which to diagnose a person retrospectively.

TDV's summary turns the fruit into an apple, whereas the Arabic image read says “a pomegranate from Arish” {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri} {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. This small difference is retained because tracing a story includes its details, rather than choosing a fruit from memory.

## For young readers
Imagine arriving sick at Ibn Tulun's hospital. The historian says someone kept your money and clothes safe, then you received hospital clothing, bedding, food and medicine. When you recovered and could eat, your belongings were returned {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}. Endowed properties helped support the hospital, but it was not open to everyone: the rules excluded soldiers and mamluks {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 scan p. 2337}.
