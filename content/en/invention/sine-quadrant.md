## Summary
The sine quadrant — Arabic rub mujayyab, "the quarter marked with sine" — is a wooden or brass quarter-circle that measures the altitude of the sun and celestial bodies, tells time and direction, and performs trigonometric computation with a sliding cord on a sine grid. Al-Khwarizmi described it in ninth-century Baghdad, and it served prayer-timekeeping across the Islamic world for a thousand years {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en} {cite:king-islamic-astronomy-1999}.

## What it is
A quarter-circle arc divided into ninety degrees numbered both ways; two straight sides divided into sixty sexagesimal units crossed by the "sixty" and "extended sine" grid lines; a weighted plumb cord (khait) with a sliding bead through a hole at the apex; and two pinhole sighting plates (hadafatani) {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en}.

## The world before it
Altitude was measured on a plain graduated quadrant and computed from paper sine tables or the astrolabe's projection — three tools for three jobs, which the sine quadrant united {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en}.

## The story
The earliest named witness is al-Khwarizmi's description of the instrument in ninth-century Baghdad; it then became the muwaqqit's everyday instrument in mosques and observatories until Ibn al-Shatir at fourteenth-century Damascus united the quadrant family in his complete quadrant {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en} {cite:king-ibn-shatir-bea}.

## The maker(s)
A communal instrument with no single inventor: al-Khwarizmi is the first named witness, and generations of muwaqqits and instrument makers refined it — Ibn al-Shatir bringing the type to completion {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en} {cite:king-ibn-shatir-bea}.

## How it works
Aim the two pinholes so sunlight passes through onto a finger, and the plumb cord reads the sun's altitude on the arc; for calculation slide the bead along the sixty-grid to read sine, cosine and shadow visually, with no paper table {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en}.

## The spread
The instrument spread from Iraq to Egypt, Syria and al-Andalus, in a hollowed maritime form to spare the hand from wind, down to Ottoman wooden and brass specimens of the nineteenth century {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en}.

## What it was built on
Two inheritances: the old graduated altitude quadrant, and the Indian sine tables that arrived with the Sindhind material — the instrument is a sine table engraved on metal {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en}.

## What was built on it
Its culmination is Ibn al-Shatir's complete quadrant, joining the functions of the altitude, simplified and sine quadrants; its graphical idea survives in teaching instruments {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en} {cite:king-ibn-shatir-bea}.

## Impact on daily life
It put prayer-timekeeping in the muwaqqit's hand: noon and afternoon solar altitudes read in a moment, computed on the grid rather than looked up in tables {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en} {cite:king-islamic-astronomy-1999}.

## How it changed history
It moved trigonometry from the table page onto brass — a pocket analogue computer that accompanied practical Islamic astronomy {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en}.

## Who invented it?
No named inventor is known; the earliest witness is al-Khwarizmi's Baghdad description, so it most plausibly arose among ninth-century Baghdad astronomers {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en} {cite:king-islamic-astronomy-1999}.

## Today
Specimens are preserved in instrument museums, and astrolabe hobbyists rebuild them and read their grids {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en}.

## Surviving examples
Ottoman wooden and brass pieces in collections — among them an 1840 Iznik piece with an astrolabe quadrant on its reverse {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en}.

## In culture
The rub mujayyab became a standard heading in timekeeping and instrument books, an emblem of applied Islamic astronomy {cite:wikipedia-sine-quadrant-en}.

## For young readers
A wooden quarter-disc with a hanging string: point it at the sun and read where the string falls — that is the sun's height, and that is how Muslims found prayer times a thousand years ago.
