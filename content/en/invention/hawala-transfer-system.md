## Summary
Hawala is the art of moving money without moving it. A debtor — or a treasury — instructs a third party who owes him to pay his creditor instead: value settles by instruction and trust, and the coin never travels. Documented already in Abbasid finance, the hawala ran the fiscal systems of the Abbasids, Seljuks, Ilkhanids and Ottomans, stands as a contract with its own chapter in Islamic law, and survives today as the informal remittance network of migrant workers across Asia and Africa {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-hawala-en}.

## What it is
Two things under one word. In Islamic law, hawala is the contract by which a debtor transfers his obligation to a third party — a legal assignment of debt. In finance it is the instrument built on that contract: an order for payment drawn on a fund held elsewhere — the suftaja and sakk mandates of the Abbasid administration, the assignations on a tax-farm that paid stipends, bought public works and moved provincial revenue to the capital {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The world before it
Cash had to travel physically: coins in saddlebags, bullion under guard, exposed to robbery and the slowness of revenue arriving piecemeal from the provinces. Settling a debt across a thousand miles meant risking the road — or not settling it at all {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The story
EI2 finds hawala "already established in these senses in Abbasid finance": the tax-collectors of Ahwaz, Fars and Isfahan transmitted their revenues to the centre by suftaja mandates, with the jahbadh — the state banker — managing encashment. Seljuk administration used it widely. The Ilkhanid registers show the machinery in detail: assignations recorded monthly under mukarrariyya and iflaqiyya headings, paid to judges, scholars, soldiers and public works, and abused badly enough that Ghazan Khan tried to rebuild revenue collection around it. Ottoman finance ran on muqata'a and hawala for five centuries — barats and yaftajas on tax-farms paid soldiers' salaries and state purchases — until the Tanzimat abolished tax-farming in 1839. Alongside all of it, fiqh kept its own "Book of Hawala" governing the private contract {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The maker(s)
A contract and a fiscal technique, not a person: drafted by the Abbasid chancery and the fuqaha together, operated by the jahbadh bankers, the sarrafs of the Ottoman towns and today's hawaladars {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it works
Three parties: the transferor owes a debt, the transferee accepts it, the beneficiary is paid. In the medieval treasury version, a barat on a provincial tax-farmer told him to pay the named claimant out of his farm's yield; the farmer later submitted his honoured barats for audit and received a clearance. In the private version, two hawaladars settle a migrant's remittance at both ends and square accounts later — no money crosses a border {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-hawala-en}.

## The spread
From Abbasid fiscal practice into the mainstream of Islamic state finance — Seljuk, Ilkhanid, Ottoman — and into private commerce, where its sibling instruments (the suftaja letter and the sakk payment order, ancestor of the cheque) travelled the trade routes. In the modern era the name lives on in the informal broker networks of West Asia, North Africa, the Horn of Africa and the subcontinent {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-hawala-en}.

## What it was built on
The legal transferability of obligation — a debtor's power to substitute a solvent third party — and the documentary habits of the diwans: registers, mandates, audits and the trust between bankers at each end of a route {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What was built on it
The cheque's ancestor the sakk belongs to the same family; Ottoman sarrafs later moved tax-farm revenue by bill of exchange (police); and every modern regulated remittance service — two ends, netting without physical transfer — repeats the hawala's logic inside the banks {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-hawala-en}.

## Impact on daily life
It paid soldiers without coin convoys, let a merchant in Basra draw on a banker in Baghdad, and let a pilgrim or trader travel light. Today it lets a worker in the Gulf send wages home to a village with no bank — fast, cheap and, where unregulated, also open to misuse {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-hawala-en}.

## How it changed history
It gave Islamic states a fiscal spine that ran on paper and credit rather than bullion, and it gave Islamic law a sophisticated doctrine of debt transfer that scholars have compared to Europe's agency and aval — whether it shaped them remains argued, not proven {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-hawala-en}.

## Who invented it?
Contested at the roots. The medieval hawala is documented first and richest in Islamic sources — Abbasid, Seljuk, Ilkhanid. The modern informal system's deeper origin is disputed between the Islamic legal contract (Schramm and Taube document hawala back to 1327 inside Islamic legal theory) and India's hundi tradition, which a US FinCEN report names as the birthplace. Both lineages are honestly stated here {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:schramm-taube-hawala-2003} {cite:wikipedia-hawala-en}.

## Today
Hawala operates worldwide — parallel to banks, often cheaper than them — and is regulated or licensed in many jurisdictions; where it slips outside oversight it can also be abused, which is why regulators track it without being able to replace it {cite:wikipedia-hawala-en}.

## Surviving examples
The practice itself is the specimen: Ottoman muqata'a registers in the archives preserve the machinery, the fiqh books preserve the contract, and the living hawaladar counters of the Gulf and South Asia run it every day {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-hawala-en}.

## In culture
Hawala carries an honour meaning — "transfer" in the sense of entrusted debt — and in South Asian commerce the hundi remains a byword for a promissory economy run on reputation {cite:wikipedia-hawala-en}.

## For young readers
How do you send money to a faraway city without a single coin travelling? You give your money to a broker here; his trusted friend in the other city pays your family from his own pocket; the two settle up later. The money moved on trust, not on a horse — and people have been doing it for more than a thousand years.
