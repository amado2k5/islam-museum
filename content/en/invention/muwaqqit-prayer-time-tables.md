## Summary
The muwaqqit — "the timekeeper" — was a professional astronomer employed by a great mosque to compute the five daily prayer times and publish them as ready-made tables for his city. The legal hours are astronomical events: the sun's decline at noon, the lengthening afternoon shadow, the fading of twilight — and they shift with season and latitude, so someone had to do spherical astronomy as a public service. The office is first documented in thirteenth-century Mamluk Egypt, flowered in Damascus under Ibn al-Shatir and al-Khalili, and lasted into the Ottoman muvakkithanes — the direct ancestor of every printed timetable and prayer app today {cite:wikipedia-muwaqqit-en} {cite:king-synchrony-heavens-2004} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it is
An office, a discipline and a product. The discipline is 'ilm al-miqat, the science of the appointed times, which computes from the sun's daily geometry. The office is the salaried post inside the mosque — modest wages, EI2's records suggest, alongside the muezzins. The product is the timetable: for each locality, the first and last legal limits of zuhr, 'asr, maghrib, 'isha' and fajr laid out for every day of the year, plus the tables that found the qibla from anywhere {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-muwaqqit-en}.

## The world before it
Mosques ran on the muezzin's eye: shadow lengths for the day prayers, twilight phenomena for the night ones, lunar stations for general night timekeeping — serviceable tradition, but each city's muezzin gauged it locally, and the legal definitions were already precise enough to demand better {cite:wikipedia-muwaqqit-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The story
The law books had fixed the five prayer windows early — zuhr from the sun's decline to a shadow equal to the object, maghrib from sunset to the red twilight's end, fajr from dawn to sunrise. The astronomers could compute these; what remained was to institutionalise the computation. By the mid-thirteenth century it had happened: the first muwaqqit known by name, Abu l-Hasan 'Ali ibn 'Abd al-Malik ibn Sim'un, served the Mosque of Amr in Fustat for thirty years and died in 685/1286-7, followed by his son and grandson. Under other titles the same work ran in the west — the Ibn Baso family computed prayer times for Granada's great mosque in the late thirteenth century, and al-Qarawiyyin employed the astronomer al-Sanhaji as mu'addil. Damascus then made the office glorious: Ibn al-Sarraj built instruments at Aleppo, Ibn al-Shatir led the Umayyad Mosque's team of muwaqqits and produced his zijes, and al-Khalili wrote the Damascus prayer timetables and universal qibla tables. Not everyone approved — the qadi Taj al-Din al-Subki complained the muwaqqits' ranks were full of astrologers — but by 1400 the office stood in Egypt, Syria, Palestine, the Hejaz, Tunis and Yemen, and the Ottomans later built it houses of its own {cite:wikipedia-muwaqqit-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:king-synchrony-heavens-2004}.

## The maker(s)
A professional class rather than an inventor — and some of its members were astronomers of the first rank. Ibn al-Shatir of the Umayyad Mosque produced planetary models mathematically identical to ones later proposed by Copernicus; his colleague al-Khalili wrote the finest known prayer timetables for a single city; in Cairo the fifteenth-century Azhar muwaqqit Sibt al-Maridini wrote the simpler manuals that outcompeted the advanced texts {cite:wikipedia-muwaqqit-en}.

## How it works
Define the legal moments astronomically — solar meridian for zuhr, shadow-length thresholds for 'asr, sunset for maghrib, twilight depression angles for 'isha' and fajr — then compute them day by day for the city's latitude from a zij's solar tables, and publish the result as a timetable the muezzin can read without any astronomy at all {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:king-synchrony-heavens-2004}.

## The spread
Mamluk Egypt and Syria first, with parallels in Granada and Fez; by the fifteenth century Cairo's al-Azhar was the centre; under the Ottomans the muvakkithane — a purpose-built "timekeeper's lodge" — became a pious endowment peaking in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and muwaqqits became the keepers of the new mechanical clocks too. King notes no evidence of the office further east than Syria and Egypt {cite:wikipedia-muwaqqit-en}.

## What it was built on
'Ilm al-miqat itself is older — Abbasid astronomers such as Ibn Yunus had computed prayer times and universal auxiliary tables centuries before. The muezzin's empirical craft supplied the human frame; what the muwaqqit added was permanence, salary and the published table {cite:king-synchrony-heavens-2004} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What was built on it
The printed annual timetable and the Ramadan imsakiyya, the mosque's electronic clock, and every prayer-time application — all of which do the muwaqqit's job for a village as easily as for a capital {cite:wikipedia-muwaqqit-en}.

## Impact on daily life
It took a daily uncertainty — has the shadow reached the 'asr length? has the twilight gone? — and made it a printed certainty for a whole city, synchronising five pauses a day across thousands of households {cite:wikipedia-muwaqqit-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it changed history
It is the medieval world's most sustained public application of mathematical astronomy: for six centuries, salaried specialists turned the science of the zijes into a civic utility, and the greatest of them did world-class astronomy between prayer times {cite:wikipedia-muwaqqit-en} {cite:king-synchrony-heavens-2004}.

## Who invented it?
The office has no inventor — it crystallised where mosque, muezzin and astronomer met, first documented in thirteenth-century Mamluk Egypt with analogues under different titles in the Islamic west. Its deeper root, 'ilm al-miqat, belongs to the Abbasid astronomers centuries earlier {cite:wikipedia-muwaqqit-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Today
Official religious agencies and observatories issue the tables now, rendered by apps and the mosque's electronic clock; Istanbul's surviving muvakkithanes stand as small elegant reminders that once a man with an astrolabe kept the city's sacred time {cite:wikipedia-muwaqqit-en}.

## Surviving examples
The manuscripts of the timetables themselves — al-Khalili's Damascus corpus above all — the surviving Ottoman muvakkithanes attached to Istanbul mosques, and the continuous, living practice of published prayer times {cite:wikipedia-muwaqqit-en} {cite:king-synchrony-heavens-2004}.

## In culture
The muwaqqit is one of Islamic civilisation's quiet institutions — an office that made scholarship a public amenity, and whose product every Muslim still consults five times a day {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
Before clocks and phones, how did a whole city know it was time to pray? The big mosque hired its own star-scientist: he sat with astronomical tables, worked out when the noon shadow and the evening twilight would come for his town, and wrote a timetable for the muezzin. His job was called "muwaqqit" — the timekeeper — and some of these timekeepers were among the best astronomers in the world.
