## Summary
What is "first visibility" in al-Farghani's account? It is the condition that makes a star or planet visible to the naked eye: its emergence from under the Sun's rays after sunset — its "heliacal rising" — with a sufficient angular distance and zodiacal elongation. In "Elements of Astronomy" al-Farghani devotes two chapters of the Jawami' to appearance and concealment: chapter twenty-four "On the planets' morning and evening risings, settings and concealment in the sun's rays", and chapter twenty-five "On the risings of the crescents and the waxing and waning of the moon's light". This is a systematic astronomical treatment of the problem of visibility, among the oldest subjects the ancient astronomical tradition addressed. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 33}

The record documents a transmitted method: the framework itself — elongation angles, the five angles, the conditions of concealment and visibility — is an old astronomical heritage, and al-Farghani's contribution is presenting it with clear figures in a systematic book. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy index}{cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}

## What it is
It is an observational problem: when a body is very near the Sun in the sky it is drowned in its rays and cannot be seen, and when it separates far enough it becomes visible to the naked eye. The condition itself is not one for all bodies; the Sun itself has risings and settings, the Moon has first visibility (the crescent) and disappearance, and the planets have heliacal rising — their morning appearance before the Sun, or evening setting after it — different observational conditions with different numbers. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 33}

Al-Farghani treats all of this with mathematical tools: the chapters concerned with orbs and angles, the tables of refined directions, and the discussion of light and darkness at the horizon. The subject is not a general description but a computation with precise conditions. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}

## The world before it
The old astronomical tradition — Ptolemy's in particular — had set the rules of first visibility: planets visible to the naked eye must be at a certain zodiacal distance from the Sun before they emerge from its rays. The Arabic book al-Farghani read gathered these rules into a single system. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy preface}

But the problem was not fully solved even in the Abbasid age: the question of planetary appearance — when they rise or disappear — remained a complex computational question, because the answer depends on observational data, the Sun's position, and elongation angles. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}

## The story
al-Farghani wrote this part of the Jawami' within the setting of his book composed in the Abbasid era, dedicating to it chapter twenty-four "On the planets' morning and evening risings, settings and concealment in the sun's rays", and chapter twenty-five "On the risings of the crescents and the waxing and waning of the moon's light". They are late chapters of the book, meaning al-Farghani gathered material from multiple sources into an organised treatment. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy index}

The book itself al-Farghani wrote for teaching and practical purposes: he condensed Ptolemy's Almagest into thirty chapters with clear figures and detailed tables — which made it a reference for later generations. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}

## The maker(s)
The name attached to the record is al-Farghani in the role of "transmitter". The subject of first visibility — whether the body appears to the eye or not — existed in old astronomy centuries before his book, but it had not been treated in Arabic with this kind of detailed systematic method. al-Farghani did not invent the rule; he selected the old framework and presented systematic chapters on it. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}

There are no other partners in this part; the book is a single-author work. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}

## How it works
The basic idea is computational: can the body appear to the naked eye at a given time and place, or not? The computation depends on three things: the Sun's position in the sky, the body's position relative to it (the elongation angle), and the state of darkness or light at the horizon at the time of visibility. If these conditions are met the body is visible; if not, it remains drowned until they are. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}

Al-Farghani is careful to clarify that these computations assume trained observation and precise data: he gives figures and criteria, but the final judgement "did the body really appear?" rests with the observer's visual judgement. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 33}

The same chapters carry specific approximate distances: on the mean computation used by the zij authors, if the arc of the zodiac circle between the Sun and the planet at rising or setting is as stated for the crescent — fifteen degrees for Saturn, eleven for Jupiter, seventeen for Mars, seven for Venus and thirteen for Mercury ⁦(OCR readings needing visual check)⁩. Beside them stand the figures Ptolemy worked on for these planets: he set the visibility-limiting distances as the interval between the Sun and the horizon at the moment the planet reaches the horizon, since only that interval makes the sun's light at the horizon — called twilight at the night's beginning and dawn at its end — uniform across the zodiac, and he fixed it in the middle clime at one value: Saturn eleven and a half parts, Jupiter ten, Mars eleven, Venus five and Mercury ten. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}

The difference between the two sets of numbers — the "mean" figures and "Ptolemy's" — is what makes the chapter a historical document: it does not merely transmit a rule but displays two versions of the figures side by side, letting readers compare the parameters as later astronomers did. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}

## The spread
This part travelled with the whole book: chapter twenty-four "On the planets' morning and evening risings, settings and concealment in the sun's rays" was among the material translated into Latin twice — Johannes Hispalensis in 1134 and Gerard of Cremona in 1175 — then into Hebrew in the mid-thirteenth century, then the Golius edition of 1669. Through them the computation of visibility became part of the European astronomical tradition. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya transmission}

In Arabic this chapter remained a reference for the later zijes that needed computations of the Moon's and planets' times. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

## What it was built on
This part was built on four astronomical foundations: (1) the horizon and twilight as boundaries between night and day, (2) the Sun's position and its apparent path across the zodiac, (3) the rules of the five angles and zodiacal degrees, (4) the assumption that bodies move regularly in their orbs. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy index}

Al-Farghani presents these foundations before he begins the visibility chapters, so the structure "reads" the earlier chapters as a prelude to the computations of chapters twenty-four and twenty-five. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy index}

## What was built on it
Built on this part, later on, were the astronomical instruments meant for visual observation: altitude-measuring and horizon instruments that became known in his age and after, since visibility computations called for real observations. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}

In the wider history, the subject of visibility was part of a larger question: when does the lunar month begin? When do the planets first appear? All of them are questions in which angles and models are used. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy index}

## Impact on daily life
First visibility was directly tied to something very everyday: the first sighting of the crescent that begins the lunar month, and the first appearance of the planets that signals the seasons. The chapters concerned with visibility are not abstract theory; they are computations serving the calendar and the reading of the sky on which people depend for agriculture and worship. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy index}

In later books these chapters remained a reference for people wanting to know "can I see the planet tonight?" — a question every age's ordinary observer asks. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}

## How it changed history
The record changed history by being part of the Jawami', which became "al-Farghani's book" in Muslim scientific consciousness — the first teaching book to explain astronomy to learners. The subject of visibility in particular was one of the Jawami''s subjects that persisted in the tradition until later visibility writings — even into modern times — kept asking the same questions in a more refined wording. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

The influence was at the level of "how it is computed", not "what the result is"; the rules themselves did not change much, but the systematic form reached generations. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}

## Who invented it?
The honest answer: al-Farghani did not invent the subject of first visibility, and no one in the Islamic world did; it is an old astronomical subject present in Ptolemaic and earlier astronomy. al-Farghani's work was organisation, simplification and presentation — not the creation of a new rule. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}

Nor do we know today, with certainty, who first fixed the conditions of first visibility; ancient astronomy accumulated on itself, and the problem was the language of astronomy long before. That is why the record is classed "transmission", not "creation". {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}

## Today
Today an astronomer does not need al-Farghani's computations to know whether a planet will appear tonight; modern planetarium software answers that in seconds. But the subject itself has not died: computations of first visibility are still studied in the history of astronomy, and "visibility rules" remain part of the astronomical foundation in the tradition. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

## Surviving examples
The direct surviving witness is the printed text itself: the Golius edition of 1669 which preserved the Arabic text and the Latin translation together, and in which chapters twenty-four and twenty-five hold their content. Among indirect witnesses are the Latin translations that made the subject a reference in European astronomy, and Sezgin's facsimile edition of 1986. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy preface}

## In culture
The subject of visibility entered Muslim consciousness through the Jawami' as part of teaching astronomy. In contemporary heritage al-Farghani is named in lists of great Islamic astronomers alongside al-Battani and al-Khwarizmi, though he lacks the popular fame some gained because his role was "transmitter" rather than "originator". {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 519}{cite:tdv-fergani-kaya biography}

## For young readers
Imagine you look at the night sky and want to know "will the Moon appear tonight?" — that is exactly what al-Farghani computed in his book! al-Farghani did not invent the idea of visibility, but he set clear computational rules: how far must the Moon be from the Sun before it appears? How many degrees of angle? These rules were useful for people who read the sky — the first sighting of the crescent that begins the month, and the first appearance of the planets that signals the seasons. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 30}
