## Summary
During [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]]'s reign, 786–809, a bimaristan was established on the Kerhaya canal in [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]], on the model of the hospital at [[place/jundishapur|Jundishapur]], according to the TDV Encyclopedia of Islam {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. The encyclopedia names [[person/jibril-ibn-bukhtishu|Jibril ibn Bukhtishu]] in its establishment, Abu Yuhanna Masawayh as its director and his son [[person/yuhanna-ibn-masawayh|Yuhanna ibn Masawayh]] as his successor {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. This was the transfer of a hospital model and medical expertise to the Abbasid capital, not the invention of hospitals from nothing {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. The source gives neither an exact opening year nor a known closing date, but says the foundation provided an example for later hospitals in Baghdad and other cities {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}.

## What it is
Bimaristan is a Persian word combining bimar, a sick person, with the place suffix -stan; the encyclopedia's introduction also lists names such as dar al-shifa and dar al-sihha, and notes that mustashfa is now the usual Arabic term for a hospital {cite:tdv-bimaristan introduction}. This record concerns one institution commissioned by Harun, not every place where patients received care before or after him {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}.

The account identifies its canal-side location, its institutional model, the physician involved in its establishment and its medical directors; it does not describe bed numbers, room arrangements or admission rules {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. The accompanying diagram is therefore a relationship diagram: Jundishapur and its physicians, Harun's commission, then later hospitals. It is not a plan of a building the source does not describe.

## The world before it
Medical care in Baghdad did not begin with Harun. In its earlier history of bimaristans the encyclopedia describes [[person/rufayda-al-aslamiyya|Rufayda al-Aslamiyya]]'s tent at Medina, then reports [[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik]]'s foundation in 88 AH/707; these have separate records, [[invention/rufayda-medical-tent|Rufayda's medical tent]] and [[invention/walid-bimaristan|al-Walid's bimaristan]], because neither was Harun's hospital {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}.

The immediate institutional model named by the encyclopedia is Jundishapur. It describes a medical school and hospital reaching back to the Sasanian period and emphasizes Nestorian physicians and Indian medicine in their history {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. This is the encyclopedia's account of the earlier institution: the ancient sources behind its foundation dates were not independently read for this record, so that detailed chronology is not presented as a fresh investigation.

In 148 AH/765, before Harun's foundation, al-Mansur summoned Jirjis ibn Bukhtishu from Jundishapur to treat him. The encyclopedia credits physicians trained there with important contributions to medicine and hospital development under the Abbasids {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. People and knowledge were moving before the account of the institutional transfer.

## The story
TDV relates that Harun summoned Jibril ibn Bukhtishu to Baghdad and commissioned a hospital on the Jundishapur model. He brought Abu Yuhanna Masawayh to direct it, and Masawayh's son Yuhanna succeeded him {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. “Abu Yuhanna” matters here: the account distinguishes father from son rather than attributing the initial direction to the son alone {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}.

Jibril's biography in the entry “BUHTÎŞÛ‘” places him in the network of the Barmakids and the court. His father presented him in 791 as a private physician to Ja'far ibn Yahya; he then entered Harun's court after successfully treating one of the caliph's concubines {cite:tdv-buhtisu §Cibrâîl b. Buhtîşû‘}. That is a date in his service to Ja'far, not the hospital's foundation year; this record does not transfer a date from one event to another.

The hospital entry also describes a separate Baghdad institution founded by the Barmakid family. They placed an Indian physician named Ibn Dahn in charge and commissioned him to translate medical works from Sanskrit into Arabic {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. In this account that was not another name for Harun's canal-side hospital: the two foundations belong to a city receiving several medical traditions {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}.

## The maker(s)
The browser records Harun as commissioner, not as the physician who devised treatments. Al-Suyuti's biography places his caliphate in 170–193 AH, gives Rayy as his birthplace and describes his frequent pilgrimage and campaigning {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p. 456}. His account of Harun's prayer, charity and regard for scholars supplies evidence of Muslim identity, rather than a deduction from his name. The Sunni designation follows the museum's default frame, explained in the person record {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p. 457}.

The patron's role does not absorb everyone else's work. Jibril belonged to a family TDV explicitly identifies as Nestorian Christian; he is neither recast as a Muslim inventor nor omitted {cite:tdv-buhtisu §introduction}. Abu Yuhanna Masawayh's direction and his son's succession remain named in the hospital account {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. The source read does not name the builders or the rest of the staff, and their work is not attributed to the caliph.

## How it works
Three institutional steps can be followed in the account: a ruler commissions a hospital, expertise is brought from an earlier medical centre, and medical management passes from father to son {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. That explains organization and knowledge transfer; the passage does not supply a particular treatment, specialist wards or an internal pharmacy for this hospital.

Details from later Abbasid institutions are not automatically assigned to it. For example, the encyclopedia gives monthly expenditures of 600 dinars for the Sayyida hospital and 200 for the Muqtadiri hospital in 918; these concern two foundations after Harun, not the operating accounts of his own hospital {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. The difference between a later example and evidence for the earlier institution is essential to reading the diagram and article together.

## The spread
TDV says Harun's hospital provided an example for subsequent foundations in Baghdad and other cities, although it does not know how long it continued to function {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. It then describes a growing network, including Ali ibn Isa ibn al-Jarrah's hospital in Baghdad's Harbiyya quarter in 914, and the Sayyida and Muqtadiri hospitals in 918 {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}.

It mentions hospitals at Wasit, Raqqa and elsewhere, but does not document a route along which Harun's staff moved from one city to the next {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. The record's dated spread map therefore remains empty: the multiplication of institutions is not a documented itinerary of this particular foundation.

## What it was built on
The closest antecedents named are the Jundishapur hospital model and its physicians' expertise: Jirjis had entered al-Mansur's service before Jibril appeared in the account of Harun's commission {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. The introduction to “BUHTÎŞÛ‘” describes the family's contribution through translations, writings and physicians serving the Abbasid court {cite:tdv-buhtisu §introduction}.

The parallel Barmakid hospital adds another dimension: interest in Indian medicine and translation, alongside Greek medicine and Nestorian expertise {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. This is Baghdad's intellectual context, not evidence that every book translated there formed part of a curriculum in Harun's hospital.

## What was built on it
The succession established by the source is general: Harun's foundation served as an example for hospitals in Baghdad and other cities {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. This does not mean every later building copied its plan or every modern hospital practice began there.

Among later Abbasid developments, TDV describes the vizier Ali ibn Isa's order for physicians to visit prisons daily, examine patients and prepare their medicines, and the sending of a mobile hospital to the Sawad villages. It says correspondence about the mobile service shows that Muslims and non-Muslims received treatment {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. These illustrate expanding care, not the services of Harun's hospital a century earlier.

## Impact on daily life
The clearest change in Harun's account is the establishment of a medical institution in the capital, with named people involved in founding and managing it {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. The source gives no patient totals or personal testimonies and does not establish whether treatment there was free to everyone.

The later network's effects are more concrete in the material read: examination and medicine reached prisons and the Sawad villages, and the mobile hospital treated non-Muslims as well as Muslims {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. The museum distinguishes these layers of evidence instead of turning silence into a promise of services the source does not describe.

## How it changed history
The encyclopedia calls the Abbasid period the first flowering of bimaristans, following beginnings under the Umayyads, and presents Harun's foundation as an example for later establishments {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. This episode matters for bringing political patronage and incoming medical expertise together in an institution, not for assigning the whole history of hospitals to one person {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}.

Jundishapur, the Barmakids and Indian translations make this a story with several paths: the medical history described by the encyclopedia involves Greek, Indian and Nestorian traditions, not a single line cut off from what came before {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}.

## Who invented it?
**The Sunni biographical source for the patron.** Al-Suyuti presents Harun as a Muslim caliph and describes his worship and regard for learning; his biography is not the source used here for the hospital foundation {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 pp. 456–457}.

**The academic account in TDV.** The encyclopedia describes the institution as following Jundishapur and calls Harun the founder of Baghdad's first bimaristan; the same entry discusses Rufayda's tent and al-Walid's earlier foundation {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. The local priority it assigns in Baghdad is not priority in inventing hospitals worldwide or in Islam.

The museum classifies this contribution as transmission and Harun's role as commissioner. The material read does not present a documented dispute over the name of this patron, so the structured perspectives field does not invent one. TDV names the physicians in the story and identifies the Bukhtishu family as Christian in its separate entry {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri} {cite:tdv-buhtisu §introduction}.

## Today
The encyclopedia does not establish when Harun's hospital closed or name a surviving remnant to visit; the passage supplies no basis for connecting it directly to a particular modern hospital {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. Its introduction traces the word bimaristan across languages and regions and notes that mustashfa is now the common Arabic term {cite:tdv-bimaristan introduction}.

## Surviving examples
The witness used for this record is an encyclopedia account referring to Ibn Abi Usaybia. His pages were not independently read for this batch, and the account supplies no surviving building or plan from Harun's reign {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}.

The accompanying photograph shows the entrance to Nur al-Din's bimaristan in Damascus, not Harun's hospital. It is a surviving example from the wider history of the institution: TDV describes the Damascus foundation among hospitals retaining their original form, but it is centuries later than the Baghdad establishment {cite:tdv-bimaristan §Selçuklu Bîmâristanlarının Mimari Karakterinin Menşei}. Its decoration or rooms are not used to reconstruct what is unknown about Baghdad.

## In culture
The encyclopedia's introduction records local forms of the word bimaristan, including maristan, and its later association in some usages with the treatment of mental illness {cite:tdv-bimaristan introduction}. The source read does not establish a novel, film or stamp depicting Harun's particular hospital; the institution's history is not conflated with Harun's broader literary image.

## For young readers
Harun al-Rashid wanted a hospital in Baghdad and turned to physicians from Jundishapur, where an older hospital existed. The encyclopedia says he had one built on that model {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}. He did not invent medicine or hospitals: he commissioned the institution, and the doctors had their own work, for which we name them. This source gives us no drawing of its rooms {cite:tdv-bimaristan §İlk Bîmâristan Tesisleri}.
