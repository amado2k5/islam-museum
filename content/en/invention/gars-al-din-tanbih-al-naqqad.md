## Summary
Gars al-Din ibn Ahmad ibn Khalil al-Halabi — a Syrian astronomer — wrote a treatise revising the famous astronomical hay'a in 958 AH/1551 CE, criticising Ptolemaic principles such as the equant and eccentric deferents in planetary orbits, transmitting alternative models from Ibn al-Shatir and Mu'ayyad al-Din al-Urdi, and noting that observed planetary diameters differ from what the theory assumes {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 31} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 32}. This is a documented scientific critique, not a claim of heliocentrism.

## What it is
A treatise critiquing the "famous hay'a" — the planetary astronomical model based on Ptolemy — dealing with its basic principles: the equant principle (the point from which motion is measured) and the eccentric deferent (the off-centre orbit). It does not merely reject; it relies on revised alternative models transmitted by the author from Ibn al-Shatir and Mu'ayyad al-Din al-Urdi, and notes discrepancies between what observation measures and what the theory assumes {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 31} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 32}. This is a critical "method" — a mode of testing — not mere rejection.

## The world before it
Before Gars al-Din, the debate over Ptolemy was old in the Islamic tradition: Ibn al-Haytham's Shukuk, the Maragha observatory models, and the works of Ibn al-Shatir and al-Urdi which fixed some of the hay'a's problems. Gars al-Din did not come from nothing; he came in a late context of this debate {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 32} {cite:tdv-ibnun-nakib-el-halebi}. The treatise itself is read in the light of this heritage, not as an isolated invention.

## The story
Gars al-Din wrote his treatise in 958 AH, and the surviving manuscript copy was transcribed at Istanbul in 985 AH, preserved in the Yeni Cami library no. 1181, fols. 147-154 {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 31}. The author died in 971 AH/1563 CE according to Suter, as Heinen transmits {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 31}. His birthplace is not precisely known; Heinen describes him as a Syrian astronomer, while Ottoman sources say he was of Aleppo, and this difference is unsettled here {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 31} {cite:tdv-ibnun-nakib-el-halebi}.

## The maker(s)
Gars al-Din ibn Ahmad ibn Khalil al-Halabi, an astronomer and writer, from a Syrian-Ottoman milieu in the tenth century AH {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 31}. His Muslim identity is documented by his being an Arabic author in the Islamic hay'a tradition, and his Sunni label is the museum's declared frame; Ottoman sources say he was a Shafi'i scholar of Aleppo, but this link to the treatise is unconfirmed here {cite:tdv-ibnun-nakib-el-halebi}. The name "Gars al-Din" matches "Garsüddin Khalil" in the Ottoman source — a name match, not a verified biographical identification {cite:tdv-ibnun-nakib-el-halebi}.

## How it works
The treatise does not present a new complete alternative model; it is a critical examination of the famous principles: it asks "does this principle match what we see?" then answers with observational notes, and transmits the revised models proposed by Ibn al-Shatir and al-Urdi {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 32}. Among its most important observations is that the diameters of the planets as measured differ from what the theoretical model assumes — an empirical note, not mere dialectic {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 32}.

## The spread
The treatise did not spread widely; the only surviving manuscript known to us is the Istanbul copy, and it was not translated into Latin or Hebrew as far as we know {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 31}. We establish no direct effect here on European astronomy or on modern astronomy {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology pp. 31-33}.

## What it was built on
It was built on the tradition of Ptolemaic criticism in the Islamic heritage: Ibn al-Haytham's Shukuk, the Maragha models, and the works of Ibn al-Shatir and al-Urdi which the author cites explicitly {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 32} {cite:tdv-ibnun-nakib-el-halebi}. The author did not come from nothing; he was defending positions current in the astronomy of his time.

## What was built on it
We establish no later book here taking its method directly; the text remained a single manuscript in Istanbul, and Heinen cited it in the context of analysing the sources of al-Suyuti's Islamic cosmology {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology pp. 31-33}.

## Impact on daily life
The text had no effect on people's daily lives; it was a specialised scientific discussion among astronomers. Its value lies in documenting that astronomical criticism did not end with the medieval period, but continued in late Syrian-Ottoman circles {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology pp. 31-33}.

## How it changed history
What changes nothing in later practice does not change history, and here we do not claim his treatise produced a transformation in astronomy; but it is a witness that "the culture of testing" continued in the Islamic tradition until the tenth century AH, and that Ptolemaic criticism was not confined to al-Andalus or the early East {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 32}. This is a history of ideas, not of instruments.

## Who invented it?
We do not claim that Gars al-Din invented astronomical criticism or heliocentrism; these are older than him in the tradition {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 32}. What is documented is that he authored a dated critical treatise against the famous hay'a's principles, transmitting revised models and citing observational notes; this is an improvement in the mode of discussion, not priority for the principle.

## Today
Today no one reads the "hay'a" in this way, but the text remains a witness that Islamic astronomy was not mere transmission from Ptolemy; it was a living debate that continued for centuries. The accompanying image is a page from an Islamic astronomical manuscript in the Khalili collection — contextual to the astronomical-manuscript tradition, not a page of Tanbih al-naqqad {cite:commons-khalili-jaghmini-mss1164 Description}. We establish no direct continuity of his critique in modern astronomy.

## Surviving examples
The most important witness is the manuscript preserved in Istanbul's Yeni Cami library no. 1181, fols. 147-154, copied in 985 AH, which Heinen studied {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 31}. No critical edition of this text is known {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology pp. 31-33}.

## In culture
This text represents a model of "internal criticism" in the Islamic tradition: the author does not attack Ptolemy blindly, but tests his principles against observation and relies on revised alternatives; this is a model of scientific honesty in the heritage {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 32}.

## For young readers
Hundreds of years ago scholars believed planets moved in complex circles set out by Ptolemy. A Syrian astronomer named Gars al-Din wrote a treatise saying some of those circles did not match what we see in the sky, and cited revised models from earlier scholars — a scientific check of the theory, not blind rejection.
