## Summary
What is "parallax" in al-Farghani's account? It is the difference in a body's apparent position when seen from two different points on the Earth's surface: the Moon, for instance, appears in a slightly different place if the observer's position on Earth changes, because its nearness makes the line of sight to it shift direction. In "Elements of Astronomy" al-Farghani presents this principle in his chapters, showing that the Moon, Venus and Mercury display a computed difference of perspective when read from two places on Earth, that the superior planets — Mars, Jupiter and Saturn — have no such deviation at all, and that the Sun's deviation is not perceptible by measurement. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

The record documents a transmitted principle: the principle itself — that nearby bodies appear with a small displacement between two positions — existed in old astronomy, and al-Farghani's contribution is presenting it with clear figures in an influential text. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}{cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}

## What it is
It is an optical principle: when one looks at a nearby body from two different points on the Earth's surface, it appears in two slightly different places in the sky. The nearer the body, the greater the displacement; the farther it is, the smaller the displacement becomes until it practically vanishes for distant bodies. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

In al-Farghani's account this principle is not a mere observation: he states explicitly that the Moon — the nearest body — shows the greatest difference of perspective, gathering at the horizon about one degree and forty-four minutes in its nearest orb and fifty-four minutes in its farthest ⁦(an OCR reading)⁩, that Venus and Mercury show a computed deviation, that the superior planets — Mars, Jupiter and Saturn — have no such deviation at all, and that the Sun's deviation is not perceptible by measurement. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 28}

## The world before it
Old astronomy had known for a long time that nearby stars appear in slightly different positions from two places on Earth; sight from two different horizons passes along two diverging lines, and the nearby body appears to move between them. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy preface}

But the old astronomers differed on the size of this difference: some treated it as a precise computation to be used in correcting positions, others as a small effect worth mentioning only for the Moon. The question was not purely theoretical; it was tied to a deeper one — is the Earth the centre of the world? If everything revolves around the Earth, the difference of perspective between two places on its surface must appear in the nearby bodies — which makes the deviation a measure for testing the structure of the world itself. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

But the computational problem — how large is this difference? And does it suffice to explain anything in apparent motion? — remained an open question until al-Farghani wrote his chapters. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

## The story
al-Farghani wrote this part of the Jawami' within the setting of his book composed in the milieu of the Abbasid caliphate, where he was among the leading mathematicians and astronomers of his age. The TDV encyclopaedia records that the book condensed Ptolemy's Almagest into thirty chapters. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya biography}{cite:iranica-fargani-ahmad chapter plan}

In the passages of the Jawami' concerned with the computation of ascensions and vertical circles — directly tied to risings and settings — al-Farghani presents the principle of parallax as part of a complete computational system, not as a "self-evident truth" but as a rule that must be taken into account when computing the positions of bodies. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

## The maker(s)
The name attached to the record is al-Farghani in the role of "transmitter". The principle of parallax itself existed in old astronomy centuries before his book; what he actually did was present the principle with clear figures in influential Arabic, and link it to the computations of the later chapters. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

There are no other partners in this part; the book is a single-author work attributed to al-Farghani. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya work}

## How it works
The optical idea is simple: when an observer stands in two different positions on the Earth's surface, each line of sight passes along a different straight line; so the nearby body — such as the Moon — appears in two slightly different positions, while the distant body — such as the stars — appears in practically the same position. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

Al-Farghani links this principle to astronomical computation: knowing a body's distance from the Earth determines the size of its apparent displacement between two positions, and knowing that displacement determines whether the computations must be corrected or not. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

He distinguishes the deviation in two directions: part of it falls in longitude — when the zodiac circle stands on the observer's zenith at the moment of observation the whole deviation falls in longitude and none in latitude, so the body appears advanced or retarded from its true place according to its side — and part falls in latitude when the circle through the pole passes over the zenith, making the whole deviation latitudinal. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

He also gives maximum figures for the deviation: the Moon gathers at the horizon about one degree and forty-four minutes in its nearest orb and one degree and fifty-four minutes in its farthest ⁦(OCR readings)⁩, Venus and Mercury show a computed deviation, the Sun's deviation is imperceptible by measurement though computed from its distance it gathers at most three minutes at the horizon ⁦(OCR reading)⁩, and the superior planets above the Sun have no such deviation at all. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 28}

## The spread
This principle travelled with the whole book: the parallax passages were part of the material translated into Latin twice — Johannes Hispalensis in 1134 and Gerard of Cremona in 1175 — then into Hebrew in the mid-thirteenth century, then the Golius edition of 1669. Through them the principle of parallax became part of the European astronomical tradition. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya transmission}

In Arabic this principle remained a reference for the later zijes that needed precise computations of the positions of bodies from different places. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

## What it was built on
This principle was built on three foundations: (1) that sight travels in straight lines, (2) that nearby bodies appear with a greater displacement than distant ones, (3) that this displacement can be computed if the body's distance is known. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

Al-Farghani places this principle within the context of the outer sphere's judgements — that is, within the general framework of old astronomy — not as a "new discovery" but as a "rule that must be taken into account". {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy index}

## What was built on it
Built on this principle, later on, were the observational instruments that measured the positions of bodies from two different places; the computations of the later zijes used parallax to correct the computed positions. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

In the wider history, parallax was part of a larger question: how is a body's position computed accurately if the observer moves on the Earth's surface? {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

## Impact on daily life
The principle was directly tied to something practical: the computation of the positions of bodies on which people depend for agriculture and worship. Knowing that the Moon appears with a greater displacement than the Sun means that computations of the Moon must be more precise than computations of the Sun. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

In the Islamic calendar, knowing the positions of the Moon and planets meant knowing the seasons of the year, the seasons of sowing, and the worships tied to them; parallax was a computational instrument within this system. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy index}

## How it changed history
The record changed history by being part of the Jawami', which became "al-Farghani's book" in Muslim scientific consciousness — the first teaching book to explain astronomy to learners. The principle of parallax in particular was one of the Jawami''s subjects that persisted in the tradition until later astronomical writings kept asking the same questions in a more refined wording. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

The influence was at the level of "how it is computed", not "what the result is"; the principle itself did not change much, but the systematic form reached generations. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

More importantly, the Jawami' made the deviation a rule rather than an exception: instead of remaining a "known fact" an astronomer mentions in passing, it became in the Jawami' an explicit computational rule with its maxima — that the Moon deviates nearly a degree at its nearest orbit, and that the superior planets have no deviation at all — so that every astronomer reading the book knew that computing the Moon's position must begin with the deviation. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 28}

## Who invented it?
The honest answer: al-Farghani did not invent the principle of parallax, and no one in the Islamic world did; it is an optical principle present in old astronomy centuries before his book. al-Farghani's work was organisation, simplification and presentation — not the creation of a new rule. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

Nor do we know today, with certainty, who first fixed the principle of parallax; ancient astronomy accumulated on itself, and the principle was the language of astronomy long before. That is why the record is classed "transmission", not "creation". {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}

## Today
Today an astronomer does not need al-Farghani's computations to know the positions of bodies; modern astronomical computation rests on precise celestial mechanics and modern astronomical observation. But the principle has not died: it is still taught in the history of astronomy as a computational model, and displayed in science museums as a picture of a particular astronomical way of thinking at a particular time. {cite:tdv-fergani-kaya reception}

## Surviving examples
The direct surviving witness is the printed text itself: the Golius edition of 1669 which preserved the Arabic text and the Latin translation together, and in which the parallax passages hold their content. Among indirect witnesses are the Latin translations that made the book a reference in European astronomy until the fifteenth century, and Sezgin's facsimile edition of 1986. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy preface}{cite:tdv-fergani-kaya transmission}

## In culture
The principle of parallax entered Muslim scientific consciousness through the Jawami' as part of teaching astronomy. In contemporary heritage al-Farghani is named in lists of great Islamic astronomers alongside al-Battani and al-Khwarizmi, though he lacks the popular fame some gained because his role was "transmitter" rather than "originator". {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 519}{cite:tdv-fergani-kaya biography}

## For young readers
Imagine you stand on a platform and watch a boat passing on a river — the nearer boat seems to move more than the farther one, doesn't it? That is exactly what happens with the Moon: when you look at it from two different places on Earth it appears in two slightly different positions. al-Farghani did not invent this idea, but he wrote it clearly in an Arabic book that became very famous, until it was translated into Latin and stayed a reference in Europe for five hundred years. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 27}
