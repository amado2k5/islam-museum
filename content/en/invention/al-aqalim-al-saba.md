## Summary
The seven climates is a geographical-astronomical system that divided the inhabited world into seven climates, each equivalent to a maximum day length and pole height above the horizon, and paired each climate with a zodiac sign or planet. Muhammad ibn Kathir al-Farghani recorded it in the eighth and ninth chapters of his 'Jawami' 'ilm al-nujum' before 857 CE, dividing the inhabited quarter into climates, fixing their bounds by day length and pole height, and listing each climate's countries in the ninth chapter {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100}. Ali ibn al-Husayn al-Mas'udi presented it in Muruj al-Dhahab as a full list of climates with their signs, planets and his discussions of Ptolemy and the Geography, treating the settlement bound from the equator and naming the middle climate his homeland {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}. The system is inherited from the Ptolemaic traditions, not an Islamic invention, but Muslims recorded it in the schoolbooks of astronomy and geography and it became a template for describing the known world for several centuries {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}.

## What it is
The seven climates ⁦(al-aqalim al-saba'⁩ is the division used by Muslim astronomers and geographers to describe the inhabited world: seven climates, each roughly equal east-west, bounded by clear astronomical limits, each with a sign in the sky and a ruling planet, and each with lands, peoples and coasts. Al-Farghani makes each climate's bounds nearly equal in length and width, estimating each climate's extent at about nine hundred farsakhs in each direction as transmitted from earlier traditions {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100} {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}. It is not only a scheme for measuring the earth on a map, but a conceptual frame joining astronomy — through the signs and planets attached to each climate — with geography — through the ordering of countries within it {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}.

## The world before it
Before the scheme was recorded in Arabic, scholars described the inhabited world in older climatic zones: geographical-astronomical patterns reaching back to the Greek traditions, foremost among them what Ptolemy organised in his 'Geography' and astronomical works. Al-Mas'udi discussed Ptolemy's Geography at length in Muruj al-Dhahab and counted it among the major geographical references on which Islamic description of the world rests {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}. Muslim astronomers — foremost the authors of hay'a treatises — treated these climates as a ready frame: al-Farghani himself, an Abbasid scholar in the service of the caliphs, presents the climate division in his book's chapters as a matter known to the curriculum, not a new discovery {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100}. So 'the world before it' is an ancient scholarly tradition that transcends Islamic civilisation, and the system al-Farghani and al-Mas'udi recorded is its refined Arabic form. It is worth noting that the two recorders were not in the same place: al-Farghani wrote in the Abbasid caliphal capital within the official astronomical curriculum, and al-Mas'udi wrote in Baghdad after his journeys through the lands, so each saw the system from a different angle and recorded it in his own idiom {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100} {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}.

## The story
The known story begins inside the sources we read with al-Farghani's 'Jawami' 'ilm al-nujum'. In his eighth chapter al-Farghani divides the inhabited quarter into seven climates, fixing each by its longest day and the pole's height above the horizon, so that the first climate is where day and night are shortest and the seventh where the day lengthens to its utmost. Then in his ninth chapter he lists each climate's countries: the lands of Fars, Khurasan and Iraq in one, the lands of Syria, Rum and the Jazira in one climate, the lands of the Turks, Khazars, Daylam and Saqaliba in one, and the lands of Debel and China in a farthest climate {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100}. About a century later al-Mas'udi came in Muruj al-Dhahab and presented the system in a broader encyclopaedic form: a list of the seven climates with each one's sign, planet and contents, a discussion of the settlement bound from the equator, and an extended debate on Ptolemy's Geography, the measure of the earth and the size of the degree {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}. Thus the system moved from a chapter in an astronomical book to a central subject in the literature of historical geography. The recorders' work was not confined to text: the treatises discuss variant readings of the climates — their number, names and each climate's extent — showing the system was living scholarly material studied and corrected, not merely transmitted information {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100}.

## The maker(s)
There is no single named inventor of the seven climates; the system is inherited, and those who recorded it in Arabic are described as describers, not originators. The first is Muhammad ibn Kathir al-Farghani ⁦(d. c. 861 CE⁩, the Abbasid astronomer of the Jawami', who organised the division in two consecutive chapters and made it part of the astronomical curriculum he taught {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100}. The second is Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn al-Husayn al-Mas'udi ⁦(d. c. 345 AH/956 CE⁩, the Baghdadi historian and traveller, who presented the full list of climates with their signs and planets in Muruj al-Dhahab and commented that the middle climate was his homeland {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}. The museum record also names the geographer al-Muqaddasi, author of Ahsan al-Taqasim, who offered an alternative division of the inhabited world into fourteen regions — a documented offshoot of the seven-zone frame {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim}.

## How it works
The system rests on two simple astronomical principles. First: every point on the inhabited world has a 'longest day' — the longest daylight duration in the year — and a 'pole height' — the altitude of the pole star above the horizon. So the first climate is where the longest day is shortest, and the seventh where it lengthens to its utmost. Second: each climate in the list has a zodiac sign attached to it and a ruling planet — the first climate is paired with Cancer, Gemini and the superior planets, and so on — so geographical description becomes a sky-map laid over a land-map {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100}. Al-Farghani fixes the seven climates in a single table balancing length and width, making each climate about nine hundred farsakhs in each direction — dimensions resting on the Abbasid astronomers' measurements of the earth's diameter {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}.

## The spread
The system spread through Arabic books of astronomy and geography from the third century AH onward. After al-Farghani fixed it in the Jawami' — the book that became the astronomical curriculum in the caliphs' schools — al-Mas'udi presented it in Muruj al-Dhahab within the wider literature of the history of nations and cities {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}. Later divisions branched from it: al-Muqaddasi offered fourteen regions instead of seven in Ahsan al-Taqasim, an attested modification showing the frame was alive and being reconsidered {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim}. In the sixth century AH the frame became the template for al-Idrisi's maps in Nuzhat al-mushtaq: the Bodleian's surviving manuscripts organise the description of the world into its seven climates, the earlier MS. Greaves 42 covering the first three climates and MS. Pococke 375 from the 1550s covering all seven with painted maps {cite:bodleian-idrisi-maps}. And the world map al-Idrisi presented to Roger II of Sicily in 1154 CE survives today under the public-domain mark {cite:commons-idrisi-world-map}.

## What it was built on
The system was built on astronomical and geographical traditions older than Islamic civilisation. Foremost among them is the hay'a astronomical tradition — the mathematical astronomy that organised the heavens into spheres and orbs — from which the climates derive their bounds from day length and pole height {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100}. It was also built on Ptolemy's 'Geography', which al-Mas'udi discussed at length and treated as a reference for geographical bounds {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}. And it rests on the Abbasid tradition of astronomical measurement: al-Mas'udi linked reports of an observational programme under al-Ma'mun — solar observations at Bariza Sinjar — to the size of the equatorial degree and the earth's diameter on which the climates' dimensions were built {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}.

## What was built on it
Later geographical divisions were built on the seven-zone frame. Al-Muqaddasi's division into fourteen regions in Ahsan al-Taqasim is a direct or parallel offshoot of the frame, but it kept the principle 'climate = astronomical-geographical unit' {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim}. Al-Idrisi's maps of the known world were built on it, organising each climate separately in Nuzhat al-mushtaq and making each regional map part of an illustrated geographical book {cite:bodleian-idrisi-maps}. And the frame became an implicit reference in the literature of routes and kingdoms: al-Bakri's Masalik wa-l-Mamalik and later geographers' writings use the division of the world into known climates {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim} {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}.

## Impact on daily life
The seven climates had no direct effect on everyday life as a practical tool; their effect was on 'the way of seeing the world': travellers, merchants and readers found it in books the frame by which lands were described. When a reader encountered a description of Egypt, Syria or Khurasan in an Arabic geographical book, they read it in its astronomical place: its sign, its planet, its bounds {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}. And this frame unified the language of geography across the Islamic world: whoever read 'the fourth climate' knew it meant Egypt, Ifriqiya and al-Andalus, and whoever read 'the fifth climate' knew it meant Syria, Rum and the Jazira {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100} {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}. It also made geographical learning easier: instead of memorising a random list of countries, the student memorised seven climates with each country in its place, holding the map of the world as a whole rather than scattered pieces.

## How it changed history
The system changed — not through direct events but through the structure of geographical knowledge — the way the description of the world was organised. Instead of country descriptions being a chaos of scattered reports, every land gained a fixed place in a sevenfold astronomical map balancing length and width {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100}. This organisation allowed later mapmakers — like al-Idrisi — to give each climate its own map, so that a 'geographical book' could be imagined visually, not merely a textual record {cite:bodleian-idrisi-maps}. It also changed the very standard of geographical description: 'where does this country lie astronomically' became a legitimate question before 'what are its reports' {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}.

## Who invented it?
The honest answer: nobody in Islamic civilisation invented it; the system is inherited from the Greek astronomical traditions transmitted through the hay'a books. Those who recorded it in Arabic — al-Farghani and al-Mas'udi — are 'describers', not 'inventors'. But that does not make it valueless for Islam: the documented contribution is that Muslims carried the system into Arabic, organised it in the schoolbooks of astronomy and geography, tied it to the Abbasid tradition of measuring the earth's diameter, and made it the template for describing the world for centuries {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100}. So the record registers 'a transmitted system carried and documented' — not 'an original Islamic invention'.

## Today
The seven-climates division is no longer used in school geography or modern maps; latitude and longitude lines and geodetic systems have replaced it. But it survives in editions of the Jawami' and Muruj al-Dhahab and in Idrisi manuscripts, displayed on old Islamic world maps {cite:commons-idrisi-world-map} {cite:bodleian-idrisi-maps}. Reading it today shows how scholars of Islam organised geographical knowledge before the digital age, and how they joined the sky of mathematics to the earth of geography in a single system.

## Surviving examples
The main surviving witnesses: al-Farghani's 'Jawami' 'ilm al-nujum' in the 1669 Golius edition fixing the climates in two chapters {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100}; al-Mas'udi's Muruj al-Dhahab presenting the full list with its signs and planets {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522}; the Bodleian's Idrisi manuscripts organising world maps into the seven climates {cite:bodleian-idrisi-maps}; and the 1154 world map under the public-domain mark {cite:commons-idrisi-world-map}. There are no 'original' specimens of the system itself — it is not a material thing to be preserved — but the witnesses are the texts and maps that recorded and transmitted it, all in known editions and manuscripts {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100}.

## In culture
The seven climates entered the culture of Arabic geographical description: 'the fourth climate' and 'the fifth climate' became geographical names the specialist reader understood, appearing on drawn maps of the Islamic world. In historical literature the reference to 'the climates' remained a way of geographical framing {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522} {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim}.

## For young readers
Imagine that ancient astronomers divided the world into seven great climates, each with a sign in the sky and a planet ruling it — like a sky-map laid over a land-map. Al-Farghani taught this system in his book more than eleven hundred years ago, al-Mas'udi set it out in his history, and mapmakers like al-Idrisi used it to draw beautiful maps of the world. The system was not invented in Islam, but it was carried, documented and made the template for describing the world for centuries {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 92–100} {cite:muruj-al-dhahab pp. 519–522} {cite:bodleian-idrisi-maps}.
