## Summary

In al-Andalus, [[person/al-zarqali|Ibrahim ibn Yahya al-Zarqali]] developed an independent equatorium, an instrument representing planetary geometry to assist computation of positions {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Deferents and associated circles occupy both sides of one plate, epicycles a second; Roser Puig dates construction and use treatises approximately1080/1081 and1081/1082 {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Ibn al-Samh had an earlier equatorium: this is not planetary computation invented from nothing {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. It differs from his [[invention/universal-astrolabe-al-zarqali|universal astrolabe plate]], and our photograph depicts a much later object, not his own handiwork {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260} {cite:commons-liverpool-equatorium Description}.

## What it is

An equatorium is a geometrical calculating instrument, not a telescope enlarging a planet or a self-running clock; plates embody elements of a model {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. A deferent is a larger circle associated with the motion of the centre of a smaller circle, the epicycle; the inherited Ptolemaic tradition used compound motions to account for a planet's apparent position {cite:zarqali-mactutor Mercury discussion}. We distinguish the celestial body, a theory describing its motion, and an instrument for using that theory: the plate is not a true-scale picture of the planetary system {cite:zarqali-mactutor Mercury discussion}.

## The world before it

Astronomers were not waiting for al-Zarqali to discover calculation or instrument making: Puig identifies Ibn al-Samh's equatorium, circa1025/1026, as a specific predecessor {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Al-Zarqali's own environment combined making instruments, observation and astronomical tables organising data and calculations {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. The useful historical question is therefore how he rearranged an existing means of modelling planets, not who first knew that planets existed {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

## The story

The scholarly biography places al-Zarqali between metalworking, study and observation, describing work around [[place/tulaytula|Toledo]] and a move to [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]] at an uncertain point between1081 and1085 {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. His equatorium treatises are dedicated to al-Mu'tamid, not the Abbasid al-Mamun: this is the patronage setting of the Andalusi kingdoms {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. These clues do not establish the design workshop's precise city, so the record names al-Andalus without an unjustifiably exact construction point on a map {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

The two treatises distinguish construction from use: describing where circles belong differs from explaining what an operator does with them {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Their approximate dates are not a documented invention day or the date of surviving original hardware {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

## The maker(s)

Al-Zarqali's role is improver of a particular arrangement, not inventor of all equatoria; this preserves Ibn al-Samh's earlier contribution without attributing the later two-plate arrangement to him without evidence {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. MacTutor explicitly identifies al-Zarqali as a Muslim instrument maker and astronomer, and Bibliotheca Alexandrina names him in its Muslim-astronomers section {cite:zarqali-mactutor Summary} {cite:bibalex-astrolabe-949 The Role of Muslim Astronomers}. Sunni is the museum's explicit default frame, not personal legal-school testimony inferred from his name or court {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260} {cite:bibalex-astrolabe-949 The Role of Muslim Astronomers}.

## How it works

Read the arrangement in three stages: planetary deferents and associated circles, then their epicycles, then use of these relationships to represent positions; Puig specifies the first group on both sides of one plate and the second on another {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Several planets do not imply seven independent plates, and the biography does not describe an engine driving the instrument or finding the date automatically {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Our diagram is a teaching chain of relationships, not manufacturing instructions with historically measured diameters and graduations {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

Imagine a small circle whose centre follows a larger path: this illustrates the deferent–epicycle relationship, not every detail of Mercury's special arrangement {cite:zarqali-mactutor Mercury discussion}. For Mercury, Puig mentions an elliptical deferent representation, while MacTutor discusses the epicycle centre's oval path within inherited geometry {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260} {cite:zarqali-mactutor Mercury discussion}. This does not mean all planets orbit the Sun under a modern law, or that al-Zarqali formulated Kepler's theory before Kepler {cite:zarqali-mactutor Mercury discussion}.

## The spread

The sources read here do not establish a dated route carrying this exact arrangement to every workshop or user, so the record draws no confident diffusion arrows {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Nor do the universal plate's translation dates automatically become the equatorium's history: the biography distinguishes these instruments and treatises {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. A later European equatorium witnesses the history of the instrument family, not, by itself, a documented chain of transmission from al-Zarqali {cite:commons-liverpool-equatorium Description}.

## What it was built on

The named predecessor is Ibn al-Samh, and the mathematical inheritance is deferent–epicycle geometry: the universal plate is not inserted as the precursor merely because the same scholar made it {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260} {cite:zarqali-mactutor Mercury discussion}. This distinction matters in reading the diagram: a predecessor addresses the same problem, rather than listing everything a scholar made or everyone living at the time {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. This research did not directly inspect Ibn al-Samh's construction treatise or an original physical prototype {cite:zarqali-puig-bea Selected References}.

## What was built on it

Cambridge's project offers a useful comparison, not a proven genealogy: a late-fourteenth-century English manuscript describes an equatorium and is identified as John Westwyk's, after an earlier attribution to Chaucer {cite:cambridge-equatorie-project project account}. Derek Price made a physical model in modern times; a later project combined digitisation with a virtual model and instructions {cite:cambridge-equatorie-project project account}. This is a separate story: the institutional account does not say every detail derives from al-Zarqali's two plates {cite:cambridge-equatorie-project project account}.

## Impact on daily life

The directly supported effect concerns the astronomer's equipment: multiple circles consolidated in an independent two-plate instrument, an achievement distinct from making observations themselves {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. The sources read supply neither production counts and prices nor measured adoption among sailors and farmers {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. A specialised computational contribution should therefore not become an allegedly documented transformation of everybody's life or a guarantee of accurate navigation {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

## How it changed history

This example shows that innovation can rearrange elements without replacing an entire science: inherited planetary geometry remained, alongside a distinct instrumental arrangement and explanatory treatises {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Naming Ibn al-Samh preserves a cumulative history rather than isolated heroes each beginning from nothing {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Saying that this produced modern orbital laws goes beyond the Mercury representation: MacTutor explicitly calls out an interpretation that went too far {cite:zarqali-mactutor Mercury discussion}.

## Who invented it?

**Sunni museum frame:** the attribution preserves a scholar's contribution whom the sources identify as Muslim, while the frame does not substitute for personal school testimony {cite:zarqali-mactutor Summary} {cite:bibalex-astrolabe-949 The Role of Muslim Astronomers}. **Academic description:** Puig attributes the independent two-plate improvement to al-Zarqali and names the predecessor and construction/use treatises {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. **Limit of comparison:** MacTutor warns against claiming discovery of modern elliptical orbits centuries before Kepler; an oval representation in an instrument is not itself a general physical law {cite:zarqali-mactutor Mercury discussion}.

## Today

Our photograph shows a later World Museum Liverpool instrument, described as circa1600 and possibly French; plates, arms and small circles overlap a graduated disc {cite:commons-liverpool-equatorium Description; image}. Reptonix's photograph is CC BY3.0, not a reconstruction of al-Zarqali's instrument {cite:commons-liverpool-equatorium Licensing}. Cambridge's simulation helps explain another instrument: its reported accuracy is not assigned to the Andalusi design, which the project account read does not test {cite:cambridge-equatorie-project project account}.

## Surviving examples

Puig's scholarly account is this display's evidence for the arrangement and treatise dates; this research did not directly read the original construction/use treatises or inspect surviving hardware made by al-Zarqali {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260; Selected References}. The English manuscript in Cambridge's project is a different, later, identified witness, not al-Zarqali's manuscript {cite:cambridge-equatorie-project project account}. Keeping these apart allows a design's documented history to be understood without displaying a comparison object as an original {cite:commons-liverpool-equatorium Description}.

## In culture

MacTutor mentions the lunar crater Arzachel and an astronomy school bearing his name, but these honour the person rather than proving use of this particular equatorium {cite:zarqali-mactutor closing paragraphs}. Digital museum interpretation can also repeat a mistaken author or instrument attribution: Cambridge's project corrects its manuscript's attribution from Chaucer to Westwyk {cite:cambridge-equatorie-project project account}. Names remain research clues, not substitutes for identifying which text and which design a story concerns {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

## For young readers

Imagine a diagram helping you follow a planet's position: al-Zarqali gathered larger circles on one plate and smaller ones on another; this represents a calculation, not the sky itself {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Looking at the photograph, ask whether it is the scholar's own instrument or something made later: here it came centuries later and is shown for comparison, so its parts need not match his description {cite:commons-liverpool-equatorium Description}. A good question preserves the difference between admiring a picture and understanding the evidence behind its story {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.
