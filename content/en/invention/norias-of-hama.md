## Summary
On the Orontes at Hama, seventeen giant wooden wheels still turn. Each is a noria — a current-driven water-raising wheel: the river itself pushes the wheel round, box-shaped compartments on its rim fill at the bottom and tip their water into an aqueduct at the top, and the city drinks. The largest, the noria al-Muhammadiyya built in 761/1361, is about twenty metres across and was registered in 2006 as a Historic Mechanical Engineering Landmark. EI2 takes the Hama wheels as the pattern of the medieval Islamic noria {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-norias-of-hama-en}.

## What it is
A noria (Arabic na'ura, pl. nawa'ir — probably an Aramaic word) is a water-raising wheel powered by the current it drinks from. Timber framing, an axle in bearings between two piers in the stream, paddles that catch the flow, and compartments or pots around the rim that carry the water up and spill it into a channel feeding gardens, baths and fountains {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The world before it
The machine is older than Islam. Vitruvius gives the first known description — brief but unambiguous — in his tenth book on architecture, and a mosaic dated to about 350 CE at Apamea, downstream on the same Orontes, shows a water wheel at work near Hama centuries before the conquests {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-norias-of-hama-en}.

## The story
Under Islamic rule the noria grew to its greatest size. Al-Baladhuri records the first Islamic mention: Bilal ibn Abi Burda erected norias on a canal in the Basra area about the year 700. Al-Muqaddasi counts large numbers of them — calling them dawlab — at Ahwaz and on the river Kur in Fars. Al-Idrisi describes one of about 90 cubits, some 50 metres across. And at Hama, where the Orontes runs slow between high banks, the wheels became the very machinery of the city: possibly turning there by the ninth century, many of them by the late twelfth {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-norias-of-hama-en}.

## The maker(s)
Generations of unnamed Hama wheelwrights. The record's named figures are witnesses and patrons rather than inventors: Bilal ibn Abi Burda, the early Islamic commissioner of norias near Basra; the geographers who measured and described — al-Muqaddasi, al-Idrisi; and the unknown sponsors of 1361 whose wheel, the Muhammadiyya, still turns {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-norias-of-hama-en}.

## How it works
The wheel stands in the river on bearings between two columns. Paddles on the rim dip into the current and the flow itself rotates the wheel — no animal, no gears, no fuel. Compartments (or lashed earthenware pots) around the outer rim fill at the bottom of their travel, rise with the wheel, and discharge at the top into the aqueduct that carries the water to town and field. At Hama the aqueduct crowns the wheels — twenty metres of timber held in a single vertical plane {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The spread
Across the whole Islamic world and beyond it. EI2 notes their abundance in the sources, maps the norias of Islamic Iberia (Valencia's are plotted by Glick), and records the machine's passage into other parts of Europe and eventually to the New World — the wheel at Murcia still worked in 1990. Hama is the surviving capital of the type {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it was built on
The Roman water-lifting wheel described by Vitruvius and pictured at Apamea; the same hydraulic tradition that produced the animal-driven saqiya; and Syria's river landscape, where a gentle gradient and high banks make a current-driven lifter the obvious answer {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-norias-of-hama-en}.

## What was built on it
The noria colonies of al-Andalus and the Maghreb, Fes's own noria tradition, the wheels that crossed the Atlantic with the conquest era — and, at a conceptual remove, every water wheel that takes free power from a flowing stream {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Impact on daily life
At Hama, total: gardens, orchards, baths, mosques, drinking water — all raised by the river itself. The sound of the wheels, a deep slow creaking, became part of the city's identity; people built their evenings and their proverbs around it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-norias-of-hama-en}.

## How it changed history
It did not change history so much as sustain it — for a thousand years the norias made a riverside garden city possible where pumping water by animal power would have been ruinously expensive. Their historical weight is endurance: the oldest continuously working machines of their scale anywhere {cite:wikipedia-norias-of-hama-en}.

## Who invented it?
Not a Muslim invention, and the record says so: the machine is Roman, perhaps older — Vitruvius described it, and the Apamea mosaic shows it near Hama before Islam. What Islamic civilisation did was make it bigger, more numerous and longer-lived than anywhere else — the Hama wheels are the medieval summit of the type {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Today
Seventeen norias remain, kept working as living heritage. The al-Muhammadiyya noria of 1361 carries the American Society of Mechanical Engineers' landmark plaque (2006). They survived war and neglect; their restoration has been a point of civic pride {cite:wikipedia-norias-of-hama-en}.

## Surviving examples
Hama's seventeen — a unique concentration. Elsewhere, norias survive or survived at Murcia, at Fes, across Andalusia's huerta; but nowhere else do so many medieval wheels turn together on one river {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-norias-of-hama-en}.

## In culture
The groaning of the nawa'ir is the sound of Hama — poets and travellers remarked on it, and the wheels became the city's emblem, photographed, painted and defended as heritage {cite:wikipedia-norias-of-hama-en}.

## For young readers
Imagine a wooden wheel as tall as a six-storey building turning slowly in a river. Little boxes around its edge fill with water at the bottom, ride to the top, and pour into a channel that carries the water to the city's gardens. The river itself pushes the wheel — no engines needed. In the Syrian city of Hama, seventeen of these wheels have been turning for hundreds of years, and they still creak and sing today.
