## Summary

[[person/al-mahani|Al-Mahani]] tried to move an Archimedean problem from solid geometry into algebra: where should a plane cut a sphere so that the two resulting volumes have a prescribed ratio? He reached an equation involving a cube, a square and a number, but did not solve it. MacTutor and Dold-Samplonius report Khayyam's account and al-Khazin's later solution using conic sections {cite:mahani-mactutor cubic} {cite:dold-samplonius-mahani-dsb cubic}.

The contribution recorded is formulation, not the solution he could not complete, the invention of sphere cutting, or all cubic equations. It remains distinct from [[invention/khayyam-geometric-cubics|Khayyam's systematic geometric solutions]]. Changing the language of a question can alter what later investigators can attempt, even when its proposer cannot finish the solution {cite:mahani-mactutor historical-assessment} {cite:tdv-mahani-fazlioglu algebra}.

## What it is

The requested ratio concerns volumes, not heights or surface areas. A plane near a pole creates a small cap and a larger remainder; its position must match the specified proportion. The inherited problem is from On the Sphere and Cylinder, book II, proposition4. The museum does not attribute the original question to al-Mahani {cite:dold-samplonius-mahani-dsb cubic}.

TDV writes the modern form x³+c=ax², while Dold-Samplonius uses different coefficient names. Different letters do not create different historical problems. ## How it works

This is a modern museum explanation, not a reconstruction of every original step. Let R be the sphere's radius and h the cap height measured from its pole to the cutting plane. The cap volume is V=πh²(3R−h)/3; the whole sphere has volume4πR³/3. The cut-circle radius satisfies a²=h(2R−h). These are three-dimensional volumes, not disk or dome surface areas {cite:weisstein-spherical-cap eqs2-6}.

Set t=h/R and let q be the cap's share of the entire sphere volume. Cancelling units gives q=t²(3−t)/4, hence t³+4q=3t². A cube, squares and a number have appeared from geometry. The physical interval is0≤t≤2: endpoints are an empty cap and a whole sphere, while two positive pieces require0<q<1. The normalization is ours, not a transcription of al-Mahani {cite:weisstein-spherical-cap eq6}.

Our independent example t=1/2 gives q=5/32. The cap-to-remainder ratio is5:27, not5:32; the latter compares cap to whole. Substitution gives t³+4q=1/8+5/8=3/4, also3t²=3/4. These numbers check the formulation, not a claim that al-Mahani used them or completed the historical solution.

For half the volume, q=1/2, the modern equation becomes t³−3t²+2=0, factorized as(t−1)(t²−2t−2)=0. Its roots are1,1+√3 and1−√3. Only1 lies in the physical cap interval. Not every algebraic root represents a possible cut, and choosing an easy central-section example does not solve the original problem for every prescribed ratio.

In the modern check, the derivative of q with respect to t is3t(2−t)/4, positive between0 and2. Increasing cap height therefore increases its volume share, with one interior height for each interior share. Other polynomial roots do not multiply the physical cuts. This proves a property of our modern model, not that al-Mahani used differential calculus or that his lost reasoning has been recovered {cite:weisstein-spherical-cap eq6}.

## What it was built on

The given condition is a ratio between two pieces; the required relation connects cap volume and cutting-plane position. The model assumes an ideal sphere and plane, not irregular fruit or a vessel of unknown thickness. The modern example shows why reformulation requires defining the quantity, not merely writing a cube beside something round {cite:weisstein-spherical-cap definition-and-eq6}.

For a prescribed ratio m:n, the cap share is q=m/(m+n), not m/n. Finding the corresponding height returns the algebra to geometry. This is our explanation of the reported ratio condition, not a literal translation of Archimedes or a claim that his original text was examined for this batch {cite:dold-samplonius-mahani-dsb prescribed-ratio}.
