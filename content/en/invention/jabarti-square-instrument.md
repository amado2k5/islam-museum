## Summary
Al-ala al-murabba'a — "the square instrument" — is an instrument invented by Hasan ibn Ibrahim al-Jabarti, the Egyptian Hanafi jurist, mathematician and astronomer (1110–1188 AH / 1698–1774 CE). In his biography of his father inside Aja'ib al-athar, the historian Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti records that he made instruments, "among them the square instrument for finding directions and azimuth" — designed, that is, to fix the bearings of landmarks and the azimuths of celestial bodies. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} A wider recension of the obituary reads the same line with the addition "and inclinations, by the easiest means and shortest way", and names alongside it "the historical circle" and "barqar al-daraja" among his invented instruments. {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary pp. 458–459} No surviving specimen is known and no construction account is read; the record remains a testimony to an instrument's name and function inside a documented scholarly household.

## What it is
"The square instrument" is the name as it appears in the obituary; the sources read give no shape, dimensions or graduated divisions, so the description stays confined to what the obituary line says: an instrument whose purpose was finding directions and azimuth. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} Directions here means the quarters of the earth or sky, and azimuth is the horizontal angle measured around the horizon to a body's position — its bearing from the observing point, as distinct from its altitude measured up from the horizon. {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary p. 458} In the wider recension the obituary adds "inclinations" — the tilts of bodies or landmarks away from their tracks or positions — and describes the instrument as delivering the result "by the easiest means and shortest way". {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary p. 459} The general field is therefore clear — an observing and orienting tool — but its technical form is not something this record claims to reconstruct.

## The world before it
Al-Jabarti lived at the tail of a long tradition of astronomical-instrument making in Egypt, Syria, Morocco and al-Andalus: astrolabes, quadrants, sundials and altitude instruments, with which prayer times, qibla direction and the altitudes of bodies were read. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 515} He himself had read "the science of the quadrant" with Shaykh Muhammad al-Najjahi from 1144 AH, and went on by himself into the quadrant, sundial projections and the zij until "the leadership of the craft passed to him" — until he became the reference of the craft, not merely its pupil. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 510} In his house he gathered the brass astronomical globes bought from the estate of Hasan Efendi al-Ruznamaji, "the altitude instruments, the astrolabes, the quadrants and the tools of carpenters, turners, binders and goldsmiths", and clockmakers and craftsmen of the trades used to gather at his door. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 515} His environment was thus a living workshop of knowledge: books, tools and makers who frequented him — and out of this milieu, not out of nothing, came his square instrument.

## The story
Hasan al-Jabarti was born in 1110 AH into a family of the land of Jabart — the land of Zayla' in Abyssinia — whose people trace their descent to Aqil ibn Abi Talib, and whom his father describes as following the Hanafi and Shafi'i schools, with residential halls in Medina, Mecca and al-Azhar mosque. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 502} He memorised the Qur'an at ten and then the standard texts, and was licensed by the son of Shaykh al-Shurunbulali at thirteen after reading his father's Nur al-idah with him. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 503} He then perfected thuluth and naskh hand with Abd Allah Efendi al-Anisi and Hasan Efendi al-Diya'i, then ta'liq, and learned Persian and Turkish so well that Persians and Turks often took him for one of their own. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 510}

In 1144 AH he took up mathematics under Shaykh Muhammad al-Najjahi, reading the timekeeping texts until his teacher's knowledge was exhausted, then went on alone into the quadrant, the sundial projections and the zij "until the leadership of the craft passed to him". {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 510} When Shaykh Husam al-Din al-Hindi, versed in the mathematical and philosophical sciences, came to Egypt he took him into his house and read with him al-Jaghmini with Qadizade's commentary, the Tadhkira, al-Abhari's Hidayat al-hikma, Ashkal al-ta'sis, the Tahrir of Euclid, the Intermediate books, arithmetic, geography and surveying — and declined to learn from him alchemy, "the divine art". {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 510} He learned the science of magic squares from Shaykh Muhammad al-Ghulani al-Kashnawi. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 510}

Then came the practical science: he acquired fine books, and Sultan Mustafa sent him copies from his library; among the foreign books gathered at his place were the Gulistan, the Diwan of Hafiz, the Shahnama and Kalila wa-Dimna; from the estate of the al-Ruznamaji he bought the brass astronomical globes, and he collected the altitude instruments, astrolabes, quadrants and the tools of carpenters, turners, binders and goldsmiths — and clockmakers and craftsmen of the trades used to gather at his house. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 515} His son relates that students from the Franks came to him and read geometry with him in 1159 AH, gave him gifts of their own manufacture, and — in the son's view — spread that science in their lands. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 515} He drew many quadrant-dials and sundials on marble and set them up at al-Azhar, the Ashrafiyya, the mosque of Qawsun and the shrine of Imam al-Shafi'i. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 515}

When his students had become proficient he gave up teaching these sciences, referring Arab students to his pupil Muhammad ibn Isma'il al-Nafrawi and Persians and Turks to Mahmud Efendi al-Nishi, and devoted himself to law and fatwas until the judges trusted no fatwa but his. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} His son says he bothered with authorship only in matters of importance, and lists his treatises: Nuzhat al-ayn fi zakat al-ma'danayn, Raf' al-ishkal bi-zuhur al-ushr fi al-ushr, al-Aqwal al-mu'riba 'an ahwal al-ashriba, Bulugh al-amal fi kayfiyyat al-istiqbal, al-Jadawil al-bahiyya on prosody, al-Durr al-thamin fi 'ilm al-mawazin, two unfinished glosses, and a hajj manual — "and instruments he invented, among them the square instrument for finding directions and azimuth". {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} The instrument is thus no footnote to his biography but an entry in his list of works and products alike.

In the wider recension of the obituary a longer list of compositions appears — among them a work on the steelyard, "the most just instrument" of weights, and a short compendium on the astrolabe-quadrant — then: "and the respectable drawings he had and the invented useful instruments, among them the square instrument for finding directions, azimuth and inclinations by the easiest means and shortest way, and the historical circle and barqar al-daraja". {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary pp. 458–459} The notice then turns to the reform of Egypt's weights and measures: when defect was found in the balances and the standard domes, he called in blacksmiths and coppersmiths, drew up the weights, dishes and standards by the method of extraction on the basis of practical science, spent his own money, showed the chief weighers where the error lay, until the measures were rectified and set right. {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary p. 459} The Moreh editor notes that in the margin of a Chester Beatty manuscript of Raf' al-ishkal there is a note in his hand about laying out the basin of the Husayni mosque in 1175 AH on a design he drew — evidence that his drawings were engineering, not ornament. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516}

## The maker(s)
The maker is documented by name and lineage: Hasan ibn Ibrahim ibn Hasan ibn Ali ibn Muhammad ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Zayla'i al-Jabarti al-Aqili al-Hanafi, kunya Abu al-Tadani and laqab Badr al-Din. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 502} His son opens the obituary by calling him "the philosopher of Islam" and the authority of his age in Abu Hanifa's law — a Hanafi jurist among the scholars of al-Azhar — a description that fixes both his school and the standing in which he was remembered. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 502} He was born in 1110 AH and died on the first of Safar 1188 AH — 13 April 1774 CE — aged seventy-seven, after twelve days of bilious cholera. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 524} His profession was that of the jurist-mufti to whom the judges' fatwas came, not that of the craftsman; yet he was a working astronomer who gathered instruments and read their sciences until "the leadership of the craft passed to him" — and it is from that station that his invented instruments come. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 510} {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516}

## How it works
The sources read do not reconstruct the instrument or describe its mechanism, so every geometric account of it would be undocumented here. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} What is known is the purpose, not the mechanism: "finding directions and azimuth" — that observation through or upon it returns to the user directions and the azimuths of bodies. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} In the wider recension "inclinations" is added — in the old astronomical idiom the tilts of bodies or landmarks from their paths or positions — and the instrument is said to deliver the answer "by the easiest means and shortest way". {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary p. 458} This places it in the family of combined observing instruments: what it gathers on one disc or board once needed more than one tool — and in al-Jabarti's milieu, where he had read the quadrant, the projections and the zij and collected astrolabes and quadrants, the instrument looks like an extension of the same logic of combination that marked his output. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 510} {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 515} But this is a reading of context, not a transmission of mechanism, and the record claims no more.

## The spread
No source read says the square instrument travelled beyond al-Jabarti's house or entered a maker's workshop or was copied into an instrument book. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} Its only reach is a line in his son's obituary, inside the history of Egypt's events — the instrument's trace lies on the book's own chronological axis, not on any independent network. {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary pp. 457–460} Its appearance in the list of "invented useful instruments" — together with "the historical circle" and "barqar al-daraja" — suggests it was not an isolated oddity but part of an integrated instrumental output in an environment where the craft was exercised in his presence. {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary p. 458} And the instrument's legacy stayed locked in the text: no known copy, no traced drawings, and no name of a maker who executed it if al-Jabarti was designer rather than executor.

## What it was built on
Al-Jabarti most likely built on three overlapping traditions he had read and lived: the science of the quadrant and sundial projections he had carried "until the leadership of the craft passed to him", his own collection of astrolabes, quadrants and altitude instruments, and the craft of the artisans who gathered in his house — carpenters, turners, binders, goldsmiths and clockmakers — so that he knew what hands could make and what they could not. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 510} {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 515} The very name "square" suggests a simple geometric form — a square board or disc — but that is an inference from the naming, not a sourced description. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} The text names no earlier model he copied or improved; in the record the instrument is a personal invention inside a general tradition, not a documented refinement of a specific piece.

## What was built on it
No source read names a later instrument built on the square instrument or credits it with an improvement. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} In the wider recension "the historical circle" and "barqar al-daraja" are named alongside it among his invented instruments — other instruments of his, with no proven descendants of their own. {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary p. 458} Its appearance in his obituary beside the list of his compositions — "the most just instrument" of weights and a short compendium on the astrolabe-quadrant — suggests that instrumental production and authorship ran parallel in his work: he describes an instrument and writes on its kin. {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary pp. 458–459} But there is no proven chain of dependence from his instrument to any known later tool.

## Impact on daily life
The instrument's daily effect — had it been used as its function suggests — would have been to shorten the bearing calculation for whoever carried or set it up: instead of gathering several references or memorising the sky, one reads off a single board the bearing of landmarks or their position relative to oneself. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} And this pattern — joining the reading of time and direction in one tool — was a practical want in a community that keeps prayer times, qibla direction and the stars together. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 515} But the record does not prove the square instrument actually lived among people; most likely it remained a household instrument — al-Jabarti's own house, where the craftsmen gathered — not a market commodity. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 515} {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary pp. 457–460}

## How it changed history
Its record — not the instrument itself — changed our understanding of instrument history in Ottoman Egypt: it proves that a jurist and man of faith made observing instruments, that al-Jabarti's house was a meeting point of number-science, jurisprudence and craft, and that the obituary lists instruments among the compositions, not in the margins of trades. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary p. 458} More importantly the documentation comes from the son himself — the historian Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti — who recorded his father's biography with critical fidelity, so the son's testimony here is not flattery but a family record written by a hand that knew the craft. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 502} {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 524} This record does not claim the instrument caused a general technical change; its role in history is that it survived as a witness to the level of instrument making in the late eighteenth century AH, when scholars in Egypt still made instruments with their own hands and described them to their sons.

## Who invented it?
The answer is in the obituary text: al-Jabarti himself. His son says of his compositions: "and instruments he invented, among them the square instrument for finding directions and azimuth" — the verb "invented" has al-Jabarti as its subject, not an anonymous or collective one. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} The wider recension strengthens the attribution: "and the respectable drawings he had and the invented useful instruments" — invention attributed to him by name inside his own biography. {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary p. 458} No source read names a rival or partner; and the craft in his house was integral and individual — he designed, drew and described — not a workshop project, so the question "which maker executed it" does not arise here. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 515} The methodological caveat stands: all documentation comes from a family obituary; no model was inspected and no treatise describing it was read, so the attribution is "original" in the sense of the text, not of an examined piece.

## Today
Al-ala al-murabba'a remains a subject in the history of astronomical instruments: a name in a line, a function in words, with no model or drawing on display. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} And its practical function — finding directions and azimuth — is served today by entirely different tools: the pocket compass, the compass needle in the phone, digital ephemerides that compute a body's azimuth moment by moment, and computer astrolabes. {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary p. 458} The sources read establish no direct lineage from the square instrument to any of them; similarity of purpose does not make a chain of descent. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} Displayed in this museum is a photograph of an instrument of the same broad family — a Moroccan astrolabe-quadrant dated 1804, held by the Museum of the History of Science, Oxford — not as a substitute for ours but as a witness that joining observation and direction in a single piece remained a living inclination in Islamic instrument making two centuries after al-Jabarti. {cite:commons-mhs-astrolabe-quadrant}

## Surviving examples
No surviving specimen of the square instrument is known, and no drawing of it appears in the sources read; the only witness is the obituary text itself — a line in Aja'ib al-athar naming it and its function inside the list of his invented instruments. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary pp. 458–459} The wider recension of the obituary adds the names of his other instruments — "the historical circle" and "barqar al-daraja" — and shows the square instrument was part of a multiple instrumental output, not a single isolated oddity. {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary p. 458} And his engineering drawings remain a parallel witness: a note in his hand on a Chester Beatty manuscript about designing the basin of the Husayni mosque in 1175 AH shows he drew for executors, not only for contemplators. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} These textual witnesses — not the piece — are all the record has.

## In culture
The square instrument — as far as the sources reach us — never entered the literature of instruments or the theoretical books of astronomy; it is confined to a family biography. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} But its place inside Aja'ib al-athar itself makes it a cultural witness: a book written by a historian of Egypt that documents how men of religion and jurisprudence in Cairo made instruments and described them to their sons, and how craftsmen used to gather in their houses. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 515} {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary p. 460} The picture is completed by the fact that the obituary itself came inside Aja'ib al-athar, the Egyptian history written by his son Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti — so the elder al-Jabarti was a scholar of instruments inside a house that was writing the history of its country, a rare type in our heritage. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 502} {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} His name is read today in the biographies of al-Azhar's scholars and in catalogues of old instruments, not in instrument museums; his proper place is the history books that preserve what hands made, not what survived of their making.

## For young readers
Imagine a square board marked with lines and names, which al-Jabarti — an Egyptian scholar of more than two hundred years ago — set up so that through it one could learn which way one faced. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 516} In his house there were books of jurisprudence and stars together, craftsmen who came to repair his instruments, and Frankish students who read geometry with him — and his son the historian wrote all that down in Aja'ib al-athar. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p. 515} Nothing of his board survives, but its name does, in a small line recording that he invented instruments — among them "the square instrument" — to know directions and azimuth by. {cite:arabicgenius-ajaib-al-athar obituary p. 458}
