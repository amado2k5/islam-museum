## Summary
Flowing water can help lift other water: that is the idea of a paired-piston pump described in [[person/al-jazari-engineer|al-Jazari's]] book, driven by a waterwheel, gears and a slotted rod {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. Rotation becomes back-and-forth motion in the piston rods; Salim al-Hassani describes a synchronized pair of suction pumps {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506} {cite:fstc-jazari-mechanical-genius pump discussion}. The book belongs to his Artuqid service at Amid; the references read give 1205 or 1206 for its completion {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506} {cite:fstc-jazari-mechanical-genius biography}. The contribution is a particular engineering arrangement, not invention of all water lifting; the museum model is a reconstruction {cite:smg-jazari-double-pump catalogue}.

## What it is
This is a pump powered by flowing water, not a bucket lifted by hand. TDV traces the drive from wheel to two gears, then to the rod and piston rods {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. The piston is the moving part inside the pump body; the reference follows its rod's forward and backward travel rather than stopping at the rotating wheel {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}.

The Science Museum Group calls it a double-piston pump; al-Hassani describes double action and suction pipes {cite:smg-jazari-double-pump catalogue} {cite:fstc-jazari-mechanical-genius pump discussion}. These name the documented arrangement, without adding cylinder dimensions or the details of every valve from memory.

## The world before it
In TDV, Sadettin Ökten places al-Jazari within an engineering tradition extending from the Hellenistic period to the Banu Musa, describing his effort to perfect earlier machines {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. Clocks, mechanical devices and water raising therefore did not begin with this one book.

That general context does not identify a particular machine as this pump's direct prototype. What can be followed closely is the arrangement the reference actually explains.

## The story
TDV records al-Jazari's Artuqid court service at [[place/amid|Amid]] in 1181–1206 and the book commissioned by Nasir al-Din Mahmud {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. It describes fifty devices in six divisions, including water raising rather than clocks and banquet devices alone {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}.

The encyclopedia dates completion to 1205; al-Hassani gives 1206, while the Science Museum catalogue calls the published design “c. 1205” {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506} {cite:fstc-jazari-mechanical-genius biography} {cite:smg-jazari-double-pump catalogue}. The difference is preserved, not repaired with an invented day. These date the documentary witness, not a dated installation certificate for a pump in a particular well.

## The maker(s)
The three references attribute the arrangement to al-Jazari as author and engineer; the record credits him as an improver in an existing tradition, following TDV's characterization {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506} {cite:fstc-jazari-mechanical-genius pump discussion} {cite:smg-jazari-double-pump catalogue}. It does not award him invention of every gear and pin.

TDV calls him an Islamic scholar, the basis for Muslim identity here. The museum's Sunni default frame is stated explicitly, not presented as a personal school established by that phrase {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. Service dates are not converted into birth/death dates; unnamed assistants are not supplied.

## How it works
TDV's drive can be followed step by step {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}:

1. Flowing water turns the wheel.
2. The wheel drives a gear in a vertical plane.
3. This engages another gear in a horizontal plane.
4. A vertical pin near the horizontal gear's rim passes through a slot in a rod.
5. The rod's other end is fixed by another pin; the piston rods are connected to it.
6. As the gear rotates, the slotted rod makes an angular movement and the piston rods travel back and forth, operating the pumps.

Rotation has not disappeared: the linkage translates its effect into another movement. Al-Hassani's synchronized pair helps show the machine as an interconnected arrangement, not two pistons independently worked by hand {cite:fstc-jazari-mechanical-genius pump discussion}.

The published manuscript illustration lets the reader inspect wheel, gears, rods and water channel, but is not a measured flow-rate or lifting-height experiment {cite:fstc-jazari-mechanical-genius figure 6}. The site's diagram is a historical relationship chain, not a tracing of that drawing or a scale construction plan.

## The spread
The sources follow the book, its study and reconstruction, but give no dated itinerary of working pumps travelling from Amid to other cities {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506} {cite:fstc-jazari-mechanical-genius pump discussion}. Modern fame is not converted into a diffusion map.

## What it was built on
TDV supplies ancient engineering and the Banu Musa as context, not a component-by-component chain for this pump alone {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. It also discusses al-Jazari's component manufacture, assembly, casting and models: text, picture and practical work belong together {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}.

That context explains “improvement”: a particular arrangement of wheel, gears, rod and paired pistons, rather than a sweeping claim to mechanical engineering itself. It does not mean one reference has surveyed every earlier pump worldwide.

## What was built on it
TDV records partial German translations in 1908–1921, Donald Hill's complete English translation in 1974 and Ahmad Yusuf al-Hassan's Arabic edition in 1979 {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. These are documented stages of studying the book, not factories proven to have borrowed their machines directly.

The modern physical witness is the Science Museum Group model; al-Hassani also presents a digital reconstruction of the synchronized pair {cite:smg-jazari-double-pump catalogue} {cite:fstc-jazari-mechanical-genius figure 7}. Both help investigate the old description. A successful reconstruction does not establish every intervening historical use.

## Impact on daily life
TDV judges water-raising machines economically more important than elaborate clocks, although the book gives them less space {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. This offers a different view from entertainment automata: flowing-water power doing lifting work, not only producing a visual and audible display.

The source gives no irrigated acreage, labour saving or annual financial return for this pump. The page does not fill the gap with a fictional farmer or performance figures read off a drawing.

## How it changed history
Ökten emphasizes double action, conversion of rotation into reciprocation and what he calls the first known use of a suction pipe {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. His assessment calls it an early example for later pumps and the steam engine {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}.

That is an attributed historical assessment, not an independently documented transfer to a particular steam engine. The reader can examine a described arrangement, its drawing and reconstruction while keeping technical resemblance distinct from evidence of transmission.

## Who invented it?
**The Islamic-history reference:** TDV presents an engineer improving earlier traditions, gives this pump particular importance and advances a suction-pipe priority claim {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}.

**The modern engineering survey:** Al-Hassani emphasizes paired pumps, suction pipes and motion conversion among the book's innovations, alongside a digital model {cite:fstc-jazari-mechanical-genius pump discussion}.

**The museum record:** The Science Museum attributes the design to al-Jazari and calls its object a half-size model {cite:smg-jazari-double-pump catalogue}. The page establishes attribution of this device, not a global verdict that he first discovered every form of pumping or reciprocation.

## Today
The Science Museum Group catalogues model 1976-482, listing wood, copper, iron and steel and crediting the World of Islam Festival Trust {cite:smg-jazari-double-pump catalogue}. It can be compared with text and historical drawing, not treated as an object excavated from al-Jazari's workshop.

## In culture
Al-Hassani presents the pump alongside clocks and banquet devices in his public account of al-Jazari, connecting it to FSTC's modern work and *1001 Inventions* {cite:fstc-jazari-mechanical-genius survey}. This documents a place in public science history, not an unchecked appearance in a film, stamp or banknote.

## For young readers
Imagine flowing water turning a wheel. Its teeth drive another gear, and a pin with a slotted rod converts rotation into back-and-forth movement in the pump rods, as the reference explains {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. Al-Jazari described the idea in his book; the Science Museum's model is a reconstruction that helps explain it, not an original from his lifetime {cite:smg-jazari-double-pump catalogue}.
