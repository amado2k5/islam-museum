## Summary

Water rises in a single column, then the view changes to six curving jets without somebody redirecting the nozzle at each change. This is the fountain [[person/al-jazari-engineer|al-Jazari]] describes in CategoryIV, chapter1, assigning one constant hour to each display while water continues to arrive {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.157–158}. Behind the spectacle are two buckets, a tilting pipe and two tanks: filling a bucket switches the supply. This is a particular assembly within an older fountain tradition, not the invention of flowing water, the first automatic fountain, or a machine supplying its own energy {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.157–158,263}.

## What it is

One fountain in a pool changes its appearance through two separate delivery routes. A narrow pipe serves the central opening; a wider passage feeds a sphere with six holes around its upper part. The former gives a vertical jet, the latter arcs directed over the pool {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.157}. The metal does not transform from a stick into six curves: water reaches different outlets.

The two-bucket title does not cover every fountain in the book. Chapter2 describes another arrangement with two fountains, and later chapters introduce different mechanisms and shapes; their features are not silently combined here {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.158–159,263}. Nor is each nozzle a separate invention. Automatic means the change repeats under the stated supply conditions, not that the installation needs no resource, preparation or attention.

## The world before it

Al-Jazari opens the category by discussing arrangements he attributes to the Banu Musa: vanes turned by wind or water, then a balance-like pipe and a small accumulating vessel {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.157}. His discussion places the fountain within an earlier technical tradition. The contribution presented here is a particular choice of switching timer and its integration with two water displays, not the first appearance of every component.

His criticism is not a complete trial of earlier engineers. One account seems to him to restore the pipe before its water has finished emptying, but he explicitly does not know whether the confusion belongs to the original or a copy {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.157}. We retain that qualification rather than declare all Banu Musa devices failures or treat a later drawing as transparent evidence of their actual hardware.

## The story

The problem is not simply to produce motion but to let viewers see its effect. Al-Jazari considers a change at every vane revolution too rapid for the display to appear fully {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.157}. His arrangement keeps display water flowing while a small branch accumulates timing water. The functions meet when the bucket tips, rather than tying every change directly to rapid rotation.

TDV places the author in Artuqid service at [[place/amid|Amid]], writing his book at Nasir al-Din Mahmud's request {cite:tdv-cezeri pp.505–506}. This is the author's setting, not a surviving fountain site examined here. Hill's introduction derives1204 or1206 from manuscript differences, against TDV's1205; the book-date disagreement remains, without inventing this fountain's inauguration year or a Hijri date {cite:jazari-hill-1974 introduction-life} {cite:tdv-cezeri pp.505–506}.

## How it works

Above the two tanks a pipe pivots about its centre, receiving supply water through a funnel. When tilted to one side it delivers most water to that tank; a small branch with a calibrated outlet directs a smaller amount into the associated bucket {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.157}. The larger flow serves the spectacle, the smaller accumulates toward the switching event. Distinguishing those functions makes the drawing intelligible.

When full, the bucket tips and empties into its tank. Its rear end raises the pipe through a connected rod, lowering the opposite end so supply now enters the other tank and starts filling its timing bucket. That bucket's eventual movement returns the assembly to the first state {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.158}. The account needs no person to perform each switch, but repetition depends on continuing external supply, not water raising itself back to the inlet.

Separate delivery paths distinguish the vertical column from the six arcs. The narrower pipe runs inside the wider and projects above the sphere, while the other route serves the holes around it {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.157–158,263}. Understanding this relationship does not require copied historical diameters or soldering instructions. We do not convert Baghdadi ratls and old length units into unsupported modern fabrication dimensions.

Hill explains that the machinery house needs access to a suitable water source and sufficient elevation above the display pool; dimensions depend on flow, supply and delivery head, and the intended jet height {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.263}. Consequently “one hour” is a stated design interval, not measured precision certified for every site. Calibration, bucket capacity and supply conditions matter. No model was built here and no timing error was experimentally recorded.

## What it was built on

The assembly uses accumulating water, a central pivot, a vessel tipping when filled, and separate passages to fixed outlets. The author himself refers back to earlier descriptions of bucket form and motion {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.157–158}. Its interest lies in arranging relations and timing events, not claiming each simple element as a new discovery.

Comparison with the [[invention/jazari-musical-boat|musical boat]] highlights a supply difference: the boat carries finite water and collects it internally; this fountain requires continued external inflow {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.107–109,157–158}. This is a teaching comparison, not a new ancestry edge. Likewise Hill's emphasis on surveying the site's levels does not establish direct dependence on a particular book by [[person/al-karaji|al-Karaji]] {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.263}.

## Impact on daily life

The direct effect described is visual: water holds one form for a time, then another, rather than presenting an unchanging view {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.157}. Delaying alternation can be read as attention to the viewer's time, since the author criticizes intervals too brief for the effect to appear. This is a bounded interpretation of display purpose, not evidence for the comfort or water consumption of an entire city.

Beauty must also be distinguished from drinking-water or irrigation service. The chapter describes a pool fountain and its feed; it establishes neither water sterilization nor agricultural savings {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.157–158}. Inclusion in the water domain does not transfer the benefits of every water project to it, or show equal access to its spectacle across society.

## Who invented it?

**Author's account within the declared Sunni frame:** Al-Jazari explains his two-bucket arrangement and discusses earlier descriptions, expressly allowing copying error; we credit this specific account without judging all Banu Musa work from his remarks {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.157–158}. A maker-author explaining his approach is not a worldwide survey of every older fountain.

**Academic interpretation:** Hill explains operation but identifies bearing orientation, omitted projections and details requiring site planning {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.263}. These are not competing claims for a different inventor, but different levels of evidence. Attribution here is a described improvement and integration, not global priority or proof of precise performance from a drawing alone.

## In culture

The text calls the vertical jet a *qadib* and the curved jet a *sawlajan*, explained by Hill through stick and polo-stick imagery {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.157-note}. These words help readers imagine shapes. They are not verified plant names, and shapes from other fountain chapters are not imported into this account.

Writing and drawing together show a water machine as something to see and explain. The story needs no promotional “first smart fountain” label to remain engaging.

## For young readers

Try a museum-made paper activity, not a historical experiment. Draw two cards: one column and six arcs. Imagine a timing bucket holding6 units, receiving2 per step. After3 steps change the card and reset the counter. Repeat for the second state: a complete cycle takes6 steps. These are invented teaching quantities, not Baghdadi ratls or measurements of al-Jazari's machine.

Now give the second side only1 unit per step: it needs6 steps, making a9-step cycle. Equal capacities do not ensure equal times when inflow differs.
