## Summary
The pointed arch — two curving sides meeting at a sharp crown — was an early member of the Islamic architectural family: Umayyad and Abbasid builders used it from Qusayr Amra onward, the Abbasids perfected the four-centred form at Samarra, and it covered Egypt, Persia and al-Andalus before the Gothic made it the grammar of the cathedral. Whether Europe received it from Islamic building or found it again inside its own Romanesque practice is a question this record leaves honestly open {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it is
A two-centred arch: its form is drawn from the intersection of two circles, so the two curved sides meet at a relatively sharp angle at the top. Against a semicircular arch of the same span it carries load with less sideways thrust — the property that let Gothic walls rise and thin {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en}.

## The world before it
The pointed shape is old. Crude pointed arches and pointed drains survive at Bronze Age Nippur and Nineveh; late Roman, Byzantine and Sasanian work knew the form — the Karamagara bridge's seventeen-metre pointed span and the seventh-century Chytroi-Constantia aqueduct in Cyprus being the clearest survivors {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en}.

## The story
In the new Islamic world the arch found a career. Umayyad builders used it alongside the semicircular and horseshoe arch — the sequence from Qusayr Amra (712-715) to Fustat (861-862) shows its centres widening from a tenth of the span to a third, making it ever sharper. Abbasid Samarra gave it its four-centred form at the Qubbat al-Sulaybiyya and Qasr al-Ashiq; the mosque of Ibn Tulun in Cairo (876-879) displays it at scale; and it became characteristic of Fatimid, Persian and Andalusi building {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The maker(s)
No inventor on record. The named patron on this record is Ahmad ibn Tulun, whose congregational mosque in Cairo is the most visited early witness; Warren's scholarship meanwhile shows the form's pre-Islamic Byzantine fabric, so the question is transmission, not authorship {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en}.

## How it works
The arch works by geometry: two arcs struck from separate centres carry the load to the springing points while the raised crown reduces lateral thrust. Twelfth-century builders in Sicily, England and France put it under stone ribs, creating the rib vault — thin ribs channelling weight to pillars, freeing the walls for stained glass {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en}.

## The spread
Within Islam it ran from Umayyad Syria to Abbasid Iraq to Fatimid Egypt and the Persianate east; in Europe it surfaced in the early rib vaults of Durham, Saint-Denis, Lessay and Cefalu, then ruled the cathedrals of Chartres, Reims and Amiens — and the Islamic four-centred type even returned to England as the Tudor arch {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en}.

## What it was built on
The Roman-Byzantine and Sasanian repertoire — the Karamagara bridge, Ravenna and Hagia Irene among Warren's witnesses — which the first Muslim builders inherited with the rest of Near Eastern craft {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en} {cite:warren-creswell-pointed-arch-1991}.

## What was built on it
The Gothic cathedral itself: the pointed arch, rib vault and flying buttress together made height and light structural, from the sexpartite vaults of Sens and Notre-Dame to the quadripartite spans of Chartres and Beauvais {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en}.

## Impact on daily life
Inside the mosque, palace and bathhouse it framed courts and doors; inside the church it framed light — the shape people under both roofs met whenever they looked up {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en}.

## How it changed history
It gave one skyline two languages: the same geometry that arches Samarra and Cordoba arches Chartres and Reims — the clearest stone proof that medieval building was a shared, not a walled, craft {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en}.

## Who invented it?
Contested — and doubly so. The form predates Islam (Byzantine, Roman and Bronze Age witnesses), and its Islamic development for centuries before the Gothic is documented; the open question is whether the Gothic borrowed the form from Islamic building — the Islamic-influence thesis — or grew it independently for structural reasons, as the Durham evidence suggests. The museum keeps both voices {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en} {cite:warren-creswell-pointed-arch-1991} {cite:bony-french-gothic-1983}.

## Today
It stands everywhere: in living mosques and madrasas from Samarra to Isfahan, in the cathedrals, and in the pointed windows of the nineteenth-century Gothic Revival {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en}.

## Surviving examples
Qusayr Amra and Hammam al-Sarah, the Samarra portals, the Ibn Tulun mosque and the Fustat remains on the Islamic side; the Karamagara bridge and the Cyprus aqueduct before it; Durham, Saint-Denis, Cefalu and the great French cathedrals after it {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en}.

## In culture
It became the sign of two arts at once: "Gothic" to Europe, a quiet constant of Islamic building — and the debate over who gave it to whom is itself part of the history of how the two civilisations are read {cite:wikipedia-pointed-arch-en} {cite:bony-french-gothic-1983}.

## For young readers
Imagine a normal arch like half a circle, then squeeze it from both sides so its head rises like an arrow tip: the pointed arch carries the wall higher and leaves room for great windows. Muslims built it in their palaces and mosques for many centuries, and it later became the face of Europe's great churches.
