## Summary
A small gold piece bearing writing rather than a ruler's portrait opens a story about political language and monetary standards. Museum records associate this change in the dinar with [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan]] and a new weight standard; they do not make him the inventor of money or the first person to use gold in exchange {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton}. Our pictured specimen is catalogued by the Met as79AH/698–99, not the British Museum's77AH/696–7 piece described elsewhere as a first-issue coin {cite:met-dinar-453447} {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton}. The distance between a reform and a surviving witness must remain visible in its caption.

## What it is
This entry concerns a particular gold-dinar standard: an inscription-based design without a human figure, alongside the weight change described in the British Museum record from Byzantine4.55g to4.25g {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton}. Those are published standard weights, not the result of weighing our illustrated specimen. The name dinar does not make every object bearing it, in every century, identical in weight or design. The reform combines issuing language, a recurring visual form and a material standard; coinage itself is older. The mention of silver in Abd al-Malik's TDV biography does not transfer the date of one gold type to every silver dirham {cite:tdv-abdulmelik-yildiz}. This subject is deliberately narrower than all Islamic monetary history.

## The world before it
TDV describes Byzantine and Sasanian money circulating in Muslim-ruled territories, while the British Museum record identifies Byzantine precedents for earlier designs {cite:tdv-abdulmelik-yildiz} {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton}. These precedents are essential: the reform did not arrive in a society waiting without money for a sudden invention. AshmoleanHCR6573 supplies a transitional standing-caliph type before inscription-only coinage in the museum's explanation {cite:ashmolean-standing-caliph}. Continuing an older instrument and changing the authority it represents are different processes. Innovation can organize and redefine an existing practice without creating its material from nothing. Calling a series transitional does not make every specimen within it identical, or prove that all regions adopted its successor simultaneously.

## The story
Suyuti transmits al-Askari's narrative linking new papyrus markings, an imperial threat, consultation with Khalid ibn Yazid and a proposal for coinage; that account gives75AH {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.359}. It is a later literary account, not contemporary meeting minutes. The MWNF record describes a76AH reform but dates its British Museum piece77AH/696–7 and calls it a first-issue specimen: this difference within the source is preserved, not quietly erased {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton}. Our Met photograph represents79AH/698–99 {cite:met-dinar-453447}. These dates belong to a narrative, a reform description and two specimens. They are not four interchangeable answers about one day, and no exact day is invented to reconcile them.

## The maker(s)
**Declared Sunni frame:** Abd al-Malik's connection to Islam is not inferred merely from his name, family or office. Suyuti reports, through Ibn Sa'd, personal worship in Medina before accession and traditions about his jurisprudence; TDV describes his own Quran, hadith and Islamic legal studies {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.356} {cite:tdv-abdulmelik-yildiz}. The Sunni designation is the museum's stated interpretive frame, not membership in a later sectarian institution or a verdict on private sincerity.

**Historical reading:** his recorded role is commissioner and political sponsor, not the claim that he engraved dies himself. The read records do not name the maker of our Met face; its API artist and reign fields are blank, and guesses do not fill them {cite:met-dinar-453447}. Recognizing administrators and craftsmen clarifies rather than erases commissioning.

## How it works
**Design.** The British Museum record describes central lines surrounded by a marginal inscription on each side, explicitly without the caliph's or mint's name {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton}. Political communication on money need not consist of a named ruler. We explain the move to writing without reproducing or completing Quranic inscriptions from memory.

**Standard.** The arithmetic difference between4.55g and4.25g is0.30g. This is a modern educational comparison of the published standards, not a loss calculated for every user, an inflation rate or an assay of reduced gold purity {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton}. A standard and the measured weight of a surviving object are different.

**Checking.** The photograph permits observation of raised writing, a circular inscription and an imperfectly circular outline; it cannot replace a balance, a metal assay or examination of the other side {cite:met-dinar-453447}. A museum reading brings description, accession number, date and attribution together. Attractive photography does not certify every historical inference.

## The spread
TDV connects gold and silver issues to Abd al-Malik's administration and changed dependence on older currencies; the Ashmolean situates an Islamic currency within broader state reorganization {cite:tdv-abdulmelik-yildiz} {cite:ashmolean-standing-caliph}. These summaries do not provide a dated map for every market or mint. They cannot establish an instantaneous regional changeover, and a museum specimen alone cannot prove withdrawal of every older piece. Ashmolean's697 catalogue date for its transitional coin overlaps the696–7 range of the British epigraphic issue. That is a reason to resist an oversimplified timeline, not permission to invent a new reconciled date. Types, objects and administrative transitions need to be identified before their chronology can be compared.

## What it was built on
The reform drew on older gold currency, visible political messages on metal and an administrative/craft setting capable of issuing a recurring type. The museum evidence establishes Byzantine precedent and a transitional figural issue {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton} {cite:ashmolean-standing-caliph}. [[invention/tiraz|Tiraz]] offers a comparison for official writing on different media, not another name for the dinar. Suyuti's papyrus story connects them in a particular causal narrative; it is not automatically the sole explanation for every monetary change {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.359}. Cloth, papyrus and gold differ in technique and use even when all express public authority. Comparing these media should preserve those differences instead of multiplying the same contribution under interchangeable labels.

## What was built on it
MWNF describes subsequent prominence of inscription-based coinage, and the Ashmolean explains the new type's removal of the caliph's figural presence {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton} {cite:ashmolean-standing-caliph}. This helps explain a design language, not a direct genealogy connecting this specimen to every modern currency or calligraphic tradition. The79AH object demonstrates a later surviving example of an epigraphic type, not a complete chain of mints or weight changes {cite:met-dinar-453447}. Another present-day product is organized knowledge: connecting an object to its description, dating and uncertainties. Leaving an unrecorded craftsman's name blank is more informative than supplying an invented one, because the reader can distinguish what the chain actually documents from what remains open.

## Impact on daily life
Money circulates, but seeing a museum coin does not tell us what one particular person bought with it or the value of a full day's wages. The described changes in weight standard and issuing design allow concrete questions about recognition and monetary authority, not ready-made answers about food prices or every household's circumstances {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton}. No conversion into today's currency is offered, and gold weight is not confused with purchasing power. The defensible change is narrower: an issuing authority adopted an epigraphic message and a described standard. Users' responses, regional differences and individual transactions need other evidence not contained in the photograph's collection record {cite:met-dinar-453447}.

## How it changed history
The Ashmolean places reform within Abd al-Malik's reorganization after internal conflict; TDV links monetary change with administrative measures including Arabic-language records {cite:ashmolean-standing-caliph} {cite:tdv-abdulmelik-yildiz}. That context makes currency part of a larger policy, not the sole cause of political unity or every institutional success. A coin can document how authority represented itself: writing could convey public identity without a person's portrait or name, as the British Museum description specifies {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton}. The object's survival does not deliver a moral verdict on the state, ruler or opponents. Other attributed accounts in the biography remain intact, rather than disappearing behind a celebratory innovation story.

## Who invented it?
**Sunni transmission:** Suyuti's account assigns Abd al-Malik a decision and Khalid advice, with75AH as its date. Those roles are attributed to the narrative, not independently authenticated through every transmitted chain {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.359}.

**Museum and academic reading:** Byzantine precedent and the transitional type make reform more accurate than invention of money. The Ashmolean reports recent research questioning a simple explanation of the standing-caliph type as a response to Justinian's coin; one confident causal story is not replaced with another unverified one {cite:ashmolean-standing-caliph}. Crediting the ruler does not recover craftsmen's names or settle every differing date. The claim remains specific: commissioning, a described design/standard change and dated witnesses, each read within its limits.

## Today
The catalogue itself needs internal reading. Its detailed British Museum description says that neither caliph nor mint is named, while the page's short description speaks generally about rulers' names on new coinage {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton}. We do not transfer the general sentence to this object as though we had read a name on it. The specific witness description takes precedence for that particular observation, with the other statement's scope made explicit. A museum website does not remove the need to distinguish description from generalization, or to preserve differences rather than silently correct the source.

A visitor can read a catalogue before looking at a coin, then return to the image to distinguish observation from supplied information. The Met lists gold, Syria,2.1cm and79AH/698–99, but the read data do not name a mint city or give this specimen's weight {cite:met-dinar-453447}. A missing field is not an invitation to guess. The public-domain flag in the collection API supports showing the image with attribution; image rights do not establish any particular historical interpretation. New research can change the relationship proposed between types, while the accession number remains a way to trace the same object. Good reuse keeps that stable identifier alongside the uncertainty.

## In culture
Writing makes the coin a readable surface as well as a material for exchange. MWNF characterizes the script as angular Kufic and describes a religious message; the Ashmolean explains the transition from a figure to inscriptions without the caliph's pictorial presence {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton} {cite:ashmolean-standing-caliph}. These are historical descriptions, not rulings imposed on every artwork or later object. Absence of a figure does not remove politics, and religious wording cannot establish the private faith of every holder or die-cutter. Culture here means studying media and attributed meanings, not generalizing about unknown people's inner convictions. No Quranic inscription needs to be transcribed to make that distinction intelligible.
