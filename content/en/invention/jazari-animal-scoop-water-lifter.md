## Summary

In Category V, chapter 4, [[person/al-jazari-engineer|al-Jazari]] describes an animal-powered scoop lifter in which gears carry rotation to a pin that moves through a slot in the ladle stem, raising and lowering the scoop {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185}. The bounded contribution is this integrated arrangement and its written explanation, not a claim that al-Jazari invented animal power, gearing, the crank, or water-lifting everywhere {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185} {cite:tdv-cezeri pp.505–506}. The book's documentary date differs among sources, and a folio in the Met dated 715 AH/1315 CE is a later manuscript witness rather than surviving machinery {cite:jazari-hill-1974 introduction-life} {cite:tdv-cezeri pp.505–506} {cite:met-jazari-scoop-451298 Object record}.

## What it is

This is a machine described for raising water from a well or pool with a scoop carried by a moving stem {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185}. The animal supplies motion to the driving assembly, and the geared mechanism turns that input into a repeated lifting movement {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185} {cite:met-jazari-scoop-451298 Catalogue Entry}. Hill's translation gives dimensions and capacities in the units used by the text; this account leaves them there rather than converting them into modern measures without a verified basis {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185}.

## How it works

The animal's movement turns the driving pole, supplying the energy for the device rather than merely appearing as a decorative figure {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185} {cite:met-jazari-scoop-451298 Catalogue Entry}. Gears transmit that rotation to the lifting assembly described in the chapter {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185}. The arrangement matters because the source of motion and the water-carrying part have different jobs {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185}.

A pin on a rotating part engages the slot in the ladle stem {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185}. As the pin travels with the turning assembly, its movement within the slot drives the stem upward and downward rather than carrying the ladle around in a circle {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185}. The scoop can therefore descend into the water, collect it, and rise again toward the channel shown in the later folio {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185} {cite:met-jazari-scoop-451298 Catalogue Entry}.

This is a conversion from animal-powered rotation to reciprocating movement of a water carrier {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185}. The pin and slot explain the movement; they should not be detached from the rest of the machine and counted as a separate invention {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185}. The text supplies dimensions and capacities for its own design, but neither a measured operating rate nor a modern engineering test is part of the evidence used here {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185}.

The difference from the chapter 3 lifter is especially useful for reading the power path {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.182–185}. In chapter 3, water turns a lower wheel and a jar chain, while a wooden cow is carried above the pool; here an animal supplies the drive for a scoop mechanism {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.182–185}. The chapter 5 pump uses a different arrangement of pistons and water movement, so the shared presence of gears does not make the three designs interchangeable {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.182–188}.
