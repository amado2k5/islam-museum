## Summary

The Remaining Signs of Past Centuries is not a single calendar nor a calculating instrument but a method: in it Abu Rayhan al-Biruni gathered the dates used by the nations of his age, examined their origins and principles, and set them side by side to measure where they agree and where they differ {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179} {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 4–6}. He wrote the book in his twenty-eighth year, as the biographical notices report, and dedicated it to Shams al-Ma'ali Qabus {cite:tdv-biruni-tumer works} {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}. When Hajji Khalifa catalogued the book in the seventeenth century he described it as a "useful book" containing "the dates that nations use and the divergence in the principles that are their foundations" {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}. The Ilkhanid manuscript preserved in the University of Edinburgh Library, copied in 707 AH (1307–08 CE), carries twenty-six paintings showing that the book stayed alive in copying and illustration centuries after its composition {cite:commons-edinburgh-biruni-ms161}.

## What it is

The book is an encyclopaedic digest of history and calendars: after its preliminaries it presents the dates and calendars of the nations — of Persians, Greeks, Romans, Jews, Copts, Muslims and others — with their origins and the principles on which they are computed {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 4–6} {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}. It holds no single table for a single calendar; it holds parallel measures: the epoch from which each calendar counts, the way its years or months are counted, its rules for intercalation, and the seasons of its feasts. On this basis the reader can move a date from one calendar to another and test the divergences between accounts of a single event.

The method is comparative by nature: al-Biruni does not content himself with presenting each system separately but sets it beside the others to show where the nations agree in reckoning time and where they part. This is the "divergence in the principles that are their foundations" that Hajji Khalifa compressed into one line {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}. The book also contains chapters of astronomical computation that help fix times, from the measures of the Sun, the Moon and the seasons {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 407–409} {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 416–417}.

## The world before it

Before this book, calendrical knowledge was distributed: the Persians, Greeks, Jews, Copts and Egyptians each had inherited systems of reckoning, and among Muslims there was the hijri calendar that grew from the Prophet's emigration to Medina and its early rules. No single historian or astronomer had gathered these systems into one work that sets them side by side and measures the differences; each nation kept its calendar and explained it to its own people, and rarely was it compared with another's {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 4–6}.

Al-Biruni's age — the fourth century AH — was an age of transmission: Greek, Roman, Persian and Indian books circulated in translation, and observatories and schools in Bukhara, Gurgan, Ghazna and Isfahan exchanged computations and dates. In this atmosphere a scholar needed a single reference preserving the nations' dates with their principles, rather than travelling among many books each belonging to one nation {cite:tdv-biruni-tumer biography}.

The book does not merely gather dates; its author declares in the opening that the surest road to what was asked is "knowing the reports of the nations of old", and that the duty is to "take the nearest of it, then the next nearest, and the most famous of it, and obtain them from their people, and correct from them what we can correct, and leave the rest as they are", so that what is produced becomes an aid to the seeker of truth — warning that most of what enters reports is false and that a man's lifetime does not suffice to know even one nation thoroughly, let alone all of them {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 4–5}.

## The story

Abu Rayhan Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Biruni al-Khwarazmi — named for Khwarazm, his birthplace — began his life in a village near Bukhara, then journeyed through Transoxiana, Iran, Iraq and Syria, served at the court of Shams al-Ma'ali Qabus lord of Tabaristan and Gurgan, and finally settled in Ghazna under Sultan Mahmud {cite:tdv-biruni-tumer biography}. In these long journeys he gathered the languages, computations and customs of nations; he has many treatises in astronomy, mathematics, history and religion {cite:tdv-biruni-tumer works}.

Among those treatises is the Remaining Signs of Past Centuries, which the biographical notices report he composed in his twenty-eighth year, that is about the year 1000 CE, dedicated to Qabus {cite:tdv-biruni-tumer works} {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}. No dated autograph survives from his own time; but later copies prove that the book was copied, revised and illustrated across the centuries, the most famous surviving witness being the Ilkhanid Edinburgh manuscript copied in 707 AH and decorated with twenty-six paintings {cite:commons-edinburgh-biruni-ms161}.

## The maker(s)

The book is the work of [[person/al-biruni-muhammad-ibn-ahmad|Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Biruni]] alone, a Muslim scholar of Khwarazm who lived in the late Abbasid period and wrote in Arabic {cite:tdv-biruni-tumer biography} {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}. Hajji Khalifa describes its author as "the learned shaykh" — a title for learning, not office {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}. The museum records al-Biruni's Muslim identity from his person record and claims no particular madhhab, since no text attests one for him.

## How it works

The book works by three repeated steps in every chapter: first, it states the nation's time-system — from where its reckoning begins (a conquest, an emigration, the era of kings, the epoch of Nabonassar and the like), how it counts its years or months, and whether it has a leap year or a periodic intercalation {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 4–6}. Second, it sets this system beside the others it mentions, comparing origins and principles and seeking the points of agreement and difference {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 86–92} {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}. Third, it uses astronomical computation — the positions of Sun and Moon and the seasons — to establish dates rather than merely transmit them {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 407–409}.

The result is not a single table but a comparative matrix: one date expressible in several calendars, with differences computed rather than guessed. This is what made the book a reference for those who came after in history and calculation alike.

Inside it the problem of intercalation recurs: lunar months do not match solar years exactly, so the seasons drift from their times unless managed. The book treats this problem not theoretically but calendar by calendar, recording for each nation how it solved or ignored intercalation, so that the reader faces many models rather than one {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 86–92} {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}.

The book also gives a living example of this kind of comparison in the bounds of day and night: the Arabs begin the day-with-its-night at sunset because their months are built on the Moon's courses, tied to sighting the crescent rather than computation; the Romans and Persians begin it at sunrise because their months are extracted by calculation and not tied to the Moon's states; and the astrologers set its bound on the celestial meridian circle because it runs uniformly in all places, unlike the horizon circles that differ in every locality — and he answers those who said the day runs from dawn to sunset by noting that the fast's beginning was fixed at dawn without its end being fixed likewise, since sunset is a known, agreed matter needing no bound {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 5–8}.

## The spread

The book was not translated whole into Latin, but it stayed alive in the Islamic world: it was copied across later centuries, revised, and illustrated — as the Edinburgh manuscript shows — and it entered bibliographic catalogues, for Hajji Khalifa included it in the Kashf al-Zunun as a "useful book" {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179} {cite:commons-edinburgh-biruni-ms161}. Later readers returned to it as a treasury of the nations' dates, whenever they needed to fix an origin or compare two calendars.

Nor is the Remaining Signs an isolated book: al-Biruni composed around it other treatises on dates, geography and genealogy, so this book was one of a family, and its tables and comparisons fed later writing that used them {cite:tdv-biruni-tumer works}.

A sign of its endurance is that the Edinburgh manuscript was written in the Ilkhanid era — some seven centuries before the modern edition studied here — by a scribe named Ibn al-Qutbi, and decorated with twenty-six paintings including scenes of the various religions and customs of its age; the manuscript itself shows that the book did not remain a dry scientific text but was copied and illustrated in a multi-confessional world {cite:commons-edinburgh-biruni-ms161}.

## What it was built on

The book rests on a long heritage: Persian, Greek and Roman calendrical science circulating in translation before his age, and the knowledge of India and China that reached him through his books and journeys {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 4–6} {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}. The book names no single source for every calendar it presents; it is a critical compilation, not blind transmission — it takes from each system what proves a computation and sets aside what does not.

It also rests on al-Biruni's own astronomical computation: he was among the most capable astronomers of his age at converting dates into fixable calculations, and he has treatises on astrolabes, maps and the uses of astronomy {cite:tdv-biruni-tumer works}. The book thus joins "astronomy" and "history" in its very title, as Hajji Khalifa describes it: "on the stars and on history" {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}.

## What was built on it

No single named work is established in the sources read as having been "built on" this book; what is established is that its tables and information were used in later works of history and calculation, and that library catalogues continued to point to it as a reference on dates {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}. The museum claims no direct sequence from the book to any particular historian or astronomer; it describes it as part of the tradition of chronological comparison that fed later historical writing {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 416–417}.

## Impact on daily life

The book was not a daily instrument for ordinary people, but it was an instrument for scholars and rulers: whoever needed to fix a date or compare religions and nations in reckoning time found a reference in it. Its tables and study remain a subject in the history of computation and calendrics today, not as a working tool but as a witness to a comparative method {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 407–409}.

## How it changed history

The book changed the course of "the science of history" itself: before dates became a separate branch, al-Biruni gathered in one work the dates of nations and the divergence of their principles, so that every event came to have more than one "correct" date, and every nation its own system and principles. This comparative method — setting one calendar beside another and fixing differences by computation rather than intuition — is what remained in scientific and calendrical history after him {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 4–6} {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}.

## Who invented it?

The book is not an "invention" in the literal sense — al-Biruni invented no calendar nor instrument — it is a "method" in presenting and comparing calendars. The attribution here is sound and bounded: the author is one known figure, the book is ascribed to him in every copy and catalogue, and the age (twenty-eight) and the dedication to Qabus are fixed in the biographical notices {cite:tdv-biruni-tumer works} {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}. But the museum does not claim this was the "first" book to gather the nations' dates; the knowledge was distributed before him, and the book compiled and organised it — it did not create it from nothing.

## Today

Today the Remaining Signs is studied in the history of science and calendrics as a model of comparing time-traditions. Its tables are not used in daily calculation, but its method — gathering dates and setting them side by side — is still taught in the history of scientific method {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 407–409}.

## Surviving examples

The clearest witness is the Ilkhanid manuscript in the University of Edinburgh Library: Or MS 161, copied in 707 AH (1307–08 CE), decorated with twenty-six paintings, titled the Remaining Signs of Past Centuries {cite:commons-edinburgh-biruni-ms161}. Other copies survive in the treasuries of Islamic libraries, and Hajji Khalifa mentioned it in the seventeenth century as a useful book {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}. The modern edition studied in this record is the Cairo 2008 printing from which the worker read the introduction and selected chapters {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008}.

## In culture

The book did not become a "cultural icon" the way famous illuminated manuscripts did, but it remains in the scientific imagination of the Islamic world as al-Biruni's "book of dates". In the Edinburgh manuscript its paintings show how people read the book in an Ilkhanid age of many religions, and how copyists decorated a text that gathered the dates of nations {cite:commons-edinburgh-biruni-ms161}.

## For young readers

A thousand years ago a Muslim scholar named al-Biruni gathered the calendars of the ancient nations — Persians, Greeks, Jews, Muslims and others — into one book, and set them side by side to compare them. Instead of saying "we have the best calendar", he asked "how does each people reckon its time?" and placed the tables beside one another. This book — the Remaining Signs — is the first great "comparison of calendars" in history, and it is still studied today {cite:athar-baqiya-thaqafa-2008 pp. 4–6} {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 179}.
