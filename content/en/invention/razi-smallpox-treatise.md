## Summary

Al-Razi's short book "On Smallpox and Measles" is the first known medical text to tell two dreaded diseases apart. Before it, smallpox and measles were one confusing pox; after it, physicians could name which patient faced which danger — the earliest written exercise in what medicine now calls differential diagnosis. Translated into Latin and printed in the fifteenth century, it became the first medical book ever printed {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848}.

## What it is

A clinical treatise: definitions of smallpox and measles, their differing signs — the fever's character, the eruption's look and course — their causes as al-Razi's theory framed them, and regimen for each. Its invention is the separation itself {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848}.

## The world before it

Both diseases were ancient and both were seen — but as one "pox" family. No known earlier text separated them as independent diseases with their own courses; treatment was generic.

## The story

Abu Bakr al-Razi (d. 925), head of Baghdad's hospital and the most empirical clinician of his age, wrote the treatise as part of his practical corpus. Its fame in translation was enormous: printed at Venice in 1498 and repeatedly thereafter, it is counted the first printed medical book {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848} {cite:who-smallpox-overview}.

## The maker(s)

Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi (c. 854–925), physician of Rayy and Baghdad; his faith is recorded on the person record under the museum's declared Sunni frame with the academic debate noted there.

## How it works

The method is comparison by sign: lay the two courses side by side — onset, fever quality, eruption timing, distribution, sequelae — until the clusters of signs resolve into two diseases. Once separated, each gets its own prognosis and regimen {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848}.

## The spread

Latin as "De variolis et morbillis," it travelled into the European curriculum, was among the earliest medical incunabula, and stayed in print for three centuries.

## What it was built on

On accumulated clinical observation of both diseases in Greek and Arabic practice — the diseases were known; the differentiation was new.

## What was built on it

The whole habit of differential diagnosis: separating look-alike diseases by discriminating signs, the daily grammar of modern medicine.

## Impact on daily life

Correct naming meant correct nursing: smallpox and measles differ in danger, course and care, and telling them apart changed what a family could expect.

## How it changed history

It founded the differential method in infectious disease — and as Europe's first printed medical book it carried that method into print culture itself {cite:who-smallpox-overview}.

## Who invented it?

Al-Razi, on the record read: no earlier differentiation text is known, and later writers cite his. The claim is textual priority, not discovery of the diseases {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848}.

## Today

Smallpox is eradicated (1980) and measles is vaccine-managed — but every "rule out" in a clinic descends from the method this treatise pioneered.

## Surviving examples

The Arabic text in Greenhill's 1848 translation and the manuscript tradition behind it {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848}.

## In culture

A fixture of "firsts in medicine" lists — the book that told two plagues apart.

## For young readers

Long ago two diseases looked so alike that people treated them as one. Al-Razi wrote: watch the fever and the rash closely, and you will see they are two different enemies — and must be fought differently. That careful watching is what doctors still do every day.
