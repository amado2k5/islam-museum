## Summary
Muqarnas is the honeycomb of Islamic architecture: tiers of small niche-like cells, each corbelled over the row below inside a geometric frame, building a three-dimensional surface that shatters light and shadow like a crystal. It fills the transition where a square room rises into a round dome, crowns portals and mihrabs, and carries minaret balconies. Its earliest traces are ninth-tenth century stucco fragments at Nishapur and possibly Fustat; by the eleventh century it had spread from Egypt to Central Asia until it became, with the arabesque and the inscription band, the signature of the architecture itself {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-muqarnas-en}.

## What it is
A masonry and plaster technique: a limited set of prismatic cells — niche units of simple geometry — repeated and combined under strict rules around a few axes of symmetry, stacked in tiers so each cell projects over the one beneath. In stone in Syria, Egypt and Turkey; in brick and faience in Iraq and Iran; in plaster and wood in the Maghreb {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-muqarnas-en}.

## The world before it
Dome transitions were plain: the squinch — a small arch across the corner — or the triangular pendentive did the structural work and disappeared into the wall. Ornament was flat: carved bands, painted stucco, inscriptions. Nothing yet turned the structural corner itself into a field of geometry {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The story
The trail begins underground: the Metropolitan Museum's excavations at Nishapur turned up carved and painted stucco niches of the ninth or tenth century, reconstructed as muqarnas facing for walls and corners of houses; painted stucco muqarnas in a Fustat bath, decorated in the Samarra style with a female dancer and a cupbearer, may be just as early — in which case Baghdad, seat of the caliphate, could be the birthplace. The first monuments standing are tripartite squinches: the Arab-Ata mausoleum of 977-8 near Samarkand, the Gunbad-i Qabus of 1006-7 and the Duvazdah Imam mausoleum of 1037-8 at Yazd. Then the full muqarnas dome of the Imam Dur mausoleum at Samarra in 1090 — and after that the technique explodes across the whole Islamic world: Seljuk Isfahan's trilobed squinches, Cairo's minaret of Juyushi (1085) and al-Aqmar mosque (1125), the Sultan Hasan cornice, the scalloped arches of the Kutubiyya and Tinmal in the west, the Qutb Minar's balconies in the east {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-muqarnas-en}.

## The maker(s)
Anonymous masons and stucco-workers. EI2 quotes Diez's judgement that muqarnas "was not an individual invention whose inventor could be located; rather it is rooted in Islamic mentality and culture" — a craft's collective answer, spread by travelling builders {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it works
Each cell is a quarter-dome in miniature — a squinch repeated small — and the cells assemble in horizontal courses inside a geometric armature: fill the transition zone under a dome, dress a portal's half-vault, corbel a cornice or a balcony. The strict rules admit endless compositions; from below the surface reads as one shimmering field because the faceted cells break the light differently at every angle {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-muqarnas-en}.

## The spread
By about the eleventh century muqarnas was everywhere Islamic builders worked — Egypt, Syria, Iraq, Iran, Anatolia, the Maghreb and al-Andalus, then India and Central Asia — each region fixing its own material and its own cell grammar. In the Muslim west the sister-form is called muqarbas, whence Spanish mocárabe {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it was built on
The squinch it imitates in miniature, the stucco-carving tradition of Samarra and Nishapur, and the Islamic mason's geometry — the same compass-work that generated the arabesque {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What was built on it
Al-Kashi's fifteenth-century mensuration of muqarnas surfaces in the Miftah al-Hisab — the earliest written mathematics of the form — the muqarbas vaults of the Maghreb and al-Andalus, the Alhambra's starred domes, and the medieval builders' drawings preserved in the Topkapi Scroll that modern parametric designers still study {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-muqarnas-en}.

## Impact on daily life
It dressed the buildings ordinary people lived under — mosque, bath, palace, minaret — turning a structural necessity into a spectacle of light, and it made the prayer niche's half-dome shimmer where the community faced to pray {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it changed history
It became the one form found from Fez to Delhi that says "Islamic architecture" instantly — and it showed that ornament could do structural rhetoric: the cells look load-bearing even when, as EI2 notes, they only create "the illusion that it carries the thrust above it" {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Who invented it?
Genuinely contested — and possibly unanswerable. The candidates in the record: northeastern Persia (the Nishapur fragments, favoured by the ornamental-origin school), Abbasid Baghdad (if the Fustat bath fragments are Abbasid, and per Tabba's argument from the Imam Dur dome and the rapid spread), and Creswell and Marçais's simultaneous parallel development in many regions at once {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-muqarnas-en} {cite:tabbaa-muqarnas-dome-1985}.

## Today
Muqarnas is still built: Moroccan plaster masters and Iranian masons keep the craft, restorers recompose it on historic monuments, and architects study it as a modular geometric system — the earliest surviving builder's plans of it are in the medieval Topkapi Scroll {cite:wikipedia-muqarnas-en}.

## Surviving examples
The Nishapur fragments and the Fustat bath stuccos; the Arab-Ata (977-8) and Gunbad-i Qabus (1006-7) tripartite squinches; the Seljuk domes of Isfahan; Cairo's Juyushi minaret and al-Aqmar façade; the Sultan Hasan cornice; the Alhambra's muqarbas domes; the Qutb Minar's balconies {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:wikipedia-muqarnas-en}.

## In culture
More than ornament, muqarnas became the poetry of Islamic sacred space — the surface that makes stone seem weightless — and its western name, mocárabe, survives in Spanish as a byword for Islamic Spain's beauty {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
Look up inside an old mosque and the ceiling can look like a giant upside-down honeycomb: hundreds of tiny niches stacked like a crystal cave, glittering with light and shadow. Builders invented this trick about a thousand years ago to turn the corner between a square room and a round dome into the most beautiful part of the building — and you can still see it from Spain to India.
