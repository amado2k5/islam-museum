## Summary
Every half hour figures move, a ball drops and a sound is heard: this is how the Met's catalogue describes the elephant clock in [[person/al-jazari-engineer|al-Jazari's]] book {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}. The rider strikes the elephant with his implement, the bird moves and a ball passes from falcon to dragon to a vessel containing a gong; counting accumulated balls indicates elapsed time {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}. The contribution is a timed display device within an earlier clock-and-automata tradition, not invention of clocks or robots. TDV dates al-Jazari's book to 1205; the illustrated folio here is a later copy dated 715/1315 {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506} {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Overview}.

## What it is
The clock takes the form of an elephant carrying a decorated structure with figures, a bird and dragon-shaped mechanisms, visible in the museum's folio 57.51.23 {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Overview}. The elephant is the machine's form, not a living animal performing the work; the drawing belongs to an instruction book, not a photograph of an original clock.

Francesca Leoni's entry places it in a book describing fifty devices, including court entertainment {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}. Automatic motion is not turned into a claim that the machine thinks, chooses or is equivalent to a contemporary robot.

## The world before it
TDV places al-Jazari within an engineering tradition extending from the Hellenistic period to the Banu Musa, describing his concern with improving earlier machines and references to older clocks, fountains and musical devices {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. Neither clocks nor automata begin with the elephant.

This is general engineering context, not proof that the elephant clock copied one particular prototype. The diagram does not make those predecessors a literal invention chain unsupported by the passages read, or assign each part to a Greek engineer from memory.

## The story
TDV records al-Jazari's Artuqid service at [[place/amid|Amid]] in 1181–1206 and his book commissioned by Nasir al-Din Mahmud, completed in 1205 according to the entry {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. The Met's essay connects the work with the same ruler's commission in the early thirteenth century {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}.

The record dates the documentary description in the book, not a securely established construction of a particular physical clock. The illustrated copy is distinct: dated 715/1315 and copied by Farrukh ibn Abd al-Latif, probably in Syria or Iraq according to the museum {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Overview}. Where the picture was copied does not relocate the machine's original context.

## The maker(s)
The museum attributes the device to al-Jazari as author and engineer. Following TDV's account of his practice, the browser credits him as an improver in an older tradition {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry} {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. This does not claim each component was unprecedented.

TDV calls him an Islamic scholar, the Muslim identity basis recorded here, with the museum's Sunni default frame explicitly stated because the source used gives no personal school {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. Farrukh copied the surviving folio later; he is not credited with the original engineering design {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Overview}.

## How it works
Leoni describes a half-hour signalling sequence {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}:

1. The rider moves his implement and the bird turns.
2. The falcon releases a ball into the dragon's mouth.
3. The dragon drops the ball into a vessel, striking a gong.
4. The ball comes to rest in a bowl at the vessel's bottom; counting collected balls gives the elapsed time.

The page's short overview describes the bird whistling. Its wording and the catalogue's turning bird are retained as their respective descriptions rather than replaced with conjecture {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Overview}.

This explains the visible signal described by the source, not a complete construction plan. TDV discusses weights, floats and sinking bodies in al-Jazari's machines generally; that does not establish hidden-bowl dimensions, flow rates or a particular calibration procedure for this clock {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. The accompanying diagram shows historical relationships, not those hidden parts.

## The spread
What is documented here is the transmission of book and image: a 1315 folio from a dispersed manuscript, with other leaves identified in the Freer and Sackler collections by the catalogue {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}. Multiple manuscript copies are not a count of physical elephant clocks built and operated.

The passages read give no route taking this clock to a particular European factory or public clock. No conjectural routes are drawn on a physical-spread map.

## What it was built on
The attested context is earlier clock and automata traditions; TDV names Hellenistic machinery and the Banu Musa in discussing al-Jazari's broader practice {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. References to a clock attributed to Archimedes or to Banu Musa fountains do not make all of them proven direct elephant-clock prototypes.

The improvement is the arrangement of successive actions in a particular device, with assembly instructions identified by the Met {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}. It does not mean inventing time measurement itself.

## What was built on it
TDV describes the book's modern study: partial German translations in 1908–1921, Hill's complete translation in 1974 and Ahmad Yusuf al-Hassan's edition in 1979 {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. This is textual and scholarly continuation, not a sequence of industrial machines proving every later robot descends from the elephant.

It also reports two reconstructed working clocks from the book, one for London's 1976 World of Islam Festival and another at half scale at Istanbul Technical University. The passage does not call both elephant clocks; neither does this article {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}.

## Impact on daily life
In the court context described by the museum, a half-hour becomes movement, sound and a countable ball {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}. That effect does not require inventing a story about regulating a whole city's working hours.

The catalogue gives no number of machines built, cost or measured accuracy. The article therefore assigns no ordinary-household adoption or economic saving absent from the sources.

## How it changed history
The verifiable legacy here is engineering knowledge preserved in text and image. TDV describes instructions for making components, assembly and operation, with coloured drawings and letters identifying parts {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. Leoni reads the folio as an important manuscript-painting witness retaining earlier visual formulas {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}.

Engineering and illustration can thus be studied together without declaring this clock a direct cause of the Industrial Revolution. Transmission of a particular mechanism requires evidence specific to it, not resemblance of appearance or movement alone.

## Who invented it?
**The Islamic-history reference.** TDV describes al-Jazari as an engineer improving earlier traditions and places his book in Amid under Artuqid patronage {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. It does not give him priority for every device and component mentioned.

**The museum catalogue.** Leoni attributes the elephant clock to a chapter of his book and describes its signals, distinguishing the device from the illustrated copy of 1315 {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}.

The attribution is improvement of a specific clock. The record adopts no “first robot” title or claim that each painted dragon proves a distinct engineering school.

## Today
The displayed witness is a Met folio in ink, opaque watercolour and gold on paper, thirty centimetres high and 19.7 centimetres wide according to its metadata {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Overview}. Those are paper dimensions, not the machine's dimensions.

Accession 57.51.23 can be checked in the catalogue. The survival of a page does not mean an original clock from al-Jazari's workshop survives {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Overview}.

## Surviving examples
The folio is dated 715/1315. The museum credits the Bequest of Cora Timken Burnett, 1956, and marks the image Public Domain {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Overview}. This permits showing a witness from the book rather than an imagined machine.

Letters beside the drawing's components, ornament, figures and coiled dragons can be examined visually, but the page is not by itself a precise measurement of every hidden part {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}. Image and text remain different tools for understanding the work.

## In culture
Leoni connects the rider's form, turban and garment bands with thirteenth-century Arab painting traditions in Iraq and Syria, and notes the dragon formula in the earliest copy known to her, dated 1206 {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}. This is an art-historical reading, not automatic proof that each element represents a particular civilization.

The elephant's decoration is not turned into a story of one people's or religion's superiority. Author, image source and engineering traditions are named, with the limits of each source visible.

## For young readers
Imagine a machine telling you half an hour has passed: figures move, a ball falls and a gong sounds. In the museum's description you count balls to know the elapsed time {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}. The elephant is a machine's shape in al-Jazari's book, and the picture a later copy, not a living animal or a photograph of his original clock {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Overview}.
