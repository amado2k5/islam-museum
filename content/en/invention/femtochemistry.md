## Summary
Femtochemistry is a measurement method founded by the Egyptian chemist Ahmed Zewail at the California Institute of Technology in the late 1980s. The idea is to strike a molecule with a laser pulse of femtosecond length ⁦(10⁻¹⁵ seconds, one part in a million of a billionth of a second)⁩ so its atoms are excited and the reaction begins, then to probe it with a second, weaker pulse arriving after an adjustable delay, reading its spectrum moment by moment as the transformation proceeds. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

The result was that its maker saw for the first time in history atoms breaking chemical bonds and forming them - not in the aggregate outcome but in the transition state itself, which theory had described and assumed to be a passing instant no eye could reach. The Nobel press release put the prize motivation in one sentence: Zewail showed "that it is possible with rapid laser technique to see how atoms in a molecule move during a chemical reaction". {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

The Nobel Foundation named the whole field "femtochemistry" after the method and awarded Zewail the 1999 Nobel Prize in Chemistry alone, with the full share, making him the first Egyptian to win a Nobel in a scientific field. And to avoid overstating the credit: femtosecond lasers and spectroscopy existed before him in laser physics; the documented originality is the fusion of the two pulses into one method applied to reaction chemistry, plus its first results on real systems. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release} {cite:nobelprize-zewail-facts}

## What it is
This record is for a "method", not a device: pump-probe femtosecond spectroscopy. A strong pump pulse excites the molecule so the reaction starts, and a weaker probe pulse passes over the molecule after a delay tuned by moving mirrors along the light path, recording its spectrum at that precise instant. Varying the delay varies the frame of time: every spectral reading is a different moment in the reaction's life. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

The scale is the femtosecond: a second divided by a million billion, that is 10⁻¹⁵ seconds. The pulses are built that short because chemical reactions themselves run on that timescale; any slower camera would capture only the final outcome and miss the moment of transformation on which reaction chemistry is decided. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

The field born of the method was named by the Nobel press release itself "femtochemistry", and today it is a full branch of physical chemistry that studies reaction mechanisms on their real timescale, extending from gases and fluids to solids, surfaces and biological systems. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

## The world before it
Before the method, reaction chemistry worked on a timescale that did not allow direct vision. The chemist measured the reaction in aggregate: the rate at which products formed against time, or the rate constant's change with temperature as in the Arrhenius equation. These numbers describe what happens but do not show it: how many molecules in a million break a bond right now? Which bonds break first? These were questions the older methods could not answer. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

Meanwhile laser physics had already taken a step: phase-locked femtosecond pulses could be made and measured, and ultrafast spectroscopy existed, but it had not yet been brought into the heart of reaction dynamics. The pulse existed and the scale existed; what was missing was the idea of placing two pulses on a chemical reaction and reading what lay between them. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

Above all this stood theories describing what could not be seen: Eyring-Polanyi transition-state theory of the 1930s pictured the moment "on the top of the hill" between reactants and products, and the Arrhenius equation described statistically how rate rises with temperature. These theories were useful but remained assumptions; nobody had ever watched an atom cross the barrier itself. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

## The story
The story began in Egypt: Ahmed Zewail was born in Damanhur in 1946 and raised in Desouk on the banks of the Nile, then studied at Alexandria University, where he took the bachelor's degree in 1967 and the master's in 1969, and where scientific questions engaged him early enough that he later recalled being designated an "outstanding" student in chemistry. {cite:nobelprize-zewail-facts} {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail}

He travelled to the United States, took his doctorate at the University of Pennsylvania in 1974 under R. Hochstrasser, then spent two years at Berkeley with C. Harris working on molecular dynamics; Caltech's oral history says this work "set the stage for his future forays into laser-based femtoscience". In 1976 he declined an offer to teach in Iraq and chose a faculty position at the California Institute of Technology, where he stayed forty years until his death. {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail} {cite:nobelprize-zewail-facts}

A decade at Caltech: the oral history describes "the decade of work that led in 1987 to the first direct observations of the making and breaking of chemical bonds", in collaboration with R. Bernstein and with decisive institutional support from the institute. In those successive experiments of the late 1980s Zewail and his colleagues saw what nobody had seen: the dissociation of the ICN molecule in 200 femtoseconds, the separation of NaI, the formation of a HOCO intermediate in the hydrogen-plus-carbon-dioxide reaction lasting 1,000 femtoseconds, the sequential breaking of C2I2F4 bonds, the reaction of benzene with I2 in 750 femtoseconds, the opening of the cyclobutane ring through a 700-femtosecond intermediate, the cis-to-trans photoisomerisation of stilbene, the motion of retinal in 200 femtoseconds, and chlorophyll. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release} {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail}

The Royal Swedish Academy announced the 1999 Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Zewail alone; the press release states that his late-1980s experiments "were to lead to the birth of the research area called femtochemistry". Before it he had received the King Faisal Prize in Saudi Arabia and the Wolf Prize in Israel, and the oral history presents them as milestones on the road to Stockholm. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release} {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail}

## The maker(s)
The maker is Ahmed Hassan Zewail, an Egyptian-born chemist, professor of chemical physics and physics at Caltech, holder of the Linus Pauling Chair since about 1990 according to the Nobel facts page. He was born in Damanhur in 1946 and died in Pasadena in 2016. {cite:nobelprize-zewail-facts} {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail}

His religious identity is documented in his own words: in a 2009 Fountain Magazine interview he spoke of "the way I grew up as a Muslim in Egypt", described the Sidi Ibrahim al-Desuqi mosque as having "defined my early childhood", recalled studying in the mosque after prayer, named al-Azhar "one of the centers of enlightenment of the world", and said he saw no conflict between religion and science. In Caltech's oral history he describes the "moderate, intellectually rich Islam" of his youth, reading books with friends in the mosque and the imam's blessing of their pursuit of knowledge. {cite:fountainmagazine-zewail-interview-2009} {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail}

His role here is "inventor" rather than "improver" in the available taxonomy: he did not invent the laser or spectroscopy, but the pump-probe method applied to reaction chemistry and its first applications are a documented creation, and the field's name itself came from the prize's language. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

## How it works
The practical arrangement is simple in principle and hard in execution: the first laser pulse, the "pump", is strong enough to excite the molecule violently so the reaction begins chaotically; then the weaker "probe" pulse waits a set time and passes over the molecule, capturing its spectrum at that instant. The time gap between the pulses is the exposure time by which the reaction is read: every delay yields a different frame. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

The gap is tuned with physics fit to the scale: an extra light path of 0.03 millimetres equals 100 femtoseconds, because light covers that distance in that time; moving a mirror by a microscopic amount changes the delay in femtosecond steps. Thus the reaction can be filmed frame by frame from the moment of impact to the settling of the products. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

Spectral reading is the detection mechanism: the reactant molecule, the product and the intermediate each leave a fingerprint in light absorption at a suitable wavelength; choosing the probe's wavelength selects which "member" of the reaction ensemble is measured at that instant. This is where details such as the HOCO intermediate, which lived 1,000 femtoseconds before transforming, come from. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

## The spread
The method first spread within laser physics and physical chemistry: the press release describes femtosecond studies proceeding "intensively the world over" after Zewail's work, so that many laboratories built their own pulsed spectroscopies. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

Then its horizon widened theoretically and experimentally: scientists applied the method to diverse chemical systems and then to biological ones; the release cites studies of retinal, a light-sensitive protein that changes shape in 200 femtoseconds, and of chlorophyll, so that talk of "femtochemistry in the living" - femtobiology - began. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

And the method found its way into education and institutional research: after the prize Zewail founded Caltech's Physical Biology Center for Ultrafast Science and Technology and devised four-dimensional electron microscopy, which films time in another domain - an extension of the same spirit of "imaging with time". {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail}

## What it was built on
It was built on femtosecond laser technology: phase-locked femtosecond pulses were not invented by Zewail; the Nobel release itself acknowledges that "rapid laser technique" was what revealed the motion, and the documented role is its application to reactions. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

It was also built on molecular dynamics: Zewail himself came to the research from his Berkeley work on the theoretical simulation of large molecules, moving from one who computes a molecule on a computer to one who watches it move in the laboratory. {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail}

And it was built on standing chemical rules: the Arrhenius equation for reaction rate against temperature, van't Hoff's rule, and Eyring-Polanyi transition-state theory; the method did not abolish these theories but made them visible, so that the earlier mathematics could now be read through direct numbers from experiment. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

## What was built on it
Built on it was the field named after it: femtochemistry became a family of branches studying reaction mechanisms on their real scale, from simple gas reactions to catalyst systems, polymers and surfaces. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

Built on it were specialised offshoots: femtobiology studying proteins and enzymes on their own timescale, and ultrafast science which Zewail himself continued with four-dimensional electron microscopy and his center at Caltech. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release} {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail}

And built on it was a new understanding of enzymes, photoreactions and materials: when you watch retinal transform in 200 femtoseconds or the HOCO intermediate vanish after 1,000 femtoseconds, you understand a mechanism that numerical prediction alone could never supply. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

## Impact on daily life
Its impact on daily life is indirect but deep: medicines, materials and catalysts are designed today through an understanding of their mechanisms at the level of bonds, and since femtosecond spectroscopy is the tool that sees those mechanisms, the results flow into pharmacy, materials and industry. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

Its impact on science is closer to "changing the rules of play": before it, reaction chemistry was narrated after the fact; after it, the reaction is watched as it happens. That difference between "knowing the outcome" and "seeing the process" is what made the word "mechanism" in chemistry more trustworthy. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

It also had an impact on scientific inspiration itself: the story of a Muslim scientist from Desouk who rose to the highest scientific platform in the world became a tale told in Egypt and the Muslim world - not as a religious story but as evidence that the road is open through science. {cite:fountainmagazine-zewail-interview-2009} {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail}

## How it changed history
It changed history in three ways. First, it ended an old debate in reaction chemistry: theory said the transition state was a "moment" that could not be seen; experiment answered: we saw it, and we can say when each bond breaks, in what order. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

Second, it created a new language for science: "femtochemistry" is a name the prize itself coined, so it now describes a whole branch of knowledge working in laboratories worldwide. When the term is mentioned, its maker is mentioned; that is among science's rarest gifts. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

Third, it turned one Arab Muslim scientist's career into a "scientific teacher" for a generation: Zewail's weaving of science education, the Zewail City of Science and Technology he discussed with President Mubarak, his role as the first science envoy to the Middle East - all extensions of the deeper question he raised in his interviews: does our scientific heritage hold anything equal to what is being made now? {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail} {cite:fountainmagazine-zewail-interview-2009}

## Who invented it?
The precise answer from the prize's own documents: femtosecond laser technology is not his invention; the Nobel release itself says the laser technique already existed and that his role was "with rapid laser technique to see". What was missing before Zewail was not "the pulse" but "the applied idea": he used two pulses on the chemical reaction and read what lay between them, then fixed the pattern across a series of systems. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

This is why we recorded the attribution as "original" within what the sources support: creating the pump-probe method for diagnosing reactions and its first applications is documented, while the rest of the components ⁦(the laser, spectroscopy, transition-state theories)⁩ predate him and are named in the prize release itself. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

On the question of identity: Zewail is Muslim by his own attestation, not inferred from a name or a country; we recorded that in the faith block with self-identification from the two interviews the drafter read. {cite:fountainmagazine-zewail-interview-2009} {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail}

## Today
Today femtosecond spectroscopy works in laboratories worldwide on gases, fluids, solids, surfaces, polymers and biological systems; it is a "method", not a single device one buys, but a laser-and-probe-and-delay arrangement assembled in the laboratory. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

As the technology advanced, branches grew from it: Zewail's own four-dimensional electron microscope films time in other domains, femtobiology studies proteins and enzymes on their scale, and femtosecond spectroscopy has become a standard tool for understanding drug and materials mechanisms. {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail} {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

And femtochemistry remains a witness to a deeper point: sometimes the "invention" is not a new substance but a new "way of seeing"; when what we can see changes, what we can understand and build changes with it. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

## Surviving examples
There is no unique "device" preserved like a museum piece: the method itself is the witness, and it appears in every laboratory practising femtosecond spectroscopy. But there are documentary witnesses: the 1999 Nobel press release describing the method and the experiments, and the oral-history file in Caltech's archives recording the decade of work from 1976 to 1987. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release} {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail}

Among material witnesses is an archival photograph of Zewail himself: a 1986 picture of the institute's professor in the student yearbook The Big T, released to the public domain, capturing him in the years of building the method, thirteen years before the prize. {cite:commons-zewail-1986}

And Zewail's books remain a record: his autobiography "Voyage through Time", published by the American University in Cairo Press in 2002, documents his path from Desouk to Stockholm, while his 2009 Fountain Magazine interview documents the human and faith side that does not appear in scientific papers. {cite:zewail-voyage-through-time-2002} {cite:fountainmagazine-zewail-interview-2009}

## In culture
Zewail entered public culture not only as a scientist but as "Egypt's success story": he became the first Egyptian to win a Nobel in a scientific field ⁦(not literature)⁩, and the press described him as "the son of Desouk" who changed the rules of chemistry. This tale is told in schools and science programmes as proof that Arab science is possible. {cite:nobelprize-zewail-facts} {cite:fountainmagazine-zewail-interview-2009}

He has a second cultural afterlife: Zewail himself wrote and spoke about the relationship between science and faith, saying in interviews that science and religion do not conflict; this idea became part of the "message" he left - not as a sermon but as the position of a Muslim researcher who lived the same tensions himself. {cite:fountainmagazine-zewail-interview-2009}

In Arab scientific memory his name appears in lists of "names that changed the world": beside Einstein and Nikola Tesla in the same row, not merely as a Nobel laureate but as a founder of a branch. This is a rare scientific standing, though a common one in journalism. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

## For young readers
Imagine you have a very, very fast camera: its flash lasts one part in a million of a billionth of a second! When Ahmed Zewail strikes a molecule with this light camera, he sees something nobody had seen before: atoms breaking bonds and building new ones, moment by moment - as if you were watching in slow motion a very fast film. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release}

Zewail is a Muslim scientist from Egypt, born in Damanhur and raised on the banks of the Nile, who studied at Alexandria University then travelled to America and worked at a famous institute called the California Institute of Technology. In his laboratory he made "two flashes" of laser: the first wakes the molecule so the work begins, and the second photographs it at the moment you choose. {cite:nobelprize-zewail-facts} {cite:caltech-oral-history-zewail}

With this method he witnessed for the first time in history the dissociation of a molecule like ICN in 200 femtoseconds, and watched living molecules like retinal transform in 200 femtoseconds. Because his invention mattered so much, Sweden gave him the Nobel Prize in 1999, and they named his new field "femtochemistry" in his honour. {cite:nobelprize-chemistry-1999-press-release} {cite:nobelprize-zewail-facts}
