## Summary
Islamic potters built a ceramic body that nature does not provide: powdered quartz bound with glass frit and a little white clay. The result — hard, white, fused to its glaze — carried the lustre dishes and minai bowls that are the glory of medieval Islamic pottery. The recipe survives in a potter's treatise of 1301; the date and place of the first invention are honestly debated {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## What it is
Fritware, or stonepaste: a manufactured paste of about ten parts ground quartz, one part glass frit and one part white clay — the formula Abu'l-Qasim al-Kashani records for the Seljuq potters — fired into a white body the alkaline glaze could not flake off {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## The world before it
The Abbasid potters of Samarra and Baghdad had tin-glazed earthenware and the first lustre painting; the body was still common clay under a white glaze. Egypt had practised composite quartz-frit pastes in ancient times, and the Fatimids made hard white wares with alkaline glaze {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The story
Admiring the thin, ringing translucency of Chinese porcelain, the potters of the Islamic world — Lane gives Persia the probable credit, in the workshops of Rayy and Kashan — rebuilt a white porcelain-like body without kaolin, from local quartz and glass. From Seljuq times the technique spread everywhere and, in Lane's words, completely revolutionised fine Islamic pottery {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## The maker(s)
The makers are the unnamed potter families — among them Kashan's Abu Tahir line, whose Abu'l-Qasim wrote the craft's surviving recipe in 1301. A craft transmitted through workshops, not a named inventor {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## How it works
Quartz pebbles are powdered and melted with potash to make a glass frit; the frit is ground and mixed with more quartz and a little white clay into a paste. Body and glaze share the same substances, so in the kiln they fuse into one material — white, hard, sometimes translucent, needing no slip {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## The spread
The EI2 FAKHKHAR survey records the composite body's centres — Fustat, Rayy, Raqqa, Kashan, Sultanabad, Samarkand — and its offspring: lustre tilework, minai enamel painting, the underglaze-painted wares of Kashan {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## What it was built on
Ancient Egyptian composite pastes, dormant since the ninth century; the Abbasid tin-glaze and lustre tradition; and the admired model of Chinese Ting and ying-ching porcelain, which the white body imitated without copying its material {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## What was built on it
Kashan lustre and tilework, minai polychrome, the pierced 'window' bowls, Iznik and the later glazed traditions of the Islamic world — and, structurally, Europe's eighteenth-century soft-paste porcelains, built of the same three ingredients {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## Impact on daily life
It put a white, durable, colour-carrying tableware into the houses of the Islamic world and a tile skin onto its architecture — the material of the mihrab revetments still visible at Kashan and elsewhere {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it changed history
It made Islamic pottery a match for porcelain on its own terms — an engineered material rather than a borrowed one — and gave the craft its two greatest schools of painted ware {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## Who invented it?
No single inventor is known. Lane credits the Persian potters for the decisive technique; the same scholarship records earlier Egyptian white pastes and an ancient Egyptian ancestry — a re-composition with debated origins, recorded as such {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Today
Fritware fills the Islamic galleries of the world's museums, and the composite-body idea survives wherever potters build a white vitreous body — the craft's modern descendants are everywhere ceramics are engineered {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## Surviving examples
Kashan and Sultanabad lustre vessels and tiles in the V&A and Cleveland (one pictured at media/photo-kuz-kashan-1170); Rayy and Raqqa finds; the Abu'l-Qasim treatise manuscript of 1301 in Istanbul {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## In culture
The white body became the canvas of Islamic ceramic art — lustre gold, minai enamel, cobalt and black under clear glaze — and its tilework gave mosques their coloured skin {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
Potters long ago wanted cups as white and fine as Chinese porcelain, but they did not have the special clay. So they made their own white clay from ground-up sand, melted glass and a little real clay — like a recipe. Their pots came out so white and strong that painters could cover them in shiny gold colours, and you can still see those pots in museums today.
