## Summary

Hunayn ibn Ishaq's "Ten Treatises on the Eye" (d. 873 CE) is the earliest known book to organise ophthalmology into a single sequential course of ten treatises: the eye's anatomy, tunics and nerves, then the diseases of each part with their drugs and operations. Its medical substance is largely Galenic, but the book's contribution is turning that scattered heritage into a teachable curriculum; its editor Meyerhof (1928 edition) calls it the earliest known systematic textbook of ophthalmology {cite:wellcome-hunayn-ten-treatises-eye-1928}.

## What it is

An Arabic ophthalmology book divided into ten graded treatises: from the eye's nature and anatomy, to the nourishment of brain and sight, to the diseases of the lids, conjunctiva, cornea, pupil and lens, ending with the eye's compound drugs and operations. It gathered into one volume what lay scattered through general medical books {cite:wellcome-hunayn-ten-treatises-eye-1928}.

## The world before it

Before Hunayn, ophthalmology existed in Arabic as scattered chapters inside the translated medical encyclopaedias — Galen, Oribasius, Paul — with no independent ordered textbook for teaching. A physician assembled his own material from many sources.

## The story

Hunayn lived in Abbasid Baghdad, the greatest translation city of the age, and ran a translation enterprise that carried Greek medicine and philosophy into Arabic without parallel. From that work came the Ten Treatises for the Arabic reader: a teaching course that begins with structure and ends with treatment — written to teach, not only to cite {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The maker(s)

Hunayn ibn Ishaq (c. 809–873), a Nestorian Christian from al-Hira, the greatest translator of the Abbasid era and physician to al-Mutawakkil. His person record documents his faith and work; his "invention" here is the systematic organisation of an existing science, not new anatomical discovery.

## How it works

It works as a curriculum: each treatise is one thematic unit — anatomy, visual physiology, then each organ's diseases with diagnosis and drugs — so the reader learns the whole of ophthalmology step by step. That curricular ordering is the innovative product {cite:wellcome-hunayn-ten-treatises-eye-1928}.

## The spread

The book entered the Arabic medical manuscript tradition and passed into Latin (as De oculis), influencing medieval European eye books. Later Muslim ophthalmologists — Ali ibn Isa and Ammar al-Mawsili — built on it {cite:wellcome-hunayn-ten-treatises-eye-1928}.

## What was built on it

The great later Arabic eye books were built on it — Ali ibn Isa's "Memorandum for Oculists" and Ammar al-Mawsili's "Choice" — which extended the ten-treatise frame with new operations and observations.

## What it was built on

On Galenic anatomy and Greek medicine, much of which Hunayn himself translated; by its editor's own account the book is an original re-organisation of transmitted content rather than new content {cite:wellcome-hunayn-ten-treatises-eye-1928}.

## Impact on daily life

It made the oculist a teachable profession: instead of mining ten books, one course sufficed for foundations — helping establish the kahhal (eye doctor) as a trade in Islamic cities.

## How it changed history

It founded the "coursebook" method in medical specialties: the first time an entire field was organised into ten treatises for teaching. The pattern later reached other specialities.

## Who invented it?

The priority of the organisation is settled for Hunayn by his editor Meyerhof; the medical content is Galenic; so the record says: the first systematic course on the eye, not the first knowledge of it {cite:wellcome-hunayn-ten-treatises-eye-1928}.

## Today

The book remains a historical landmark; modern ophthalmology rests on imaging and microsurgery, but the graded-course idea — anatomy to treatment — survives in medical textbooks.

## Surviving examples

Arabic and Latin manuscripts, and Meyerhof's Arabic edition with English translation (Cairo 1928) catalogued at Wellcome {cite:wellcome-hunayn-ten-treatises-eye-1928}.

## In culture

Historians cite it as an achievement of the Baghdad translation school: Greek science made Arabic, made into a curriculum, made into a European reference.

## For young readers

Imagine that an eye doctor twelve hundred years ago had to gather his knowledge from many scattered books. Hunayn came and wrote one book in ten chapters: how the eye is built, what diseases it gets, and how to treat it — the first textbook of eye medicine history knows.
