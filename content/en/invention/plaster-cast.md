## Summary
Immobilising a fracture with a dressing that hardens around the limb: surgical histories record that al-Zahrawi (960-1013) near Cordoba described casting materials such as clay-gum mixtures and flour-and-egg-white, and that tenth-century Arabs used liquid plaster in orthopaedic treatment. The modern plaster-of-Paris bandage is the nineteenth-century work of Mathijsen (1852) and Pirogov {cite:szostakowski-plaster-history-2017} {cite:wikipedia-orthopedic-cast-en}.

## What it is
A bandage impregnated with a hardening material (lime, gypsum, clay with gum, starch, or albumen) wrapped around the reduced limb; it sets into a rigid shell that holds the bone alignment until healing {cite:wikipedia-orthopedic-cast-en}.

## The world before it
Fractures were treated with bound wooden splints and ordinary dressings: they shifted, demanded long bed rest; the ancient ideas (Hippocratic wax-and-resin bandages, Celsus's starch) never made a snug-fitting mould {cite:szostakowski-plaster-history-2017} {cite:wikipedia-orthopedic-cast-en}.

## The story
Surgical histories record that al-Zahrawi at Cordoba (al-Tasrif, c. 1000) described stiffening materials, that the earlier Rhazes Athuriscus described casting, and that tenth-century Arabs used liquid plaster; the practice stayed alive in the East until William Eton described gypsum plaster moulded around broken limbs in Turkey c. 1800 {cite:szostakowski-plaster-history-2017} {cite:wikipedia-orthopedic-cast-en}.

## The maker(s)
No named inventor for the tradition: al-Zahrawi is its great textual witness in al-Tasrif, Ibn Sina a contemporary witness in the Canon; the modern naming to Mathijsen (1852) invents the technique, not the principle {cite:szostakowski-plaster-history-2017} {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi}.

## How it works
The powdered material (hemihydrate calcium sulphate in plaster of Paris) is wetted and wrapped or poured around the limb; it returns to its insoluble form, hardening in minutes into a made-to-measure mould {cite:wikipedia-orthopedic-cast-en}.

## The spread
From Andalusi and Iraqi medical literature the principle stayed in Arabic and Turkish practice until Eton's report (c. 1800); Europe then carried it through Lafargue (1839), Mathijsen (1852) and Pirogov in the Crimea {cite:wikipedia-orthopedic-cast-en}.

## What it was built on
Wooden splints and the ancient wax-resin and starch dressings, plus the Islamic medical materials (shell-lime, albumen, clay-gum) — a stiffening technique, not a single substance {cite:szostakowski-plaster-history-2017} {cite:wikipedia-orthopedic-cast-en}.

## What was built on it
The modern cast: Mathijsen's bandage, then heavy poured casts, commercial Cellona bandages (1931), and today's fibreglass and thermoplastic successors {cite:wikipedia-orthopedic-cast-en}.

## Impact on daily life
Freed the patient from long bed rest: a fitted shell heals fractures faster and safer, letting the injured walk during recovery {cite:wikipedia-orthopedic-cast-en}.

## How it changed history
It changed trauma surgery: from weeks of bed rest to weeks of walking; from the bonesetter's craft to a standard technique every surgeon learns {cite:wikipedia-orthopedic-cast-en}.

## Who invented it?
The principle is old and shared across civilisations; in Islam al-Zahrawi and the tenth-century sources document its materials, while the modern plaster bandage is named to Mathijsen (1852) {cite:szostakowski-plaster-history-2017} {cite:wikipedia-orthopedic-cast-en}.

## Today
Plaster of Paris remains an option for simple fractures — cheap and snug — alongside fibreglass; 3D-printing now reproduces the mould digitally {cite:wikipedia-orthopedic-cast-en}.

## Surviving examples
Its witness is textual: the fracture chapters of al-Tasrif (c. 1000); museum collections of orthopaedic surgery hold early cast specimens {cite:szostakowski-plaster-history-2017} {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi}.

## In culture
"The cast" became the popular image of a healing fracture — a heavy white shell friends sign with a pen {cite:wikipedia-orthopedic-cast-en}.

## For young readers
Broke your arm? The doctor wraps a wet bandage around it that dries hard like a shell, holding the bone in place until it heals — and the doctors of Cordoba knew this trick a thousand years ago.
