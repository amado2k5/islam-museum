## Summary
The obliquity of the ecliptic is the angle between the ecliptic — the Sun's yearly path across the sky — and the equinoctial {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112}. In his book al-Farghani records two successive values: Ptolemy's old figure of 23 degrees 51 minutes, and a verified measurement ordered in al-Ma'mun's time on which the scholars agreed at 23 degrees 35 minutes {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150}. The precise measurement was not al-Farghani's invention; he transmitted an Abbasid measurement made in al-Ma'mun's age that differed from Ptolemy's value, documented accurately in the 'Elements' {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}.

## What it is
'Mayl' — obliquity — in ancient astronomy is the angle between the ecliptic — the circle the Sun's centre travels yearly through the twelve signs — and the equinoctial — the circle that divides the celestial world into northern and southern halves {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112}. Because the ecliptic is 'tilted' from the equinoctial by this angle, the Sun swings north and south between the summer and winter solstices, so the seasons and the lengths of day and night change {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150}. The value al-Farghani gives — 23 degrees 35 minutes — is the 'maximum declination of the Sun', that is, the greatest distance the Sun reaches from the equinoctial line {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112}. This number is not a theoretical detail: by it are computed the times of the solstices and equinoxes, the lengths of day and night, and the Sun's stations on its sphere — the basis of every astronomical people's calendar {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}.

## The world before it
Before al-Ma'mun's measurement, Greek astronomers had estimated the obliquity for centuries: Ptolemy in the Almagest set the value at 23 degrees 51 minutes, which remained the accepted figure in Hellenistic astronomy and reached Arabic through the translators {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112}. Ptolemy's own estimate was the fruit of long observation: ancient astronomers had noticed that the seasons change and the day lengthens and shortens, so they inferred that the 'Sun's path' tilts from the 'equinoctial line' by an angle — but they had not yet measured that angle by a precise direct measurement {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112}. Indian zij tables knew similar values, but the 'verified' value — measured, not transmitted — had not yet been made in al-Ma'mun's age {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. The world before al-Farghani held an inherited 'estimate' spanning centuries and one new 'measurement' — the one al-Ma'mun ordered in his time and gathered the scholars upon {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112}. Al-Farghani himself was not 'the measurer'; he was the one who 'recorded' both numbers in his book — the old and the verified — to serve as a reference for students.

## The story
In the 'Elements' al-Farghani mentions the obliquity in two places: in his chapter on spherical computations, and in his chapter on angles and inclinations {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112, 150}. He states plainly: Ptolemy estimated the obliquity at 23 degrees 51 minutes, then 'it was measured' in al-Ma'mun's time and found to be 23 degrees 35 minutes 'on which the scholars agreed' — that is, a number of scholars confirmed the measurement and assented to it {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112}. He repeats the same value in his later chapter on astronomical computations, fixing both numbers in the text {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150}. Al-Ma'mun — who made Samarra his seat and a refuge of learning in the first half of the third century AH — is the one who ordered the measurement and gathered the scholars for it, so the figure is the fruit of a 'scientific project', not a 'lone observation' {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. And al-Farghani, who died before 857 CE, recorded this result in his book so that the Arabic text would carry the official pair of numbers.

## The maker(s)
There is no 'inventor' of either the measurement or the concept: the concept — the tilt of the ecliptic from the equinoctial — is known in pre-Islamic astronomy, and the verified measurement was ordered by al-Ma'mun in the third century AH {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Al-Farghani's role is precisely documented: he is the 'transmitter' who recorded the two numbers in the 'Elements' — Ptolemy's old value and the new Abbasid measurement — and he is not recorded as the measurer {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112}. The record claims no priority for al-Farghani; it claims 'fidelity' in transmission — that he preserved both numbers without erasing one in favour of the other {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112, 150}.

## How it works
The obliquity works on a simple principle: the Earth turns on its axis while 'tilted' slightly from the plane of its orbit around the Sun, so the Sun's yearly path across the sky appears 'tilted' by a small angle from the celestial equator line {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150}. This tilt is what makes the day shorter in winter and longer in summer: when the Sun is at its greatest distance north the day is longer, and when it is at its greatest distance south it is shorter {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150}. The angle — 23 degrees 35 minutes — is the 'range' of this swing: the greater the angle, the greater the differences between the seasons {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112, 150}. The modern diagram 'Ecliptic vs equator full globe' shows the two circles — red for the small circle and blue for the equinoctial — on a single celestial sphere, so the angle between them is seen with the eye {cite:commons-ecliptic-equator}.

## The spread
The number spread with the 'Elements': everyone who read al-Farghani's book — students of astronomy in Baghdad and beyond — carried the two values 23°51′ and 23°35′, which became 'the two numbers' in the Arabic astronomical text {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112, 150}. Later zij tables and astronomical treatises — such as the 'Zij al-Safaeih' and the 'Treatise on the Sphere' — described the same corrected value, so 23°35′ remained the basis of astronomical tables for centuries {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. As Arabic astronomy passed into Persia, India and al-Andalus, the number travelled with it — 23°35′ became the standard figure in the centres of Islamic astronomy {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. The number was not replaced until modern times, when precise astronomical instruments refined it — the modern accepted value is about 23°26′ and drifts slowly {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150}.
Its passage was not mere 'copying': every generation of astronomers re-examined it — should we take Ptolemy's transmitted value or the verified Abbasid one? — so the debate over '23°51′ or 23°35′' became part of the history of Islamic astronomy itself {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112, 150} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. This debate — between 'the transmitted' and 'the verified' — is what made the Abbasid value an 'event', not merely a 'number'.

## What it was built on
The obliquity was built on two foundations: an ancient observation — that the seasons change — and a ready mathematical model, the computations of the tilted sphere and its circles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112}. Above that it rested on the 'culture of measurement' that al-Ma'mun patronised: the order to measure and gather the scholars upon it was an institutional project, not an individual one {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Al-Farghani neither invented the principle nor measured it; he recorded the result in an official Arabic text {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112}.

## What was built on it
On the corrected value was built everything that depends on 'the Sun's tilt': computations of prayer times — because day length follows the tilt — tables of the qibla — because the Sun's direction follows the seasons — and the zij tables of the calendar — which need 'the maximum declination of the Sun' to fix the times of the solstices and equinoxes {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Every astronomical 'rule' — from the astrolabe to the celestial globe — presupposes that you know 'the obliquity of the ecliptic' first {cite:commons-ecliptic-equator} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. The value 23°35′ was therefore not 'a number in a book' but 'an entry point' for every computation that followed.

## Impact on daily life
The obliquity touched ordinary life only at its farthest horizon: the farmer knows the seasons from the Sun, not from the angle, and the worshipper knows the time from the shadow, not from the figure {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. But its impact was in the 'calendar system': the four seasons — spring, summer, autumn and winter — are computed on the Sun's tilt, and every agricultural or religious calendar that depends on fixing these seasons depends on the number {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150}. Al-Ma'mun's measurement was thus not a 'scientific experiment' but a 'service to the calendar' — because knowing 23°35′ means knowing when summer begins and when it ends {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Fixing the seasons was among the oldest duties of astronomy in every civilisation: the farmer needs to know when to sow and when to harvest, and the jurist needs to know when the lunar month begins {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. The obliquity was 'the key' by which all these computations open — hence the precision of the figure mattered not as an 'experiment' but as a 'tool' for daily life.

## How it changed history
The Abbasid measurement — which al-Farghani recorded — changed a figure inherited by Islamic astronomy from the 'Almagest': instead of 23°51′ it became 23°35′, a documented 'correction' of a Greek value within the Islamic astronomical tradition {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112}. It opened the way for a culture of 'verified measurement' — that a transmitted value can be corrected by a new measurement the scholars agree upon {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. The corrected figure remained the basis for centuries: from al-Farghani's book to the 'hay'a' zij tables to the qibla tables — all built on 23°35′ {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150}. This correction was a symbol of a larger shift: from 'transmitting the value' to 'measuring it' — the very shift that distinguishes Abbasid astronomy from mere translation of texts {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}.

## Who invented it?
The honest answer: there is no single inventor. The concept is ancient — known to Greek and Indian astronomy — the verified measurement was ordered by al-Ma'mun in the third century AH, and al-Farghani 'recorded' the result in the 'Elements' {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. His role — as his text describes it — is 'transmitter', not 'inventor': he neither measured nor claimed priority, but preserved both numbers {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112, 150}. The question 'who invented the obliquity of the ecliptic?' is a modern question that does not match the nature of ancient science: knowledge was cumulative and collective {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}.

## Today
The concept of obliquity remains the basis for understanding 'why the seasons and equinoxes succeed one another' — because the tilt of the Earth's axis is what makes the day shorter in winter and longer in summer {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150}. The historical value 23°35′ is still taught in histories of Islamic astronomy as 'the Abbasid figure' that al-Farghani recorded {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150}. The modern accepted value — about 23°26′ — drifts slowly because of the wobble of the Earth's axis, but it is 'close' to the value measured by al-Ma'mun's scholars twelve centuries ago {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150}. The modern diagram 'Ecliptic vs equator full globe' is still used in teaching books to display the two circles and their angle {cite:commons-ecliptic-equator}.
Nor has the 'feeling of tilt' vanished with the precision of instruments: when a person looks at the sky at the solstices he sees the 'Sun's path' shifting north and south — the same visual picture al-Farghani proved a thousand years ago {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150}. The principle he wrote in a school chapter remains 'the angle of the seasons' in human consciousness today — even if the language we name it with has changed {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}.

## Surviving examples
The Arabic text is the surviving witness: the 'Elements' mentions the obliquity in two places — the old value 23°51′ and the verified measurement 23°35′ — together in the same text {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112, 150}. Copies of the 'Elements' carried the figure through the tradition of astronomical teaching {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy pp. 112, 150}. ## In culture
The obliquity did not enter popular consciousness under its name, but 'the tilt of the seasons' — summer and winter — became part of the daily understanding of time {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150}. In the books of astrology the 'stations of the Sun' — the twelve positions on the ecliptic — continued to be read on this tilt, so the figure became the background for reading the 'ascendant' and 'birth' {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}. Thus a 'geometric measurement' turned into a 'cultural image' — the four seasons became 'children' of this tilt in the imagination. And the figure '23 degrees 35 minutes' — the number al-Farghani recorded — lived on in later astronomical books as 'the Abbasid number' that corrected Ptolemy's value {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150} {cite:tdv-ilm-i-felek}.
