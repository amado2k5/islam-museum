## Summary

In "Kitab al-Asrar" al-Razi (d. 925 CE) describes distilling crude petroleum in clay vessels until it yields "white naft" — a clear liquid that lights safely and serves as lamp fuel — functionally the production of kerosene. Bilkadi's historical survey calls this the earliest known written account of kerosene-making, and recounts two methods: direct distillation, and absorption into clay then distilling what is absorbed {cite:aramco-bilkadi-oil-weapons-1995}.

## What it is

An early petroleum-refining process: heating black crude oil in a vessel (a clay qaddah or alembic) so the volatile fractions rise and condense into a lighter, cleaner-burning liquid — "white naft" — leaving heavy tar behind. The text describes the product as "safe to light" {cite:aramco-bilkadi-oil-weapons-1995}.

## The world before it

Crude petroleum was gathered from surface seeps at Baku, in Iraq and Iran, and used as it was: a skin and mange remedy, an adhesive, a crude fuel, and a war material (burning naft, flaming tar). No text read had written down how to purify it by distillation {cite:aramco-bilkadi-oil-weapons-1995}.

## The story

Al-Razi recorded in his alchemical Book of Secrets two ways of making white naft: distil the petroleum directly in a clay vessel, or mix it with clay so it is absorbed and then distil the absorbent so the clean part flows. The text repeats that the product became "safe to light" — success measured by the cleanness of the flame {cite:aramco-bilkadi-oil-weapons-1995}.

## The maker(s)

Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi (c. 854–925), physician of Rayy and Baghdad and their chemist, author of the Hawi and the Asrar. His role here is "describer": he wrote the process into his chemical compendium, and the sources read cannot decide whether he devised it or documented existing craft practice.

## How it works

The principle is separation by boiling point: on heating, the lighter hydrocarbons vaporise first and are collected condensed, parting the clean fuel from the non-volatile heavy tar. Repeating the distillation — as the text recounts — increases purity until the product is fit for safe lighting {cite:aramco-bilkadi-oil-weapons-1995}.

## The spread

In the tradition read, the text never became a continuous industry; it remained a written account inside al-Razi's chemistry. Industrial kerosene returned in the nineteenth century on an independent path (Gesner, Kier, the Pennsylvania wells).

## What was built on it

The very concept of refining by distillation was built on it: every modern refinery — with its giant fractionating towers — executes the same principle at industrial scale, even without a direct line of descent from al-Razi's text.

## What it was built on

On the old distillation techniques known to the alchemists (perfume and rose-water distillation), and on a crude material the region had used unpurified since antiquity {cite:aramco-bilkadi-oil-weapons-1995}.

## Impact on daily life

It showed that a crude fuel can be technically improved into a cleaner, less dangerous one — the idea of "fuel refining" that later grounded the lighting industry.

## How it changed history

It recorded for the first time — in the record read — the "petroleum refining" step that nine centuries later became the base of the world's largest fuel industry. Its value is historical: the earliest written witness to the technique.

## Who invented it?

The text is al-Razi's without dispute; but whether he devised the technique or documented an older Iraqi-Persian craft is unsettled — hence his role is recorded as "describer" while the text's priority stands {cite:aramco-bilkadi-oil-weapons-1995}.

## Today

The same principle lives in every refinery: fractional-distillation columns split crude oil into gas, petrol, kerosene, diesel and tar — the same ordering glimpsed by the tenth-century text.

## Surviving examples

The manuscript and printed text of Kitab al-Asrar, and Bilkadi's 1995 historical reading of it in Saudi Aramco World {cite:aramco-bilkadi-oil-weapons-1995}.

## In culture

It appears in the narrative of "the beginnings of the oil industry" and in chemical-technology historiography as the link between crude use and modern refining.

## For young readers

Eleven hundred years ago a physician named al-Razi wrote a strange recipe: take the sticky black oil, boil it in a clay pot, and collect the vapour — you get a clear white oil that lights lamps safely. That is the very idea today's refineries use to make fuel for cars and planes.
