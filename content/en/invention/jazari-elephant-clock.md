## Summary

Around 1206 CE the Artuqid court engineer al-Jazari built for his sultan the most famous water clock of the Middle Ages: a life-sized elephant bearing a castle, driven by a hidden float-and-pulley mechanism that made a bird sing, a scribe's pen rotate to mark the half-hour, a dragon dip to a trough, and cymbals sound — a mechanical pageant he documented himself in his illustrated "Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices" {cite:jazari-hill-1974}.

## What it is

A feedback-controlled water clock: water drains slowly from a tank inside the elephant, a float sinks, and pulleys convert its descent into timed releases that trigger the figures in sequence. The mechanism resets each cycle — an automated show, not just a timekeeper {cite:jazari-hill-1974}.

## The world before it

Water clocks were ancient — Greek clepsydrae and earlier Indian and Persian bowls measured time silently. What did not exist was a clock that *performed*: figures moving, birds singing, all reset automatically, at this documented scale.

## The story

Badi' al-Zaman al-Jazari (fl. 1200) served the Artuqid rulers of Diyarbakir for about twenty-five years as chief engineer. At their request he compiled his book of fifty machines — pumps, clocks, automata, fountains — each with instructions "so that it may be built." The elephant clock crowns the clock chapters and became the book's emblem {cite:jazari-hill-1974}.

## The maker(s)

Al-Jazari (Isma'il ibn al-Razzaz), the Artuqid court engineer — a Muslim craftsman-scholar documented as such in the biographical sources; the record notes his self-authored book as the attestation.

## How it works

Inside the elephant's belly a perforated tank (a "regulator") sinks in a basin at a controlled rate; ropes over pulleys link the float to the scribe's pen, the bird's whistle, the dragon's tilt and the striker's cymbal. A siphon resets the cycle each half hour {cite:jazari-hill-1974}.

## The spread

The machine itself was court art — built once, in Diyarbakir. The book spread: lavish manuscript copies carried the designs across the Islamic world, and scholars cite al-Jazari's mechanisms in the genealogy of automata.

## What it was built on

On the Hellenistic water-clock tradition — Archimedes' and Ctesibius's float devices, the Automata of the Banu Musa that al-Jazari cites — plus Indian elephant iconography and Persian court motifs; he fused them into one machine.

## What was built on it

The European automaton tradition was built on the same family of ideas — the great astronomical clocks and clockwork figures of later centuries share his vocabulary of floats, cams and staged releases.

## Impact on daily life

It turned the palace into a theatre of engineering: time announced itself as spectacle — and the book turned courtly craft into teachable engineering literature.

## How it changed history

It stands as the documented summit of medieval automata: a Muslim engineer publishing reproducible instructions for self-moving machines two centuries before comparable European texts {cite:jazari-hill-1974}.

## Who invented it?

Al-Jazari, without rival for this machine: he names his precedents (the Banu Musa, Archimedes) and claims the elephant clock as his own design — the sources read accept the claim {cite:jazari-hill-1974}.

## Today

Working replicas demonstrate the mechanism at the Met, in Istanbul and elsewhere; the manuscripts (Topkapi, Leiden, Oxford) are the standard corpus of early machine engineering.

## Surviving examples

The illustrated manuscripts and modern reconstructions; no original mechanism survives {cite:jazari-hill-1974}.

## In culture

The elephant clock is the icon of "Islamic Golden Age" engineering — on stamps, logos and documentaries worldwide.

## For young readers

Eight hundred years ago an engineer built a clock with an elephant, a singing bird and a feeding dragon — all moved by dripping water, no electricity. He wrote and drew exactly how it worked so anyone could rebuild it. That engineer was al-Jazari.
