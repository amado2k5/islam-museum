## Summary
The tarsh is the Islamic world's own printing craft: an engraved block of wood or tin, inked and pressed onto paper, in use from about 900 to 1436/1444. More than a hundred prints survive — mostly small amulet strips, but also official stamps and decrees — yet no tarsh block itself has ever been found {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en} {cite:schaefer-enigmatic-charms-2006}.

## What it is
A relief matrix carved in reverse: the printer inks its face and presses it onto paper or parchment, producing a page in a single impression. Surviving prints range from bookmark-sized amulet strips to a 124.6 cm talisman, printed in one block or assembled from several — different blocks for decoration, different scripts, even different sections of text, a modular system tailored to each client {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en}.

## The world before it
Before the tarsh, every copy of a text was written by hand. China had block printing for centuries, but whether the craft travelled west with paper or was invented independently in the Islamic world is the record's open question {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en} {cite:bulliet-tarsh-1987}.

## The story
The witnesses run from Ibn al-Nadim's Fihrist — where Egyptian magicians use stamps — through Abu Dulaf's panegyric of c. 952, which names the tarsh engraver who sells printed amulets to the illiterate, to al-Jawbari's Kashf al-Asrar of c. 1232-1248, where matrices called turush print amulets every day. States used it too: Fatimid chancery stamps reading "al-Imam al-Hakim" marked tax receipts, Almohad decrees were printed, and the Ilkhans printed paper money in 1294 {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en} {cite:schaefer-enigmatic-charms-2006}.

## The maker(s)
The printers were mostly the Banu Sasan, a confederation of socially marginal people whose dialect supplies the whole craft vocabulary — tarsh for the block, tarash for the printer, sarmat for the printed amulet. Secrecy around the craft was part of the trade: printed amulets were sold as if handwritten {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en}.

## How it works
The engraver cuts the design into the block in reverse, the printer inks the raised face and presses paper onto it — or rolls the block across the sheet — then cuts the printed roll into individual amulets. Multiple blocks could be struck on one sheet; eight surviving sets of near-identical multiples show the wood wearing down between impressions {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en}.

## The spread
Centred on Egypt — prints were excavated at Fustat and at the Red Sea port of al-Qusayr al-Qadim — the craft reached Syria, al-Andalus by the tenth century, and Persia where the Ilkhanids printed currency. Kristina Richardson argues Ghuraba Romani migrants may have carried it into Central Europe around 1400 {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en}.

## What it was built on
Older habits: seal-stamping on clay and paper across the Near East, Chinese woodblock printing somewhere in the background, and the amulet trade's need for identical texts at scale {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en}.

## What was built on it
Possibly more than we know: the Ilkhanid paper-money experiment of 1294 is direct state use, and if Richardson's Romani transmission holds, European block printing may descend partly from the tarsh. The craft itself vanished after 1436/1444 and was forgotten until the nineteenth century {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en} {cite:schaefer-enigmatic-charms-2006}.

## Impact on daily life
For ordinary people the tarsh meant cheap access to the written word — an amulet for the neck or the doorway against the evil eye, fever or a rival's tongue, bought ready-printed rather than commissioned letter by letter {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en}.

## How it changed history
It is proof that the Islamic world knew printing — mechanically multiplying text — five centuries before the press became transformative elsewhere, and its disappearance without becoming book printing is one of the great puzzles of print history {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en}.

## Who invented it?
Unknown. The craft was anonymous workshop work; whether it was carried from China or invented on Egyptian soil is the contested question this record keeps open {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en} {cite:bulliet-tarsh-1987}.

## Today
The hundred-odd prints sit in Vienna, Cairo and American collections, catalogued by Schaefer and studied by Bulliet, Muehlhaeusler and Richardson — every original block still lost {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en} {cite:schaefer-enigmatic-charms-2006}.

## Surviving examples
Amulet strips in Kufic and naskh, quadrilingual prints of the Seven Sleepers in Arabic, Hebrew, Syriac and Coptic borders, a Hebrew print from the Cairo Geniza, Fatimid tax-receipt stamps, and the 124.6 cm talisman of 1436-1444, the last known tarsh print {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en}.

## In culture
The amulets themselves are the culture: folded into lead cylinders worn at the neck, some dissolved in water and drunk, carrying basmalas, the names of God, magic squares and geometric seals {cite:wikipedia-tarsh-en}.

## For young readers
Imagine a wooden stamp carved with Arabic writing: dip it in ink, press it on paper, and a written page appears in a second. Craftsmen in Egypt and Syria made thousands of identical amulets this way — hundreds of years before the printing press.
