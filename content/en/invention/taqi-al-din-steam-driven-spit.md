## Summary
In the Al-Turuq al-saniyya fi al-alat al-ruhaniyya of 1551, Taqi al-Din Muhammad ibn Ma'ruf described a roasting spit turned by steam: a jet of vapour from a boiling vessel strikes the vanes of a wheel on the spit's axle and the roast rotates by itself — the earliest known description of the impulse steam-turbine principle {cite:wikipedia-taqi-al-din-en} {cite:wikipedia-steam-engine-en}.

## What it is
A kitchen machine that is also a power idea: boiler below, jet pipe rising, a vane wheel on the spit's horizontal axle — steam does the turning that a cook's hand or a hot-air draught wheel once did {cite:wikipedia-taqi-al-din-en}.

## The world before it
Steam had been a toy for fifteen centuries: Hero of Alexandria's aeolipile spun on escaping jets in the first century, and roasting spits were turned by hand, by dogs in wheels, or by hot air rising past a fan — nobody had yet put steam to work {cite:wikipedia-steam-engine-en}.

## The story
Taqi al-Din wrote the treatise during his twenty Egyptian years, before the call to Constantinople made him chief astronomer and builder of the great observatory; the same book carries his four water-raising machines, among them the six-cylinder pump the museum already records {cite:wikipedia-taqi-al-din-en} {cite:taqi-turuq-hasan-1976}.

## The maker(s)
Taqi al-Din Muhammad ibn Ma'ruf — Damascus-born, educated in the religious sciences, teacher, qadi in Palestine, Damascus and Egypt, then Ottoman chief astronomer: the polymath whose ninety books span astronomy, mechanics and optics {cite:wikipedia-taqi-al-din-en} {cite:taqi-fazlioglu-bea}.

## How it works
Water in a closed vessel boils; the steam rises through a pipe and issues as a jet directed onto the blades of a wheel; the wheel spins, and since it sits on the spit's axle the meat turns steadily over the fire — impulse force, the same principle Parsons would industrialise {cite:wikipedia-taqi-al-din-en} {cite:wikipedia-steam-engine-en}.

## The spread
It did not spread: the manuscript's copies were few, the device stayed a page in a book. The next impulse-turbine description is Giovanni Branca's of 1629, some eighty years later in Italy, with no documented link between them {cite:wikipedia-steam-engine-en}.

## What it was built on
The aeolipile's demonstration that steam pushes; the kitchen's older answer of hand-turned and draught-driven spits; and the same engineer's toolkit of axles, wheels and jets that runs through the whole treatise {cite:wikipedia-taqi-al-din-en} {cite:wikipedia-steam-engine-en}.

## What was built on it
Directly, nothing — the lineage is of ideas, not workshops. But the steam-engine history reads the device as the first link after Hero: Taqi al-Din 1551, Branca 1629, then the pump engines of Savery, Newcomen and Watt {cite:wikipedia-steam-engine-en}.

## Impact on daily life
None in its own day — the machine's life is documentary. Its meaning for us is that the working-steam idea existed in an Ottoman book before it existed in a European drawing {cite:wikipedia-taqi-al-din-en}.

## How it changed history
It dates the mindset: the conviction that steam can do work appears in the Islamic record in 1551, a hundred and fifty years before the first commercial engine {cite:wikipedia-steam-engine-en}.

## Who invented it?
Taqi al-Din — as a described design in his own treatise. No built machine is recorded, and the museum's claim is exactly that: the first written description of a steam-driven rotating device {cite:wikipedia-taqi-al-din-en} {cite:taqi-turuq-hasan-1976}.

## Today
The text survives in the 959 AH manuscript and al-Hasan's 1976 edition; historians of engineering cite the spit whenever the steam turbine's prehistory is told {cite:taqi-turuq-hasan-1976} {cite:wikipedia-taqi-al-din-en}.

## Surviving examples
The device itself: none — it was a described machine. The manuscript tradition of al-Turuq al-saniyya is the survivor, edited by Ahmad Yusuf al-Hasan in 1976 {cite:taqi-turuq-hasan-1976}.

## In culture
It sits in Taqi al-Din's book of "spiritual machines" — automata and clever devices — the same courtly engineering culture that gave the elephant clock and the six-cylinder pump {cite:taqi-turuq-hasan-1976}.

## For young readers
Imagine a pot boiling under a roast: steam rises, pushes the blades of a little wheel, and the wheel spins the meat. A Muslim scholar drew this steam-working machine in his book four hundred years ago — long before steam trains.
