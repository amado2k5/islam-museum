## Summary

This is the story of moving a craft to a city, not inventing paper again: its Chinese origin precedes Islam by centuries, and Central Asia was part of its westward spread {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}. Osman Ersoy attributes the proposal for a paper workshop in [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] in178 AH/794–795 to [[person/al-fadl-ibn-yahya-al-barmaki|al-Fadl ibn Yahya al-Barmaki]], under [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]] {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}. Jonathan Bloom supports manufacture in Baghdad by the late eighth century without independently confirming that year or proposer {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}. The browser records al-Fadl's specific act of transmission and proposal, not the first sheet of paper or machinery undescribed in the sources read {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}.

## What it is

Paper is a mat of cellulose fibers: its material is broken down in water, collected on a screen and dried into a writing sheet; the fibers may come from plants, rags or old ropes {cite:iranica-paper-i opening definition}. It is not simply a slice of wood, an animal-skin parchment or papyrus made from crossing strips: material and method of formation are part of the history of writing {cite:tdv-kagit opening materials} {cite:iranica-paper-i opening definition}.

“Establishing papermaking” here means local production of an existing craft in Baghdad, rather than merely receiving imported bundles {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}. The reference supplies a proposal, place and date, but not workers' names, floor area or a machinery drawing; this record cannot describe a particular Baghdad production line as if its plan survived {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}.

## The world before it

Writing materials included stone, wood, metal and bone tablets, papyrus and prepared skins, with methods of preparation unlike forming paper from fibers suspended in water {cite:tdv-kagit opening materials}. Paper's arrival did not mean every older material disappeared overnight: Bloom describes Iranian Muslims initially adopting it for records and only gradually replacing parchment in book copying, including Qur'an manuscripts {cite:iranica-paper-i early manuscripts}.

In Bloom's account, Buddhist monks and missionaries carried paper through parts of Asia before Islam, and it was known in Central Asia before the Islamic conquests {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}. Baghdad therefore belongs within a long transmission chain, not at its beginning; the history of all that paper cannot be credited to one person appearing in the late eighth century {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}. Al-Fadl's local story is narrower than the material's history: a proposed production establishment in an Abbasid city {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}.

## The story

In TDV, Ersoy places the Baghdad workshop under Harun, gives178 AH/794–795, and says it was founded at the suggestion of al-Fadl ibn Yahya, the Barmakid governor of Khurasan {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}. This identifies a proposal and transfer, rather than equating everything during Harun's reign with the caliph's personal invention {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}.

In Iranica, Bloom places Baghdad manufacture by the late eighth century within paper's movement from Central Asia to Iran and the wider Islamic lands {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}. The references agree on the broad setting, but the second paragraph neither names al-Fadl nor supplies178 AH; that agreement is not two independent witnesses to every workshop detail {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph} {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}.

No contemporary foundation document or workshop accounts were read for this article: the proposal and date are reported from the identified modern reference, not a document the museum claims to have inspected {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}.

## The maker(s)

Al-Fadl was an Abbasid administrator, the son of [[person/yahya-ibn-khalid-al-barmaki|Yahya ibn Khalid]] of the [[group/barmakids|Barmakids]] and Harun's foster-brother; his biography records eastern governorships and responsibility for al-Amin's education {cite:tdv-bermekiler al-Fadl career}. Hakki Dursun Yildiz describes his work in Khurasan, including irrigation canals, his return to Baghdad and his changing administrative position before the family's fall {cite:tdv-bermekiler al-Fadl career}. None makes him the craftsperson who built the molds: his role here is the proposer credited with establishing the industry {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}.

**Museum Sunni frame and identity basis:** Yildiz personally names al-Fadl in the802 pilgrimage party and associates him with Ramadan mosque lighting; this is biographical evidence for Muslim identity, not an inference from name, ancestry or office {cite:tdv-bermekiler pilgrimage and mosque lighting}. His record makes the default Sunni frame and evidential limits explicit, without inventing a personal legal-school declaration, and displays the family allegations reported by Yildiz without adopting them {cite:tdv-bermekiler allegations discussion}.

The paragraph read does not name Baghdad's working papermakers; that gap is not filled with invented names of prisoners or artisans {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}.

## How it works

The shared principle can be explained without inventing al-Fadl's machinery:

1. **Prepare the fibers:** use plant material or textile and rope waste; Bloom explicitly includes rags and old ropes among fiber sources {cite:iranica-paper-i opening definition}.
2. **Break them down wet:** pound or grind the material with water to make a substance that can be suspended in it, rather than leaving the fabric or rope in its original form {cite:iranica-paper-i opening definition}.
3. **Form a sheet:** collect the suspended material on a screen, turn it out and dry it; this reconstitutes fibers rather than bonding crossing papyrus strips {cite:iranica-paper-i opening definition} {cite:tdv-kagit opening materials}.
4. **Prepare the writing surface:** Bloom describes starch sizing and burnishing in Iran, while Ersoy explains sizing and polishing to reduce ink spread and prepare a surface for calligraphy {cite:iranica-paper-i opening definition} {cite:tdv-kagit sizing and burnishing}.

Ersoy describes soaking rags and ropes in limewater, beating them, and later water-driven stones in the history of the industry; this does not establish the arrangement of the Baghdad workshop {cite:tdv-kagit manufacture discussion}. “Workshop” cannot therefore establish priority in inventing a water-powered paper mill or a particular wheel: the account concerns an evolving craft, not a documented Baghdad-origin machine with known components {cite:tdv-kagit manufacture discussion and Baghdad paragraph}.

## The spread

Bloom places manufacture in Baghdad by the late eighth century and Egypt by the late ninth, and paper use in Iberia by the late tenth; these are different acts, so “used” does not establish manufacture at every location {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}. Ersoy names later centers in Damascus, Tripoli, Hama, Yemen and Egypt, and the Xativa industry described by al-Idrisi in the twelfth century {cite:tdv-kagit diffusion discussion}.

These places and dates show expansion, not that every workshop received its workers or molds directly from al-Fadl's establishment {cite:tdv-kagit diffusion discussion} {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}. Nor is a prisoner route drawn from Talas to Baghdad as a documented itinerary of named people: that is precisely where Bloom challenges the popular transmission story {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}.

## What it was built on

The earlier foundation is Chinese papermaking; Baghdad's closer background is the movement of paper and skills through Central Asia, including Samarkand, not a material vacuum suddenly filled by an Abbasid workshop {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}. Bloom reports the *Hudud al-Alam* account of a Manichaean community producing paper for export in Samarkand; this is reported through his article, not an original edition read for this account {cite:iranica-paper-i medieval paper professions}.

Earlier communities and crafts remain named where the reference identifies them: contributions outside Islam are not erased to make the chain look simpler {cite:iranica-paper-i early history and medieval paper professions}. Conversely, their mention does not itself prove a direct link to the Baghdad workforce; that exceeds the evidence in the paragraphs read {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}.

## What was built on it

Wider paper use supported copying, administration and preparation of the page, but the movement from records to books was gradual in Bloom's account {cite:iranica-paper-i early manuscripts}. He identifies a paper Qur'an manuscript dated361 AH/971–972 and later Iranian books and copies: a later surviving witness is neither the first paper used everywhere nor automatically a product of al-Fadl's workshop {cite:iranica-paper-i early manuscripts}.

Ersoy reports paper types from the *Fihrist*, including Ja'fari, Talhi and Tahiri; a type named after someone does not prove that person invented its material or every manufacturing step {cite:tdv-kagit paper types paragraph}. Sizing, burnishing, calligraphy and illumination extend the story as uses and treatments, not one patent stretching from Baghdad to every later page {cite:tdv-kagit sizing and burnishing}.

## Impact on daily life

Bloom's clearest distinction is paper's adoption for records and then books: the material belongs within the history of scribal work and book production {cite:iranica-paper-i early manuscripts}. Ersoy connects its abundance and diffusion with flourishing centers of knowledge and culture, naming Samarkand as an example {cite:tdv-kagit diffusion discussion}.

Use extended beyond books: Ersoy reports Nasir Khusraw's mention of paper among materials for wrapping goods in Egypt as evidence of diverse use and plentiful production {cite:tdv-kagit Nasir Khusraw paragraph}. That supplies neither Baghdad prices in794 nor a literacy rate; an example of wrapping from another period cannot become an economic statistic for the earlier workshop {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad and Nasir Khusraw paragraphs}.

## How it changed history

This story makes a material side of knowledge visible: fibers and surface preparation move alongside texts, and Bloom describes paper's spread as transformative for emerging Islamic civilization {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}. It is not a single-cause explanation: the sources read do not make al-Fadl's proposal the origin of every translation movement, library or science {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph} {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}.

Baghdad has a specific place here: local production of an incoming material, a named proposer in one encyclopedic account, and independent support for the broad chronological setting {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph} {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}. That contribution can be described without turning the transmitter into paper's worldwide inventor {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}.

## Who invented it?

**Sunni reference, TDV account:** Ersoy places the origin in China, describes Central Asian transmission stories including Talas-associated prisoners, and attributes the Baghdad initiative to al-Fadl; these are different levels of historical account, not a contemporary workshop witness {cite:tdv-kagit origins and Baghdad paragraphs}.

**Academic view, Jonathan Bloom:** Bloom criticizes the Talas-prisoner story, citing its late reporting, Central Asia's earlier knowledge of paper and the absence of historical evidence requiring Chinese papermakers in that particular transfer {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}. He also finds no hard evidence behind repeated assertions of Sasanian use of Chinese paper, unlike Ersoy's affirmative account; the difference is preserved rather than silently merged into a certain story {cite:iranica-paper-i early history} {cite:tdv-kagit pre-Islamic diffusion}.

The record concerns a localized transfer in Baghdad with a specified attribution, not certainty about every earlier story or global priority for someone the references do not call paper's inventor {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph} {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}.

## Today

Defining paper as a sheet of fibers collected from water and dried remains useful despite varying raw materials and treatments {cite:iranica-paper-i opening definition}. The [[media/konigil-paper-bark-preparation|photograph shown]] is Enric's2018 view of mulberry-bark preparation at Meros, Konigil: modern craft context, not the Abbasid workshop or proof it used mulberry rather than rags {cite:commons-konigil-paper-enric file metadata}.

Photographer, location, date and licence are documented in the media record; no photographed worker's religion or Baghdad artisan's identity is inferred from it {cite:commons-konigil-paper-enric file metadata}.

## Surviving examples

The immediate evidence for the proposal's attribution here is a modern reference text; no surviving building or tools of al-Fadl's workshop were identified in the sources read {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}. Bloom describes the scarcity of direct evidence for early Islamic paper production and use in Iran, including preservation conditions unfavorable to fragile organic materials {cite:iranica-paper-i early manuscripts}.

The dated sheets and books he describes attest use in particular places and times; survival alone cannot make them securely identified products of that Baghdad establishment {cite:iranica-paper-i early manuscripts}.

## In culture

Ersoy connects paper with calligraphy, illumination, bookbinding and other arts, its treatment helping determine the surface receiving ink and ornament {cite:tdv-kagit sizing and burnishing}. Bloom describes smooth, burnished sheets accommodating flowing nasta'liq in the Iranian tradition: a later artistic use, not decoration retrospectively assigned to an eighth-century workshop {cite:iranica-paper-i opening definition}.

The page brings material and labor together: preparation of fibers and surfaces, then use by a scribe or calligrapher; the references reveal that material side of book culture without inventing names for unknown artisans {cite:iranica-paper-i opening definition and early manuscripts} {cite:tdv-kagit sizing and burnishing}.

## For young readers

Imagine an old rope: while it remains a rope, you cannot write across its threads as a single page; papermaking separates fibers in water, gathers them into a new surface and dries it {cite:iranica-paper-i opening definition}. A sheet is therefore not simply flattened tree bark, although some fibers can come from bark {cite:iranica-paper-i opening definition}.

When asking “Who made it?”, ask another question: the first paper, or bringing a craft to a city? Here the earlier origin is Chinese, and al-Fadl's story is a proposal to establish Baghdad production; different questions prevent crediting one person with a craft that began centuries before him {cite:iranica-paper-i early history} {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}.
