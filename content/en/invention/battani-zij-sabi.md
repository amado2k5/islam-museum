## Summary
Al-Zij al-Sabi is al-Battani's great astronomical book, which early authors called "Zij al-Battani" because his name was better known than the later title. It gathers fifty-seven chapters with tables, filled with his own observations of the Sun and Moon at al-Raqqa and Antioch, and corrects their motions and those of the five planets as given in Ptolemy's Almagest. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40}

This is not the invention of the zij genre or of the astronomical table; computational astronomy is much older than him. The documented contribution is that al-Battani built his zij on new observational data gathered in two places, so the resulting constants were more accurate than what had been transmitted from Ptolemy, and the book became a computational reference for planetary positions, eclipses and timekeeping. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 41} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

Its sources date a first version to before 288 AH, around 900 CE, and describe a second version carrying Antioch observations of January and August 301 AH. The Latin translation reached print at Nuremberg in 1537, followed by a Castilian version, carrying the tables to scholars who did not read Arabic. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 41} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59}

## What it is
This record describes a "method" of observational zij composition: an astronomer gathers his own observations into a single book of chapters and tables, corrects inherited constants with them, and frees the calculator from returning to Ptolemy directly. Al-Zij al-Sabi is not a device or a single event; it is an organised written work that converts observation into daily computation. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17}

Its content is fifty-seven chapters covering the foundations of astronomical calculation, the stations of the planets, eclipses, crescent visibility, and astrolabe-style preliminaries in the manner of known zijes, all built on a sexagesimal numerical table. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17} {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.5}

The book is therefore a "computational system" in the strict sense: observational inputs and table outputs, with rules for extracting the positions of celestial bodies at any given hour. This is what later writers called "al-Zij al-Sabi", and what the Latin tradition knew as De scientia stellarum. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59}

## The world before it
Before al-Battani there was an established computational astronomy resting on the transmission of Ptolemy's constants from the Almagest and on earlier zijes that carried ready-made tables to readers. The goal was not for every astronomer to redo observation from scratch; it was enough to open a zij and find the tables ready. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40} {cite:battani-mactutor}

Among the most transmitted items were tables of planetary motions, the obliquity of the ecliptic, and several constants such as the Sun's apparent inclination and the planetary year lengths. Yet these constants carried accumulated error from old observation, so checking them against new observations was scientifically important before it was a luxury. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

In Abbasid Raqqa a culture of astronomical observation flourished; there was a real observatory tradition and scientific institutions, and al-Battani was one of the astronomers of his age who did not content himself with transmission but measured with his own eyes. This environment made writing a zij from new observations possible. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:bibalex-azhar-astronomy-manuscripts-2006 p. 17}

## The story
The story begins with long observation: al-Battani spent years at Raqqa and then at Antioch measuring risings of the planets and of the Sun and Moon, recording eclipses and timings, and comparing what he saw with the Almagest. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 41} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

From these observations came the book: he arranged its chapters beginning with astronomical preliminaries, then chapters on computing stations, eclipses, crescent visibility, and closing with the tables of the bodies. The work summary states it runs to fifty-seven chapters with tables, holding his observations of the Sun and Moon at Raqqa and Antioch and corrections to their motions and those of the five planets. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17} {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.5}

As for the exact dating of the versions there is a story: Nallino holds that a first version predates 288 AH because Thabit ibn Qurra, who died in February 901, cited its chapter 52; the Escorial recension and Plato of Tivoli's translation mention Antioch observations of January and August 301 AH, so they represent a later second version. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 41}

After al-Battani's death the book kept being read and commented on: Plato of Tivoli translated it into Latin in Barcelona in the first half of the twelfth century CE, later bibliographers named it "the Sabian Zij", and al-Biruni is reported to have written a commentary on it titled Jala' al-adhhan fi zijihi. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59}

## The maker(s)
Al-Battani is Muhammad ibn Jabir ibn Sinan al-Battani al-Harrani, then al-Battani by the name of a village of his work, an Abbasid Arab astronomer who lived at Raqqa and died in 317 AH. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

His role here is "improver", not "inventor": he did not invent the zij, the table, or observation itself; he improved an existing genre by gathering new observations and correcting Ptolemaic constants. That is the classification the evidence imposes, no more. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

His Muslim identity is documented in his biography: he grew up in a scientific Islamic environment, wrote in Arabic, served in Raqqa's scholarly institutions, and Arabic astronomical sources describe him as a Muslim scholar without needing further proof. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

## How it works
The zij works as a computational guide: give it a city, a time, and a place in the heavens, and the reader returns to the tables to find the planet's position for a period, then applies the corrective motions al-Battani observed to obtain the true position at a given hour. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17}

Its methodological chapters cover how to extract risings and settings, how to compute night and day hours, the equations of the Sun and Moon, eclipse tables, and chapters on crescent visibility and its astronomical conditions, all built on a sexagesimal base like its predecessors. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17} {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.5}

The book's computational strength lies in its tables: instead of the astronomer computing everything from Ptolemy's formulas, he finds ready tables for the elementary functions he needs. This is what made the zij a practical reference in schools and observatories. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40}

## The spread
The book spread first through the Islamic world: the late Arabic Escorial recension survives as a witness to its copying and circulation, and astronomers of East and West read it. Then it crossed the sea: Plato of Tivoli translated the text into Latin in Barcelona, and the translation appeared in print at Nuremberg in 1537 alongside al-Farghani's work and the printer's additions, reaching European scholars. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59}

A Castilian translation followed around the thirteenth century AH, carrying the zij to al-Andalus and Castile. Thus "Zij al-Battani" became one of the first Islamic astronomical books to enter Europe's printed libraries. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40}

This spread was not mere copying; later scholars modified its constants, as the Maragha and Sana'a zijes did in inheriting its observational-correction method. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

## What it was built on
The zij rests on three foundations: first, Ptolemy's Almagest, from which it took models of motion (the eccentric and epicycle) and the mode of presentation, with a declared programme of correction rather than blind imitation. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17} {cite:battani-mactutor}

Second, the tradition of earlier Islamic astronomical zijes that inherited the "book of chapters and tables" format from the astronomers of al-Ma'mun and those before them, so the general structure is not al-Battani's invention. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40}

Third, his own observations: risings, eclipses and measured quantities at Raqqa and Antioch over years, which are the basis distinguishing this zij from a mere rearrangement of earlier books. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 41} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

## What was built on it
Built directly on it: Plato of Tivoli's Latin translation printed in 1537, the Castilian version, and al-Biruni's reported commentary Jala' al-adhhan fi zijihi, which the work record names as a commentary on it. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40}

Built on it also: the later zijes that inherited its corrections - the Sana'a and Maragha observational programmes, and the zij later writers called "al-Zij al-Sabi" itself, which became a root of astronomical computation schooling. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

In Europe, the 1537 edition was read by Renaissance astronomers, and al-Battani's tables entered the readings that fed corrected astronomical reckonings in the Latin astronomical books read by European scholars. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59}

## Impact on daily life
The zij had no direct effect on a Muslim's daily household as a device, but it touched a deeper institution: the computation of prayer times, eclipse timings and crescent visibility, matters bound to daily worship. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

Any error in the Sun's and Moon's tables reflects on mosque prayer times and on the lunar-month calendar that requires crescent sighting; accuracy here is not a luxury but service to a religious institution. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17}

Merchants, sailors and geographers also benefited: the latitudes and longitudes its tables compute enter map-making and the fixing of landmarks. These effects are indirect but real. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40}

## How it changed history
The zij changed astronomical history in two ways. First, it proved that the inherited Greek tradition could be corrected by new observations from Islamic lands, so it forced the question "do we settle for transmission or redo observation?" - and the zij answered: redo observation. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 41} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

Second, it carried that correction into Europe itself: the 1537 edition made al-Battani's tables available to scholars who did not read Arabic, so "al-Zij al-Sabi" became a bridge between two astronomical traditions that did not share a language. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40}

In this sense al-Zij al-Sabi was a turning point: from "mere translation" to "new observations correcting the heritage" - the step later observatory ages would build on. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

## Who invented it?
The precise answer: not an "inventor" but an "author-improver". Al-Battani did not invent the zij genre, the table, or observation; before him there were zijes, a table method and an observing method. What he authored is this book itself: his zij, with his observations and his corrections. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17} {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

That is why his attribution is recorded as "improvement", not "original": the real contribution is that his zij rested on new local observations and corrected Ptolemy - something not every zij author before him did to the same breadth. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 41} {cite:battani-mactutor}

On identity: al-Battani is a documented Muslim in his biography, his book is in Arabic, and Arabic sources name it "Zij al-Battani" before later writers called it "the Sabian Zij". {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

## Today
Today nobody computes with al-Zij al-Sabi, but its value remains historical and scientific: Nallino's edition (Milan, 1899, Arabic/Latin/tables) returned the text to modern reading, and the Byblion 2005 edition made it more widely available. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 41} {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.5}

Reading it today shows how Islamic astronomical tables were built: how elementary functions are extracted from observation, how tables are arranged, and how authors corrected inherited constants. That is the zij's real lesson: not a single number but a methodology. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17}

Its study still matters to historians of astronomy, to museums displaying the origins of Islamic astronomy, and to anyone asking how Muslims knew planetary positions before computers: the answer is zijes like al-Zij al-Sabi. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

## Surviving examples
The surviving copies: the Arabic Escorial manuscript no. 908, in 245 folios in Maghribi script, copied in the late eleventh or early twelfth century CE by Nallino's estimate. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 70} {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.5}

The printed editions: Part III of Nallino's Opus astronomicum holds the Arabic text from the Escorial manuscript (Hoepli, Milan, 1899), Part I the Latin chapters, and Part II the tables; Dar wa-Maktabat Byblion reprinted the Arabic text in 2005. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 870} {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.5}

The Latin translation: published at Nuremberg in 1537, the earliest European printed edition of the zij, still a reference for historians of astronomy. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59}

## In culture
Al-Zij al-Sabi did not become a popular icon, but it is a scientific one: every history of Islamic astronomy names it among the great zijes. Its very name "al-Sabi" tells a story: later writers saw it as a revision of the Sabians' (Magian) astronomy and named it thus, while the ancients knew it by its author's name. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40} {cite:battani-mactutor}

In the teaching tradition it became the standard example of the "observational zij": a book built on its author's own observations rather than transmission - an example studied wherever the question "how does Islamic astronomy work?" is asked. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography}

There are no surviving material monuments "of the zij" itself, but its images in printed facsimiles - such as the lunar-phases plate in Nallino's edition - give a sense of what this book looked like to the eye. {cite:commons-battani-zij-phases}

## For young readers
Imagine an old book like a "computer program" written on paper: it has 57 chapters, and each chapter teaches you how to calculate something in the sky - where the Sun will be tomorrow, when the crescent can be seen, how long an eclipse lasts. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17}

The hero is al-Battani, a Muslim scientist who lived in the city of Raqqa a thousand years ago. He would lift his eyes to the sky and measure, then write down what he saw in tables. Instead of copying the old numbers, he corrected them. {cite:tdv-battani-muftuoglu biography} {cite:battani-mactutor}

This book, "al-Battani's Zij", travelled to Europe after him, where scholars who did not know Arabic read it. That is how they learned a new astronomy. This book teaches us that science knows no borders of language or religion - it knows only those who ask and measure. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40}
