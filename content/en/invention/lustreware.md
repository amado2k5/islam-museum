## Summary
Lustre is the pottery that looks like metal. A pigment of silver and copper oxides, painted on a glazed pot and fired a second time in a smoky, air-starved kiln, leaves a film of metal on the glaze that no engraving could match — gold, ruby, mother-of-pearl. Muslim potters took the technique from glass to pottery in Abbasid Iraq, made it the most valued ceramic of the Islamic world for five centuries, and handed it, through Muslim Spain, to the potters of Europe {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it is
An overglaze metallic painting technique. The pigment — Lane describes it as sulphur compounded with silver oxide and copper oxide, carried in an ochre vehicle — is painted onto an already-fired glazed vessel and fired again in a reducing kiln: little air, plenty of smoke. The metallic elements deposit on the glaze as a glittering film not perceptible to the touch. It is hazardous: too much heat burns the pigment away entirely {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## The world before it
Egypt had glazed and painted for millennia; lustre-painted glass was made in early Islamic Egypt — Lane judges it was "first used in Egypt, to decorate glass, sometime in the first two centuries of Islam". What nobody had yet done was put the metallic sheen on pottery — and pottery, unlike glass, was the art that fed every table {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## The story
The excavations of Samarra, the Abbasid capital of 838-883, turned up the beginning: earthenware painted in polychrome lustre, the potters visibly experimenting with different metallic combinations — a rich ruby red among them, won by accident. About 862 a batch of lustred wall-tiles went out to the Great Mosque of Kairouan in Tunisia, accompanied by a Baghdad potter; they are still in the mihrab. By the tenth century the ware had simplified to monochrome and was exported as far as Samarkand, Sind, Egypt and Cordoba's Madinat al-Zahra. Then Baghdad's production dwindled and the craft moved: to Fatimid Fustat, where twelfth-century potters signed their golden dishes (the potter Sa'd signs several), and on to Persia — Rayy, then Kashan, whose thirteenth-century star-tiles and mihrabs are the technique's most famous flowering {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The maker(s)
A workshop tradition, not a named inventor — the way a craft actually moves. The written witnesses are late but precious: Abulqasim of Kashan, himself of the Abu Tahir potting family, wrote a technical treatise in 1301 recording the Seljuk potters' ingredients and kiln practice, which Lane cites. The named hands are the signers — Sa'd at Fatimid Fustat — and the anonymous potters of Samarra's kilns {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## How it works
Two firings. First the pot is glazed — the Abbasid way, in an opaque white tin glaze — and fired. Then the painter brushes the design in the metallic pigment mixed with ochre and a medium such as vinegar, and the vessel goes back into the kiln under a reducing atmosphere: smoke, little oxygen, no clear flame. The metals — copper for the lustrous shine, silver for the yellow stain — deposit as a film; the ochre is rubbed gently away. A thick deposit reads as solid copper or greenish gold; a thin one shimmers like mother-of-pearl {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## The spread
Everywhere the Islamic world traded: Samarra's monochrome bowls reached Samarkand, Brahminabad in Sind, Medina Azzahra in Spain; the Kairouan tiles carried it to Ifriqiya; Fatimid Egypt made it court art; Persia made it monumental. Finally, Muslim Spain — Malaga, then Manises — kept the furnaces burning under Christian rule, and the 'Hispano-Moresque' ware taught Italian maiolica the secret in the fifteenth century {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it was built on
Egyptian lustre-painted glass — the pigment and the reducing kiln were already Egyptian; the opaque white tin glaze of the Abbasid potters, made to rival Chinese porcelain; and the Tang stoneware shapes the early bowls copied {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## What was built on it
The whole golden age of Islamic ceramics: the Kairouan mihrab's hundred and fifty tiles, the Fatimid court dishes, the Kashan star-tiles and mihrabs, Rayy's figural lustre — and beyond Islam, the lustred pottery of Manises and the Italian maiolica that learned the method from the 'Moresque' wares {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Impact on daily life
Lustre was luxury tableware — the pottery that looked like gold and silver vessels while staying, as EI2 notes, clear of the religious objection to eating from precious metal. It dressed the tables of caliphs and the walls of mosques; ordinary kitchens used the humbler glazed wares around it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it changed history
It fixed the world's image of Islamic art: when museums today show "Islamic ceramics", the lustred dishes of Fustat and the star-tiles of Kashan are the first things on the wall. Technically, it is also the ancestor of every overglaze metallic decoration in later ceramic history {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## Who invented it?
Honestly contested. The technique on pottery is documented first in the Islamic world — Samarra, ninth century — and Lane places its root earlier still, on early Islamic Egyptian glass. Whether the idea came from Pharaonic-Roman Egypt's dormant glass industry or was freshly invented is debated; what is not debated is that Muslim potters made it a ceramic art and carried it to its heights {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Today
Lustre ceramics are museum icons — in the Louvre, the V&A, the Museum of Islamic Art in Doha, the Benaki — and the technique itself survives wherever potters still fire a second reducing kiln, from Iran to Valencia {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947}.

## Surviving examples
The Kairouan mihrab tiles (c. 862); Samarra and Susa fragments; signed Fatimid pieces of Fustat by the potter Sa'd; the dated and signed tiles of Kashan; and the Hispano-Moresque dishes of Manises — an unbroken chain from the ninth century to the fifteenth {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## In culture
The shimmer of lustre became the signature of Islamic refinement — the pottery chosen when a treasury's gold vessels had to stay in the treasury. Its journey from caliphal Samarra to Renaissance Italy is one of the great craft migrations in art history {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers
How do you make a clay pot shine like it is made of gold? Muslim potters discovered a trick: paint the pot with a special powder made from real metals, then bake it a second time in an oven with almost no air. A thin, rainbow-shining film of metal stays on the surface forever. Kings and caliphs ate from these shining pots a thousand years ago — and you can still see them glowing in museums today.
