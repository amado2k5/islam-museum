## Summary
The lateen — the triangular fore-and-aft sail on a long angled yard — is the most famous sail in history and one of its most contested: long told as the Arabs' own rig, recent scholarship dates it to the second-century Mediterranean and makes the Arab adoption a Coptic-mediated inheritance that still carried Islamic fleets for eight centuries and ended up on the mizzen of European caravels {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en} {cite:whitewright-lateen-almasaq-2012}.

## What it is
A triangular sail set on a yard slung obliquely across the mast, running fore-and-aft rather than square to the hull: it lets a ship work closer to the wind and turns the sail itself into a steering surface {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en}.

## The world before it
The classical Mediterranean sailed square: one great rectangular sail brailled for reefs. In the fifth century the square rig shrank and the lateen spread — Whitewright argues the change was about the cost of building and maintaining rigs, not superior sailing performance {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en} {cite:whitewright-lateen-almasaq-2012}.

## The story
The rig is documented in the Mediterranean as early as the second century — a gravestone shows the quadrilateral settee, a fourth-century mosaic the triangle — and by the sixth it had largely replaced the square sail, becoming the standard rig of the Byzantine dromon. After the Muslim conquests the Arabs adopted it through the Coptic seafaring population, keeping its Greco-Roman-derived names; the earliest securely Islamic depiction is an eleventh-century glazed dish from Denia {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en}.

## The maker(s)
No inventor on record: the transmission's named face is the South Arabian navigator Sulayman al-Mahri, pupil of Ibn Majid, whose treatises carry the Indian Ocean sailing tradition the lateen entered after 1500 {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en}.

## How it works
The yard pivots on the mast; hauling the sheets swings the triangular sail to either tack, letting the crew set the sail's angle to the wind rather than the hull's — the geometry that made Mediterranean and then Atlantic maneuvering possible {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en}.

## The spread
From the Mediterranean the rig went two ways: into the Nile, where the felucca was born, and into the Atlantic — first the caravel's lateen masts, then the mizzen of the full-rigged carrack; in the Indian Ocean, square rig held until about 1500 and then the lateen spread rapidly {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en}.

## What it was built on
The Roman settee rig — the quadrilateral ancestor of the second-century gravestone — and the whole late-antique simplification of the Mediterranean square sail {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en} {cite:whitewright-lateen-almasaq-2012}.

## What was built on it
The ocean-going rig itself: the lateen mizzen was a universal component of the sixteenth-century full-rigged ship, the hybrid of Mediterranean and Northern traditions that carried the age of sail {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en}.

## Impact on daily life
For eight centuries it was the working sail of Muslim fishermen, merchants, pilgrims and navies from Denia to Zanzibar — the everyday technology under the grand narrative {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en}.

## How it changed history
Whatever its origin, the rig's transmission is the story: a Mediterranean invention, an Islamic working life, a European ocean career — maritime technology as shared heritage, which is exactly what this record registers {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en} {cite:whitewright-lateen-almasaq-2012}.

## Who invented it?
Contested. The received account credited the Arabs; the newer evidence-based account gives the sail a Roman-Mediterranean origin and the Arabs an adoptive role through Coptic seafarers — the museum keeps both voices and no verdict {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en} {cite:whitewright-lateen-almasaq-2012}.

## Today
The rig still flies on feluccas, dhows and heritage craft, and its historiography is the field's model case of technology transfer replacing inventor stories {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en}.

## Surviving examples
The material witnesses: the second-century gravestone, the Yassi Ada II wreck rigged lateen, the Kelenderis mosaic, the Kellia graffito, the Denia dish — and every working lateen afloat today {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en}.

## In culture
The sail named a sea: Europeans called it the "Arab sail" or latin sail, and its silhouette marks the maritime identity of the whole Islamic Mediterranean {cite:wikipedia-lateen-en}.

## For young readers
Imagine a triangular sail like a great bird's wing: slanted on the mast, catching wind from both sides, letting a ship sail almost against the wind. Sailors from Egypt, Persia and Seville flew it, and Europeans learned it for their ocean ships.
