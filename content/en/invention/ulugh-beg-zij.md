## Summary

The Zij-i Sultani is the star catalogue and planetary tables produced at Ulugh Beg's Samarqand observatory around 1437–1447. Its catalogue of 1,018 stars — measured, not copied — and its recalculated constants made it the most accurate astronomical handbook before Tycho Brahe's data two centuries later. For generations it was the reference zij of the eastern Islamic world and was consulted in Europe {cite:mactutor-ulugh-beg}.

## What it is

A zij in the classical sense: planetary theory, trigonometric and calendrical tables, plus the star catalogue proper — a complete computational toolkit for the astronomer, produced by one observing programme at one site {cite:mactutor-ulugh-beg}.

## The world before it

Every astronomer had used Ptolemy's inherited star list or al-Sufi's tenth-century revision of it — positions centuries stale. Precession had shifted the sky; no large-scale Islamic re-measurement had been completed since Baghdad.

## The story

Inside his new Samarqand observatory, Ulugh Beg set Jamshid al-Kashi to direct the programme; after al-Kashi's death (1429) and al-Rumi's, the ageing ruler-mathematician carried the computation himself and finished the catalogue — the only zij ever completed under a reigning monarch's own participation {cite:mactutor-ulugh-beg}.

## The maker(s)

Ulugh Beg — director and final editor; Jamshid al-Kashi and Qadi-zada al-Rumi — the lead astronomers whose observations fed the tables; the team preserved under the institution's name. His Muslim identity is on his person record.

## How it works

Giant fixed instruments (the Fakhri sextant) measured altitudes nightly; positions were reduced to ecliptic coordinates, precession-corrected, and tabulated; the catalogue lists each star's longitude, latitude and magnitude from the Samarqand measures {cite:mactutor-ulugh-beg}.

## The spread

Copied for centuries in Persian, Arabic and Turkish; carried to Istanbul and India; reached Oxford and Paris in the seventeenth century, where European astronomers collated it with Tycho's new numbers.

## What it was built on

On Ptolemy's Almagest catalogue and al-Sufi's revision — the inherited sky it re-measured — and on the zij tradition (Zij-i Ilkhani, Zij-i Ulugh Beg's own predecessors) it crowned.

## What was built on it

Early modern oriental astronomy and European comparative catalogues used it as the pre-Tycho benchmark; the zij tradition effectively ends with it.

## Impact on daily life

Better tables meant better calendars, prayer times and astrology for the courts that used it — precision reaching the calendar on the wall.

## How it changed history

It proved that a state observatory could out-measure every individual astronomer before it — institutional precision as the new standard {cite:mactutor-ulugh-beg}.

## Who invented it?

A team product under Ulugh Beg's direction: his name heads the work, but al-Kashi and al-Rumi's observations built it. The record credits the institution, not a lone mind.

## Today

Astronomers still read its positions — the Zij's 1,018 stars are compared to Gaia values to track five centuries of precession and measurement.

## Surviving examples

Dozens of manuscripts — Oxford's Bodleian copy is famous — and the printed editions and translations from the nineteenth century onward {cite:mactutor-ulugh-beg}.

## In culture

The Zij-i Sultani is remembered as the last great medieval star catalogue — the closing chapter of the zij tradition begun in Baghdad.

## For young readers

Before telescopes, a king and his scholars measured over a thousand stars one by one, and wrote them into a book so accurate that astronomers used it for two hundred years. That book is the Zij-i Sultani — the last great star catalogue of the old astronomy.
