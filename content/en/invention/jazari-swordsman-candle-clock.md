## Summary

A candle whose top does not simply sink as it shortens: instead it rises toward a fixed cap, periodically releasing a ball and moving a sword-bearing figure at the wick. This is the swordsman clock [[person/al-jazari-engineer|al-Jazari]] describes in his seventh clock chapter, using discharged balls to count elapsed night hours {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.83–86}. Its materials are a calibrated candle, counterweight, cords and ball channel, not flowing water or a pendulum. It is not the world's first candle clock: the author himself begins with a report of an earlier device whose mechanism he did not know {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.83}.

## What it is

A candle holder carries a long sheath, a falcon near its foot and a seated sword figure near its top. Inside are candle, supporting dish, weight, cords and a row of ball chambers. A falling ball links the falcon's signal to the figure's movement {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.83–86}. The exterior conceals mechanical relationships that an explanatory image partly opens to the reader.

This entry covers the complete chapter7 assembly, not every candle clock in subsequent chapters or a separate invention for every ball, pulley and cap {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.83–86,252–253}. Calibration relates consumption of this candle to a reference duration; it does not mean drawing equal marks on any modern candle. The museum has not tested reconstructed hardware or its precision.

## The world before it

Al-Jazari says he had not read a candle-clock work or seen a completed example, but had heard of a candlestick with a lion's head releasing a ball each hour {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.83}. The opening combines transmitted knowledge with a knowledge limit: appearance and result reached him, the mechanism did not. His personal experience cannot become a worldwide absence claim.

He says he considered making something like the reported device before describing his own arrangement {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.83}. The earlier clock remains an external precursor in his account, without an invented maker or date. This is not a documented genealogy of every earlier or later clock, but an explicit engagement with an idea he had heard and sought to understand.

## The story

The account moves from the nighttime display to a uniform-section candle and measurement of its consumption during an independently measured hour. The working length is divided into fourteen periods, with two further divisions reserved in the arrangement {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.83–84}. Preparing the timekeeping material is part of the design, rather than assuming every wax and wick burns at the same rate.

The author reports repeated arrangement, calculation and trials to perfect the ball-receiving pouch's movement {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.86}. This is his account, not an experiment repeated here. TDV places his writing within Artuqid service at [[place/amid|Amid]]. Hill's1204 or1206 from manuscript differences remains alongside TDV's1205: a book-date disagreement, not a verified installation date for this clock {cite:tdv-cezeri pp.505–506} {cite:jazari-hill-1974 introduction-life}.

Al-Jazari is recorded as describer of an integrated improvement, not inventor of candles or all time measurement. His text explains relationships and acknowledges earlier hearsay; TDV identifies an engineer and mechanical author {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.83–86} {cite:tdv-cezeri pp.505–506}. Unnamed craftspeople are not supplied from guesswork, and copyist and translator work is not reassigned to the original maker.

**Sunni frame:** TDV calls the named al-Jazari an Islamic scholar, the individual identity basis, not inference from name, court or origin {cite:tdv-cezeri pp.505–506}. His biography's explicit Sunni default is the museum's frame here, not a new personal-school declaration. ## How it works

The candle rests in a dish connected by cords over pulleys to a weight. The descending weight pulls candle and dish upward, while the fixed cap permits advancement only as material disappears from the candle's top {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.83–84,252}. The wick therefore remains near the cap instead of sinking with the shortening candle. This explains a relationship, not instructions for securing flame or preparing weights.

Alongside the candle is a channel divided into fourteen chambers, each holding a ball. Candle and dish prevent its escape; as they rise, another chamber level becomes exposed and its ball falls to the lower route {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.84}. The ball does not originally measure the hour: calibrated candle consumption produces slow movement, then an uncovered chamber converts movement into a countable event.

The ball first reaches a suspended pouch whose cord is connected to the figure's arm. Its temporary load produces the described sword movement trimming the burned wick; the ball then travels into the falcon's head, its weight opening the beak before it falls to the base. Pouch and arm return ready for another signal {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.85–86}. This is a functional sequence, not a guide to making a bladed arm.

The system is limited by candle, balls and weight travel, not perpetual motion. The text provides fourteen signals and expressly limits its climatic application to the fourth region, rather than presenting a clock automatically suitable for every night everywhere {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.84,86}. Calibration against a reference period does not itself prove all subsequent periods match it. No independent timing-error measurements are available in this entry.

## What it was built on

The assembly combines calibrated consumption, weight-and-cord lifting, a ball restrained then released, and transfer of its load into movement and visible representation {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.83–86}. Integration does not make every element new. The lion-head report also shows that linking a candle to a falling ball reached the author before this description, even though its particular mechanism remained unknown to him.

Comparison with the [[invention/jazari-castle-clock|castle clock]] distinguishes timing materials: regulated water there, candle consumption and lifting here; both descriptions include preparation and calibration {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.24–25,83–84}. This is a comparison between chapters, not proof that the candle clock derives directly from every water-clock detail or that all modern clocks descend from this particular linkage.

## What was built on it

Hill draws a modern cutaway interpretation to expose mechanisms concealed by an exterior view, explicitly making the candle thicker than its real proportions {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.252}. That image is not a ruler for measuring historical hardware. He also chooses one of two sheath lengths supplied by the text: an interpretive choice, not a measurement agreed across all witnesses.

He notes insufficient weight travel and excessive ball height in Oxford manuscript figure74, comparing other drawings {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.253}. His reading of the cap as an insertion-and-turn, or bayonet, fitting interprets wording he acknowledges as obscure; it does not establish worldwide priority for every similar locking joint {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.84-note,253}.

## Impact on daily life

The stated function is knowing elapsed night hours by counting balls, alongside the wick-trimming movement {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.83,86}. It combines countable indication and mechanical spectacle, rather than a continuously graduated modern wristwatch face. Separating the signal from the timing material helps explain how discrete events can represent a continuous change.

That does not establish household ubiquity, low cost or regulation of all urban activity. The text specifies a candlestick, material and preparation, not a census of owners or public appointment records {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.83–86}. Its documented effect remains a function presented in an account; the social consequences of all later timekeeping developments are not automatically assigned to it.

## How it changed history

The account makes calibration material rather than rhetorical: actual consumption during a reference period is related to a distance that exposes a ball chamber {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.83–84}. Its historical interest is the described arrangement of consumable material, signal thresholds and represented action, not discovery or first measurement of time.

It also exposes boundaries between text, picture and interpretation. Treating the Oxford drawing as a perfect working plan would overlook Hill's weight-travel problem; treating his pedestal conjecture as archaeological fact would erase his inferential language {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.253}. The entry retains these evidence levels rather than turning machinery history into an unbroken, unambiguous march of progress.

## Who invented it?

**Author's account within the declared Sunni frame:** Al-Jazari reports an earlier lion clock and his ignorance of its mechanism, then describes what he made and repeated movement trials {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.83,86}. Credit concerns this detailed arrangement. Not having seen a completed example does not prove none existed anywhere, and the earlier report is not deleted to create absolute originality.

**Academic interpretation:** Hill praises attention to specification and calibration, but identifies drawing problems and infers pedestal details from other chapters {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.252–253}. This is not a competing-inventor claim requiring a verdict; it is bounded attribution with differing certainty levels. The cap is not split off and counted again without independent priority research.

## Today

The clock can be studied as an analogue arrangement generating discrete signals: consumption and lifting progress gradually, while ball release is a countable event {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.83–86}. This is a teaching comparison, not identification as a digital computer or template for all software. Fourteen balls do not confer calendar calculation or resource-free self-setting.

No original machine or modern reconstruction was examined for this entry. No home experiment with flame, hot wax, lead weights or a sharp arm is proposed. Explaining the sword's textual function is not recommending imitation: a paper image can show restraint, release and counting without recreating physical hazards.

## In culture

The spectacle joins a represented human, a falcon and a time signal. The text describes an enslaved character and supplies a colour description; these are historical context, not endorsement of enslavement or inference of a real person's origin from paint {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.83}. The character is not evidence for the maker's identity, and the sword is not a battle narrative in this chapter.

The reported movement trims the wick and marks a period, rather than simply displaying a weapon shape {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.83,85–86}. The picture can attract attention before inviting a reader to follow cords and channels, provided decoration does not replace reading. Distinguishing figure from user and illustration from hardware is part of understanding material culture.

## For young readers

Use paper only: draw14 circles for possible signals, then cross out one after each imaginary step. After5 steps,9 remain:14 minus5 equals9. At step14 the counter ends, rather than continuing forever. The capacity comes from the account; steps and the choice of5 are a modern teaching example {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.84,86}.

Draw a strip covering the circles, revealing one level per step without real balls, pulleys or weights. Equal game steps are not certification of historical precision. Make one story-step longer and notice that signal counting alone does not establish equal intervals. No wax, flame, blade or hot object is involved.
