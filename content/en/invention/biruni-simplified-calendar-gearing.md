## Summary

A small wheel can make a pointer turn slowly, but fewer wheels do not by themselves make a calendar correct. [[person/al-biruni-muhammad-ibn-ahmad|Al-Biruni]] described an older device called the Moon Box, then proposed simplifying its solar gearing. This entry concerns that particular proposal: a four-tooth gear driving a fifty-two-tooth gear, not invention of the whole older device {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 pp.140,158,163}.

Donald Hill derives a364day year from the ratios, whereas the translated passage mentions360days. The difference remains visible. This is not an announcement of a self-running clock or surviving exact calendar, but a technical text combining inheritance, modification, transmission errors and limits of reconstruction {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 pp.158–163}.

## What it is

The Moon Box mechanically represents mean motions: pointers and a phase disc moved through calculated tooth ratios. A user advances an input alidade by hand according to daily divisions; a complete input revolution takes a week. That is not a self-powered motor, nor does the pointer directly observe a body's actual position in the sky {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 pp.156–157,161–162}.

The bounded contribution changes the solar reduction path. The four-tooth gear shares the forty-tooth lunar wheel's arbor, so they turn together; the four then meshes with the fifty-two. A shared arbor transfers the same rotation, whereas a mesh changes speed according to tooth counts. Confusing these arrangements changes the whole calculation {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 pp.158,163}.

## The world before it

Hill associates the older machine with Hellenistic and Byzantine settings and says its original inventor is unknown. That is the scholar's historical inference, not proof that one complete machine survived continuously between two cities. The accessible Arabic account's antiquity does not make al-Biruni the first inventor of gearing or calendars {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 pp.140–142}.

The text also describes other craftsmen's arrangements before the proposal attributed to its author. An instrument's presence in a scholar's book does not make every described device his invention. Three layers remain separate: inherited device, craftsmen's variants and al-Biruni's particular concluding simplification {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 pp.157–158,163}.

## How it works

The basic design lists eight wheels with7,10,19,24,40,48,59and59teeth. Counts alone cannot reconstruct a machine: one must know what meshes, what shares an arbor and what carries each pointer. Hill warns about erroneous drawings and partly inferred assembly; his suggested mountings and dimensions are not converted into certain physical specifications supplied by the author {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 pp.156,158–161,163}.

The arithmetic begins with one input revolution every seven days. If a ten-tooth gear drives forty teeth, the latter's speed magnitude is one quarter of the former's, requiring four weeks or28days for a revolution. That is an approximate sidereal lunar cycle, not the interval between crescents or a promise of crescent visibility on a particular date {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 pp.159–162}.

The phase-disc path uses seven and fifty-nine teeth, producing a59day revolution. That revolution represents two phase cycles, giving a mean29.5day synodic month. Its difference from28does not contradict the preceding arithmetic: the same everyday word, month, covers different measurements, return relative to the stars and return relative to the Sun's direction {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 pp.160–162}.

In al-Biruni's simplification, the four-tooth wheel turns with the forty on one arbor. We do not divide speed by ten again just because their sizes differ: they do not mesh with each other. Four teeth then drive fifty-two, making the solar pointer's revolution thirteen times longer than the sidereal lunar revolution. Our modern period-magnitude calculation is7×(40/10)×(52/4)=364days {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 p.163}.

The ratio reverses when moving from speed to period: final speed is one thirteenth of the lunar arbor's speed, but revolution time is thirteen times longer. Each external gear mesh reverses direction; this teaching chain computes period magnitudes only. It does not claim to solve every arbor's direction, tooth contact or pointer mounting in a functioning replica.

The earlier design's reconstruction gives59×(59/19)×(48/24), about366.4211days. Against Hill's365.2422year reference, the earlier output is about1.1789days too long, while the364day proposal is1.2422days short. It is therefore not more accurate than the earlier arrangement on every measure: it is an attributed simplification, not an unconditional mathematical victory {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 pp.159,163}.

If both pointers start aligned, period error does not appear all at once; the angular difference accumulates with elapsed days. After364days the proposal's pointer returns to its starting point while the reference year has not completed a revolution. This is a modern mathematical explanation, not an archaeological experiment we performed. The translation's360and Hill's reconstructed364remain a disclosed discrepancy, not a number silently corrected in the original {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 pp.158,163}.

## Impact on daily life

Hill suggests that reversibility might help investigate dates, including debt dates. That is a possible interpretation of usefulness, not a transaction archive proving adoption by a court or market. The distinction brings the question close to daily life without inventing a particular merchant's story {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 pp.162–163}.

Pointers might also help visualize month and year, but do not make an observational month28days or resolve a month's beginning by gearing alone. Mean-motion representation, assigning a calendar date and seeing a crescent are different operations. This entry offers no religious ruling; it explains an engineering function and its limits.

## How it changed history

Its defensible historical value is a text showing alteration and criticism, not a slogan that one machine created modernity. The account separates inheritance from craftsmen's methods, and Hill specifically attributes the final proposal to al-Biruni. Comparison shows that simplifying assembly may bring a different period error {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 pp.157–158,163}.

Counting fewer wheels cannot establish a linear march from error to truth. A simpler arrangement might be easier to understand, but proving lower manufacturing cost or greater operating reliability would require experiments and evidence unavailable here. We describe the ratio change rather than invent a historical efficiency measurement.

## For young readers

Imagine a disc taking four weeks to turn. Attach another disc to the same arbor: both turn together even if their sizes differ. Then let a little four-tooth gear drive a fifty-two-tooth wheel. The large wheel needs thirteen small-wheel turns to return to its starting position. Four weeks multiplied by thirteen makes fifty-two weeks, or364days.

But the reference year is slightly longer, so the machine's pointer gets ahead. The machine has not stopped; its ratio is approximate. That is both the attraction and difficulty: making a model to understand the sky does not make the sky follow its teeth. This arithmetic example is modern, not a historical toy or building experiment attributed to the author {cite:hill-biruni-calendar-1985 pp.159,163}.
