## Summary
After a fatal medical error, the caliph [[person/al-muqtadir-billah|al-Muqtadir]] in ⁦(319/931)⁩ ordered that no one practise medicine without examination; [[person/sinan-ibn-thabit|Sinan ibn Thabit]] examined Baghdad's physicians — some eight hundred and sixty, excluding established practitioners and palace doctors — and licensed each according to competence {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I pp. 309–311}. This record documents one regulatory episode, not the birth of modern professional licensing.

## What it is
State authorisation to practise medicine after examination: practitioners presented themselves to the appointed authority, were tested in treatment, and received written permission calibrated to each practitioner's level {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I pp. 309–311}.

## The world before it
Before it, medical practice in Baghdad was unrestricted by the state; Sinan himself had not been examined when he reported on the incident {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I pp. 309–310} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IX p. 644 SINAN}. Early Islamic government knew no general occupational licensing {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IX p. 644 SINAN}.

## The story
Ibn Abi Usaybi'a gives a chain through 'Ali ibn Ibrahim ibn Muqla the chamberlain: a man in Hama treated a woman without knowledge and she died, and her people complained to al-Muqtadir; the caliph wrote to Sinan ibn Thabit forbidding the unexamined, and Sinan examined in Baghdad more than eight hundred and sixty physicians, apart from the established experts and palace physicians {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I pp. 309–311}. He licensed each group according to its standing, wrote to each his permitted craft, and attached the documents to the official record {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I p. 311}.

## The maker(s)
[[person/sinan-ibn-thabit|Sinan ibn Thabit ibn Qurra]] ⁦(d. 332/943)⁩, son of the bimaristan scholar Thabit ibn Qurra, conducted the examination; the caliph al-Muqtadir ordered it; Sinan himself was unexamined when he began {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I p. 309} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IX pp. 644–645 SINAN}.

## How it works
Practice is barred before testing; the physician is examined before the appointed medical authority; a written licence assigns his field and is filed with the official minutes {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I pp. 310–311}.

## The spread
The text concerns Baghdad; no provincial extension is attested {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I pp. 309–311}. Later biographical literature remembers the examination (imtihan) as an institutional event {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I pp. 309–311}.

## What it was built on
It was built on the caliph's administrative authority and on Baghdad's bimaristan culture of medical training; it measured competence rather than creating a general licensing statute {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I pp. 309–310} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. IX p. 644 SINAN}.

## What was built on it
Precedent for medieval and modern professional licensure; its similarity to today's boards is analogical, not genealogical {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I pp. 309–311}.

## Impact on daily life
It bound practice to competence rather than fame; exempting established experts and palace staff shows the rule was social, not abstract {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I p. 310}.

## How it changed history
It shows an Islamic state regulating medical competence centuries before European licensure, as a historical episode rather than a continuous system {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I pp. 309–311}.

## Who invented it?
It was an administrative decision tied to al-Muqtadir and Sinan, not an invention; public-health regulation is a generic practice of states {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I p. 309} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. VII pp. 595–596 AL-MUKTADIR}.

## Today
Modern medical licensure belongs to modern states and boards; no direct descent is claimed {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I pp. 309–311}.

## Surviving examples

## In culture
The story became a set-piece example of medical regulation in Arabic biographical literature {cite:ibn-abi-usaybia-uyun-al-anba vol. I pp. 309–311}.

## For young readers
In old Baghdad a doctor needed no permit to work. A medical mistake killed a patient, so the caliph ordered that nobody treat the sick without an examination. Sinan ibn Thabit examined more than eight hundred and sixty doctors, and gave each a paper stating what he was allowed to do.
