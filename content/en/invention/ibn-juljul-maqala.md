## Summary

How do people know who the doctors were a hundred years ago, and what they wrote? The Cordoban physician [[person/ibn-juljul|Sulayman ibn Hassan Ibn Juljul]] answered with one book: the Tabaqat al-atibba wa-l-hukama — Classes of Physicians and Wise Men — composed at [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]] around 377 AH/987 CE, arranging the ancient Greek physicians, then the physicians of the Islamic east, then those of al-Andalus down to his own generation, with their names, teachers and works {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. It is the earliest surviving biographical dictionary of physicians in the Islamic tradition {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## What it is

A book in the "tabaqat" genre: ordering men into successive generations, each generation called a class {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. It opens with the sages and physicians of Greece from Asclepius to Galen, continues with the physicians of the Islamic era in the east, and closes with the physicians of the Islamic west and al-Andalus down to the author's contemporaries {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:tabaqat-ibn-juljul-sayyid-1955}. Each man receives a short notice: lineage, teachers, books, and sometimes an anecdote or a saying {cite:tabaqat-ibn-juljul-sayyid-1955}. It is a dictionary, not a book of cures {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The world before it

History-writing by classes was already rooted in the sciences of hadith and jurisprudence — classes of Companions, Followers and hadith transmitters — but the physicians of Islam had no surviving dictionary gathering them before Ibn Juljul {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. On the Greek side there existed a history of physicians written by the translator Ishaq ibn Hunayn (d. c. 910), which Ibn Juljul used for his ancient section {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. So writing about the history of medicine existed; what was new was organising it into a single dictionary covering east and west together {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The story

Ibn Juljul lived in Cordoba during its flowering under the chamberlain al-Mansur Ibn Abi Amir, when the city was the greatest centre of learning and medicine in the Islamic west {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. He studied medicine under the city's teachers and served — as the tradition reports — within Cordoban medical circles, then judged it a duty to record the men of his profession and wrote his treatise {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. Born about 332 AH/944 CE, he lived past 384 AH/994 CE, so the book came in his relatively mature years {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. He was not alone in caring for medical memory; but what has reached us of this genre begins with him {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## The maker(s)

Sulayman ibn Hassan Ibn Juljul, an Andalusi physician and historian; the name "Juljul" is of non-Arabic origin and entered his lineage long before {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. He was a physician, not merely a chronicler, and so wrote about his craft from the inside {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. His museum record registers him as a documented Andalusi Muslim scholar; the "Sunni" label is the museum's declared frame for the Andalusi milieu, not a testimony of a personal school {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. "Inventor" here means founder of a genre of book, not maker of a machine {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it works

The method is simple and imitable: gather the reports of men, order them by their times into classes, and for each man give his name, lineage, teachers and books {cite:tabaqat-ibn-juljul-sayyid-1955}. For the ancient section Ibn Juljul relies on Ishaq ibn Hunayn's history, transmitting the chain of Greek physicians from it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. For the Islamic section he adds what he himself gathered from his teachers, contemporaries and the books that reached him {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. The result is a chain of intellectual lineages: every physician holds a place in the tree of learning {cite:tabaqat-ibn-juljul-sayyid-1955}.

## The spread

The book continued to be read and copied in east and west, and later authors of physicians' classes drew on it — most prominently Ibn Abi Usaybi'a, author of Uyun al-Anba fi Tabaqat al-Atibba in the thirteenth century {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. The text reached several libraries, and in 1955 Fu'ad Sayyid published a critical edition in Cairo that returned it to modern circulation {cite:tabaqat-ibn-juljul-sayyid-1955}.

## What it was built on

Two pillars: Ishaq ibn Hunayn's history of the Greek physicians, and the tabaqat format borrowed from Islamic biographical science {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. Then living memory: Ibn Juljul's teachers and contemporaries in Cordoba, whose reports he took by hearing and reading {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. The book thus joins written transmission and oral testimony — the authentic craft of biographical writing {cite:tabaqat-ibn-juljul-sayyid-1955}.

## What was built on it

Ibn Juljul founded a genre: after him came larger physicians' dictionaries — Uyun al-Anba and others — standing on his model and enlarging it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. In our time historians of Islamic medicine cite his book as a primary source for names preserved nowhere else, especially for early Andalusi medicine {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2} {cite:tabaqat-ibn-juljul-sayyid-1955}.

## Impact on daily life

It is not a book of cures but a book of memory, yet its effect on learned life is real: it preserved the chains of medical teaching by which a physician's standing was measured {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. Without its Andalusi section, the names of the earliest physicians of the Islamic west would be almost entirely lost {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## How it changed history

It moved the history of medicine from scattered reports inside other books into a discipline with its own dedicated works {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. Every modern dictionary of Muslim physicians — and every study of medicine in al-Andalus — stands on the line this small book drew {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. For the museum it counts as "the earliest surviving" work of its genre, a documented claim, not an exaggeration {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Who invented it?

Ibn Juljul is the uncontested author of the text; but "earliest" is bounded by the word "surviving": earlier dictionaries may have been lost, and the text itself acknowledges its debt to Ishaq ibn Hunayn in the ancient section {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. The correct attribution is therefore: founder of the preserved genre, and faithful transmitter of a lost predecessor {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Today

The book is printed and taught in the history of medicine and of al-Andalus, and has been translated into languages including English {cite:tabaqat-ibn-juljul-sayyid-1955}. It has no therapeutic value today; its value is documentary: it names for us the men who made a science a thousand years ago {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## Surviving examples

What survives is the text of the book itself in manuscript copies and Fu'ad Sayyid's printed edition (Cairo: Institut francais d'archeologie orientale, 1955) {cite:tabaqat-ibn-juljul-sayyid-1955}. No autograph copy is known; like most Andalusi books of that age, it reached us through later copies {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## In culture

Ibn Juljul's name appears in every account of the history of medicine in al-Andalus, and his book is always classed as "the earliest surviving" of its genre {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}. The book itself has become a symbol of a larger idea: a civilisation preserves itself when it writes the history of its makers {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2}.

## For young readers

Imagine you wrote a notebook with a page about every doctor you knew or had heard of: their name, who taught them, and what they wrote. Ibn Juljul did this in Cordoba more than a thousand years ago, placing the old Greek doctors first, then the doctors of Islam, right up to the doctors of his own city. Because of his notebook, we still know the names of doctors who would otherwise be forgotten. You can make "classes" like his: write a page about every scientist you learn about — their teacher's name and their favourite book.
