## Summary
What was found on Arabic in Oman: the text of Article 3 of the Basic Statute as the Constitute Project shows it, the names of the Omani and Dhofari varieties in Glottolog's tree, and the University of Manchester's remark on stress in southern Oman, with a statement of what was not found. For the place see [[place/oman|Oman]]. For the Arab states in general see [[concept/al-alam-al-arabi|The Arab states and the League of Arab States]]. ## Official status
Article 3 of Oman's Basic Statute, in the translation on the Constitute Project titled "1996 (rev. 2011)", provides that the official language of the State is the Arabic language {cite:constituteproject-oman}. The article on the Semitic languages in the Encyclopaedia Britannica lists Oman among the states where Arabic is official {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}. The two sources agree.

## Constitutional text
The Basic Statute of the State, Royal Decree 6/2021, Article 3. The research summary supplied to the museum gives this Arabic wording: «لغة الدولة الرسمية اللغة العربية.», which the museum translates: "The official language of the State is the Arabic language". The summary names the Ministry of Justice and Legal Affairs of Oman as the official source; the museum did not open that page, so the wording is taken from the summary as supplied, and the founder approved using it so on 21 November 2026 {cite:official-constitution-oman}.

## The spoken varieties
Glottolog's tree lists under Arabian Peninsula Arabic "Omani Arabic" and "Dhofari Arabic", grouping them with Ru'us al-Jibal in one Dhofaric node, and gives each an ISO code {cite:glottolog-arabic}. The University of Manchester says some communities in southern Oman differ from the rest of the Arabic-speaking world in an iambic stress pattern, and among the theses its site lists is one on Coastal Dhofari Arabic {cite:manchester-arabic-structural-variation} {cite:manchester-arabic-sociolinguistic}. No shares of speakers for each variety were found.

The same Manchester page divides the Arabic dialects into geographical units, one of them the dialects of the Arabian Peninsula; it does not name the states inside that unit, so placing this state in it is an inference from its location, and it says that in most Arab countries urban and rural dialects differ, as do those of settled and formerly nomadic (Bedouin) people {cite:manchester-arabic-structural-variation}. Glottolog's tree shows separately named varieties under "Arabian Peninsula Arabic" {cite:glottolog-arabic}; these are two different classifications, one geographical group at Manchester and distinct varieties at Glottolog, and the museum does not choose between them. The Ethnologue page was not read, so the SIL classification is not known here. For more on the dialects see [[concept/lahjat-al-khalij|The Gulf dialects]].

## The standard and the spoken varieties together
The University of Manchester's page on the dialects of Arabic describes the general situation across the Arabic-speaking world as three-layered: Modern Standard Arabic, the language of the state, education, public services and the media; Classical Arabic, found in the Qur'an, religious texts and worship; and the colloquial dialects of everyday exchange, which the page says people usually regard as of little value even though they are common in song, theatre and film {cite:manchester-arabic-sociolinguistic}. This is a description of the Arab world in general, not of this state alone, and no study measuring it here was read. For the relation of the standard to the spoken varieties see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]], and for the differences among the tribes' speech before the standard [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|The dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]].
