## Overview
Extravagance (israf), according to Zaydan Qa'dat, is exceeding the limit in consumption and spending, and it is the extreme opposed to the balanced, moderate economy in the Qur'an {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 22}.

## Moderation in Spending and Consumption
The author holds that everything God has made lawful is permissible, but without extravagance or stinginess, and that what God has forbidden are indecencies, sin, aggression, and other specified prohibitions {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 22}. In spending, he distinguishes between clenching the hand and spreading it wide, stating that the moderate person spends neither extravagantly nor stingily {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 23}. In eating, drinking, and walking, he sees the Qur'anic line as one: the middle way between extremism and deprivation, for the Qur'an does not call for excessive austerity {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 22}. The author cites the verses on extravagance in al-An'am, al-A'raf, and al-Furqan [[passage/quran-6-141|al-An'am 141]] [[passage/quran-25-67|al-Furqan 67]].

## Impact of Extravagance on Society
The author considers extravagance against oneself to be one of the ills of both the capitalist and communist systems, as it has led to unemployment, inflation, scarcity of goods, death by starvation, and suicide, as well as the squandering and waste of resources {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 11}. He holds that wasting raw materials without accounting for future generations is an extravagance against humanity itself, since mineral wealth belongs to successive generations {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 40}. He gives two examples: extracting oil without prudent management leads to economic conflicts, and neglecting grain cultivation in favor of more profitable commodities {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 58}.

## Relation to Scarcity
The author considers wastefulness in consumption, especially in luxuries, to be one of the causes of the contemporary economic problem {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 57}, and holds that scarcity is not an inherent quality of resources but the result of poor consumption and distribution {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 59}. See [[concept/al-nudra-al-iqtisadiyya|Economic Scarcity]].

## From Zaydan Qa'dat's Book: pp. 83–164 of the PDF (Extravagance in the Context of the Book)
Qa'dat presents the circle of extravagance and tyranny as the third circle of spending, stating that extravagance even in what is lawful is an economic crime because it comes at the expense of others' rights {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 118}. He regards wastefulness as squandering the nation's resources and wealth, describes the wasteful as brothers of the devils, and considers luxury, once it takes hold of a nation, a grave calamity that leads to its destruction {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 118}.

## From the Book Usul al-Iqtisad al-Islami: Part Two (pp. 101–200 of the PDF)
Islam calls for the optimal limit in consumption, forbidding both stinginess and extravagance: extravagance (israf) is spending on what is unlawful, however little, or on what is lawful beyond the limit; wastefulness (tabdhir) is going to extremes in exceeding the limit; and opulence (taraf) is worse than wastefulness and is forbidden {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 184} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 185} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 186}.

The author holds that permissible consumption lies between the limit of stinginess and the limit of extravagance, and considers opulence and wastefulness to border on polytheism or unbelief {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 193}.
