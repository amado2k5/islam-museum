## Summary
The vital faculty in Ibn Sina is that which, once present in the organs, prepares them to receive the power of sensation and movement and the acts of life; its spirit is generated from the vapour and fineness of the humours, and its seat is the heart {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.98-99}.

## The spirit and its seat
He says that as the density of the humours, by some temperament, can produce a dense substance, the organ or part of one, so the vaporous and fine part of the humours, by some temperament, can produce a fine substance, the spirit; and as the liver is for physicians the seat of first generation, the heart is the seat of the second. When this spirit arises in the temperament it should have, it is ready, after all the organs, to receive the other powers, psychic and the rest. To this faculty they also ascribe the movements of fear and anger, because of the expansion and contraction that befalls the spirit in them {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.98}.

## The argument from the paralysed limb
The psychic powers do not arise in the spirit and organs until after this faculty arises. If an organ loses its psychic powers but has not yet lost this one, it is alive, like the numb and the paralysed organ, which at present lacks sensation and movement through a temperament that prevents their reception or a blockage between the brain and it, in the nerves, and is yet alive; whereas the dead organ loses sensation and movement and decays and rots. So in the paralysed limb is a power that preserves its life, so that when the obstacle goes sensation and movement flow back to it; and this is not the nutritive power, or plants would be ready to receive sensation and movement; the preparer is something else following a particular temperament, called the vital faculty, which is the first power to arise in the spirit when it arises from the fineness of the mixtures {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.98-99}.

## The disagreement over its naming
He reports that Aristotle holds that through it the spirit receives the first principle, the first soul from which the other powers flow, while the physicians give each genus of acts its own soul; and that this faculty, besides readying life, is the principle of the movement of the fine spiritual substance to the organs and of its contraction and expansion for breathing and clearing. It resembles the natural faculties in having no will in what comes from it, and the psychic ones in the definiteness of its acts, since it contracts and expands together and makes two opposed movements. On the ancients' view, where soul means the perfection of a natural organic body, it is a psychic faculty; if soul is meant as a power that is the principle of perception and of movement from some perception and will, it is not psychic but natural, and of a higher degree than what physicians call natural. Whether it is one or more he leaves to natural science, which is a part of philosophy {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.99}.
