## Summary
Ibn Sina lists three benefits of cupping, then describes how to work with the lancet and the cup and what to do if the place where it clings swells, and ends with the cupped person's food, his age and what a bilious person takes after it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.301}.

## The three benefits
Cupping has three benefits: first, evacuation from the organ itself; second, keeping the substance of the spirit without an evacuation that follows the evacuation of the humours; and third, that it leaves the principal organs unexposed to evacuation {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.301}.

## The method
The lancet should cut deep so that it draws from the depth. The place where the cup clings may swell and make removal hard; then cloths or a sponge soaked in water warmed to a mild heat are taken and the surroundings fomented with them first. This often happens when cups are used about the breast to stop menstrual flow or nosebleed, which is why the cup should not be set on the breast itself. If the place has been oiled, the cup should be fixed at once; the first placing is light and quickly lifted, and the cupper then proceeds to slower lifting and waiting {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.301}.

## After cupping
The cupped person's food should come after an hour; a boy is cupped in his second year, and after sixty a person is not cupped at all. Cupping on the upper parts is safe from matter pouring downward. A bilious person who has been cupped takes pomegranate seeds, pomegranate juice, chicory water with sugar and lettuce in vinegar {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.301}.
