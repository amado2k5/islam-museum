## Summary
Ibn Sina opens chapter 30, on relieving pains, by saying that pains fall into two kinds and that relief comes by opposing the causes, then ranks the relievers as changers of temperament, dissolvers of matter and narcotics, and lists the relaxants and the narcotics {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.309-310}.

## The two kinds
The causes of pains fall into two kinds: a sudden change of temperament and a break in continuity; and their detail ends in a bad temperament, hot, cold, moist or dry, without matter or with chymous matter, wind or a swelling. So relief of pain comes by opposing the causes {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.309}.

## Severe pain
Every pain that grows severe kills: first the body turns cold and shivers, then the pulse grows small, then it fails, then the patient dies {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.309}.

## Three kinds of reliever
All that relieves pain either changes the temperament, or dissolves the matter, or is a narcotic. Narcotising removes pain because it takes away the sensation of that organ, and it does so by one of two causes: by excess of cooling, or by a poison in it that opposes the power of that organ {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.309}.

## The relaxants
The relaxants are among what dissolves gently, such as flaxseed, dill, melilot, chamomile, celery seed, bitter almond and everything hot in the first degree, especially when joined with some sealing power, such as plum gum, starch, white lead, saffron, labdanum, marshmallow, cabbage, turnip and their decoctions, fats, fresh hyssop and oils of those named. Relaxants are used after evacuation, if evacuation is needed, until the matter pouring to that organ is cut off {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.309-310}.

## The narcotics
The strongest narcotic is opium, and among them are mandrake and its seed and the bark of its root, poppies, henbane, hemlock, nightshade berries and lettuce seed; among this group too are snow and cold water {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.310}.
