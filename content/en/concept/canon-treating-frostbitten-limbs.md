## Summary
Ibn Sina distinguishes a limb struck by cold but not yet putrid, set in snow water or water in which figs were boiled and moved, rubbed and coated; a limb whose innate heat has died and which is open to putrefaction, treated like eating ulcers; and a limb that has begun to darken, which is scarified and bled in hot water and then coated with Armenian bole and vinegar; once rot has set in, what rots is removed promptly {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.264-265}.

## Before putrefaction
If cold has struck it but it has not yet putrefied and is on its way, it is sounder to place the limb in snow water in particular or in water in which figs were boiled; cabbage water, basil water, dill water and chamomile water are all good, taradugh is a good coating and a poultice of turnip a good remedy; the limb is kept from the fire and its vicinity, and at once the man walks and moves foot and limb, exercising and rubbing it, then oiling and coating it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.264}.

## After the innate heat has died
If cold has worked in the limb, killed the innate heat in it, penned up what used to dissolve from its substance and exposed it to putrefaction, it may need what is said in the chapter on ulcers, especially the malignant eating kind; when the limb begins to darken it is scarified and the blood allowed to flow with the limb held in hot water so that no blood congeals in the mouths of the cuts, then it is coated with Armenian bole and diluted vinegar; tar helps at the start and at the end {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.264-265}.

## When rot has set in
If the matter passes blackness and greenness and one reaches it in the course of rotting, one attends to nothing but removing what rots with haste, lest the sound part nearby rot too and the putrefaction creep, and then does what was said in its own chapter {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.265}.
