## Summary
In Chapter 22, al-Farghani continues estimating the sizes of celestial bodies after having estimated their distances, beginning with the Sun and the Moon because Ptolemy specified their diameters alone, and then measuring the remaining planets by their apparent diameters at mean distance {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 215}.

## The Moon and the Sun
Al-Farghani states that the apparent diameter of the Moon at its farthest apogee is 31 minutes, that the diameter of the Sun is approximately six times that of the Earth, that the true diameter of the Moon is a fraction of the Earth's diameter read in the text as one-thirtieth, that the volume of the Moon is one-thirty-ninth of the Earth's volume, and that the volume of the Sun is about 166 times the volume of the Earth {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 215}. The OCR readings of these fractions vary and are therefore considered doubtful.

## The Planets Relative to the Sun's Diameter
He states that the apparent diameter of Mercury equals one-fifteenth of the Sun's diameter, that of Venus one-tenth, and that of Jupiter one-eighteenth, and that the diameters of the fifteen first-magnitude fixed stars equal one-twenty-first—readings that warrant checking against the page image {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 216}.

## The Planets Relative to the Earth's Diameter
He estimates the true diameters of the planets relative to the Earth's diameter: for Mercury, one-eighteenth; for Venus, one-third (doubtful reading); for Mars, about one-half; for Jupiter, about four and one-sixteenth; and for Saturn, about four and a half (doubtful readings) {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 216}.

## Volumes
Al-Farghani holds that the volume of Mercury is a fraction of the Earth's volume read in the OCR as about one twenty-two-thousandth, that the volume of Venus is one-thirty-seventh, that the volume of Jupiter is about ninety-five times the volume of the Earth, that the volume of Saturn is about ninety times, and that second-magnitude stars equal ninety Earths, third-magnitude seventy-two, fourth-magnitude fifty-four, and fifth-magnitude thirty-three {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 216}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 217}. These figures come from the printed text with doubtful readings and have not yet been checked against the image.

## Ranking
Al-Farghani concludes that the largest celestial body is the Sun, followed by the largest of the fifteen fixed stars, then Jupiter in third place, Saturn in fourth, the stars of the remaining magnitudes in their respective ranks, then Mars in sixth, Earth in seventh, Venus in eighth, the Moon in ninth, and Mercury in tenth {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 217}.
