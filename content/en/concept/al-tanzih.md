## Summary
Sha'rawi holds that God is free of likeness to His creatures in essence, attributes and acts, and this includes freedom from offspring. {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan-pp-501-1000 p.547}

## The argument from need
A son, Sha'rawi says, is taken to keep a kind alive, for help or for security of life. God is living from eternity and needs none of these, and the world did not change with the alleged son, so God gains no perfection from it. {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan-pp-501-1000 p.547} {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan-pp-501-1000 p.548}

## A creature or a god
A son would be either a creature, and so not a god, or a god, and then there would be several gods. Since God owns all in the heavens and the earth, a son of the same kind cannot be His, for the maker does not produce something of his own kind. {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan-pp-501-1000 p.547} {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan-pp-501-1000 p.548}

## In The Oxford Dictionary of Islam
Tanzih, the doctrine of divine exemption, holds God to be utterly singular, resembling nothing created; it is the ground of the ban on anthropomorphism. The entry reports the Sufi reading: the believer must empty himself of every image and prior notion of God so that mystical experience can disclose Him. Bound to tawhid, the doctrine fed the eighteenth-century Sufi turn toward union with Muhammad's spirit instead of God — since the union of two unlike beings was held impossible — though some earlier mystics had allowed such communion on the strength of the creator-created relation. {cite:oxford-dictionary-of-islam e2333}
