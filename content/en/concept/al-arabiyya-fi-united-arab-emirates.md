## Summary
What was found on Arabic in the United Arab Emirates: the text of Article 7 of the Constitution as the Constitute Project shows it, the Library of Congress study's figure for ethnic Arabs, and what the classification tree and geographical groupings give, with a statement that no description of an Emirati dialect and no language shares were found. For the place see [[place/united-arab-emirates|the United Arab Emirates]]. For the Arab states in general see [[concept/al-alam-al-arabi|The Arab states and the League of Arab States]]. The constitutional text is added in its original wording, as supplied in a research summary that the museum did not check against the page.

## Official status
Article 7 of the Constitution of the United Arab Emirates, in the translation on the Constitute Project titled "1971 (rev. 2009)", says the official language of the Union is Arabic, and the same article gives Islam as the official religion and the Shari'a as a main source of legislation {cite:constituteproject-united-arab-emirates}. The article on the Semitic languages in the Encyclopaedia Britannica lists the United Arab Emirates among the states where Arabic is official {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}. The two sources agree.

## Constitutional text
The Constitution of the United Arab Emirates, Article 7. The research summary supplied to the museum gives this Arabic wording: «الإسلام هو الدين الرسمي للاتحاد، والشريعة الإسلامية مصدر رئيسي للتشريع فيه، ولغة الاتحاد الرسمية هي اللغة العربية.», which the museum translates: "Islam is the official religion of the Union, the Islamic Shari'a is a main source of legislation in it, and the official language of the Union is the Arabic language". The summary names the UAE Legislation Portal as the official source; the museum did not open that page, so the wording is taken from the summary as supplied, and the founder approved using it so on 21 November 2026 {cite:official-constitution-united-arab-emirates}.

## The spoken varieties
The Glottolog tree that was read names no variety after the Emirates: its Gulf Arabic group has varieties named for Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar, Al-Hasa and Zubair-Faau and none for the Emirates, which does not mean the Emirates has no dialects, only that the tree does not name them {cite:glottolog-arabic}. The encyclopedia's Qatar page says the Qatari dialect is Gulf Arabic similar to that of its neighbours {cite:britannica-qatar}; what it says of the Emirates itself was not read. So no description of an Emirati dialect and no share for it was found.

The same Manchester page divides the Arabic dialects into geographical units, one of them the dialects of the Arabian Peninsula; it does not name the states inside that unit, so placing this state in it is an inference from its location, and it says that in most Arab countries urban and rural dialects differ, as do those of settled and formerly nomadic (Bedouin) people {cite:manchester-arabic-structural-variation}. Glottolog's tree shows separately named varieties under "Arabian Peninsula Arabic" {cite:glottolog-arabic}; these are two different classifications, one geographical group at Manchester and distinct varieties at Glottolog, and the museum does not choose between them. The Ethnologue page was not read, so the SIL classification is not known here. For more on the dialects see [[concept/lahjat-al-khalij|The Gulf dialects]].

## Other languages
The Library of Congress study says about 87 percent of the Emirates' population is ethnically Arab {cite:loc-uae-population}. That is an ethnic share, not a linguistic one; the page names no language, comes from an old series and carries no date on the page, and gives no basis for a share of Arabic speakers. No other languages of the Emirates were found.

## The standard and the spoken varieties together
The University of Manchester's page on the dialects of Arabic describes the general situation across the Arabic-speaking world as three-layered: Modern Standard Arabic, the language of the state, education, public services and the media; Classical Arabic, found in the Qur'an, religious texts and worship; and the colloquial dialects of everyday exchange, which the page says people usually regard as of little value even though they are common in song, theatre and film {cite:manchester-arabic-sociolinguistic}. This is a description of the Arab world in general, not of this state alone, and no study measuring it here was read. For the relation of the standard to the spoken varieties see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]], and for the differences among the tribes' speech before the standard [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|The dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]].
