## Summary
What was found on Arabic in Syria: Article 4 of the 2012 constitution as the Constitute Project shows it, where the text is marked "Historical", the Library of Congress study's account of Syrian Arabic and of attitudes to the spoken form, and the Levantine classification at Manchester and Glottolog, with the differences between sources and what was not found. ## Official status
Article 4 of the Constitutional Declaration of 13 March 2025 says Arabic is the official language of the State; the research summary supplied to the museum gives that wording, and the context is in the next section {cite:official-constitution-syria}. This replaces the earlier reading of the 2012 constitution: Article 4 of that text, in the translation on the Constitute Project, where the page heads the text "2012 Historical", also says the official language of the state is Arabic, but the summary says the 2012 constitution was repealed after December 2024, so it is cited here only as an earlier text {cite:constituteproject-syria}. The article on the Semitic languages in the Encyclopaedia Britannica lists Syria among the states where Arabic is official {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}.

## Constitutional text
The Constitutional Declaration of 13 March 2025, Article 4. The research summary supplied to the museum gives this Arabic wording: «اللغة العربية هي اللغة الرسمية للدولة.», which the museum translates: "The Arabic language is the official language of the State". The summary names SANA, the Syrian state news agency as the official source; the museum did not open that page, so the wording is taken from the summary as supplied, and the founder approved using it so on 21 November 2026 {cite:official-constitution-syria}. The summary says the 2012 constitution was repealed after December 2024 and that this Declaration governs the five-year transitional period. It says SANA published the Declaration as scanned page images, and that the text of Article 4 was cross-checked against Al Jazeera's reproduction of SANA's text {cite:aljazeera-syria-constitutional-declaration-2025}.

## The spoken varieties
The Library of Congress study says Arabic has three forms: Classical, in the Qur'an; Modern Standard, with virtually the same structure wherever used; and the spoken dialects. It says Syrian Arabic resembles Lebanese but differs from colloquial Arabic in Iraq and Jordan, that a Syrian would find Moroccan Arabic virtually incomprehensible, and that regional differences are strong enough for speakers to identify another's province, tribe or city {cite:loc-syria-arabs}. It says Syrians tend to see their own dialect as the most refined yet few consider it correct Arabic, that most regard Modern Standard as superior to the spoken form, that many believe Bedouin speech is closest to Classical, and that the uneducated, over 40 percent in the page's words, usually understand Modern Standard but cannot speak it and may struggle with radio and television; that figure is from an old study. The University of Manchester places Syria in the Levantine group with Palestine, Lebanon and Jordan {cite:manchester-arabic-structural-variation}, and Glottolog lists Aleppo among the northern Levantine varieties {cite:glottolog-arabic}. On the differences: the old study separates Jordanian and Iraqi from Syrian, yet its own Jordan page says Jordan's dialect is shared with Syria, Lebanon and parts of Iraq, so one source is not consistent with itself, and Manchester groups Jordan with Syria and sets Iraq apart. For more on the dialects see [[concept/lahjat-al-sham|The Levantine dialect]].

## Other languages
The Library of Congress page that was read does not treat Kurdish, Armenian, French or English, and mentions Aramaic only as a Semitic language related to Arabic; its guide has pages on the Kurds and on the Armenians, which were not read. So the languages of Syria other than Arabic, and their shares, were not found.

## The standard and the spoken varieties together
The University of Manchester's page on the dialects of Arabic describes the general situation across the Arabic-speaking world as three-layered: Modern Standard Arabic, the language of the state, education, public services and the media; Classical Arabic, found in the Qur'an, religious texts and worship; and the colloquial dialects of everyday exchange, which the page says people usually regard as of little value even though they are common in song, theatre and film {cite:manchester-arabic-sociolinguistic}. This is a description of the Arab world in general, not of this state alone, and no study measuring it here was read. For the relation of the standard to the spoken varieties see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]], and for the differences among the tribes' speech before the standard [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|The dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]].
