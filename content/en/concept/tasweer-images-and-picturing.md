## Overview
Tasweer is making an image, and a likeness is what matches a thing and reproduces its form, either three-dimensional or flat, and may be fleeting, like a reflection or a shadow. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5257}

## Terms
The jurists use the word image for what reproduces a thing's form, with or without shadow. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5257} Most jurists use timthal (statue) as the same word, though some confine it to living beings. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5258} In modern use, rasm is a flat picture drawn by hand. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5259}

## Kinds
A three-dimensional image has bulk that can be felt, and a flat image has two dimensions and is only seen. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5257} Some images are fleeting: in a mirror, on water, in a shadow, or on a screen. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5257}

## Plan of the entry
The entry treats the human image, the making of images, their keeping and use, and dealings with them. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5259}

## The human image
It is good to beautify one's outward and inward form, and not lawful to mutilate the body or alter its created form; the Prophet forbade looting and mutilation. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5259} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5260}

## Manufactured objects, inanimate nature and plants
Depicting man-made things, such as a house, car or ship, is lawful by agreement. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5261} Depicting created inanimate things, such as mountains, the sun and the moon, is lawful with no dispute except from a fringe view, but making one for someone who worships it, such as sun-worshippers, is not lawful. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5261} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5262} Depicting plants and trees is lawful for the majority; the view attributed to Mujahid against fruit-bearing trees was taken up by none, and Ahmad's school is on lawfulness. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5262} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5263}

## Images of humans and animals
This is the kind that the general prohibition refers to. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5263} The hadith of the church in Abyssinia, in which the Prophet blamed those who built places of worship on graves and made images in them, is cited. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5263} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5264}

## Three views
Jurists differ on images of living beings in three views. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5264} The first holds it is not forbidden unless one makes an idol worshipped besides God, citing the verse on carving and the hadith on the sale of idols, the words «who vie with God's creation», and the reading that the hadith of the most severely punished makers refers to those who make images to be worshipped. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5264} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5265} The second, that of the Malikis, some early scholars and Ibn Hamdan, forbids only images that have a shadow, that is three-dimensional, and are complete in their limbs; a flat image is disliked, not forbidden, and a figure missing a limb whose loss would kill the animal is not forbidden. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5265} Ibn al-Arabi reported consensus that a three-dimensional image is forbidden. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5265}

## Reasons for the prohibition
Those who forbid images in general cite the hadiths of Aisha about the curtain and the cushion, the curtain the Prophet tore down, the hadith of Gabriel that angels do not enter a house with a dog or an image, the hadith of Abu Hurayra on the one who creates as God creates, Ibn Abbas's hadith that every maker of images is in the Fire, and Ali's order to efface images. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5266} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5267} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5268} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5269}
The reasons given are four. First, rivalry with God's creation, which some find too narrow, since it would allow images of the sun, mountains and trees and would not reach dolls for children; some read it as a challenge to God's power. Second, that images lead to excess and worship, as the Arabs made idols, so the law closed the way; the scope of this is disputed. Third, mere resemblance to the idolaters, which yields only dislike. Fourth, that an image keeps angels away, which is answered by the example of impurity. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5269} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5270} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5271}

## Making three-dimensional images
Making three-dimensional images is forbidden according to most scholars, except what is made as a child's toy, what is used in a demeaning way, what has a limb cut off whose loss would kill the animal, or what does not last, such as sweets or dough shapes. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5271}

## Making flat images
The first view, that of the Malikis and their companions, allows making flat images with dislike; the dislike goes if the figure lacks a limb whose loss would kill, and there is no dislike for a demeaned image. They cite the hadith «angels do not enter a house with an image except a patterned cloth», that God created living things not as surfaces but in bulk, and that images were used in the Prophet's house, in the curtain of Fatima, and on the Persian and Byzantine coins. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5271} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5272}

## The second view on flat images: prohibition
The majority of the Hanafis, Shafiis and Hanbalis, and many early scholars, forbid making flat images as they forbid three-dimensional ones, except the cut-off, the demeaned and some other things. They rely on the general hadiths cursing image-makers, and argue that using an image does not make it lawful to make one. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5274}

## Cut-off or incomplete images
The Malikis and Hanbalis do not forbid an image missing a limb whose loss would kill, such as a head cut off or a belly torn. The Shafii view is that an image with its head cut off but its body whole is forbidden, and the reverse is in doubt. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5274} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5275}

## Imaginary images
The Shafiis state that imagined images of a human or an animal, such as a winged man or an ox with a beak, are included in the prohibition. They exempt children's toys on the hadith about Aisha's winged horse. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5275}

## Demeaned images
Most scholars allow keeping and using three-dimensional and flat images, whole or cut, when they are demeaned, as on the floor, a rug, a bed or a cushion. On making such images the views differ: the Malikis allow them with lesser disapproval, the Shafiis have two views with the prohibition the sounder, the Hanafis forbid, and the Hanbalis do not state a view. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5276} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5275}

## Images of clay and sweets
The Malikis have two views on making images not meant to last, such as dough or melon-rind figures; the prohibition is the better known. The Shafiis forbid making them but not selling them. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5276}

## Dolls for girls
Most scholars except making dolls for girls from the prohibition, as the Malikis, Shafiis and Hanbalis do; al-Qadi Iyad reported permission from most scholars. They cite the hadith of Aisha playing with dolls before the Prophet and the curtain episode with the winged horse. The Hanbalis require the dolls to have no head or a limb missing; the others do not. The majority explain the exemption as training girls in raising children; al-Halimi as delight and healthy growth in children. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5276} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5277}

## Images for education
The jurists say nothing on making images for teaching beyond what they say of children's dolls; an image made to teach stands under the same rule. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5277}

## Keeping and using images
Most scholars hold that forbidding the making of an image does not forbid keeping or using it. The strong texts concern the making and the maker, not keeping; the reason of rivalry with God's creation is not present in use. The hadiths on keeping mention no punishment, so keeping a forbidden image is a lesser sin, unless one persists in it. An-Nawawi and al-Shibramalisi distinguish making from keeping. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5278}
On the other side, the use of images by the Prophet and his Companions and Successors is reported; the ring of Daniel had a lion and a lioness with a child, and it came to Umar and was given to Abu Musa al-Ashari. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5278}

## The house with images, which angels do not enter
The phrase is in the two Sahihs from Aisha, Ibn Abbas and Ibn Umar, and in other books from Ali, Maymuna, Abu Said, Abu Talha and Zayd ibn Khalid. An-Nawawi explained that angels avoid it because making images is a gross sin and rival to God's creation, and al-Qurtubi because it resembles the unbelievers who keep images. Ibn Hajar held that only an image displayed upright, not demeaned or cut, keeps the angels away. The Hanafi view is that what may be kept does not keep the angels away. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5279}
