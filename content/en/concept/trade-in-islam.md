## Overview
Al-Maraghi sees commerce as one of the widest fields of human activity, paired with industry and agriculture in moving production {cite:min-qadaya-al-mal-wal-amal-azhar p. 30}. Quoting Ibn Khaldun — that trade invites haggling, fraud and false oaths — he shows Islam surrounding it with exhortation and warning {cite:min-qadaya-al-mal-wal-amal-azhar p. 30}: exact measure and weight, disclosure of defects, prohibition of deceit, and commendation of leniency in buying and selling {cite:min-qadaya-al-mal-wal-amal-azhar p. 31} {cite:min-qadaya-al-mal-wal-amal-azhar p. 33}. He cites the hadith "I am the third of two partners so long as neither betrays the other" {cite:min-qadaya-al-mal-wal-amal-azhar p. 32}, and sets out the forms of partnership — labor and capital, mudaraba, acting as principal or agent.

## For Sprouts
Islamic teachings encourage honest trade, trustworthiness in buying and selling, helping others, and avoiding deception.

## For Explorers
Trade in Islam is an honorable economic activity founded on honesty, justice, and mutual consent between buyer and seller. Islamic law strictly prohibits fraud, monopoly, and usury while praising the trustworthy merchant, whose ethical conduct historically built cultural bridges and introduced Islam across Asia and Africa.

## For Scholars
A foundational economic domain in Islamic law governed by the principles of mutual consent, equity, and public benefit. Classical jurisprudence developed sophisticated commercial frameworks to ensure contractual clarity while proscribing usury (riba), speculative ambiguity (gharar), fraud, and monopolistic exploitation. Historically, trans-regional caravan and maritime trade routes linked the Mediterranean with the Indian Ocean, where the ethical conduct, accountability, and credit networks of Muslim merchants served as a primary catalyst for peaceful cultural exchange and the spread of Islam across Asia and Africa.
