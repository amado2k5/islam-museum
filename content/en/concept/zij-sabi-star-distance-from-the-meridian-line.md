## Summary
Chapter 22 begins with finding the altitudes of stars from the hours of the night, and within it the distance of the star from the meridian line. One takes the degree that culminates at that time, the rising degree and the setting degree, and then works out the times between the part at midheaven and the part that culminates with the star in the right sphere; the difference is its distance from the line, taking the one from the other according to whether the star's degree is on the east or the west of midheaven. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion pp.56-57}.

## The other way, through the horizon
The text has another way through the horizon: the times against the part with which the star rises in the clime and against the opposite of the part with which it sets are taken, and one is subtracted from the other according to whether the star is on the east or the west; the result is its distance from the horizon. If this is taken from the star's half day arc there remains its distance from the meridian line, whichever side it is on. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion pp.56-57}.
