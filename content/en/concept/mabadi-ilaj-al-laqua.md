## Waiting and caution
Prudence is not to move the laqua patient until the seventh — some said the fourth — and he is fed what lightens on the second day, a very soft clyster being suitable; hastening to gargles at the start is harmful, for they may draw what is near without dissolving the nearby obstacle {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.155}

## Evacuation by kind
The convulsive kind deserves the stronger: it is not evacuated with an insufficient weak one until it matures once; rushing to the sharp medicine is among the most harmful things, and the worst of treatment is to dry and thicken the matter and dry the nerve so the medicine's effect becomes hard — patience is fitter; it must be treated with the treatment of palsy or convulsion as you know by what fits (the named electuaries, confections, potions and their measures are not reproduced) {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.155}

## The relaxed and the dry convulsive
If the patient is moist, the side holding the illness's origin must be bound into the natural shape; if it is convulsive, begin by softening it first then dissolving it; if it is dry convulsive, beware the hot things among liniments, compresses, oils and things taken by mouth; the bath is avoided in the relaxed kind and kept to daily, repeatedly, in the convulsive {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.155} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.156}

## The eyelid's regimen and the food
The eyelid's muscles are not of that group; their management is purifying the fore part of the brain, plus dry compressing over these vertebrae and the jaws, rubbing them and rubbing the head, especially on a strong hunger; the fittest food is what digests quickly (the mixes of the named medicines in this chapter, the drugs, snuffs and emetics are not reproduced) {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.156} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.157}
