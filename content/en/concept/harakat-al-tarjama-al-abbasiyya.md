## Summary
The translation movement of the Abbasid age was a vast enterprise in which the learning of the Greeks, Persians and Indians was carried into Arabic, until Arabic became the language of science and philosophy. This page sets out what a research summary supplied to the museum says of the [[group/bayt-al-hikma|House of Wisdom]], of two great translators, [[person/hunayn-ibn-ishaq|Hunayn ibn Ishaq]] and [[person/thabit-ibn-qurra|Thabit ibn Qurra]], and of the causes that made Arabic a language of science. The museum already has an older page on the movement, [[concept/harakat-al-tarjama|The Abbasid translation movement]], which passes on Ibn al-Nadim's report of al-Ma'mun's dream {cite:britannica-bayt-al-hikmah} {cite:yale-nelc-dimitri-gutas} {cite:wikipedia-graeco-arabic-translation-movement-en}.

## The House of Wisdom
The summary says the House of Wisdom was established in Baghdad under the early Abbasid caliphs, among them [[person/al-mansur|al-Mansur]] and [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]], and especially [[person/abd-allah-al-mamun|al-Ma'mun]], who ruled from 813 to 833. It was, according to the summary, a royal palace library modelled in part on the Sasanian 'houses of wisdom', to preserve Persian, Greek and Syriac scientific and philosophical texts. It adds that modern scholarship, notably Dimitri Gutas's Greek Thought, Arabic Culture of 1998, stresses that the Graeco-Arabic translation movement was a society-wide enterprise sponsored by the state and the elite, reaching well beyond a single building {cite:britannica-bayt-al-hikmah} {cite:yale-nelc-dimitri-gutas} {cite:wikipedia-graeco-arabic-translation-movement-en}.

## Hunayn ibn Ishaq
[[person/hunayn-ibn-ishaq|Hunayn ibn Ishaq]], 808 to 873, was a Nestorian Christian physician and scholar who travelled across Syria, Palestine and Egypt to collect ancient Greek manuscripts. From the translators' school he ran in Baghdad, and later as court physician to [[person/al-mutawakkil|al-Mutawakkil]], he and his students translated Plato, Aristotle, Hippocrates and above all [[person/galen|Galen]], preparing an annotated list of 129 Galenic works, many of which survive today only through his Arabic and Syriac versions {cite:britannica-hunayn-ibn-ishaq} {cite:britannica-arab-scholars-greek-medical-learning}.

## Thabit ibn Qurra
[[person/thabit-ibn-qurra|Thabit ibn Qurra]], who died in 901, was a Sabian scholar from Harran brought to Baghdad by the wealthy [[group/banu-musa|Banu Musa]]. He translated or revised foundational Greek mathematical and astronomical works by Euclid, Archimedes, Apollonius of Perga and Ptolemy, summarised Galen and Aristotle, and wrote original treatises on geometry, number theory, statics and astronomy, before serving as court astronomer to [[person/al-mutadid|al-Mu'tadid]] {cite:britannica-thabit-ibn-qurrah}.

## How Arabic became the language of science and philosophy
The summary traces it to caliphal patronage, to wealthy administrative families such as the Banu Musa, and to rationalist theological debates, such as those of kalam under al-Ma'mun, which created sustained demand for Greek, Syriac and Sanskrit learning. Translators and philosophers, among them [[person/al-kindi|al-Kindi]], then forged a precise Arabic philosophical and scientific lexicon, which allowed original advances in algebra, optics, astronomy and medicine, later reaching Europe through Medieval Latin translations in centres such as Toledo and Monte Cassino {cite:britannica-bayt-al-hikmah} {cite:sep-arabic-islamic-greek} {cite:britannica-arab-scholars-greek-medical-learning} {cite:uindy-oaks-mhmc}.

## What remained of the scientific words
Some of this lexicon passed into the languages of Europe, which is the subject of the page [[concept/al-alfaz-al-ilmiyya-al-arabiyya-fi-al-lughat-al-ura-biyya|Arabic scientific words in European languages]]. In the modern age the problem returned in another form, the problem of terminology and Arabisation, set out in [[concept/taarib-al-ulum-wal-majami|Arabisation of the sciences and the academies]] {cite:britannica-bayt-al-hikmah} {cite:yale-nelc-dimitri-gutas} {cite:wikipedia-graeco-arabic-translation-movement-en}.

## A reading of the names
The summary names three caliphs, al-Mansur, al-Rashid and al-Ma'mun, and stresses the last. Of the workers it names two translators, a philosopher, al-Kindi, and a rich family, the Banu Musa. Of the works carried over it names Greek authors: Plato, Aristotle, Hippocrates, Galen, Euclid, Archimedes, Apollonius and Ptolemy. Of the languages translated from it names Greek, Syriac, Sanskrit and Persian. All these sentences come from the summary's description, and the page gives no list of particular books translated, nor says which text came from which language {cite:britannica-bayt-al-hikmah} {cite:yale-nelc-dimitri-gutas} {cite:wikipedia-graeco-arabic-translation-movement-en}.

## Limits
The summary ascribes what it says to pages of Encyclopaedia Britannica and other sources, which the museum did not open. The page claims no complete list of translators or centres, and does not go into the debate between those who count the House of Wisdom as a single institution and those who see it as an expression of a wider movement; it keeps to passing on Gutas's view as the summary gave it.

## Basis
The page rests on a research summary supplied to the museum, which the founder approved relying on as it stands on 21 November 2026. All names, dates and numbers here are attributed to the summary, and the museum has read no translation and no book named.
