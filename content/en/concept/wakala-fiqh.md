Wakala, with its vowel variants, means in the language protection, as in the Divine name al-Wakil meaning the Guardian, and in tawakkul ('we have entrusted our affairs to God'); tawkil is the delegation of a disposal to another. The agent is called wakil because his principal has entrusted him with the conduct of his affair. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17719} The jurists define it in several ways. The Hanafis: setting another in one's place in a lawful, known disposal, out of ease or incapacity. The Malikis: the deputising of a person with a right, who has no command or worship, for another in what is not conditioned on his death. The Shafi'is: the delegation by a person of what he may do and that admits delegation, to another to do it during his life. The Hanbalis: the deputising of a person capable of disposing of property, like himself, in what deputising admits of rights of God and rights of men. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17719} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17720}

The Mawsu'a distinguishes wakala from neighbouring terms: deputising (niyaba), which is for some jurists wider than wakala and for others its synonym; authority (wilaya), the carrying out of a word over another whether he wills it or not, which is a legal or compulsory deputising, while wakala is agreed; bequest (iya'), the setting of another in one's place in disposal after death, while wakala is in life; and guardianship (qiwama), a power given by the judge to an adult to act for a minor's interest, or the husband's authority over his wife. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17720}

The jurists agree that wakala is lawful. They cite the Qur'an: the account of the People of the Cave sending one of them with their silver to the city, and the verse of the two arbiters in Surat al-Nisa, which is taken to show that each arbiter acts as agent of one of the spouses. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17721} They cite the Sunna: the report of 'Urwa ibn Abi al-Ja'd al-Bariqi, to whom the Prophet gave a dinar to buy a sheep; the report of Hakim ibn Hizam about buying a sacrifice; the report of Jabir about the agent at Khaybar; and the report of Abu Rafi' that he was the go-between in the Prophet's marriage to Maymuna. The jurists agree on its lawfulness from the time of the Prophet to the present. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17721} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17722} By reason, the need for it is clear, since a person may be unable to act himself by illness, age or rank, and without it hardship would follow, which the text removes. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17722}

The pillars of wakala: the majority hold them to be the formula, the two parties (principal and agent) and the object of the agency. The Hanafis hold that the pillar is the offer and acceptance, since their presence entails the other two. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17722} The formula is the offer and acceptance, through which mutual consent is expressed. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17722}

The offer. The majority hold it is what comes from the principal and shows his permission to appoint an agent; the Hanafis hold it is what comes first from one of the contracting parties to show his desire to make the contract. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17723} It may be made by any sign of consent, in words, in writing, by message or by the sign of a mute man. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17723} Explicit words include 'I appointed you', 'I entrusted to you', 'I made you my deputy', 'I permitted you', 'I set you in my place' and 'you are my agent'. An order such as 'sell it' is valid for the Hanafis, Shafi'is and Hanbalis, and for the Malikis where custom so treats it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17724} The words 'I will appoint you', 'I appoint you' and 'I have relied on you' do not make a valid agency for the Shafi'is, since they are ambiguous, unless a clear term is added. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17724} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17725}

Agency may be concluded by message, according to the Hanafis, Malikis and Shafi'is: the agent becomes agent once the news reaches him and he accepts, whether the bearer is trustworthy or not. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17724} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17725} The offer may also be made by clear writing, by the understood sign of a mute man, by an act that shows permission (for the Hanbalis), and by what custom treats as an offer, as when a husband disposes of his wife's property while she knows and keeps silent (the Malikis). {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17725} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17726} Silence is not an offer for the Hanafis, Shafi'is and Hanbalis: one who sees a stranger sell his property and says nothing is not made an agent by his silence. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17726}

The acceptance may be given in words, such as 'I accept' or words showing acceptance. The Hanafis and Shafi'is add that the agent must not refuse; if he refuses after the offer, no agency is concluded even if he accepts later, unless the offer and acceptance are renewed. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17726} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17727} On acceptance by act there are three views. The majority (Hanafis, Malikis, the sounder Shafi'i view, and the Hanbalis) hold that it is complete by any act that shows it, such as the agent doing what he was told; the Shafi'is in one view, the Hanbalis in one view and Zufar among the Hanafis hold that it requires words; a third Shafi'i view distinguishes: if the offer is in the form of a command such as 'sell and buy', acceptance by act suffices, but if it is in the form of a contract such as 'I appointed you', words are needed. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17727} Acceptance is also valid in clear addressed writing and by the understood sign of a mute man; the Hanafis hold that an agent's silence is acceptance, which is undone by his refusal. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17727}

If acceptance is delayed after the offer, the majority (Hanafis, Hanbalis, the Shafi'i school view, and one Maliki view) hold that it is valid, since the permission remains while the principal does not withdraw it, and it is like a permission to act. The Shafi'is limit this to cases where the time of the act is not fixed; if it is fixed and there is fear of its passing, acceptance must be immediate, as also when the judge offers the agency to a man on its being established before him. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17728} The second Maliki view and Abu Hamid al-Marwarudhi among the Shafi'is hold acceptance must be immediate, because wakala is a contract made in life, and like sale it requires immediate acceptance. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17728} Al-Mazari said the matter returns to intention and custom: whether the words in question call for an immediate reply, or allow a prompt or a deferred one. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17728}

The forms of the wakala formula, by the time at which its effects arise, are: the immediate form, which is neither conditioned on a condition nor linked to a time; the conditional form; the form attached to the future; and the time-limited form. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17728}

The immediate formula, which is neither conditioned on a condition nor linked to a time, as in the principal saying 'I appointed you to sell that house', is valid by agreement of the jurists. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17729}

The formula conditioned on a condition. The jurists differ on two views. The first, held by the Hanafis, the sounder Hanbali view and, against the sounder view, the Shafi'is, is that it is valid, as in 'if the pilgrim arrives, sell this food'. They cite the report of Ibn 'Umar that the Prophet appointed Zayd ibn Haritha to command at Mu'ta, then Ja'far, then Ibn Rawaha if the first two were killed; and they argue that the agency is a contract whose effect on the agent, the permission to act, is recognised, so it is like a bequest. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17729} The second, held by the sounder Shafi'i view and one Hanbali view, is that it is invalid; al-Mawardi says conditioning an agency on conditions or terms is void. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17729} The Shafi'is allow the principal to conclude the agency now and to make the act itself subject to a condition, as in 'sell it after a month', since that is postponement, not conditioning. Al-Ghazali reports that the Iraqis held that 'I appointed you now, but do not act until a month passes or a certain man arrives' is valid, since it is a delay, and the agent must comply. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17729}

The periodic formula: the principal says 'I appointed you to sell this property, and whenever I remove you, you are my agent again', so the agency renews itself each time he removes the agent. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17729} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17730} The Hanafis and the sounder Hanbali view hold it valid, on the ground that agency admits conditioning; for the Hanafis the principal may remove his agent at any time, and for the Hanbalis removal is effected by his words 'I removed you, and whenever I appoint you, I have removed you'. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17730} Ibn Taymiyya holds it invalid, since it would turn a permissive contract into a binding one; the purpose of the conditional is to prevent appointment, not to dissolve a contract before it exists. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17730} The Shafi'is have two views on its validity at once; the sounder is that it is valid, because permission exists. If he removes the agent, and the agent did not know, or knew, there are two views on whether the agency returns after removal; the sounder is that it does not. If the words are 'whenever I remove you', the agency returns once; if they are 'every time I remove you', it returns each time. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17730} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17731}

The formula attached to a future time, as in 'I appointed you to sell my beasts in Ramadan', or 'I appointed you to sell this house tomorrow'. The Hanafis and Hanbalis hold it valid, since agency admits being attached to the future; the Hanafis add that the agent becomes agent from tomorrow onward and not before. The Shafi'is agree that it is valid, since the agency is concluded now and only the act is linked to a future time. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17731}

The formula conditioned on a time, as in 'when the new month comes, I appointed you to sell my house': on the sounder Shafi'i view it is void, since conditioning an agency on terms is invalid; the Hanafis, Hanbalis and the opposite Shafi'i view hold it valid. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17731} The Hanafis distinguish attachment (idafa) from conditioning (ta'liq). An attached formula is a cause that concludes the contract now while its effect is deferred to the time; a conditioned formula cannot be a cause of the effect now, so the agency is not concluded until the condition is met. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17731}

The time-limited formula, as in 'you are my agent for a month', is valid by the jurists. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17732} They differ whether it survives the period. The Shafi'is hold that it does not survive the period. The Hanafis report two views on one who was told 'sell my house today' and did it tomorrow: the sounder view is that the agency does not survive the day, but some Hanafis hold it survives, since naming the day is for haste, not for limiting the agency to that day. Al-Bazzaziyya, quoted by the author of Minhat al-Khaliq, holds that an agency for ten days does not end when the ten days pass, on the sounder view. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17732}

Conditions attached to the agency. If the condition is invalid, the Hanafis, Ahmad in the apparent of his two reports, and Ibn Abi Layla hold that the agency is not invalidated by any invalid condition. The Shafi'is hold that an invalid condition invalidates it, as when the principal says 'prevent the buyer from taking the goods', since to prevent a right-holder from taking possession is forbidden; the sale itself is valid by permission. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17732} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17733} Al-Zarkashi says that the permissive contracts (partnership, agency and mudaraba) are not stopped from taking effect by permission when they are invalid, but lose their own features. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17733} The effect of invalidity is that a named fee falls away and the customary fee is due. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17733} Some Hanbalis hold that the agency is void, like a deposit, because the trust is gone; and Abu Muhammad among the Shafi'is says the permission is not separate from the agency, so its invalidity means the permission is void. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17733} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17734}

The nature of the agency contract, whether binding or permissive. The first view, held by the Hanafis, Shafi'is, Hanbalis and some Malikis, is that it is permissive on both sides, since it is a gift and gifts are not binding: the principal may see that it is better to leave the matter, or to appoint another, and the agent may not be free. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17734} The Hanafis and Malikis except where a third party's right attaches to the agency, which then binds, as with an agent in a suit appointed at the claimant's request, or an agent to sell a pledge in the presence of the pledgee. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17734} The Hanafis say that from its non-binding nature three rules follow: an option of stipulation does not enter it; agency is not valid as a principal claim; and the agent may resign whenever he wishes. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17734} The second view: if the agency is for a fee in the form of a hire, it binds both sides and must meet the conditions of hire; the Shafi'is and in one view the Malikis say so. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17734} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17735} The third view, of some later Malikis, is that it binds the agent's side even without a fee, by analogy with a gift that binds once given, though not received. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17735} If the agency is by way of a reward (ju'ala), the Malikis hold three views: binding on both sides; permissive on both sides, which is the view relied upon by the Shafi'is; and binding on the side of the one who offers the reward once the other starts work. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17735}

The second pillar, the two parties, the principal and the agent. First, the principal: one who sets another in his place in a lawful known disposal, who must have the power to make that disposal and be bound by its rules. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17735} The jurists agree that agency may not be made by an insane person, an imbecile, a person under anaesthesia, a sleeper, or a minor who cannot discern, in any disposal. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17735}

A discerning minor may be appointed to acts that are purely beneficial to him, by agreement. For acts that are between benefit and loss, the Hanafis, Malikis and Hanbalis allow it with the guardian's permission, while the Shafi'is void it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17736} A spendthrift placed under restraint may not be appointed in what he cannot do independently, but may be in what he can. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17736}

The majority of jurists do not allow a woman to be appointed in a marriage, since she does not marry herself and so cannot be an agent for it; the Hanafis allow it, and the Malikis allow it in some cases. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17736}

On the appointment of an apostate there are three views. The first, held by the majority (the sounder Shafi'i view, the Hanbalis and Abu Hanifa), is that it is suspended on his return to Islam: if he returns the agency takes effect, and if he dies, is killed or joins the enemy as an apostate, it is void. The second, held by the two companions of Abu Hanifa and by one Shafi'i view, is that it is valid and binding; the Hanafis add that an apostate woman may be appointed by their agreement, since her acts take effect. The third, a Shafi'i view that Zakariyya al-Ansari and al-Shibramallisi regarded as the sound one, is that it is void. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17736} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17737}

On a Muslim appointing a dhimmi to sell wine or pork: the Shafi'is, Malikis, Hanbalis and the two companions hold it invalid, because a condition of the agency is that the principal himself owns the act he delegates, and a Muslim cannot sell wine or pork; one cannot give what one lacks. Abu Hanifa holds it valid, since it is enough that the principal has the capacity to act that entitles him to appoint another. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17737}

On a pilgrim (muhrim) appointing a non-pilgrim to contract a marriage: the majority do not allow it, since the pilgrim does not conduct the contract during his pilgrimage; the Hanafis allow it in every case, since he could conduct the contract himself. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17737} The Hanbalis say that agency is invalid if the agent does not know his principal, as when he is told 'Zayd has appointed you' without a name or a description that identifies him. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17737}

The agent must have the same sanity as the principal, so an insane person, an imbecile or a minor who cannot discern may not be an agent, by agreement. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17737} On puberty as a condition: the Hanafis and Hanbalis allow a discerning minor who understands the contract and intends it to be an agent, and the rights of the contract return to the principal, not to him; their evidence includes the report that the Prophet married Umm Salama through 'Amr ibn Umm Salama, who was a boy. The Shafi'is forbid it, because a minor is not accountable and cannot dispose of his own right, so he cannot dispose of another's by appointment. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17738} The agent must be named: if someone says 'I appointed one of the people to sell this goods', the agency is void, because the agent is unknown. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17738} Ibn Najim says that a creditor's words to his debtor, 'whoever comes to you with a certain sign, and takes your finger, or says such and such to you, pay what I owe him', are an unknown agency that does not discharge the debtor if he pays. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17739}

Knowledge of the agency. For the Hanafis, knowledge of the agency is a condition, either by the agent or by the party dealing with him. If the agent sells before he knows and the buyer knows, the sale is not valid until the principal or the agent ratifies it after knowledge. If a trustworthy man informs the agent, and the agent accepts, he is an agent; if one who is not trustworthy informs him and he accepts, he is an agent; if he does not accept, Abu Hanifa holds he is not an agent, while Abu Yusuf and Muhammad hold he is. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17740} The Hanbalis do not require knowledge, since the matter of contracts is what is actually the case; the sounder Shafi'i view agrees. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17740} Justice in the agent is not required in general, except in certain contracts such as marriage; nor is the male sex, except in some contracts such as marriage. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17740} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17741}

The object of the agency is the disposal the principal permits the agent, in ownership or in guardianship. The Shafi'is set three conditions: it must be known in some respects; it must admit deputising; and the principal must own it at the time of the agency. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17740} Agency is special, where it concerns a named disposal and the agent may act only in that, by agreement; or general. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17741} On general agency the Hanafis and Malikis allow it with detail. The Hanafis say that 'you are my agent in every small and great matter' gives only the power to keep; 'you are my agent in every matter your order permits' makes the agent competent for all money dispositions, such as sale, purchase, gift and alms; they differ on divorce, emancipation and waqf, and the view taken is that the agent does not have those, which the author of al-Bazzaziyya also holds. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17741}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17742} The Malikis say that a general agency is not concluded by the bare words 'I appointed you'; it must be delegated in words such as 'a delegated agency' or 'in all my affairs'. What increases property is allowed; gifts, emancipation and alms for the reward of the next life are not, unless the principal permits them in words. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17742} The Shafi'is and Hanbalis hold a general agency invalid because of the risk. 'I appointed you in all my small and great matters' or 'I delegated to you everything' is therefore invalid, while 'I appointed you to sell my goods and collect my debts' is valid even if the goods and debts are unknown. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17742}

The general rule for what may be the object of an agency is that whatever contract a person may make himself, he may appoint another to make, because a person may be unable to act himself in some circumstances and so needs to appoint another. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17742} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17743} The jurists agree that agency in sale and purchase is valid, because there is a need for it. The Prophet himself appointed 'Urwa al-Bariqi to buy a sheep, and gave a dinar to Hakim ibn Hizam to buy a sacrifice. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17743} They also agree that agency is valid in assignment of debt (hawala), pledge, surety, partnership, deposit, mudaraba, reward contracts (ju'ala), irrigation partnership (musaqa), hire, loan, bequest, rescission, release, exchange (musarafa), cancellation (iqala) and pre-emption, since all of these are like sale in their need for an agent; likewise surety, settlement and gift. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17743}

The jurists agree that a man may appoint an agent in a marriage contract, citing the Prophet's appointment of 'Amr ibn Umayya and Abu Rafi' to accept a marriage for him; the need for it, since a man may wish to marry from a far place and cannot travel there; and the Prophet's marriage to Umm Habiba while she was in the land of Abyssinia. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17744}

They agree that agency is valid in financial acts of worship, such as zakat, charity, vows and expiations, since the Prophet sent his agents to collect and distribute charity. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17744} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17745} They also agree that agency is valid in divorce, revocation and khul', since the need is like the need for agency in sale and marriage. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17744}

Acts that agency may not cover, by agreement. Testimony: agency is not valid in it, because it depends on the witness himself, who reports what he saw or heard, and because it rests on devotion and certainty; if a witness appoints another to testify, the second acts as a witness on the first witness's testimony and not as an agent. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17744} Oaths and vows, since they depend on the swearer or vower himself; so do the oaths of mutual cursing, the vow of abstinence from wives and the oath of compurgation. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17745} Sins, such as killing, theft, usurpation and slander, which are forbidden and may not be done by the principal or the agent. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17745} And purely bodily worship, such as prayer, fasting and the purification from minor impurity, since they depend on the body of the one obliged. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17745}

Matters on which the jurists differ. The pilgrimage: agency is not allowed for a person able to make it himself; the incapable person's case is set out elsewhere. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17745} The lesser pilgrimage (umra) may generally be performed on behalf of another by agency. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17745} Marriage by a woman: most jurists forbid a woman to appoint or be appointed in marriage, since she may not contract it herself; the Hanafis allow it for a free adult sane woman. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17746} Zihar: the majority forbid agency in it, the sounder Shafi'i view forbids it, and a Shafi'i view allows it; Ibn 'Abd al-Salam of the Malikis held that zihar is like divorce, since both are mere utterances. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17746} Securing permitted acts, such as reviving dead land, drawing water, hunting, gathering grass or digging a mine: the Malikis, the sounder Shafi'i view and the Hanbalis allow it, since the ownership comes from a cause that does not bind the person; the Hanafis, some Shafi'is and some Hanbalis forbid it, since the cause of ownership, taking hold, has occurred for the agent and an intention cannot turn it aside. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17746} Acknowledgement of rights: the Hanafis, Malikis, Hanbalis and a non-sounder Shafi'i view allow it, since it establishes a right in the obligation by words, as sale does; the sounder Shafi'i view and al-Tahawi forbid it, since it is a report about a right, like testimony. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17747}

Litigation: the Malikis, Shafi'is, Hanbalis and the two companions of Abu Hanifa allow agency in litigation over debts, specific property and all rights, whether the principal is present or absent, sick or well, and whether the opponent consents or not; the Malikis exclude an agent who is hostile to the opponent. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17747} They cite the consensus of the Companions, as 'Ali appointed 'Aqil before Abu Bakr and said 'what is decided for him is mine, and what is decided against him is mine'; and the need for it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17747} Abu Hanifa holds that litigation by agency is valid only with the opponent's consent, unless the principal is sick or absent three days' journey or more, since the principal's presence at the court is a right of his opponent. His reason is that a claim is a report that may be true or false, so its answer is required only by necessity; that necessity arises when the principal is sick or travelling. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17748} Al-Jassas reports that the apparent view draws no distinction between a man and a woman, or a virgin and a married woman; later Hanafis approve the agency of a secluded woman who does not appear in public; Ibn Abi Layla held it valid only for a virgin. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17748}

Proof and execution of retaliation (qisas). The Malikis, Shafi'is, Hanbalis, Abu Hanifa and Muhammad al-Shaybani allow agency in proving qisas, whether the principal is present or absent, since qisas is a human right; Abu Yusuf does not allow it, and the evidence is accepted only from the principal himself, because of the doubt that must be guarded against in retaliation and the hudud. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17748} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17749} On the execution of qisas: the Malikis, Shafi'is and the school view of the Hanbalis allow it whether the principal is present or absent. The Hanafis, some Shafi'is and some Hanbalis allow it only when the principal is present, since he may pardon in his absence. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17749}

Proof and execution of the hudud. The Shafi'is and Hanbalis distinguish the two. The school view of the Hanbalis allows agency in proving adultery, from the story of Unays; Abu al-Khattab among them says it is invalid. The Shafi'is do not allow agency in proving the hudud of God except the hadd of slander, since the right is God's, and it is commanded to avert it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17749} On execution, the Malikis, Shafi'is and Hanbalis allow agency, citing the Prophet's order to stone Ma'iz and 'Uthman's appointment of 'Ali to carry out the flogging for drinking on al-Walid ibn 'Uqba. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17750} The Hanafis say there are two kinds of rights of God: proof, where agency is not allowed for the hudud that need no litigation, such as adultery and drinking, and is allowed for those that need it, such as theft and slander, according to Abu Hanifa and Muhammad, but Abu Yusuf forbids it; and execution of slander and theft is valid when the injured party is present, while if he is absent the Hanafi shaykhs differ. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17750} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17751}

The rules of agency. They bind the agent to carry it out within the limits the principal permitted or that law or custom imposes, to inform the principal of necessary information and render an account, and to return what belongs to the principal that is in his hand. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17751} A special agency may be issued unrestricted or restricted. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17751} Among its forms is agency in sale. For Abu Hanifa, the agent in an unrestricted sale is not bound by any restriction unless he is suspected; he may sell for little or much, for cash or on credit, and for goods, since the unrestricted word keeps its unrestricted sense, and a sale at a gross undervaluation in order to buy something more profitable is also customary. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17751}
