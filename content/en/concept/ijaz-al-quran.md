## Summary
The inimitability of the Qur'an (*i'jaz al-Qur'an*) is the doctrine that the Qur'an cannot be matched by human beings and is the Prophet's greatest sign of the truth of his prophethood. [[person/al-qadi-iyyad|Qadi Iyad]] devotes several sections to it in chapter four of Part One of [[work/al-shifa-bi-tarif-huquq-al-mustafa|al-Shifa]], stating that God's Book contains many aspects of inimitability which, when their kinds are classified, come down to four. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.265} It is closely tied to the idea of the [[concept/mujiza|miracle]] and to [[concept/al-tahaddi-bil-quran|the Qur'anic challenge]].

## First aspect: composition and eloquence
Iyad holds that the Arabs were masters of eloquence and "knights of speech" — the Bedouin with his weighty diction, the townsman with his clear rhetoric — and that the Qur'an came and dazzled their minds, challenging them for "some twenty years" to bring a sura like it, which they did not do despite their eagerness to defeat it. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.265} {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.266} {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.267} {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.268} He cites [[person/al-walid-ibn-al-mughira|al-Walid ibn al-Mughira]]'s saying on hearing it that it had sweetness and grace, and stories of Bedouins who prostrated at its eloquence. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.269} Within this aspect he points to the concision of a single verse rich in meaning, the coherence of long narratives such as the story of Joseph, and the varied but equally fine wording when a story is repeated. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.270} {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.271}

## Second aspect: its wondrous arrangement
The second aspect is its wondrous arrangement and unusual style, unlike every Arab form of speech, with no equal before or after it in prose, verse, rhymed prose, *rajaz* or poetry; he relates al-Walid ibn al-Mughira's debate with Quraysh in which he denied it was poetry, soothsaying, madness or sorcery. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.271} {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.272}

## Third and fourth aspects: the unseen and the past
The third is its news of the unseen which came true, such as the Byzantines' victory after defeat and people entering Islam in crowds. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.275} The fourth is its account of past generations, vanished nations and abandoned laws, though his people knew he was unlettered, neither reading nor writing; the People of the Book would question him and revelation came with the answer. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.277} {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.278} Iyad says these four aspects are clear and beyond dispute. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.279}

## Further aspects
He then lists aspects counted by other imams: the awe that seizes its hearers, even those who do not understand it; reports of those who tried to imitate it and were overcome by awe, as told of [[person/ibn-al-muqaffa|Ibn al-Muqaffa']] and of Yahya ibn Hakam al-Ghazal; its preservation through the ages; that its reciter never tires of it; its gathering of sciences unknown to the Arabs; and the ease of memorising it. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.281} {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.282} {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.283} {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.286} He considers most of these to fall under eloquence and not to deserve counting as separate aspects. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.287}

## Disagreement over the cause of the incapacity
Iyad reports two positions: that of the proponents of *sarfa*, that imitation was within human power but they were turned away from it, and that of some Sunni scholars, that its like is of a kind humans could produce but God never gave them the ability, before or after. He quotes Imam al-Haramayn al-Juwayni that the Arabs' abandoning the contest despite every motive, accepting death, captivity and humiliation instead, is the plainest sign of incapacity. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.380}

## For young visitors
The Arabs were proud of their poetry and speeches. When the Qur'an came, it challenged them to make one sura like it, and they could not. Qadi Iyad explains why Muslims count this as the Prophet's greatest miracle.
