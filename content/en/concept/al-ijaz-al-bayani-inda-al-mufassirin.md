## Summary
The Qur'an contains verses that challenge whoever doubts it to bring the like of it: [[passage/quran-17-88|al-Isra 88]] with the like of the Qur'an, [[passage/quran-11-13|Hud 13]] with ten suras, [[passage/quran-10-38|Yunus 38]] and [[passage/quran-2-23|al-Baqara 23]] with a sura, and [[passage/quran-52-34|al-Tur 34]] with a discourse like it. Muslim scholars call the inability of those addressed to do so inimitability. This page sets out what four commentators said on the aspect of inimitability that lies in language and expression, and attributes each statement to the one who made it. The general register on the question is [[concept/ijaz-al-quran|The inimitability of the Qur'an]].

## A like in what
At the verse al-Baqara 23 al-Tabari asks: how can the Qur'an have a like when it is the speech of God? His answer is that the like asked for is a like in clear expression, because it came down in an Arabic tongue, and the speech of the Arabs has a like in the sense of Arabic. As for what sets the Qur'an apart from all the speech of created beings, it has no like in that. He says that God did not ask them for a sura in a tongue other than theirs, but said to them in effect: bring a sura like it from your own tongues; and if, all together, they are unable to match what one of them brought, their lying becomes plain {cite:tafsir-tabari-quran-com on 2:23}.

At the verse of Yunus he puts the argument in the Prophet's mouth: if this Qur'an is invented, you are like me, Arabs, and my tongue is like your tongue, so bring a sura like it {cite:tafsir-tabari-quran-com on 10:38}.

## The stages of the challenge
Ibn Kathir arranges the challenge in three stages: first He challenged them with the whole Qur'an, then He came down to ten suras, then to one sura. He says that the verses of Mecca repeated this and that the challenge then came in Medina in sura al-Baqara, and that they were unable despite the intensity of their enmity {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-quran-com on 10:38} {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-quran-com on 2:23}.

Al-Baghawi transmits from al-Mubarrad a reason for the sura being mentioned before the ten: the challenge in Yunus is with a sura like it in reports of the unseen, rulings, promise and warning; when they were unable, they were told in Hud: bring ten suras without report, promise or warning, being mere eloquence {cite:tafsir-baghawi-quran-com on 11:13}.

## Every sura is inimitable
Ibn Kathir holds that the challenge is general to every sura, long or short, because the word "sura" is indefinite in a conditional context and so is general. He mentions the objection of [[person/fakhr-al-din-al-razi|al-Razi]] from the short suras and rejects it, and he transmits the saying of [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]] on sura al-Asr that if people reflected on it, it would suffice them {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-quran-com on 2:24}.

## "And you will never do it"
In [[passage/quran-2-24|al-Baqara 24]] there is a denial that they will bring the like of it in the future. Ibn Kathir counts this as another miracle: a categorical report that the Qur'an will never be matched, and so it has been from his time to ours {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-quran-com on 2:24}. Al-Qurtubi says that the wording stirs their ambitions so that their inability afterwards is all the more remarkable, and that it is among the unseen matters that the Qur'an reported before they took place {cite:tafsir-qurtubi-quran-com on 2:24}.

## Describing its style
At the same place Ibn Kathir compares the Qur'an with the poetry of the Arabs. He says that most of a long qasida is description of women, horses or wine, or praise of a person, and that it contains only a verse or two that are its aim, the rest being filler. The Qur'an, by contrast, is all eloquent and of the utmost rhetoric to one who knows the speech of the Arabs and the turns of expression, whether it reports, commands, forbids, promises or warns, and its repetition only adds to its sweetness {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-quran-com on 2:24}.

Al-Baghawi gathers the aspects at the verse of al-Isra: the Qur'an is inimitable in its composition and arrangement, and in reporting the unseen, and it stands at the highest levels of eloquence and resembles no speech of created beings {cite:tafsir-baghawi-quran-com on 17:88}.

## Why the people of the tongue recognised it
Ibn Kathir says that eloquence was among the natural gifts of the Arabs, and that their poems and their mu'allaqat were the furthest reach in this field, and then there came to them what no one could withstand. For this reason those of them who believed did so because of what they knew of its eloquence, sweetness, strength and charm; they were the people who knew it best, as the magicians knew that what Moses brought could come only from one supported by God, and as the physicians knew that what Jesus brought was not medicine {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-quran-com on 10:38}.

## The view of sarfa
Ibn Kathir mentions a way taken by some of the theologians: if the Qur'an is inimitable in itself, what was claimed has been achieved, and if people were able to match it and did not, despite the intensity of their enmity, that is evidence that God turned them away from it. He comments that this way is suitable by way of concession and disputation, and that the correct view is that the Qur'an is inimitable in itself and that people cannot match it {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-quran-com on 2:24}.
