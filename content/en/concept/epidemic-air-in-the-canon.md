## Summary
In the ninth chapter of the second lesson Ibn Sina treats the harmful changes of the air that run against its natural course, and begins with changes in the air's substance, the epidemic, which he describes as a putrefaction arising in the air like the putrefaction of stagnant, stale water {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.125}.

## What he calls air
He explains that he does not mean simple, pure air, for that is not the air that surrounds us, and no pure simple body putrefies; it changes in its quality or its substance into another simple. By air he means the body spread through the sky, a mixed body made of true air, watery vapour, earthy parts rising in smoke and dust, and fiery parts. It is called air as the water of the sea and of marshes is called water, although it is a mixture in which water prevails {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.125}.

## Putrefaction and season
This air may putrefy and its substance turn bad, as marsh water putrefies, and the epidemic and the putrefaction of the air occur most at the end of summer and in autumn. He leaves the symptoms of the epidemic to another place in the book {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.125}.

## Effect on the body
When the air changes it brings effects on bodies; if it putrefies it putrefies the humours, beginning with the humour confined in the heart, since that is nearest to it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.125}.

## Change in qualities
He distinguishes this from a change in qualities, in which heat or cold goes to an unbearable extreme so that crops and offspring are spoiled, either by a like change, such as the fierce heat of midsummer gone bad, or by an opposite change, such as a bitter cold in summer by some accident {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.125}.
