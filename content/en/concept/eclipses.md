## Summary
An eclipse of the sun is the moon's passing between the sun and the earth at conjunction; a lunar eclipse is the moon's entering the earth's shadow at opposition. Al-Farghani treats them in chapters twenty-eight, twenty-nine and thirty {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 23}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 24}.

## Lunar eclipses
Al-Farghani shows that the moon is lit by the sun's light, so the half of its disc facing the sun is bright; at opposition the dark half faces us {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 26}. At opposition the moon enters the earth's shadow, which is a cone narrowing as it extends {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 25}. He gives the shadow-circle's diameter where the moon passes as about twice and two-thirds the moon's diameter (OCR reading) {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 25}.

A lunar eclipse is total when the moon's centre passes through the shadow: if the moon's latitude is less than the excess of the shadow's radius over the moon's, it is wholly eclipsed and has no duration; if the latitude equals the sum of the two radii the moon grazes the shadow and is not eclipsed {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 24}.

## Solar eclipses
At conjunction the moon is at the ascending or descending node with no latitude from the sun's path, and covers it wholly. If the conjunction falls away from the nodes, parallax makes the visible differ from the true, and an eclipse may appear partial from one place and total from another {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 23}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 22}.

## The intervals between eclipses
Al-Farghani states that the least interval between all solar and lunar eclipses is six lunar months, that two close solar eclipses may be five months apart when special conditions combine, and that two eclipses may be seven months apart when the months are short {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 20}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 19}.

## Perspectives
- **The author (al-Farghani):** eclipses are explained by conjunction and opposition, latitude and parallax, and the intervals are drawn from all of this according to the months.

## Pages 201 to 388: lunar and solar eclipses and their intervals
Al-Farghani shows that the moon takes its light from the sun, its bright half facing it, and that a lunar eclipse occurs when the moon enters the earth's shadow at or near a node {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 234}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 235}. He estimates the length of the earth's shadow-cone in Ptolemy at about 268 earth-radii; these figures are OCR readings and are doubtful {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 235}. The greatest and longest lunar eclipse is when the moon is at the node with no latitude {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 236}.

A solar eclipse occurs when the moon conjoins the sun at the node without latitude, hiding it from the observer {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 237}. Parallax shifts the apparent conjunction: the moon may appear advanced of or delayed from the true conjunction according to the observer's place {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 238}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 239}.

Al-Farghani estimates the moon's parallax at the horizon at about one degree and forty-four minutes at perigee and about fifty-four minutes at apogee (OCR reading), and the sun's at about three minutes {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 232}. He states the least interval between two eclipses, solar or lunar, is six lunar months, and may be five {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 240}, and that two solar eclipses never fall in one month {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 241}.

Among Golius's examples is a lunar eclipse Ibn Yunus observed on the night of Thursday in the year 368 of the Hijra, with the Persian, Alexandrian and Diocletian dates given; the eclipse's diameter was more than eight fingers and less than nine {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 294}. The Diocletian year is printed as "69" where 694 is correct by the other reckonings — a correction flagged, not relied on without checking.
