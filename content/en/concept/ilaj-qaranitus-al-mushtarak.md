## Evacuation and caution
What is common to its true kinds is evacuation of blood, drawing off good blood, and hastening to it as soon as the humours begin, unless a strong obstacle prevents; it must be done with care to learn his state as to fainting, whether he has fallen into it or come near it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.80} No plain sign shows much when fainting is near, but the pulse tells of it: when it trembles, or sinks, or varies without order until one beat is large and another small, that shows fainting is near {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.80} If it can be done, it is sounder to put it off while the case allows and there is no danger in delay, and not to push it so far that no blood is left in the body by which nature may be strong to wrestle with the crises and with the loss of food {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.81}

## Drawing away and cooling
The matters are drawn away downward from every side, cooling things that are not astringent are used for the most part, and at the start the pure repellents, unless it is of the great kind in which the vessels that leave the head swell in sharing with the membrane, where one begins with what has a little relaxing and quieting of pain, and then the astringents {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.81} When the illness lessens and the raving remains, moderate cooling means are used {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.81} If the illness grows long and does not go, or is heavy and comatose with stillness more than movement, the strongly cooling things are avoided and what dissolves is used {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.81}

## Dwelling, company and sleep
He is lodged in a house of tempered air that is plain, with no ornament or pictures, since his fancies grow fond of them by gazing and that harms his brain and its membranes; he is kept with his witty friends who are dear to him and kind to him, and with those he is shy of, so that on their account he holds back from the confusion and agitation that harm him {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.80} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.81} Effort is made to bring him sleep, and care is taken against what carries danger if the power is not strong, for it is deadly {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.81}

## Food and the regimen of the convalescent
He is first held to the utmost lightness of food and then moved to the heavier, with regard to the power and the illness; the stronger the signs of the illness, the more his food is lightened, unless failing of the power is feared, in which case he is fed {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.81} He is kept from very cold air, especially if the diaphragm or the viscera are swollen, and as the illness falls the food is raised by degrees and made of the cold foods {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.81} Vomiting may come upon them and they benefit from it, and when they do not pass urine for loss of mind and weakness of sense, their bladder is attended to {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.82} When they go far down in decline and are out of the bulk of the illness, they are managed with the regimen of convalescents and kept from bad and hot airs and winds, from poisons and the sun, lest they relapse, and they are fed light meats; there are many benefits in their sleeping {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.82}

## Note
Recipes, quantities, the names of drugs and plants, the names of veins and the sites of venesection, cupping and cautery, and methods of application are left out here. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 pp.80-82}
