## Summary
What was found on Arabic in Yemen: the text of the constitution in the United Nations translation on the Constitute Project, the encyclopedia's statement that the people speak various dialects of Arabic, and the names of the Yemeni varieties in Glottolog's tree, with a statement of what was not found. For the place see [[place/yemen|Yemen]]. For the Arab states in general see [[concept/al-alam-al-arabi|The Arab states and the League of Arab States]]. The constitutional text is added in its original wording, as supplied in a research summary that the museum did not check against the page.

## Official status
Article 2 of Yemen's constitution, in the translation on the Constitute Project, says Islam is the religion of the state and Arabic its official language; the constitution treats language in no other article. The text shown is titled "1991 (rev. 2015)" and has a 2015 Transitional Declaration appended in a United Nations translation, which is temporary by its own terms, so the page is not claimed to show a settled permanent text {cite:constituteproject-yemen}. The article on the Semitic languages in the Encyclopaedia Britannica lists Yemen among the states where Arabic is official {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}.

## Constitutional text
The Constitution of the Republic of Yemen, Article 2. The research summary supplied to the museum gives this Arabic wording: «الإسلام دين الدولة، واللغـة العربيـة لغتهـا الرسميـة.», which the museum translates: "Islam is the religion of the State, and Arabic is its official language". The summary names the National Information Center of Yemen as the official source; the museum did not open that page, so the wording is taken from the summary as supplied, and the founder approved using it so on 21 November 2026 {cite:official-constitution-yemen}.

## The spoken varieties
The Encyclopaedia Britannica opens its article on Yemen by saying the people speak various dialects of Arabic; only the first lines of the article were read {cite:britannica-yemen}. Glottolog's tree lists Yemeni varieties by name: Sanaani, Ta'izzi-Adeni, Hadrami and Judeo-Yemeni Arabic {cite:glottolog-arabic}. The University of Manchester notes that in some Yemeni dialects both genders occur in the first person singular pronouns {cite:manchester-arabic-structural-variation}. No shares of these varieties among the population were found.

The same Manchester page divides the Arabic dialects into geographical units, one of them the dialects of the Arabian Peninsula; it does not name the states inside that unit, so placing this state in it is an inference from its location, and it says that in most Arab countries urban and rural dialects differ, as do those of settled and formerly nomadic (Bedouin) people {cite:manchester-arabic-structural-variation}. Glottolog's tree shows separately named varieties under "Arabian Peninsula Arabic" {cite:glottolog-arabic}; these are two different classifications, one geographical group at Manchester and distinct varieties at Glottolog, and the museum does not choose between them. The Ethnologue page was not read, so the SIL classification is not known here. For more on the dialects see [[concept/lahjat-al-yaman|The dialects of Yemen]].

## Other languages
Glottolog's tree puts Yemeni Mehri, eastern and western, under the Modern South Arabian branch of the Semitic languages, a branch separate from Arabic in the tree {cite:glottolog-arabic}. Nothing was read on the number of Mehri speakers or on other languages of Yemen.

## The standard and the spoken varieties together
The University of Manchester's page on the dialects of Arabic describes the general situation across the Arabic-speaking world as three-layered: Modern Standard Arabic, the language of the state, education, public services and the media; Classical Arabic, found in the Qur'an, religious texts and worship; and the colloquial dialects of everyday exchange, which the page says people usually regard as of little value even though they are common in song, theatre and film {cite:manchester-arabic-sociolinguistic}. This is a description of the Arab world in general, not of this state alone, and no study measuring it here was read. For the relation of the standard to the spoken varieties see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]], and for the differences among the tribes' speech before the standard [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|The dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]].
