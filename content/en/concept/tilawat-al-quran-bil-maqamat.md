## Where the disagreement lies
The disagreement is not over beautifying the voice in recitation — that is recommended among the jurists — but over using musical melodies and the maqamat of Arabic song in tilawa; the books of fiqh treat it under the heading "talhin al-Qur'an" {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3}.

## What the maqamat are
The practitioners of song collected people's melodies by induction into measured modes they call maqamat, as al-Khalil collected the metres of poetry; they list six {cite:islamqa-169799}: maqam al-bayat, described as devout; maqam al-rast, preferred for narrative and legislative verses; maqam al-nahawand, described as tender; maqam al-sikah, described as slow and unhurried; maqam al-saba, described as spiritual; and maqam al-hijaz, alone Arabic in name. The point is noted that these maqamat are a codification of song tunes, and a reciter may fall into one without knowing its name.

## The positions
One group forbids or dislikes the practice when the melody takes a letter beyond its measure, adds vowels, or introduces trilling — holding that the forbidden taghani is whatever bends the recitation from the rule of tajwid into the metres of song; the fatwa collections cite Ibn Baz's ruling forbidding recitation on the song maqamat {cite:islamqa-169799}. Ibn Taymiyya defines the disliked melodies as those requiring shortening the lengthened letter, lengthening the shortened, vowelling the still and stilling the vowelled to match song tunes — and if that leads to changing the Qur'an's order and making vowels into letters it is haram {cite:islamqa-1377}. Another group permits beautifying the voice even when a melody falls in it, so long as no letter is taken beyond its due and no artifice turns the hearer from the meaning; they note that many of the early readers recited on tunes the listeners recognised without naming them by the maqamat of the musical manuals {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3} {cite:islamqa-169799}.

## Transmitted statements of the scholars
Ibn al-Qayyim reports in Zad al-Ma'ad that whoever knows the states of the salaf knows they are free of reciting with artificial musical melodies — measured rhythms and counted movements — and that they recited with tahzin and tatrib, beautifying their voices with the Qur'an with tenderness, delight and longing at turns, a thing rooted in natures that the Lawgiver did not forbid {cite:islamqa-169799}. Ibn Kathir says in Fada'il al-Qur'an that what is required by the law is beautifying the voice in a way that moves to pondering and humility, while voices with composed melodies built on musical weights and laws — the Qur'an is far above them {cite:islamqa-169799}. Al-Suyuti is quoted: reciting the Qur'an with melodies, beautiful voices and trilling, if it does not take it out of its recognised form, is a good sunnah; if it does, it is an abominable haram {cite:islamqa-1377}. And Ibn Qudama reports the scholars' agreement that recitation with tahzin, tartil and beautifying is recommended {cite:islamqa-1377}.

## What is not disputed
The people of performance agree that tajwid is not the accompaniment of instruments, nor stretching the ghunna and mudud beyond their measure, and that Ibn al-Jazari described tajwid itself as "the ornament of the tilawa and the beauty of performance and reading" — placing the beauty in exactness, not in melody {cite:ar-wikisource}. Imam Ahmad's ruling on taghani is reported as concerning the generation of extra letters and vowels through distorting and stretching letters — what takes recitation out of valid performance {cite:islamqa-1377}.
