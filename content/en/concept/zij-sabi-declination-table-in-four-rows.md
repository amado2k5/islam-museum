## Summary
Al-Battani sets the declination table with four rows of numbers: the first with the numbers of the signs from one part to ninety, the second what the first row's numbers fall short of 180, the third what they exceed 180 by, and the fourth what they fall short of 360, so that when a number falls in the first or second the declination is north and when in the last two south. He then shows how the declination is taken with minutes, by proportion on the difference between the declinations of two successive degrees. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion pp.22-23}.

## The four rows
He says the table has four rows: the first holds the numbers of the zodiac from one part to ninety, the second what the first row's numbers fall short of 180, the third what the first row's numbers exceed 180 by, and the fourth what they fall short of 360. The four rows thus cover the whole circle of the signs by the computation of a single quarter. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion pp.22-23}.

## Taking the Sun's declination from the table
To know the declination of the Sun or of any other degree of the signs, count from the beginning of Aries to the Sun's degree or the other wanted, and the result is the share of the declination. Look for it in the four rows of numbers of the declination tables and take the degrees, minutes and seconds facing it, which is the declination of the degrees you wanted. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.23}.

## Minutes and proportion
If the degrees carry minutes, take their share of the difference in the manner shown for the difference of chords: see what share the minutes are of sixty minutes, the step by which the numbers differ, take that share of the difference between the declination of the whole degree and that of one degree more, then see: if the declination of the degree in hand is the smaller, add it, and if it is the larger, subtract it. Here the difference between two successive degrees is measured by sixty minutes, whereas in the chord table it was by thirty. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.23}.
