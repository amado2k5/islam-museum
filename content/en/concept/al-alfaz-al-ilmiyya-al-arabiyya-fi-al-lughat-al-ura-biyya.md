## Summary
After the Abbasid translation movement part of the Arabic scientific lexicon passed into European languages through Latin and Romance translations of Arabic works on mathematics, astronomy, chemistry and medicine. This page sets out nine words, as a research summary supplied to the museum gives them with dictionary references: algebra, algorithm, alchemy, alcohol, zenith, nadir and azimuth, then syrup and elixir, whose origin the summary mentions in its general description {cite:etymonline-algebra} {cite:etymonline-zenith} {cite:merriam-webster-elixir}.

## algebra: al-jabr
Its Arabic source is al-jabr, which the summary glosses as 'reunion of broken parts' or 'restoration', from the title of [[person/al-khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]]'s al-Mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa-l-muqabala, a book which has the record [[work/al-jabr-wal-muqabala|al-Jabr wal-Muqabala]]; the science has the page [[concept/algebra|Algebra]] {cite:etymonline-algebra}.

## algorithm: al-Khwarizmi
The summary traces it to the name al-Khwarizmi, 'native of Khwarazm', Latinised as Algorismus, so that it is the name of a man which became the name of a method of calculation {cite:etymonline-algebra}.

## alchemy and alcohol
Alchemy comes from al-kimiya, the Arabic definite article al- with the Greek chemeia, by way of Medieval Latin alkimia {cite:etymonline-chemistry} {cite:merriam-webster-alchemy}. Alcohol comes from al-kuhl, the fine powdered antimony or kohl, which in Medieval Latin passed to sublimated powders and then to distilled spirits {cite:etymonline-alcohol}.

## zenith, nadir and azimuth
Zenith comes from samt al-ra's, 'direction over the head', shortened to samt and misread by Latin scribes, who took the m for ni, giving cenit {cite:etymonline-zenith} {cite:merriam-webster-zenith}. Nadir comes from nazir al-samt, 'opposite the zenith', from nazir, 'opposite' {cite:etymonline-nadir} {cite:merriam-webster-zenith}. Azimuth comes from al-sumut, plural of al-samt, 'the ways' or 'directions' {cite:etymonline-azimuth}.

## syrup and elixir
The summary says syrup was taken from sharab by way of Constantine the African, and elixir from al-iksir, through Latin and Romance translations of Arabic works on mathematics, astronomy, chemistry and medicine. Its references are Wikipedia and the Merriam-Webster dictionary, which are relied on here only for the origin of a word {cite:wikipedia-list-english-words-arabic-origin-n-s-en} {cite:merriam-webster-elixir}.

## The four fields
The nine words fall into four fields in the 'Field' column of the summary's table: mathematics for algebra and algorithm, chemistry for alchemy, chemistry and medicine together for alcohol, astronomy for zenith, nadir and azimuth. Of syrup and elixir the summary gives no field in the table, but speaks in its general description that joins mathematics, astronomy, chemistry and medicine {cite:etymonline-algebra} {cite:etymonline-zenith} {cite:merriam-webster-elixir}.

## How the words travelled
The summary says the passage was through Latin and Romance translations of Arabic treatises, and that Constantine the African is the go-between for syrup. It names no other go-between, so the page names none. These translations have their background in the page [[concept/harakat-al-tarjama-al-abbasiyya|The Abbasid translation movement]], where the summary names Toledo and Monte Cassino as centres of Latin translation {cite:wikipedia-list-english-words-arabic-origin-n-s-en} {cite:britannica-arab-scholars-greek-medical-learning}.

## What the words say
The words fall in three fields: mathematics, astronomy and chemistry with medicine. This agrees with what the summary says, that Arabic became a language of science in the Abbasid translation movement and that its works were carried into Latin in centres such as Toledo, which is the subject of the page [[concept/harakat-al-tarjama-al-abbasiyya|The Abbasid translation movement]] {cite:britannica-arab-scholars-greek-medical-learning}.

## What the summary does not give
The summary does not say when each word entered the European languages, names no language but English in the words column, and does not trace the shift of sense from the Arabic source to modern use: al-kuhl is a powder, and alcohol today is another substance, which is from the dictionaries' description and not the museum's. The page does not claim that these nine are all the Arabic scientific words that entered European languages; they are a selection in the summary {cite:etymonline-algebra} {cite:etymonline-zenith} {cite:merriam-webster-elixir}.

## Basis
The page rests on a research summary supplied to the museum, and the museum did not open the pages it cites; the founder approved relying on it as it stands on 21 November 2026. Every origin and explanation of a word is attributed to the summary, and Wikipedia is used only for facts about a word. The museum has not checked the derivations and the page says nothing of other words.
