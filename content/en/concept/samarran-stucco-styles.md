## Overview
Stucco is plaster mixed with cement or concrete; it was widely used in pre-Islamic Parthian and Sassanian architecture in Persia and Iraq, valued for its flexibility, ease of handling and speed in covering coarse surfaces such as brickwork with fine decoration. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.37} No stucco survives in the mosques of Samarra, but much was excavated in its ruined palaces such as Balkuwara and the Jausaq al-Khaqani; Ernst Herzfeld first classified it, and he and Creswell divided it into three styles that coexisted through the period with no firm chronology. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.37} All three are a new synthesis of motifs established in Hellenistic, Byzantine, Sassanian and Umayyad art, built on the vine with its leaves and grapes, with palmettes, rosettes and cornucopia. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.37}

## Style A
The closest to the Western tradition and the most naturalistic: five-lobed stylised vine leaves with incised veins and drilled "eyes" between the lobes, bound by stalks, scrolling along borders or rolled into six-lobed beaded panels, sometimes alternating with bunches of grapes in hexagonal panels; the carving is dense and deep, leaving leaves and grapes in sharp relief. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.37}

## Style B
Figure and ground are in balance: leaves and palmettes are more abstract, with no stems to link them, and are organised in the surface geometry of polylobes, polygons, squares and rectangles; Yeomans sees here the genesis of the Islamic [[concept/arabesque|arabesque]]. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.37} It is the dominant style in the stucco of the [[building/masjid-ibn-tulun|Mosque of Ibn Tulun]], above all in the soffits of the arches facing the sahn. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.38} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.39}

## Style C
Less rich and dense but more radically abstract: open-ended symmetrical repeat patterns over large surfaces, in shallow, undulating, bevelled relief, cast from baked clay moulds so that it could be assembled and installed very quickly. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.38} Its motifs are ambiguous, suggesting vases, bottles, spearheads, volutes, palmettes and birds, not framed in compartments, so that figure becomes ground and ground figure. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.38}

## Passage to Egypt
Yeomans holds that style C had the most enduring influence on the decorative arts of Egypt: stucco fragments from the houses of Fustat in the Museum of Islamic Art, Cairo, show it, and it became a vehicle for decorating pottery, rock crystal and woodwork, lasting into the Ikhshidid and Fatimid eras, so that its bevelled patterns appear more than a century later in the tie-beams and doors of the mosque of al-Hakim. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.39}

## Geometry in Islamic and Western art
In this context Yeomans sets out his view that geometric forms in Islamic art are dynamic, open and infinite, and that its vegetal ornament is stylised and two-dimensional, respecting the surface, unlike Hellenistic art with its pull towards naturalism and closed forms; he cites Owen Jones, for whom the leaves of Greek scrollwork grow out of the surface while Islamic design works with it. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.39}
