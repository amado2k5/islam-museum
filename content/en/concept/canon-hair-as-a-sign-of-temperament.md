## Summary
Ibn Sina treats hair as a sign by speed of growth, abundance, thickness, form and colour, and gives the views of Aristotle and Galen on the cause of grey hair and the effect of lands and ages {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.159-160}.

## Speed, abundance and form
Hair is read by quickness or slowness of growth, abundance or scarcity, fineness or thickness, straightness or curliness, and colour. Slow or absent growth, with no signs of a lack of blood, shows a very moist temperament; when heat and dryness meet, hair grows very fast, thick and plentiful, as in the young and not in boys, whose matter is vaporous, not smoky. Curliness shows heat and dryness; straightness shows the opposites {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.159}.

## Colour and greying
By colour, black shows heat, ruddy-fair shows cold, yellow and red show balance, and white shows either moisture and cold, as in greying, or extreme dryness. For Aristotle the cause of greying is a change to the colour of phlegm; for Galen it is the mouldering that comes to food turning into hair when it is cold and slow to move through the pores. Ibn Sina finds the two views close in truth {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.160}.

## Lands and ages
Lands and airs affect hair and must be allowed for: fair hair is not to be expected of a Zanji, nor black hair of a Slav, as a sign of temperament. Age too: the young are like southerners, boys like northerners and the middle-aged like those between. Much hair in a boy shows his temperament turning toward black bile when he grows up, and in an old man that he is of black bile already {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.160}.
