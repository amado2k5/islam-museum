## Summary
A body's rising from the sun's rays is its appearing after having been hidden by them; it occurs for the stars and the wandering planets according to their distance from the sun, their speed and their latitude {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 41}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 40}.

## East and west
Al-Farghani calls a rising after concealment a "mashriq" — the planet seen in the east at dawn — and an evening setting a "maghrib" — seen in the west at dusk; a planet is concealed as it nears the sun {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 41}. For the three planets far from the sun — Saturn, Jupiter and Mars — each has a dusk setting and a dawn rising {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 41}.

## Venus, Mercury and the moon
Venus and Mercury move faster than the sun, so each has a dusk rising and setting and a dawn rising and setting, since they now precede the sun and now follow it {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 41}. The moon is the fastest of the planets and does not retrograde: it overtakes the sun and sets at dawn in the east, then passes it and rises at dusk in the west {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 40}.

## Duration of concealment
The length of concealment depends on the planet's size and latitude: a northern latitude shortens it, a southern lengthens it, and concealment lengthens for a planet far south of the zodiac {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 37}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 38}. Al-Farghani represents the planet far south of the zodiac by Suhayl (Canopus), which is hidden by the sun's light for about five months of the year at the start of the fourth clime {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 37}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 38}.

## Pages 201 to 388: rising and setting in the sun's rays
Al-Farghani shows that Saturn, Jupiter and Mars are slower than the sun: they are seen in the evening in the west as they approach it, and in the morning in the east after leaving its rays {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 221}. Venus and Mercury are faster than the sun, so each has an evening and a morning rising according to its position {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 221}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 222}.

The moon, he notes, precedes the sun by its speed and is seen in the morning in the east, then crosses the sun and is seen in the evening in the west {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 222}. The first crescent needs about twelve degrees of the equinoctial between it and the sun at setting, varying by sign — eighteen degrees in Libra and ten in Aries in the example given (OCR-doubtful) {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 227}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 228}.

He records that Venus, retrograding in Pisces, stays under the sun's rays for two days, and in Virgo for sixteen days — these figures' readings are doubtful {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 229}. He lists the degrees by which each planet stands from the sun at rising in the mean table; these are OCR readings not relied on before checking the image {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 230}.
