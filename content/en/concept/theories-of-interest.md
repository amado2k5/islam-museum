## Summary
In this chapter, the author presents economists' theories of interest and analyzes them to show what they justify and what they fail to justify in contrast with Islamic jurisprudence {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 270}.

## First Discussion: Arguments for Interest
The author presents the arguments of proponents of interest in production and consumption: that capital is productive, that the borrower pays interest willingly, that consumption loans reduce savings, and that interest regulates the market {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 265} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 266}. He replies that Islam recognizes the productivity of capital and grants it a share of profit, that mutual consent does not make forbidden things permissible, and that loans for necessary consumption are closer to charity {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 265} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 267}.

## A Statistical Experiment
Citing the French translation of Samuelson, the author notes that a Belgian study of investment returns between 1913 and 1939 found real returns to be negative in most investments, with the exception of agricultural land and hoarded gold {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 268}. He adds that weak saving in France following the gap between expected and actual outcomes led to financing investment through taxation {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 269}.

## Why Rent on a Machine Is Permissible While Rent on Money Is Not
The author distinguishes between a leased asset, which remains in the lessor's ownership and under their risk and thus entitles them to rent, and a loaned asset, which passes into the borrower's ownership and liability and is returned in kind; under the maxim "gain accompanies liability," one cannot combine liability with a fixed charge above that liability {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 269}. If capital remains in its owner's ownership and under their risk, it is entitled to a share of profit, as in mudarabah {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 269}.

## Theories of Interest
The author lists eleven theories and responds to each: the risk theory, the fructification or yield theory, the use theory, the productivity of capital theory, the time theory, the time-preference theory, the liquidity-preference theory, the reward-for-saving theory, the accumulated-labor theory, the scarcity theory, and the insurance theory {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 270} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 277}.

## The Author's Assessment
The author concludes that these theories may justify a return on capital, but they do not justify that the return must be fixed interest rather than a share of profit; they may, however, be useful to Muslims in explaining the reward for lending, the price increase in deferred sales, and the permissibility of profit-sharing {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 278}. He considers the time-preference theory the best of them, noting that jurists explicitly recognized the value of time long ago {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 279} (see [[concept/riba|Riba]] and [[concept/suftaja-in-islam|Suftajah]]).
