## Summary
In the first chapter on bones, Ibn Sina sorts the ways bones meet into three: the free joint, the difficult joint that is not secured, and the secured joint. The secured joint is divided into the socketed, the sutured and the glued {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.42-43}.

## The free joint
The free joint is one in which one of the two bones can make its movements easily without the other bone moving with it; the example is the wrist with the forearm. The editor adds the modern term in the margin {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.42}.

## The difficult joint
The difficult joint, not secured, is one where movement of one bone alone is hard and small in amount, such as the joint between the wrist and the metacarpus, or between two bones of the metacarpus {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.43}.

## The secured joint
In the secured joint neither bone can move alone at all. It has three kinds. The socketed joint has a projection on one bone and a hollow on the other in which the projection is fixed without moving, as teeth in their sockets. The sutured joint has notches and teeth on each bone, like a saw, fitted into the notches of the other, as coppersmiths join sheets of copper; it is called a suture, as in the joints of the skull bones. The glued joint is either lengthwise, like the joint between the two bones of the forearm, or crosswise, like the joint of the lower vertebrae of the spine {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.43}.
