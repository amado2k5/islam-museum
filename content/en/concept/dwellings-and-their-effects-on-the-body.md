## Summary
In the eleventh chapter, on the causes that lie in dwellings, Ibn Sina restates briefly, in another order, what he said of dwellings in the chapter on changes of the air, and does not mind repeating some of it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.126}.

## What makes dwellings differ
The states of dwellings differ in their effect on bodies by their height or lowness in themselves, by the mountains near them, by their soil, whether clayey, seeping, black-muddy or with the force of a mineral, by the abundance or scarcity of water, and by what lies near them, such as trees, mines, graveyards and carcasses {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.126-127}.

## The sign of fine air
He says that, in sum, any air that cools quickly when the sun sets and warms when it rises is fine, and what is opposite to it is the reverse. The worst airs are those that squeeze the heart and narrow the breath {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.127}.

## Knowing their temperaments
He notes that the temperaments of airs are known from the latitude, the soil, the neighbouring seas and mountains and the winds {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.127}.
