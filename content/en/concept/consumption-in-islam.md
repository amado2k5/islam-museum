## Summary
The author sets guidelines for consumption that begin with oneself, then one's family, then relatives, and then those in need; follow the order of necessities, needs, and embellishments; exclude forbidden things such as silk and gold for men and vessels of gold and silver; fall between stinginess and extravagance; and are determined by financial capacity {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 183} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 184}.

## Overview
In the author's view, consumption is part of the economic cycle, as income equals consumption plus saving {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 191}. A Muslim's expenses are subject to guidelines that safeguard their needs and prevent extravagance and stinginess.

## Order of Spending
A Muslim begins by meeting their own needs, then those of their family, then relatives, and then those in need, citing the hadith "Begin with yourself and give charity to it" {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 183}. What is consumed is ordered into necessities, needs, and embellishments, where embellishments go beyond needs to comfortable living without extravagance or luxury {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 183} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 184}.

## Capacity and What Is Forbidden
The level of consumption is determined by a person's financial capacity, and consumption may not include anything forbidden, such as silk and gold for men, vessels of gold and silver, or wine and pork {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 184}.

## Between Stinginess and Extravagance
Islam calls for the optimal limit, forbidding both stinginess and extravagance, and citing the Qur'anic verses and hadiths that prohibit extravagance {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 184} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 185}. Extravagance is defined as spending on what is forbidden, even a little, or on what is permissible beyond the proper limit; squandering is worse than it, and opulence is worse than squandering and is forbidden {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 185} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 186}.

## Relative Utility
The author quotes al-Shatibi that benefits and harms are usually relative—that is, something is beneficial in one situation and not another, and for one person and not another {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 187}. He also cites Ibn al-Jawzi that the enjoyment of food is proportional to the pain of hunger {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 187}.

## Proper Use
The author states that the law of consumption aligns with the law of subjugation, as every good has its proper use, citing the Prophet's prohibition against using riding animals as pulpits {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 188}. He relates the story of Mu'adhah al-'Anbariyyah in al-Jahiz, who made use of every part of the sacrificial animal, and regards it as an example of not wasting anything that can be used, so long as its benefit exceeds its cost {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 188} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 189}.
