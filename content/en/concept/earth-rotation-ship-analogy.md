## Summary
In astronomical works connected with the Maragha observatory, the ship sailing on a sea or river, carrying passengers who move about on it as on firm ground, became a standard example in discussing "accidental motion", and then the possibility of the earth's rotation about its axis. {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 25} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 26} Anton Heinen traces this debate to explain a variant in one copy of [[work/al-haya-al-saniyya-suyuti|al-Hay'a al-saniyya]] denying that the earth moves like a ship on the ocean. {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 24} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 25}

## From Aristotle to Maragha
Heinen traces the inquiry to Aristotle's analysis of motion in the Physics, where the ship concerns the cause of motion rather than its relativity; Muslim astronomers of the thirteenth to sixteenth centuries shifted it toward relative motion — the ship relative to its passengers or the shores. {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 26} He did not find it in [[person/nasir-al-din-al-tusi|Nasir al-Din al-Tusi]]'s Tadhkira, but did in his contemporary [[person/najm-al-din-al-katibi|Najm al-Din al-Katibi al-Qazwini]], who discussed the earth's motion in Hikmat al-ayn, and found its most thorough treatment in [[person/qutb-al-din-shirazi|Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi]]'s Nihayat al-idrak fi dirayat al-aflak. {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 26} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 27}

## Al-Sharif al-Jurjani
In his commentary on al-Tusi's Tadhkira [[person/al-sharif-al-jurjani-ali-ibn-muhammad|al-Sharif al-Jurjani]] (740–816 AH) repeatedly used the ship analogy, his arguments largely borrowed from Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi. {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 27} He reported the view of some ancients that the daily motion of the stars is due to the earth's rotation from west to east, then examined the two usual refutations — that a stone thrown upward would fall west of its starting point, and that birds and arrows would be affected — and judged them unsatisfactory, since the air might move congruously with the earth and carry with it what it contains. {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 28} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 29} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 30} He then reaffirmed the earth's rest, not on those grounds but because the earth is by nature the principle of straight-line attraction. {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 30} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 31} Heinen compares his falling-stone argument with the examples of "The Second Day" in Galileo's Dialogue. {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 30}

## Gars al-Din al-Halabi
A generation after al-Suyuti, [[person/gars-al-din-al-halabi|Gars al-Din ibn Ahmad al-Halabi]] (d. 971 AH) devoted a chapter of his [[work/tanbih-al-naqqad|Tanbih al-naqqad ala ma fi al-hay'a al-mashhura min al-fasad]] (958 AH) to the subject, defending the older view of the spheres' motion, while elsewhere in the book rejecting the equant and the eccentric deferent. {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 31} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 32}

## Significance
Heinen concludes that al-Suyuti's contemporaries were still open to new ideas, and that common notions of astronomy and geography could be shaken by sharper reasoning and better observation; against this, al-Suyuti's book reinstated the early views and won popularity. {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 33} This is his academic assessment.
