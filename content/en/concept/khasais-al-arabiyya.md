## Summary
This page gathers what [[person/ibn-faris|Ibn Faris]] wrote in al-Sahibi on the excellence and breadth of the language of the Arabs, what [[person/ibn-jinni|Ibn Jinni]] wrote in al-Khasa'is in defining language and nahw, what al-Suyuti relays of their views on the origin of language, and what the Encyclopaedia Britannica says of root and pattern. Every judgement is ascribed to its speaker, and the museum does not decide between the views.

## Ibn Faris's argument
In al-Sahibi Ibn Faris opens a chapter titled on the saying that the language of the Arabs is the most excellent of languages and the widest. Among his arguments are that God describes the Qur'an as in a clear Arabic tongue in [[passage/quran-26-195|Ash-Shu'ara' 195]] and the verses before it, and that He puts the teaching of bayan before the mention of the creations in [[passage/quran-55-3|Ar-Rahman 3]] and [[passage/quran-55-4|Ar-Rahman 4]]; for him, the other languages fall short of this {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.19}.

## The objection and his answer
He states the objection that bayan exists in other languages and answers that this is the lowest rank of bayan, since even the mute is understood by gesture, and that other languages are not as wide as Arabic. His examples: Persian names the sword by only one name where Arabic has many; the non-Arabs are not as broad in figurative speech, so no translator can render the Qur'an's figures as the Gospel was rendered from Syriac into Abyssinian and Greek, and he illustrates with a word in [[passage/quran-8-58|Al-Anfal 58]] that cannot be carried over without unpacking it into a long phrase. He cites Imru' al-Qays and proverbs as compression that cannot be translated, lists Arabic's devices of lightening, such as inversion, avoiding two consecutive sukun, assimilation, elision and the omission of verbs, and ends that God knows best where to place excellence {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.19} {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.20} {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.21}. This is his argument, and the museum does not decide between his view and others.

## Ibn Jinni on language and nahw
Ibn Jinni says in al-Khasa'is: "As for its definition, it is sounds by which every people express their aims", and adds that the word lugha is a fu'la from laghawtu, "I spoke", with plurals lughat and lughun, and that laghiya yalgha is also said of one who talked nonsense {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.34}. He defines nahw as following the manner of the Arabs' speech in its inflection and derivation, such as the dual, plural, diminutive, broken plural, annexation, relation and composition, so that one who is not of the Arabic-speaking people may reach their eloquence and speak it though not one of them; and he notes that the word nahw was a general verbal noun from nahawtu, "I aimed", later specialised, as fiqh and Bayt Allah were {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.35}. For the science itself see [[concept/ilm-al-nahw|the science of grammar]].

## The origin of language in the two men
Al-Suyuti relays that Ibn Faris said in al-Sahibi that the language of the Arabs is tawqif, citing a verse in proof, and says that all this is Ibn Faris's speech and that he was of Ahl al-Sunna {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.12}. He relays that Ibn Jinni says most people of reflection hold that the origin of language is convention and agreement, not revelation and tawqif, and that his teacher Abu Ali once told him it is from God {cite:khasais-najjar 1 p.41}. For the question in general see [[concept/asl-al-lugha|the origin of language]].

## Root and pattern
The Encyclopaedia Britannica says Arabic has a root-and-pattern system: the typical root is three consonants on which patterns are built, so that k, t and b with patterns give kitab, "book", and katib, "one who writes" {cite:britannica-arabic-language}. This is the observation of a modern reference, not a saying of Ibn Faris or Ibn Jinni. For tasrif among the early authors see [[concept/ilm-al-sarf|the science of morphology]].

## What this page does not reach
Of al-Sahibi only the chapter named above was read, not its other chapters, and of al-Khasa'is only the chapters on language, on nahw and on the origin of language. The arguments of those who differ from Ibn Faris on the excellence of Arabic are not set out here, nor the rest of what Ibn Jinni wrote in al-Khasa'is. The page gives no judgement on the soundness of either man's view.
