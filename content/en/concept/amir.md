## Origin of the term
Amir is basically an Islamic term for commander, governor and prince. The Qur'an has only the expression ulu l-amr (those invested with command), but amir occurs often in the traditions. In the early sources amil and amir are used as synonyms, and in the reports on the meeting of the sakifa amir denotes the head of the Muslim community. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 460}

## Under the Medinan caliphs and the Umayyads
In the caliphate of Medina army commanders, and occasionally commanders of divisions, were called amirs, and so were the governors, who were at first the conquering generals. The Umayyads began to separate administrative and financial duties, but for most of their period amirs had full administrative and financial powers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 460-461}

## The office
The amir organized the army and appointed the sarrafs, kept order through a prefect of police, and usually had a chamberlain and a bodyguard. He appointed a postmaster to report on subordinates, supervised the mint, led prayers, built mosques, and usually appointed the kadis. He took the bay'a for the caliph or the designated heir, and his salary and administrative allowances (amala) were paid from the province. When he left his province he appointed a khalifa to represent him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 461}
