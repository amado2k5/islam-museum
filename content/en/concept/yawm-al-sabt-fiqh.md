Yawm al-sabt is a compound of day and sabt. Sabt lexically means rest, ceasing, an age, and a day of the week; the sabbath of the Jews is their ceasing from work and gain, and surah al-A'raf (verse 163) mentions their fish coming to them on their sabbath. The jurists use the word in its lexical sense. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.18022}

On fasting it: if a faster singles out Saturday, the jurists differ. The Hanafis, Malikis, Shafi'is and Hanbalis on the school view dislike singling it out, on the hadith of Abd Allah ibn Busr from his sister al-Summa, "do not fast Saturday except in what is prescribed for you" — Ibn Hajar transmits from al-Nasa'i that it is a disturbed hadith. The Hanafis add that it is prohibitively disliked when the faster means to imitate the Jews, and the Hanafis, Hanbalis and Shafi'is state it is not disliked if it falls on a day he used to fast. Ibn Taymiyya held fasting it alone is not disliked, and al-Mardawi noted that al-Ajurri recorded dislike only for Friday's fast, so apparently nothing else is disliked. Most of those who dislike it hold that joining another day before or after removes the dislike; Ibn Abidin transmits Hanafi hesitation over whether joining Sunday does so, since each day is honoured by one community of the People of the Book, but he preferred that fasting both carries no imitation, as no community honours the two together. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.18023} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.18024}

On its bearing on Jews: the Hanbalis state a Muslim does not err in pressing his Jewish wife to break her sabbath where his right is due, while the Malikis state a Muslim may not compel his scriptuary wife to what is unlawful to her in her religion. The Hanafis state that a Jew who learns his partner sold his share of their common property on his sabbath and does not claim pre-emption for that reason loses his right, his sabbath no longer being an excuse. If a Jew's adversary asks the judge to bring him to court on his sabbath, the judge obliges him to come and his sabbath is no excuse — the stated view of the Hanafis and Shafi'is, a view of the Malikis and one of the two Hanbali faces; the other Maliki view dislikes obliging him, since they were confirmed by the jizya on honouring their sabbath, and the other Hanbali face bars bringing him since its prohibition on him stands. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.18024}

On hardening the oaths of the protected people: the Hanbalis state they are sworn in at the times they honour, Saturday and Sunday; the Shafi'is state in the chapter of li'an that hardening an unbeliever's oath by time is measured by the noblest times in their view. The Malikis have two positions on letting a Muslim administer an oath to a Jew on Saturday — the first is al-Qabisi's; some confine the dispute to the Jew since the Christian honours no day, while Ibn At made it general to both; and whoever does not allow it has no oath-hardening on that day. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.18024} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.18025}

On cupping: the jurists differ on cupping on Saturday. The Malikis, and Ahmad in one narration, see no harm in it; in another narration Ahmad dislikes cupping on Saturday, and Ibn Muflih says this means without need. The Fatawa Hindiyya has it that cupping after mid-month on Saturday is good and very beneficial, and disliked before mid-month. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.18025}

On visiting the sick: the Hanafis, Shafi'is and Malikis state visiting the sick on Saturday is not disliked, on the report that the Prophet used to check on the people of Quba on Friday, ask about the absent, be told one was ill, and go on Saturday to visit him. They also state that abandoning the visit on Saturday is an innovation without basis, except that the Hanafis and Shafi'is add that if a sick person takes the Saturday visit as an ill omen and it harms him, the visit is not repeated on that day, as it would hurt him and worsen his illness. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.18025}

On obliging a hired Jew to work on his sabbath: the Shafi'is and Hanbalis state that one who hires himself for a known term spanning sabbaths is not bound to work them if he excepted them; if he did not, some — among them al-Ghazali — hold that when their custom of not working is constant, the unqualified contract counts as an explicit exception, read on the custom of both hirer and hired; a group including the judge Abu Bakr al-Shami hold he is compelled to work since the governing law is ours. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.18025} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.18026}

On visiting the mosque of Quba: the jurists recommend a Muslim visit it on Saturday and pray two rak'as in it, following the Prophet, who is reported to have come to it every Saturday, riding and walking, praying two rak'as; it is also reported that prayer in it is like an umra, and it is recommended to visit afterward the well of Aris, into which the Prophet spat, and to make ablution and drink from its water. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.18026}
