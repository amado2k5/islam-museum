## Overview
The *isnad* (chain of transmission) is the series of narrators leading to the text of a hadith, in the form "so-and-so related to us from so-and-so," until the report ends at the Prophet or a Companion. {cite:sahih-muslim} The Encyclopaedia of Islam entry on the term calls it a chain of authorities and an essential part of the transmission of a tradition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 220} Its article on hadith names two features of a tradition, the chain of authorities, isnad or sanad, going back to the source, and the text, matn. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33}

## Its function
By it the scholars of hadith knew the soundness of a report from its weakness. They studied the narrators for their probity and precision, and they looked into whether the chain was connected or broken. {cite:sahih-muslim}

## In the introduction to Sahih Muslim
Muslim opened his *Sahih* by speaking of the greatness of the isnad and of its protection from falsehood. It has been said that it is one of the distinctions of this community. {cite:sahih-muslim}

## Origins and growth
The Encyclopaedia of Islam says that little need for an isnad was felt in the earliest times, but that as the first century of Islam advanced the need to state one's authority developed. It adds that the collections of traditions compiled mainly from the 3rd/9th century onwards give complete isnads. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 220} Its article on hadith says that some men made notes for their own guidance quite early, among them [[person/urwa-ibn-al-zubayr|Urwa ibn al-Zubayr]], who died in 94/712 or 99/717 and is quoted as transmitting many traditions from his aunt [[person/aisha|A'isha]], and [[person/muhammad-ibn-shihab-al-zuhri|al-Zuhri]], who died in 124/741, settled in Syria and was one of the most widely quoted authorities. It also mentions written leaves in which some Companions collected traditions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33} Among the classified collections that later took precedence, the article names the Sahihs of [[person/al-bukhari|al-Bukhari]] and [[person/muslim-ibn-al-hajjaj|Muslim]] as the most authoritative, followed by the Sunan works of [[person/abu-dawud-al-sijistani|Abu Dawud]], [[person/al-tirmidhi-muhammad-ibn-isa|al-Tirmidhi]], [[person/al-nasai|al-Nasa'i]] and [[person/ibn-majah|Ibn Maja]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33}

## Fabrication and the care taken over chains
The article says that before the recognised books were compiled the body of tradition had grown enormously, and serious students recognised that much of it was fabricated. It describes storytellers who invented extraordinary traditions and attached seemingly impeccable isnads to them, to astonish ordinary people and be paid, and says their falseness was easily seen. It describes others who fabricated traditions to spread false doctrines, sometimes so cleverly that detection was unlikely, and pious men who invented traditions to urge righteous living. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 34} As an example of the second kind it gives a saying in which the Prophet is made to speak of no prophet after him unless God wills; the article says the phrase could easily pass unnoticed, but men of insight noticed the heretical tendency in spite of the excellent isnad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33} It reports that some who had invented traditions later confessed, though their material had already entered books. Transmitters were also criticised for carelessness, for inaccuracy in old age, and for pretending to transmit after losing their books and relying on a faulty memory; the article says that [[person/al-hakim-al-nisaburi|al-Hakim]], who died in 405/1014, accused some of tracing back to the Prophet traditions that went back only to Companions or Successors. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 34}

## Biographical works on narrators
The article says that the effort to test the genuineness of traditions led to biographical works on the people who appear in isnads. It explains that their birth and death years mattered, since these show whether they could have met the people they are said to have quoted. Statements on their trustworthiness were also recorded, but, the article notes, these were frequently contradictory. It says the books were mainly compiled from the third century onwards, although the material is said to have been collected from the first, and it reasons that, since the Arabs were genealogists, the materials for the earliest periods may well have been available. Some of these works, called rijal works, were confined to a district, a school or a century, and the article refers readers to [[concept/jarh-wa-tadil|the criticism and vindication of narrators]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 34}

## Grades of reports
The article says that traditions were divided into sahih, sound, hasan, good, and da'if or saqim, weak. It lists seven grades of [[concept/al-hadith-al-sahih|sound reports]]: those given by both al-Bukhari and Muslim, those given by al-Bukhari alone, those given by Muslim alone, those given by neither but fulfilling the conditions of both, those fulfilling al-Bukhari's conditions, those fulfilling Muslim's conditions, and those sound in the opinion of other authorities. [[concept/al-hadith-al-hasan|Good reports]] are, it says, not considered quite so strong, but are necessary for establishing points, and most legal traditions are of this type. Weak traditions also have degrees; the article says allowance is made for using them on exhortation, stories and good behaviour, but not on matters of law or on what is allowed or forbidden. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 34}

## Terms describing the chain
The article says that most of the technical terms, mainly dealing with the isnad, acquired a stable meaning, though not all authorities agreed on some of them, and it arranges them in groups. Regarding the number of transmitters, a [[concept/al-hadith-al-mutawatir|mutawatir]] report has so many transmitters that collusion could not be supposed; a [[concept/al-hadith-al-mashhur|mashhur]] report has more than two; an [[concept/al-hadith-al-aziz|aziz]] report comes from one man of sufficient authority when two or three share in transmitting it; a [[concept/al-hadith-al-gharib|gharib]] report comes from only one Companion, or a single man at a later stage; and a [[concept/al-hadith-al-shadhdh|shadhdh]] report comes from a single authority and differs from what others report, being rejected if it differs from what people of greater authority transmit. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 34} Regarding the nature of the chain, muttasil is an unbroken isnad traced back to the source, muttasil marfu' if it reaches the Prophet and muttasil mawquf if it reaches a Companion; musnad is generally a report with a fully connected isnad to the Prophet; marfu' is a report traced to the Prophet whether or not the isnad is complete; mawquf goes back only to a Companion; and maqtu' goes back to a Successor, regarding his words or deeds. The article adds that the word [[concept/munqati|munqati]] has been used of an isnad including unspecified people, or of one in which a person later than a Successor claims to have heard someone he did not hear. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 34} Among the terms for accepted reports, makbul is a report that fulfils the requirements and is sahih or hasan; among those for rejected reports, munkar is one whose transmitter is alone in transmitting it and differs from a reliable one, matruk comes from a transmitter suspected of falsehood in tradition, and mawdu, fictitious, is the worst type of all. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 35}

## Receiving and wording a transmission
The article describes the study of tradition as the sciences of hadith. It names [[person/al-ramahurmuzi|al-Ramahurmuzi]], who died in 360/971, as the first to attempt a comprehensive work, and says al-Hakim wrote a more systematic one in 52 categories, and that the classical work of [[person/ibn-al-salah|Ibn al-Salah]], who died in 643/1245, has 65 categories. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 36} Following Ibn al-Salah, it lists seven recognised ways of receiving traditions: hearing from the shaykh's mouth, considered superior to the others; reciting to the shaykh; licence, ijaza; [[concept/munawala|handing over]], munawala; correspondence; bequest; and a find, when one comes upon a shaykh's handwriting and explains how one got it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 36} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 37} It says some of these methods were used in the 3rd/9th century and possibly earlier, and that al-Bukhari and al-Tirmidhi mention some of them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 37}

The article also discusses the words used in transmission: haddathani, he told me; haddathana, he told us; akhbarani, he informed me; akhbarana, he informed us; samitu, I heard; anba'ani, he announced to me; and 'an, on the authority of. It says that views of the 2nd/8th and 3rd/9th centuries suggest no general agreement on their relative importance, and that al-Hakim is more precise: haddathani when one hears a shaykh with no others present, haddathana when others are present, akhbarani when one reads to a shaykh alone, akhbarana when one hears another reading, and anba'ani when one submits traditions and is given a spoken licence. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 37} It says that 'an is generally agreed to be allowable when the transmitter is reliable, and that [[person/al-khatib-al-baghdadi-ahmad|al-Khatib al-Baghdadi]], who died in 463/1071, justifies it especially when the isnad is long, since it avoids tedious details. Under the strictest rules, it adds, one must write on one's manuscript the name of the shaykh, how and when the traditions were received, and the line of authorities through whom he heard them, so that a whole collection as well as each tradition has an isnad. The article says that printing has largely made this unnecessary, though manuscripts always give details of the transmission. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 37}

## The weight of the chain
The article says it is often said that the validity of a tradition depends not on its text but on its isnad, and that this is generally true but not the whole story. It reports that al-Hakim mentions some traditions with very reliable men in the isnad that he holds to be faulty and weak, and that he argues that detecting this requires considerable knowledge and discussion with people learned in the subject. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 37}

## Academic views on isnads
The article states that Western scholars have tended to argue that the criticism of traditions did not go far enough. It says that [[person/ignaz-goldziher|Goldziher]] showed that hadith rests on less firm ground than the conventional doctrine supposes, and it lists features that Western scholars point to: phrases from the Old and New Testaments put into the Prophet's mouth, references to towns far from Arabia and to parties of the early Muslim period, references to the Umayyads and the Abbasids, and many miracles attributed to the Prophet. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 35} It also reports that J. Schacht argued that isnads grew as time passed, so that legal traditions belonging to a later period were eventually traced back to the Prophet. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 35} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 36} The article's author does not think the whole body of tradition can be explained away on these lines, but judges that much later material has been attributed to the Prophet, which makes it difficult to find a satisfactory criterion for what is genuine, and he adds that one must be wary of accusing men like [[person/abu-hurayra|Abu Hurayra]] of inventing many traditions. These are the author's judgements, set beside the classical hadith-science account above, and the museum gives no verdict between them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 36}

## In Abu Ishaq al-Heweny's «Difaf al-Kawthar»
At a board, [[person/abu-ishaq-al-heweny|Abu Ishaq al-Heweny]] explains concealment (tadlis) in the chain of transmission. He says tadlis in the language means darkness, or the mixing of light and darkness, and technically a defect in the chain that the narrator hides, which the critic, had he seen it, would have used to weaken the hadith. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:02:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:04:00} He lists six kinds: tadlis of the chain, tadlis of levelling (taswiya, also called tajwid), tadlis of teachers' names, tadlis of places, tadlis by cutting off, and tadlis by conjunction; and he estimates that tadlis of the chain alone makes up about ninety-five per cent of what occurs; this is his estimate. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:05:00} He defines it as a narrator relating from a teacher he did hear from something he did not hear from him. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:06:00}

To explain it he sets out high and low chains: a high chain ('uluww) has fewer intermediaries between the narrator and the Prophet, and the fewer the men the nobler the chain; he compares the Prophet to a light-giving lamp whose light grows stronger the nearer one climbs. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:11:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:12:00} He gives the example of Malik from Nafi' from Ibn Umar, two men between Malik and the Prophet; al-Shafi'i from Malik, three; Ahmad from al-Shafi'i, four; al-Bukhari from Ahmad, five; and he says hadith scholars would sell their houses and borrow to travel to the ends of the earth for a higher chain. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:12:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:14:00} Tadlis, he says, has one of two motives: to keep the chain high, or, worse, because the dropped narrator is weak and naming him would get the report rejected; the concealer says "from" (an) and not "he told me", for if he said "he told me" of what he did not hear he would be a liar. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:16:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:17:00}

His board example is a hadith on the merit of prayer with the tooth-stick (siwak) that [[person/ibn-ishaq|Muhammad ibn Ishaq]], author of the Sira, narrates from Mu'awiya ibn Yahya al-Sadafi from al-Zuhri from [[person/urwa-ibn-al-zubayr|Urwa]] from [[person/aisha|A'isha]]: Ibn Ishaq drops al-Sadafi and says "from al-Zuhri". {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:20:30} He says Urwa is one of the Seven Jurists, al-Zuhri trustworthy, eminent and an imam, and Ibn Ishaq truthful and fair in hadith, a man the scholars differed over and whom he himself judges usable as proof; {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:21:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:22:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:22:30} al-Sadafi, by contrast, is abandoned (matruk), very weak, so the critic takes the chain for good when it is worthless. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:25:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:26:00} His rule is that a concealer's report is not accepted when he says "from", and is accepted when he states hearing explicitly: "he told me", "he informed me", "I heard". {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:27:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep13 00:28:00}
