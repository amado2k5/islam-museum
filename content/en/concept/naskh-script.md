## Summary
Naskh is one of the [[concept/al-aqlam-al-sitta|six pens]] canonised by [[person/ibn-muqla|Ibn Muqla]], paired as the small partner of [[concept/thuluth-script|thuluth]]; for Maryam Ekhtiar it was ideal for ordinary books and small Qur'ans. {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 25} She also lists naskh among the chancery scripts of some periods. {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 26}

## Ibn al-Bawwab and the Ottomans
The only surviving work in [[person/ibn-al-bawwab|Ibn al-Bawwab]]'s hand is a small Qur'an in naskh copied at Baghdad in 1000–1001, noted for compact spacing and a dense page. {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 26} In the Ottoman period [[person/shaykh-hamdullah|Shaykh Hamdullah]] is credited with reforms of naskh that lasted; in an album in his hand of about 1500, a large thuluth above is paired with a minute naskh (Turkish nesih) below, with vowels carefully marked for legibility. {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 42}

## Revival naskh
"Revival naskh" is, for the author, a later form of naskh known for its large proportions, elegance and clarity, codified by [[person/ahmad-nayrizi-calligrapher|Ahmad Nayrizi]] (active 1682–1739) in Iran to make naskh larger and more readable, with well-spaced lines and bold, vocalised letters. It was used mainly for Qur'ans and prayer books, part of a movement in later seventeenth-century Iran to make Arabic religious texts more accessible to Persian speakers, which is why Nayrizi's Qur'ans often carry interlinear Persian translations. The poet-calligrapher Muhammad Shafi' Visal-i Shirazi and his sons carried the style into the nineteenth century. {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 29} {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 30}
