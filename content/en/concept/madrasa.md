## Overview
The historic Islamic institution of education. It arose in Khurasan and spread through the endowment system, teaching jurisprudence, creed and the sciences down to today. {cite:wikidata}

## Origins and arrival in Egypt in Yeomans
Yeomans writes that madrasas began in eastern Persia as small informal establishments attached to the houses of teachers and Sufi shaykhs; when upholding Sunni orthodoxy became a political necessity, state-funded madrasas followed, the first being the Nizamiyya founded by the Seljuk vizier Nizam al-Mulk in Baghdad in 1068, which became the model for later ones. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.56} He sees the rise of the Fatimids and the success of their mission as the spur for Sunnis to build them. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.55} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.56}

In Egypt, after Sunni Islam was restored, ten madrasas were founded under Salah al-Din and twenty-five in the whole Ayyubid period, mostly Shafi'i with some Hanafi and Maliki, their sites chosen for religious, political and economic reasons, as Neil MacKenzie notes. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.111} Syrian madrasas of the age were usually small and devoted to one school, often holding the founder's tomb, like the Nuriyya, Aziziyya and Adiliyya in Damascus; the first tomb in Cairo to form part of a madrasa complex, after al-Shafi'i's, was al-Salih's at the [[building/al-madrasa-al-salihiyya-cairo|Salihiyya]], also the first madrasa in Egypt for all four schools and the earliest surviving madrasa with an [[concept/iwan|iwan]] courtyard. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.115} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.116} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.118}
