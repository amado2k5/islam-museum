## Summary
al-Sallabi presents the Twelver doctrine of the twelve divinely designated Imams, outlines the arguments they cite from the Quran and the Sunnah, responds to them from the Sunni perspective, and cites Shi'i books themselves to show that limiting the Imams to a specific number emerged later and that Shi'i opinions on the matter varied. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 260}

## The List as Cited by the Author
The author states that the Twelvers believe the Prophet designated the Imams by name, and that there are twelve of them, neither fewer nor more: Ali ibn Abi Talib, al-Hasan, al-Husayn, Ali Zayn al-'Abidin, Muhammad al-Baqir, Ja'far al-Sadiq, Musa al-Kazim, Ali al-Rida, Muhammad al-Jawad, Ali al-Hadi, al-Hasan al-'Askari, and Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Mahdi. He also gives the years of their deaths as recorded in his book. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 260} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 261}

## Origin of the Testamentary Designation According to the Author
The author states that the claim of a testamentary designation of the caliphate for Ali began with Abd Allah ibn Saba', as mentioned by al-Nawbakhti and al-Kashshi, and that some extremists later extended it to his descendants. He holds that the early Shi'ah operated in secrecy and that those to whom the matter was attributed among the Prophet's household disavowed it. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 261}

## Response to the Testamentary Designation and Explicit Appointment
The author presents Sunni reports denying any testamentary designation: A'ishah said that the Prophet died in her lap and did not appoint Ali; Ali said to al-Abbas that if they asked the Prophet and he refused them, the people would never give it to them afterward; and the Ansar at the Saqifah did not mention any explicit appointment, saying instead, "From us an emir and from you an emir." He also cites Ali's statement after the Battle of the Camel that the Prophet had not entrusted them with anything regarding leadership, so they chose Abu Bakr as caliph. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 262} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 263}

The author cites Ibn Taymiyyah's statement that the report of Ali's explicit appointment is not found in any of the reliable books of the scholars of Hadith, and Ibn Hazm's statement that he found no report of this appointment among the scholars except one transmitted from an unknown narrator. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 263}

## Where the Restriction of the Imams to a Specific Number Came From
The author reports from Shi'i sources that Shaytan al-Taq (Mu'min al-Taq) was the first to spread the idea of restricting the imamate to specific individuals from the Prophet's household, that Zayd ibn Ali denounced him for this, and that Hisham ibn al-Hakam joined him in promoting it in Kufa. He quotes Muhibb al-Din al-Khatib as saying that, in his view, Shaytan al-Taq was the first to invent this doctrine. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 264}

The author cites books on sects (al-Ash'ari, al-Nawbakhti, and al-Qummi) to show that the Imamiyyah differed over the number of Imams—between five, seven, eight, twelve, and thirteen—and that belief in twelve only became known after the death of al-Hasan al-'Askari. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 264} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 265}

## Arguments from the Quran and the Sunnah According to the Author
The author presents the argument based on the Verse of Wilayah (al-Ma'idah 55), claiming it was revealed about Ali when he gave his ring in charity while bowing in prayer, and responds that according to Ibn Kathir the report is completely unsound, that al-Tha'labi is alone in transmitting it, and that the authentic occasion of revelation concerns Banu Qaynuqa', Ubadah ibn al-Samit, and Abd Allah ibn Ubayy. He also presents his responses regarding the word "wali": that it means affection and support, and that Ali was not among those obligated to pay zakat on silver. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 268} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 269} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 270} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 271}

The author presents their argument based on the Verse of Mubahalah (Al 'Imran 61), in which they claim "ourselves" refers to Ali, and argues that the Arabs refer to a paternal cousin as one's own self, that the Prophet called the people closest to him, and that the apparent context does not indicate the imamate. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 272} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 273} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 274}

The author presents their argument based on the Verse of Affection for Kin (al-Shura 23), noting that the surah is Meccan whereas Fatimah and Ali married after the Battle of Badr, and that Ibn Abbas interpreted "kin" as the Prophet's kinship ties within Quraysh, not affection for the Prophet's household {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 275} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 276}.

The author presents the Hadith of Ghadir and the Hadith of the Two Weighty Things (al-Thaqalayn), to which he has devoted a separate entry under Ghadir, and notes here that the author holds that the Hadith of the Two Weighty Things does not prove the imamate of the Twelve, and that the Prophet's family will not unite upon misguidance in a general sense rather than in a specific number {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 280} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 281}.

## The Hadith of the "Twelve Caliphs"
The author cites the hadith of Jabir ibn Samurah and the hadith in Muslim, "The affairs of the people will continue to be conducted as long as twelve men rule over them," as well as the hadith, "There will be twelve emirs, all of them from Quraysh." The Twelvers read the hadith as proof of their number, while the author holds that it describes an era of strength and dignity for Islam, and that Ibn Taymiyyah counted these as Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, Ali, Mu'awiyah, Yazid, Abd al-Malik and his sons, including Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz; the descriptions do not apply to those whom the Twelvers claim as their Imams, since among them only Ali and, for a brief period, al-Hasan held the caliphate. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 266} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 267}

## The Imamate Above Prophethood Among Some Shi'ah
The author mentions three Shi'i sects that ranked the prophets above the Imams, as presented by al-Ash'ari, and a fourth according to al-Mufid, and notes that the school later settled on ranking the Imams above all prophets except the Messengers of Firm Resolve, as he quotes from al-Majlisi. He presents the Sunni response that the Quran chooses the prophets above all peoples of the world, and that scholarly consensus on the superiority of prophets over saints is binding proof. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 313} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 314}

## What Is Not Mentioned Here
The museum does not display here the hadiths that the author describes as fabricated, such as the Hadith of the Bird and the hadith "I am the city of knowledge and Ali is its gate," both of which are mentioned in their proper place in the book. Likewise, Shi'i reports regarding the Imams' knowledge of the unseen are not presented here, as they are a subject of dispute requiring direct consultation of Shi'i sources.
