## Summary
In the fourteenth chapter Ibn Sina distinguishes the pulse of one bathing in hot water from that of one bathing in cold, and notes the effect of drying and heating bath waters {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.180-181}.

## Hot water
Bathing in hot water at first brings the rulings of power and need, and when it dissolves to excess it weakens the pulse. Galen says the pulse is then small, slow and widely spaced. Ibn Sina comments that weakening and smallness are certain, but that when hot water warms the inside of the body with its accidental heat it may not last, nature's own demand, cooling, overcoming it, or it may last and cling; if the accidental quality prevails the pulse is quick and frequent, if the nature prevails it is slow and widely spaced, and when the accidental heating dissolves power so far that fainting is near, the pulse is again slow and widely spaced {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.180}.

## Cold water and the waters of the baths
Bathing in cold water, if its cold sinks deep, weakens and shrinks the pulse and brings wide spacing and slowness; if it does not sink but gathers the heat, power rises, the pulse grows slightly greater and speed and frequency fall. Of the waters in the baths, the drying ones make the pulse harder and lessen its greatness, and the heating ones make it quicker unless they dissolve power {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.180-181}.
