## Summary
This page sets out the science of rhetoric as three places read describe it: al-Khatib al-Qazwini's division of the sciences of eloquence in al-Idah into ma'ani, bayan and badi'; al-Sakkaki's two definitions in Miftah al-Ulum; [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]]'s account of these branches and their history; and what Muhammad Abd al-Mun'im Khafaji, the commentator on al-Idah, says of their rise. The three sciences have pages of their own, [[concept/ilm-al-maani|the science of ma'ani]], [[concept/ilm-al-bayan|the science of bayan]] and [[concept/ilm-al-badi|the science of badi']], and every statement here is ascribed to its author as it stands in the pages read.

## The eloquence of speech and of the speaker
[[person/jalal-al-din-al-qazwini|al-Khatib al-Qazwini]] begins by distinguishing fasaha from balagha. Balagha of speech, for him, is its matching the demand of the situation together with its fasaha. His commentator explains that the situation is what moves the speaker to put the speech in a particular form: a hearer's denial of a statement is a situation that calls for emphasis, and emphasis is what the situation demands; if the situation calls for plain statement, the speech comes without emphasis {cite:idah-khafaji-dar-al-jil 1 p.41}. Balagha of the speaker is a faculty by which he can compose eloquent speech. Al-Qazwini draws two conclusions: every eloquent thing, whether speech or speaker, is fasih, but not everything fasih is eloquent, because speech can be fasih without matching the situation {cite:idah-khafaji-dar-al-jil 1 p.49}.

## Three sciences
Al-Qazwini divides what balagha covers by what it guards against. What guards against error in conveying the intended meaning is ma'ani; what guards against obscurity of meaning is bayan; and what teaches the ways of embellishing speech, once matching the situation and fasaha are observed, is badi' {cite:idah-khafaji-dar-al-jil 1 p.50}. He notes that the first two are together called the science of balagha because of their closer link with it, that many people call all three bayan, and that some call the first ma'ani, the second and third bayan, and the three together badi' {cite:idah-khafaji-dar-al-jil 1 p.50} {cite:idah-khafaji-dar-al-jil 1 p.51}. His book follows this plan: an introduction, three arts and a conclusion, the first art guarding against error in conveying the meaning, the second against obscurity of meaning, and the third guarding against nothing but only embellishing the wording {cite:idah-khafaji-dar-al-jil 1 p.17}.

## al-Sakkaki and the two definitions
In the third part of Miftah al-Ulum, which al-Qazwini epitomised, [[person/al-sakkaki|al-Sakkaki]] defines ma'ani as the following up of the properties of speech structures in conveying meaning and what goes with it of beautifying, so as to guard by knowing them against error in fitting speech to what the situation demands; and he defines bayan as knowing how to bring one meaning in different ways, more or less clear in what they indicate {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.161} {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.162}. He holds that whoever interprets the divine speech needs both, and says woe to him who undertakes tafsir while on foot in them, that is, without a mount {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.162}. Al-Qazwini objects to this definition: following-up, he says, is not a science, and knowing the structures of the eloquent depends on knowing balagha itself, so the definition runs in a circle {cite:idah-khafaji-dar-al-jil 1 p.54}.

## Ibn Khaldun: three kinds, and a history
Ibn Khaldun says bayan arose in the community after the science of Arabic and of language, and is a science of the tongue since it concerns words and what they convey. He divides it in three: one kind in which wording matches every demand of the situation, called the science of balagha; one that looks at indication of the necessary and what necessitates it, as in metaphor and metonymy, called the science of bayan; and a third that was attached to them, the adorning of speech by saj', jinas, tarsi' or tawriya, called the science of badi' {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.761}. He says Ja'far ibn Yahya, al-Jahiz and Qudama wrote on it in dictated notes that fell short, that al-Sakkaki then refined its questions in the Miftah, and that later scholars epitomised him, among them Jalal al-Din al-Qazwini in al-Idah and al-Talkhis {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.761}. He holds that the easterners are stronger in this art than the westerners, and that the westerners specialised in badi' and wrote on it, naming Ibn Rashiq and his al-Umda {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.762}.

## The beginnings of writing, in Khafaji
In the introduction to his commentary Khafaji traces the start of writing on the subject: [[person/ibn-al-mutazz|Ibn al-Mu'tazz]] in al-Badi', Tha'lab in Qawa'id al-Shi'r, Qudama ibn Ja'far in his criticism of poetry and then of prose, Abu Hilal al-Askari in al-Sina'atayn, then [[person/abd-al-qahir-al-jurjani|Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani]] in two books: in [[work/asrar-al-balagha|Asrar al-Balagha]] the studies of bayan, simile, trope and metaphor, and in [[work/dalail-al-ijaz-al-jurjani|Dala'il al-I'jaz]] the roots of ma'ani; then al-Zamakhshari in his commentary {cite:idah-khafaji-dar-al-jil 1 p.7}. The theory of nazm that al-Jurjani sets out in the Dala'il, that nazm is only placing one's speech as the science of grammar requires {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992 1 p.81}, has its own page, [[concept/nazariyyat-al-ijaz-wal-nazm|the theory of i'jaz and nazm]].

## The fruit of the science, in Ibn Khaldun
Ibn Khaldun says the fruit of this art is in understanding the i'jaz of the Qur'an, because its i'jaz lies in the indication's meeting every demand of the situation, and that whoever grasps some of it is one with a taste gained by living with the Arabic tongue. The exegetes need it most, he says, and al-Zamakhshari followed the verses by the rules of this art; he adds a reservation that al-Zamakhshari's commentary, in his view, supports the doctrines of the people of innovation, and for that reason many avoid it {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.762}. The museum sets this statement down as his and passes no judgement on it. I'jaz in the commentaries has its page, [[concept/al-ijaz-al-bayani-inda-al-mufassirin|the rhetorical inimitability in the exegetes]], and the general page is [[concept/ijaz-al-quran|I'jaz al-Qur'an]].

## Does the Qur'an's eloquence vary?
In a note Khafaji reports that rhetoricians hold the Qur'an to vary in its eloquence, because a situation may call for ten particulars and the text gives five of them out of regard for human capacity. He differs, holding that its eloquence is one: matching the hearer's situation is complete eloquence, and what counts is quality, not quantity {cite:idah-khafaji-dar-al-jil 1 p.48}. This page ascribes both views to their holders and does not decide between them.

## What this page does not reach
Pages of al-Idah were read, but the copy read does not carry the sections on bayan and badi', so al-Qazwini was not read in the chapters of those two sciences. Not read: al-Talkhis, the Tibyan that Ibn Khaldun names, 'Arus al-Afrah, al-Sa'd's commentary, or the section on fasaha in the Miftah. Asrar al-Balagha itself was not read. What Khafaji wrote is a late commentary, not al-Qazwini's own text, and the citations keep the two apart.
