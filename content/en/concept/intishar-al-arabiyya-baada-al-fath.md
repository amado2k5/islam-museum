## Summary
What a research summary supplied to the museum says about how Arabic spread beyond the Arabian Peninsula after the 7th century: through military conquest, administrative reform, urban settlement and later tribal migrations. This page is the first doorway to four: this one, [[concept/taarib-al-dawawin|The Arabisation of the dīwāns]], [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-al-andalus-wa-siqilliyya|Arabic in al-Andalus and Sicily]] and [[concept/taarib-al-maghrib|The Arabisation of the Maghrib]]. The museum did not open the pages the summary names, and the founder approved using them as supplied on 21 November 2026.

## What this page is
This page gives the general picture before the detail: how Arabic passed from the tongue of communities in the Peninsula to a tongue of administration, learning and daily life in regions that spoke Greek, Aramaic, Coptic, Persian, Berber and African Latin. It relays what the summary says, ascribed to its pages, and it distinguishes what Wikipedia said, what a university source (a lecture of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology) said, what a chapter by Fred Donner hosted on the Almuslih site said, and what the Encyclopaedia Britannica said on its Maghrib page, which the museum read itself. The museum passes no judgement here on "conquest" or on "Arabisation"; it relays statements and names their holders.

## The conquest period, 7th and 8th centuries
The summary says that after the death of Muhammad in 632 CE the Rashidun and Umayyad Caliphates expanded from the Peninsula across the Levant, Egypt, Iraq, Persia, North Africa, Iberia and Central Asia, creating one of the largest empires in history {cite:wikipedia-early-muslim-conquests} {cite:wikipedia-arabization}. This is the ground over which the museum follows the pages on Arabic; for the place of Arabic today in parts of it see [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-al-alam-al-yawm|Arabic in the world today]]. Wikipedia supports names, dates and places only here; the description of the empire's extent is ascribed to the summary.

## The garrison towns (amsar)
The summary names towns founded by the first Arab armies: Basra and Kufa in Iraq, al-Jabiya in Syria, Fustat in Egypt and Kairouan in Tunisia, and says they became major administrative and cultural centres where Arabic-speaking settlers interacted with local populations {cite:wikipedia-amsar} {cite:mit-civic-architecture-in-islamic-history-l2}. The museum has pages for the places: [[place/basra|Basra]], [[place/kufa|Kufa]], [[place/al-fustat|Fustat]] and [[place/qayrawan|Kairouan]]. The page notes that these towns are the place of the linguistic encounter, and gives no count of their inhabitants or languages, since the summary gives no number.

## A gradual shift, not compulsion
The summary says that in the earliest decades the Arab ruling elite formed a largely separate aristocracy and did not force a change of language, and that Arabic spread over the following centuries through conversion to Islam, intermarriage, trade and the prestige of state administration {cite:wikipedia-arabization} {cite:donner-the-islamic-conquests-almuslih}. This is the summary's statement about the two pages, which the museum did not read. Against it stands what the museum read on the Encyclopaedia Britannica page Maghrib: that after the conquest of the 7th and 8th centuries the Arabs imposed Islam and Arabic on the native peoples, in the page's word {cite:britannica-maghrib}. The two sources describe the same process with two different verbs, spread and imposed, and the museum records both and does not choose.

## The four strands the later pages follow
The summary divides the story into four strands. First, the Arabisation of the dīwāns, that is of administration, between 697 and 742, the subject of [[concept/taarib-al-dawawin|The Arabisation of the dīwāns]]. Second, al-Andalus, Sicily and Malta, with the end of Arabic in Spain, in [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-al-andalus-wa-siqilliyya|Arabic in al-Andalus and Sicily]]. Third, the Maghrib in its urban and Bedouin waves, in [[concept/taarib-al-maghrib|The Arabisation of the Maghrib]]. Fourth, the shape of the language today, in [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Fusha and the dialects]]. This division is the summary's, and the museum follows it because it matches the chapters of general books.

## How the sources differ
The sources named for this page are of three kinds. Wikipedia pages on the conquests, Arabisation and the garrison towns, which support names, dates and places only. A university lecture and the Donner chapter, two academic references that the summary says support, respectively, the description of the garrison towns and the slowness of the change. And the Encyclopaedia Britannica, which the museum read directly and whose wording about imposing Arabic differs in tone from the summary. The museum does not blur these kinds: it names each and says what it said.

## What this page did not reach
The museum did not open any of the five pages the summary named for this section, so everything above about them is ascribed to the summary as it arrived, except the Britannica page Maghrib, which was read directly. The museum gives no figures for the populations of the garrison towns or for the speed of language change in each region, since the summary carries none. Each region mentioned has disputed detail, for example how much the older languages left in the new Arabic, that the summary did not reach and the museum did not invent.
