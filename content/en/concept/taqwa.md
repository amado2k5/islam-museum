## Overview
*Taqwa* (piety), in Ibn Rajab's commentary on the counsel "Fear God wherever you are," is for the servant to place between himself and what he fears and dreads a shield that protects him from it. The piety of the servant toward his Lord is that he places a shield between himself and what he dreads of His wrath, His displeasure, and His punishment, and it is the doing of His obedience and the avoidance of His disobedience. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.378}

## What it is attributed to
Piety is attributed to the name of God, so that it becomes a guard against His displeasure and anger, which is the greatest thing to guard against. It is attributed to His punishment, as with the Fire, and to His time, as with the Day of Resurrection. Full piety includes doing the obligations and abandoning the forbidden and the doubtful. Its highest degree is including the doing of the recommended acts and the abandoning of the disliked. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.379}

## The sayings of the early generations
Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz said: "The piety of God is not fasting by day or standing in prayer by night, but abandoning what God has forbidden and performing what He has obligated." Talq ibn Habib said: "That you act in obedience to God by a light from God, hoping for His reward; and abandon His disobedience by a light from God, fearing His punishment." Abu al-Darda' said: "The completion of piety is that the servant guard against the weight of an atom, until he leaves some of what is lawful as a barrier between himself and the unlawful." Al-Thawri said: "They were only called the pious because they guarded against what is not guarded against." Ibn Mas'ud explained "God's piety as is due to Him": that He be obeyed and not disobeyed, remembered and not forgotten, and thanked and not disbelieved. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.380}

## Its enjoinment
Piety is God's charge to the first and the last, and the charge of His Messenger to his community. He used to enjoin it on the commander of a secret expedition. He said to Abu Dhar: "I charge you with the piety of God, for it is the head of the whole matter." Among what most admits people to Paradise is the piety of God and good character. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.383}

It is connected with [[concept/wara|scrupulousness]].

## Etymology and range of meanings in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Supplement to the Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, defines *taqwa* as a term of Islamic religion denoting piety, a verbal noun built on the idea of fearing God, with the adjective *atqiya*, "pious", as a close synonym of *muttaqi*. Depending on context, the article lists renderings such as godliness, devoutness, piety, God-fearing, pious abstinence and uprightness. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 856}

The article adds that the word began with a social sense, a faithful and dutiful bearing toward one's tribe or relatives, which most later ethical thought set aside. It cites M. M. Bravmann for the view that in the poetry of [[person/labid-ibn-rabia|Labid]] this social meaning had merged with the Qur'anic ideal of fear of God, so that respectful conduct toward kin and reverence toward God became interchangeable. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 856}

The same pages trace two principles, fear and abstinence, through Sufi writing. The earliest Persian work on Sufism, the commentary on the Taarruf by Abu Ibrahim Mustamli of Bukhara, who died in 434/1042-3, is quoted as saying that taqwa has two principles, and that the devotee's attitude has two senses: fear of punishment, and fear of separation from God. Fear of punishment is said to produce observance of God's commands, while fear of separation means being content with nothing less than God. The Kubrawi master Muhammad Lahiji, who died in 912/1507, gave an almost identical definition, adding fear of one's own lower self. The article remarks that after more than five hundred years of speculation the two foundations remained intact. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 857}

## Taqwa in the Qur'an, as the article presents it
The article calls taqwa one of the most frequently mentioned religious concepts of the Qur'an and describes its sense as piety, abstinence and God-fearing obedience, a faith animated by works and works quickened by faith. It places the concept at the very start of revelation, pointing to the early passage at [[passage/quran-96-9|Qur'an 96:9]] to [[passage/quran-96-12|96:12]] and to the opening of the first Medinan sura at [[passage/quran-2-2|2:2]], where the Qur'an is described as guidance for the God-fearing. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 857}

It then gathers further passages. In [[passage/quran-49-10|49:10]] the believers, called brothers, are told to make peace among themselves in awareness of God, and [[passage/quran-9-123|9:123]] assures the God-fearing that God is with them; the article compares the latter with a hadith that sets taqwa beside jihad, the second being called the monasticism of the Muslim. It notes that salvation in this world and the next is tied to taqwa in [[passage/quran-39-61|39:61]] and [[passage/quran-39-73|39:73]], and that the true mosque is the one founded on it at [[passage/quran-9-108|9:108]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 857}

For the early exegete [[person/muqatil-ibn-sulayman|Muqatil ibn Sulayman]], quoted through Paul Nwyia, taqwa is treated as synonymous with sincerity, *[[concept/ikhlas|ikhlas]]*, and the verb used for guarding oneself is rendered as abstaining from disobedience and from associating others with God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 857}

The article also reads [[passage/quran-49-13|49:13]], which ranks the most God-fearing as the noblest before God, as showing taqwa to be a shared mark of the person of faith across sects, nations and ethnic lines. It cites the commentary ascribed to [[person/jafar-al-sadiq|Jafar al-Sadiq]], in the recension of [[person/al-sulami|al-Sulami]], which says the truly God-fearing person has cut his ties to created things for God's sake. Two sayings are given as marks of the concept: "Faith is naked and taqwa its dress", and the Prophet's answer that the family of Muhammad means every pious God-fearing person. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 857}

## Taqwa and iman
The article treats taqwa as an essential element of the inner side of the act of faith, *[[concept/iman|iman]]*. It quotes a report in which the Prophet says that submission is public while faith is in the heart, and points three times to his chest, saying that taqwa is here. As part of faith, taqwa is described as an inward and contemplative attitude of the heart rather than outward ritual, and the article links this to [[passage/quran-22-37|22:37]], which says it is piety, not the flesh or blood of the sacrifice, that reaches God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 857}

[[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|Al-Ghazali]], commenting on that verse in the [[work/ihya-ulum-al-din|Ihya]], is quoted as meaning by devotion a quality that gains control of the heart and disposes it to comply with commands. [[person/ibn-arabi-muhyi-al-din|Ibn al-Arabi]], reading the reference to the piety of the hearts at [[passage/quran-22-32|22:32]], is reported to have held that, as the heart fluctuates with every breath, genuine taqwa is a wary awareness of God with every breath. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 857} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 858}

Some Sufis took taqwa as the supreme proof of certitude. The article quotes the early Khurasani mystic Abu Bakr al-Warraq, who died in 294/906-7, saying that after certitude lights up a devotee's state he is able to reach the rank of the pious. It adds that this inner reading was not always shared by the exoteric scholars: [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]], an opponent of Ibn al-Arabi's Sufism, treats *iman*, righteousness and taqwa as synonyms when used in an absolute sense in the Qur'an, so that believers equal the God-fearing, who equal the upright, as set out in his [[work/kitab-al-iman-ibn-taymiyya|Kitab al-Iman]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 858}

## Taqwa in Sufi teaching
The article distinguishes two strands in the mystical theology of taqwa. In the first, taqwa is abstinence: a holy fearfulness and watchfulness against following one's passions, the heart's awe of the ever-present God. It cites the saying of [[person/sahl-al-tustari|Sahl al-Tustari]], recorded by [[person/al-qushayri-abu-l-qasim|al-Qushayri]], that taqwa is the mainstay of spiritual practice, and the statement of [[person/ibn-khafif|Ibn Khafif]], who died in 371/981, that piety is to distance yourself from everything that distances you from God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 858}

In the second strand, taqwa is the abstinence of the heart. The article gives a very early definition attributed to Jafar al-Sadiq, that for those on the spiritual path piety is that you find nothing within your heart but God, and cites [[person/abu-said-al-kharraz|Abu Said al-Kharraz]], who spoke of a heart vigilant against passion and a soul that guards itself against occasions of sin. It adds [[person/abu-al-husayn-al-nuri|Abu al-Husayn al-Nuri]], who named the light of piety as the soul of Sufi ethics. After the fifth/eleventh century, the article says, this interior notion largely faded from Sufi vocabulary, though [[person/ruzbihan-al-baqli|Ruzbihan Baqli]], who died in 606/1209, still described the root of taqwa as detaching one's inmost consciousness from all but God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 858}

From the late third/ninth to the fifth/eleventh centuries taqwa regularly appears in lists of the Sufi's moral virtues, close to fear, asceticism and scrupulousness. [[person/al-harith-al-muhasibi|Al-Harith al-Muhasibi]], who died in 243/857, is quoted as building a chain in which obedience rests on scrupulousness, scrupulousness on taqwa, and taqwa on self-examination, which rests on fear and hope; he also tells his reader that godliness is his capital and supererogatory works his profit. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 858}

In al-Qushayri's *[[work/al-risala-al-qushayriyya|Risala]]* taqwa is the fourth of the first ten stations, after repentance, spiritual struggle and retreat, and before scrupulousness and asceticism. Yet the article notes that several later classical authors do not mention it as a station or term, among them [[person/abu-nasr-al-sarraj|al-Sarraj]], [[person/abu-talib-al-makki|Abu Talib al-Makki]], al-Sulami, [[person/al-ansari-al-harawi-abd-allah|Abd Allah al-Ansari of Herat]], [[person/umar-al-suhrawardi|Umar al-Suhrawardi]] in the *[[work/awarif-al-maarif|Awarif al-maarif]]*, and [[person/abu-al-najib-al-suhrawardi|Abu al-Najib al-Suhrawardi]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 858} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 859}

Where it does resurface, mostly in Persian works, it can be prominent. Ansari, in his treatise on the hundred fields, makes taqwa the sixteenth, after scrupulousness, and describes three kinds of God-fearing people. The lowest keeps his profession of divine unity free of associationism, his sincerity free of hypocrisy and his worship free of innovation; the middle keeps his service free of show, his food free of doubtful matter and his state free of corruption; the highest never blemishes his gratitude with complaint. Later writers such as Said al-Din al-Farghani treat it as a necessary companion of scrupulousness or asceticism, among the stations of the first of three ascending degrees of annihilation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 859}

## Irony and paradox in Persian poetry
The article ends with a counter-current among Persian Sufi poets, who often cast taqwa as the posture of cold-hearted ascetics and formalist preachers. It quotes [[person/saadi-al-shirazi|Saadi]], who says that where the sultan of love appears no strength remains in the arm of taqwa, and suggests that he was criticising the self-consciousness taqwa could breed in less sincere followers, not rejecting its classical meaning. It places beside this the saying of Abu Bakr al-Wasiti, who died in 320/931, that piety is to abstain from one's own self-indulgent piety. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 859} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 860}

The article judges that this paradox is best seen in [[person/hafiz-al-shirazi|Hafiz]], who contrasts libertinism with purity and piety and treats the path of piety with irony. It sets him against [[person/abu-al-darda|Abu al-Darda]], the Companion revered by early Sufis, who is reported to have preferred piety to forty years of ritual worship. In the article's own concluding judgement, the journey runs from a Qur'anic concept, through an ascetic ideal of fear and abstinence, to the Sufi notion of piety as the faith of the heart, and ends in a paradox that dissolves the mystic's need for his own ladder of perfection. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 860}

## Taqwa in Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lessons (episode 6)
The Sheikh says taqwa is hard and is best known by example, and recounts that Umar asked a man about it, and the man answered that he lifts his garment and walks among thorns (needs_source). {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep06 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep06 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep06 00:05:30}
He applies the image to gatherings of backbiting: on entering one, a person must lift his heart, body and tongue from taking part, and he says he left a room where children were watching a film (his view). {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep06 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep06 00:06:30}
He cites a prayer attributed to a scholar, asking God to break the leg that walks away from gatherings of remembrance, and holds that the prayer asks God to close the doors of wrongdoing (needs_source). {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep06 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep06 00:07:30}
He says the first principle of taqwa is humility, and that a Muslim does not look down on anyone (see the entry on humility). {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep06 00:08:30}

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 6)

The Sheikh says that the key to paradise is patience and gratitude, then notes in the verse ([[passage/quran-78-31|Qur'an 78:31]]) that the pious have a place of triumph that is the result of this taqwa in the hereafter, and that the word here carries the sense of a place {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep06 00:02:00}. He observes that the wording of the verse points to a place and not merely to success, because in this world a person is two things, a place and its occupant, and when his heart yearns for the house of God it yearns for the place from which he draws his charge of faith {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep06 00:02:30}. He says that the reward is guaranteed by God, so what is asked of the servant is taqwa, and that the verse is a clear map for whoever wants to be among the pious {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep06 00:08:00}.

The Sheikh warns that a person should not think that the visible causes before him are what settle matters, but should knock at the door of God, who has only to will a thing for it to be {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep06 00:21:00}. He then mentions the fourth point of taqwa, which is that the servant seeks forgiveness every time he sins {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep06 00:21:00}.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 7)

The Sheikh holds that it is remarkable that the Companions who were given glad tidings of paradise grew more fearful of God after the tidings reached them from the truthful and infallible one {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep07 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep07 00:20:00}. He says that whoever deals with a great person, such as a distinguished scholar or a virtuous head of state, comes to revere him more the better he knows his character, and learns when he becomes angry {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep07 00:20:00}.

## In Ahmad Farid's lessons 'The Boat'
In the last seven lessons of [[work/the-boat-ahmad-farid|'The Boat']] [[person/ahmad-farid|Ahmad Farid]] turns from faith to piety, which he calls the highest degree of faith. {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep15 00:00:30} He gives several definitions and says they support rather than contradict one another: the heart's knowledge of the Lord's nearness; leaving what one desires for what one fears of God's punishment; leaving sins, as in the image of a man walking warily along a path of thorns; and leaving doubtful matters for fear of falling into sin. {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep15 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep15 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep15 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep15 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep15 00:05:00}

Piety, he says, is God's counsel to the first and the last, the Prophet's counsel to his community and every prophet's to his people, and the counsel of the early generations, among them [[person/sufyan-al-thawri|Sufyan al-Thawri]]'s words to [[person/ibn-abi-dhib|Ibn Abi Dhi'b]] that whoever fears God is spared people, and whoever fears people is not spared God. {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep16 00:00:30} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep16 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep16 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep16 00:02:30} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep16 00:03:00} It is the finest garment, the best provision and the measure of nobility, and it is the goal of worship: the first command in the Qur'an, to worship the Lord, ends 'that you may be pious', and so do the verses on fasting and on sacrifice. {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep16 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep17 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep17 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep18 00:00:30} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep18 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep18 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep18 00:02:30} Every fruit of faith is a fruit of piety too; in this world he counts five from sura al-Talaq: a way out, provision, ease, the expiation of sins and a greater reward ([[passage/quran-65-2|al-Talaq 2]]–[[passage/quran-65-5|5]]), and in the next, the inheritance of paradise and a place above others. {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep19 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep19 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep19 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep19 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep20 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep21 00:03:00}

## In al-Tabari's commentary: "the God-fearing"
Al-Tabari holds that God described the God-fearing by taqwa in general, so its sense may not be confined to some of what its people deserve and not to others, unless by a proof that must be accepted. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 234}
He rejects the view that taqwa here means guarding against polytheism and disavowing hypocrisy, since a wrongdoer may guard against polytheism without being among the God-fearing. He says the holder of that view may still be right about the meaning of "the God-fearing", even if he disagrees about calling those who commit grave sins and neglect obligations hypocrites, a name some scholars did give them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 234}
