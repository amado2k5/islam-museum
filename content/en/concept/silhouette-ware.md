## Summary
Arthur Lane explains that the alkaline glaze, like the earlier lead glaze, tended to smudge decoration painted under it; the Samarkand potters of the ninth and tenth centuries had solved this by mixing colours with a thick opaque clay slip, and much the same procedure was followed in a group of twelfth-century Persian wares, but with black alone, laid directly on the white paste. {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 52} Knife and brush were both used to sharpen outlines, the design was sometimes carved through a black ground, and over all lay a transparent glaze, ivory-tinted or deep turquoise. {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 52} {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 53}

## Characteristics
- Lane notes that the vivacity of its single figures, such as the dancer on one plate, has invited comparison with shadow-play puppets, but thinks the intensity is inherent in the technique. {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 53}
- The vessels are thinner than *lakabi* and of a coarser paste, perhaps made at Rayy; dishes often stand on a high pedestal foot, and several shapes became standard in the Saljuq overglaze-painted wares of the late twelfth and thirteenth centuries. {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 53}
- Lane sees it as the forerunner of brush painting in thinner underglaze colours. {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 65}
