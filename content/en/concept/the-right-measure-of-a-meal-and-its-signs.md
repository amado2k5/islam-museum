## Summary
Ibn Sina says the amount of food is set by habit and strength, and the right amount is that which brings no heaviness, stretching of the flanks, bloating, rumbling, floating, nausea, ravenous hunger, collapse, dullness of mind or sleeplessness; a taste lingering long afterwards marks worse food {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.229}.

## The measure
The amount of food depends on habit and strength; for the sound and strong it is the amount that, once taken, does not weigh, does not stretch the flanks, does not bloat, rumble or float {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.229}.

## The signs
With it no nausea, no ravenous hunger, no collapse, no dullness of mind and no sleeplessness arise, and the taste of the food is not found in belching after some time; whatever is tasted for longer is worse {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.229}.

## Pulse and breath
A sign that the food is moderate is that no big pulse with small breath follows it, which arises when the stomach crowds the diaphragm so that breath shrinks and quickens, the heart's need grows, and the pulse becomes big while strength weakens {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.229}.

## Special cases
One whose food brings heat and warmth should not eat all at once but little by little, so as not to suffer something like a rigor followed by a fever like an ephemeral fever; one who cannot digest enough should eat more often in smaller amounts {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.229}.
