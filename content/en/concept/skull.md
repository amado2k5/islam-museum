## Summary
Ibn Sina begins the anatomy of the head with the skull (qahf). He holds that its whole use is as a covering shield for the brain, protecting it from harm, and that its making in many parts and several bones has uses that concern the bone itself and uses that concern what it holds {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.43}.

## Why many bones
For the bone itself, he says that if a harm such as a fracture or putrefaction strikes one part, it need not spread over the whole skull as it would if it were one bone, and that one bone would not be given differences of hardness and softness, looseness and density, thinness and thickness among its parts {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.43}. For what the skull holds, the sutures serve as a way for vapours to dissolve out and cleanse the brain, a passage for the fibre of the nerve that leaves the brain for the parts of the head, a way in for veins and arteries, and a place where parts of the thick membrane are anchored so that it does not weigh on the brain {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.43}.

## Shape
The natural shape of the skull is round, for two reasons: the round figure has the greatest area among figures of straight lines with the same circumference, and it suffers less from blows than a figure with corners. It is also somewhat elongated, since the roots of the brain's nerves lie along its length, and it has a projection forward and one behind to protect the nerves descending from the two sides {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.43-44}.

## Unnatural shapes
He lists three unnatural shapes: the front projection missing, the back projection missing, or both missing so that the head is like a ball. He reports Galen's argument that in the spherical shape the dimensions are equal and so the division of the sutures should be equal too, and that a fourth unnatural shape, with length less than breadth, could not occur without some part of the ventricles or substance of the brain being lacking, which is against life. He says Galen upheld [[person/buqrat|Hippocrates]] in counting only four shapes of the head {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.44}. The editor's note on Hippocrates gives his birth and death dates as roughly 460 and 377 BC {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.44}.

## The bones below the skull
Beyond the skull the head has five bones, four like walls and one like a base. The first wall is the frontal bone, bounded above by the coronal suture. The walls to right and left hold the ears and are called the two stony bones for their hardness. The fourth wall, at the back, is bounded above by the lambdoid suture. The base of the brain is the sphenoid, which carries all the other bones, and it was made hard because hardness helps bearing and because it resists putrefaction from the residues that flow onto it constantly. The hardest of the walls is the back one, since it is hidden from the guarding senses; at each temple two hard bones cover the nerve that passes there {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.44-45}.
