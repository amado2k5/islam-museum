## Nature and selection
It has earthy and watery parts. In the author's lands there is a kind called shah amrud, large, very round, thin-skinned and a fine colour, as if translucent, like thickened sugar-water set solid, that breaks from being set and not from coarseness of substance, very sweet-smelling, and that dissolves if it falls from its tree to the ground. This is the pear kind with no harm in it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.534}.

## Temperament
The pear known as Chinese is cold in the first degree and dry in the second; the shah amrud is moderate and moist {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.534}.

## Actions and properties
All its kinds are astringent and go into poultices that hold back matter; it cleanses slightly, and its humour is more and better than apple's, as Rufus says. The one known as shah amrud in Khurasan and nowhere else loosens the bowels and gives a very good chyme {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.535}.

## Wounds and ulcers
It heals wounds, especially the dried wild kind {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.535}.

## Digestion and the organs of nourishment
It tans the stomach; the Chinese in particular strengthens the stomach, cuts thirst and quiets bile {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.535}.

## Excretory organs
It binds the bowels, especially the dried. The pear has the special property of bringing on colic, so honey water with spices should be drunk after it. Its rob helps the yellow bile {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.535}.

## Poisons and bites
If mushrooms are cooked with pears their harm is less {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.535}.
