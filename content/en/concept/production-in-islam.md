## Summary
The author defines production as utilizing and allocating resources, meeting needs, and generating incomes, but restricts this chapter to resource utilization and meeting needs, deferring income generation to the chapter on distribution {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 103}. In his view it is a means rather than an end: one produces to meet needs, be independent of others, and give from it, not merely to accumulate wealth {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 122} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 124}. Producing what is unlawful is forbidden, and necessities come first, followed by needs and then refinements {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 125}. He broadens the concept of production to include creating time and place utility in trade {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 170} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 171}.

## Overview
The chapter on production opens with a definition combining four functions: utilizing resources, allocating them, meeting needs, and generating incomes. The author addresses the first two here and leaves the third to distribution {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 103}. His factors of production fall into two categories: independent—land, labor, and capital—and dependent—risk and time {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 103} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 116}.

## Production as a Means, Not an End
The author holds that a Muslim produces to meet their own needs so as not to be a burden on others, and then to benefit others and give from their surplus, thereby earning reward {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 122}. He cites the hadith of the poor Emigrants and the wealthy Companions, as well as the hadith of the single dirham that outstrips a hundred thousand dirhams because giving from little can outweigh giving from much {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 123} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 124}. He views production as a means of worshiping God, showing gratitude, and fulfilling stewardship (istikhlaf), and criticizes materialist economies that produce anything without regard to its benefit or harm, quoting Roger Garaudy {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 124}. This critique is the author's quotation of Garaudy's opinion, not a ruling of his own on the matter.

## Controls and Priorities
The author prohibits producing what is unlawful, discourages what is disliked, and makes the goal the production of wholesome things that benefit people {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 125}. He orders production by need—necessities first, then needs, then refinements—and argues that wealth distribution directs resources: if wealth is concentrated in the hands of a few, production is steered toward their demand rather than the needs of the public {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 125}.

## Utility in Production
The author uses the concept of "maximizing utilities," drawing on al-Ghazali's definition of public interest (maslahah) as securing benefit or warding off harm, and on preserving the five essential objectives {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 129}. He cites Ibn Nujaym on the usurper who alters the name of a usurped item and increases its utilities so that it becomes theirs—such as grinding wheat or slaughtering and roasting a sheep {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 130}. He notes Ali ibn Abi Talib's recommendation regarding merchants and artisans as the providers of benefits {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 130}. He states that production includes creating time and place utility, making merchants producers just like manufacturers {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 170} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 171}.

## Productive Techniques and Population
The author holds that Sharia did not prescribe the methods and techniques of production, leaving them to human thought and the advancement of knowledge, with no objection to borrowing from other civilizations {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 141}. He cites the hadith on pollinating date palms, "You know best about your worldly affairs" {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 142}. He recommends developing indigenous productive techniques among Muslims so that self-sufficiency does not turn into dependency for machinery, spare parts, and maintenance {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 140}. He regards the entire population as consumers though not all are producers, and argues that if population growth outpaces output growth, per capita income weakens {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 137}. On offspring, he notes that Islam forbade celibacy and encouraged marriage, and holds that Muslim scholars do not favor a general state policy of birth control {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 138}.

## The Intellectual Impact of Work
The author presents Ibn Khaldun's statement that crafts cultivate the intellect, as industrial experience produces scientific principles that sharpen the mind {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 140}.
