## Summary
This page sets out what a research summary supplied to the museum says about Arabic in Central Asia: its legal status, the varieties spoken and the official figures, each as the summary gives it. Central Asian Arabic is two small isolated varieties that UNESCO classes as definitely endangered, with no separate official count of speakers. The museum did not open the pages the summary names, and the founder approved using it as supplied on 21 November 2026. Where the summary says that no official source or figure was found, this page says exactly that and supplies no number of its own.

## What this page is
This page is an article on the subject "Arabic in Central Asia", in the world chapter of the Arabic browser, one of the pages that treat Arabic outside the states counted as Arab. It answers three questions: what is the legal status of Arabic there, which varieties of it are spoken, and what official figures have been published on speakers, if any. The museum keeps here to what it keeps to on all its pages: a describing voice, not a commanding one, every statement ascribed to its page, and no verdict on a disputed question. All the material comes from one research summary, so the page describes what the summary said and does not claim to have read the sources.

## Background and dates
The summary's dates here are Tajikistan's 2010 census, Uzbekistan's 2026 census, whose language results Kursiv Media reported on 13 November 2026, and the UNESCO figure of 2003 relayed from Wikipedia. It does not tell how Arabic reached Bukhara and Qashqadarya. For the general frame linking the conquests with Khurasan and beyond the river see [[concept/taarib-al-dawawin|The Arabisation of the dīwāns]], where the Arabisation of the Khurasan dīwān falls in 741-742.

## Legal status
According to the summary, neither variety has official or recognised minority-language status in Uzbekistan, where Uzbek is the sole state language, plus Karakalpak in Karakalpakstan {cite:lex-uz-law-on-the-state-language-uzbekistan}, or in Tajikistan, where Tajik is the sole state language {cite:egov-tj-article-35-state-language}. The summary adds that both constitutions and laws guarantee citizens the general right to use their native languages. This is the summary's statement; the museum did not read the two laws.

## Varieties spoken
The summary names Uzbeki Arabic (code auz), split into the Bukhari and Qashqadaryawi dialects, and Tajiki Arabic (code abh), which together form the independent Central Asian Arabic branch, showing deep convergence with Tajik, Persian and Uzbek and lacking diglossia with Modern Standard Arabic outside religious contexts {cite:wikipedia-central-asian-arabic}. For the places see [[place/central-asia|Central Asia]], [[place/uzbekistan|Uzbekistan]] and [[place/tajikistan|Tajikistan]]; for comparison with another isolated variety see [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-cyprus|Arabic in Cyprus]].

## Official figures
According to the summary, neither national census counts speakers of Uzbeki or Tajiki Arabic separately. Uzbekistan's 2026 census groups minor languages into an other languages category of 88,244 {cite:kursiv-media-uzbekistan-census-2026-language-nationality}. Tajikistan's 2010 census, in the UN Data database, records 4,184 ethnic Arabs rather than speakers {cite:undata-tajikistan-2010-census-table-26}. UNESCO classes Central Asian Arabic as definitely endangered {cite:unesco-atlas-languages-in-danger-pf0000187026}, and the summary reports from Wikipedia that UNESCO-sourced data cite 6,000 speakers in total across Afghanistan, Iran, Tajikistan and Uzbekistan in 2003 {cite:wikipedia-languages-of-afghanistan}. All these figures are as the summary gave them, and none is a separate count for either variety.

## How the sources differ
The pages named for this record are of different kinds. Official texts and statistics: Law on the State Language (Uzbekistan), lex.uz document 6813902 {cite:lex-uz-law-on-the-state-language-uzbekistan}, egov.tj article 35 (Tajikistan, state language) {cite:egov-tj-article-35-state-language} and UN Data, population by ethnic group, Tajikistan 2010 (table code 26) {cite:undata-tajikistan-2010-census-table-26}; Reference, academic and press pages: UNESCO Atlas (unesdoc ark 48223/pf0000187026), as named in the research summary {cite:unesco-atlas-languages-in-danger-pf0000187026} and Uzbek language and nationality in the 2026 census (13 November 2026) {cite:kursiv-media-uzbekistan-census-2026-language-nationality}; Wikipedia pages: Central Asian Arabic {cite:wikipedia-central-asian-arabic} and Languages of Afghanistan {cite:wikipedia-languages-of-afghanistan}. The official texts and statistics speak for the state or the statistical office; the reference, academic and press pages describe and interpret; Wikipedia supports only names, places and dates. Where the kinds differ, the museum reports each in its own voice without choosing between them, and it opened none of them.
