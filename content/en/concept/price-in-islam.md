## Summary
The author distinguishes between price (thaman), which is agreed upon in the contract, and value (qimah), which is either the cost of producing the item or its average market price; he presents the causes of low and high prices according to al-Qadi Abd al-Jabbar and Ibn Taymiyyah, and outlines the jurists' disagreement on price-fixing (tas'ir): the majority disallow it, some allow it under conditions, and Ibn Taymiyyah allows it in specific cases such as hoarding, famines, and collusion {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 157} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 167}.

## Overview
For the author, price is what the two contracting parties agree upon in the contract, whereas value has two meanings: the cost incurred in producing the item, and the item's average value in the market. If the price exceeds the cost, the seller makes a profit; if it exceeds the average value, the seller is overcharging {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 157} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 158}.

## Causes of Price Fluctuations
The author cites the causes of low prices according to al-Qadi Abd al-Jabbar: abundance of the item, low need for it, few people in need of it, and increased supply when people need to sell it {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 155} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 156}. The causes of high prices are scarcity of the item when it is needed, a large number of people in need, heightened desire, and fear among those who neglected to acquire it {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 156}. He quotes Ibn Taymiyyah that whatever has many seekers rises in price according to the need and its urgency {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 156}, and quotes al-Juwayni that prices depend on factors beyond human choice {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 156}.

## Deferred Payment and Price
He explains that a deferred price may permissibly be higher than a spot price according to the majority of jurists, because time has a share in the price {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 151} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 157}.

## Fair Market Price
The author explains that the default in sale is mutual consent, and compelling someone to sell at the fair market price (thaman al-mithl) is impermissible except with just cause, such as when someone is in dire need of another's food; he notes that some jurists permitted sale at the item's value or its established price, and considers the issue worthy of comparative study {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 158}.

## Price-Fixing
The author reports that the majority of jurists did not permit price-fixing, relying on the apparent sense of the hadith of Anas ibn Malik that "God is the Withholder and the Extender" {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 166}, and arguing that price-fixing causes suppliers to hold back goods and leads to concealment and higher prices {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 167}. He explains that some Mu'tazili, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali jurists—such as Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim—permitted it while maintaining the general rule against price-fixing {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 167}.

Al-Qadi Abd al-Jabbar permits price-fixing if people collude on prices for their own gain, and holds that price-fixing is unlawful if the set price is unfair or harms people, especially the poor {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 168}. Ibn Taymiyyah permits it during crises and famines, in cases of hoarding, in cases of market restriction, and in cases of collusion among sellers or buyers {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 168}, noting that the Prophet "did not fix prices" because the situation did not involve any of these cases {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 169}.

Those who permit it stipulate that price-fixing must be fair, with neither undercutting nor excess, and that the ruler must consult merchants and experts so as to set a price acceptable to both parties {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 169} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 170}.

## Natural Disasters Affecting Crops
The author presents the hadiths on crop blights (jawa'ih) in the sale of fruit on the tree: a blight is a celestial calamity for which no one can be held liable, such as wind, cold, or heat, and damage caused by armies and thieves is treated likewise; in Malik's school its threshold is one-third, with further disagreement over whether this means one-third of the value or of the quantity {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 169} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 170}.
