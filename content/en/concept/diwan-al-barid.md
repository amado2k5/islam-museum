## Overview
Qudama ibn Ja'far describes the *diwan al-barid* (the postal office) as an independent office to which letters from all regions were brought, and from which they were then dispatched to their destinations. {cite:masalik-mamalik-ibn-khurdadhbih p.192}

## Its responsibilities according to Qudama
- Presenting to the caliph the letters of the postal and news agents in the regions, or preparing summaries of them.
- Overseeing the couriers (*ghuwaraniqiyun*), the timekeepers (*muwaqqitun*), and the stagemen (*mu'thibun*) on the post roads, and disbursing their pay.
- Appointing the mapmakers in the cities.

{cite:masalik-mamalik-ibn-khurdadhbih p.192}

The head of this office must be a trustworthy person. {cite:masalik-mamalik-ibn-khurdadhbih p.192}

## The barid in Subh al-a'sha
[[person/al-qalqashandi|Al-Qalqashandi]] opens the conclusion of [[work/subh-al-asha|Subh al-a'sha]] — "on matters connected with the chancery other than writing" — with a chapter on the barid, drawing heavily on al-Ta'rif bi-l-mustalah al-sharif by [[person/ibn-fadl-allah-al-umari|Ibn Fadl Allah al-Umari]]. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.366} {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.372}

### The word
In its lexical sense, he says, a barid is a fixed distance of twelve miles — four farsakhs, as jurists and geographers reckon it, the farsakh being three miles and the mile three thousand Hashimi cubits. The word is also used of the relay mount and of the messenger. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.366} {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.367} He gives two views of its origin: that it is an Arabic derivation, or that it is Persian, from "burida dum", "docked tail", because Persian kings docked the tails of barid mules to mark them — reported from Ibn al-Athir's al-Nihaya. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.367}

### Its history as he tells it
- Before Islam the barid existed under the Persian and Roman emperors; the author of al-Ta'rif thinks it was laid out in exact stages. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.367}
- The first to set it up in Islam, according to Abu Hilal al-Askari as he quotes him, was [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan]], once his caliphate was secure: he summoned Persian dihqans and men from the Byzantine provinces, who organised it for him. Others say it was done under [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan]]; al-Askari says Abd al-Malik "perfected" it. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.367} {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.368} He quotes Abd al-Malik telling his chamberlain never to keep the barid waiting, night or day, "for holding up the barid for an hour may spoil a whole year's work for a people". {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.368}
- Al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik used it to carry mosaic from Constantinople to Damascus. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.368}
- It lapsed at the end of the Umayyad state; [[person/al-mahdi|al-Mahdi]] set up relays between himself and the camp of his son Harun on campaign against Byzantium, and under [[person/harun-al-rashid|al-Rashid]] [[person/yahya-ibn-khalid-al-barmaki|Yahya ibn Khalid]] restored it as it had been under the Umayyads, with mules at the stations. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.368} {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.369}
- The Buyids cut it when they took control of the caliphate, "so that the caliph should not learn of their movements"; among the Seljuk rulers there were only envoys, and the Zangid and Ayyubid states used camel couriers (najjaba). {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.369} {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.370}
- [[person/baybars|Al-Zahir Baybars]] revived it when he ruled both Egypt and Syria. Al-Qalqashandi quotes the author of al-Ta'rif: his uncle Sharaf al-Din Abd al-Wahhab, head of chancery at Damascus, proposed it after Baybars told him, "If you can see to it that I go to bed every night with news and wake every morning with news, do so." {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.370}
- Al-Qalqashandi says the barid remained in place in Egypt and Syria until [[person/timur-lang|Timur]] overran Syria and took and burned Damascus in 804 AH, "which clipped the wing of the barid"; then "this poison spread" to Egypt, and a courier on royal business now rode his own horse "at the easy pace of a traveller". {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.370}

### Its insignia: the silver plaques
The head of the chancery at the sultan's court was in charge of the barid. The barid had silver plaques kept in the chancery under the confidential secretary; one face bore the profession of faith and the words "struck in Cairo the protected", the other the sultan's name and titles. A hole in each plaque held a cord of yellow silk with two straps; the courier hung it round his neck, the plaque under his clothes and the tassel behind his back, "so that everyone who saw the tassel behind him knew he was a barid courier", and on that sign the station keepers handed over the horses; he returned the plaque to the chancery when he came back. At Damascus and Aleppo the plaques read "struck in Syria" and "struck in Aleppo the protected". {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.371} {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.372}

### The stations
The stations (marakiz) were the places where barid horses waited so that the courier could change horse after horse. Quoting al-Ta'rif, al-Qalqashandi says they were "at uneven distances", depending on water and settlement, so that one station might be two barids from the next. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.372} He arranges them in six sections:

1. The station of [[building/citadel-of-cairo|the Citadel]] and its four branches: Qus in Upper Egypt, then Aswan and Nubia, Aydhab and Suakin; Alexandria; Damietta; and Gaza. Beyond Qus the stations ended and riders took fast camels to Aswan. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.373} {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.375}
2. The stations of Gaza and their branches to al-Karak, Damascus and Safad. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.379}
3. The station of Damascus and its branches to Homs, Hama, Aleppo, al-Rahba, Tripoli and other places. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.381}
4. The station of Aleppo to al-Bira, Bahasna, Qal'at al-Rum, Ayas and Ja'bar. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.383}
5. The station of Tripoli to Latakia, Sahyun and neighbouring places. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.385}
6. The stages of the Hijaz to Mecca and Medina: "as those are fixed by stations, these are fixed by stages". {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.385} {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.388}

### Works on the road
Quoting al-Ta'rif, al-Qalqashandi lists the khans, mosques and water-wheels built on the desert road between Egypt and Gaza: a mosque built by al-Ashraf Khalil ibn Qalawun at al-Warrada, a khan and a public water-wheel at al-Arish by Karim al-Din, and a khan and water-wheel at al-Kharruba by Fakhr al-Din, secretary of the mamluks. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.378} He records stations moved for convenience, among them al-Majami' by the bridge of Usama, of which the author of al-Ta'rif says, "I was the one who proposed it, in the year 741", and the governor Tanbugha's re-routing of the road after some couriers drowned crossing the Jordan. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.380} The chapter is followed in the conclusion by the chapter on the airfields of [[concept/pigeon-post|the carrier pigeons]]. {cite:subh-al-asha-cairo-v14 p.389}
