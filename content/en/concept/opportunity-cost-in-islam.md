## Summary
The author links opportunity cost (the cost of the chosen option) to the factors of production—time, effort, and money—arguing that a rational choice must be more beneficial than the forgone opportunity, and that weighing competing interests (masalih) among jurists corresponds to what economists call sacrifice and legal theorists (usuliyyun) call forfeiture (tafwit) {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 133} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 135}.

## Overview
For the author, cost is the sacrifice of one thing to obtain another—the harm or disadvantage accepted for the sake of a higher-value benefit {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 135}. Time, effort, and money each have alternative uses, so every use forfeits another, and a choice is rational only if it is more beneficial than what is forgone {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 135}.

## Hierarchy of Interests
The author presents the legal theorists' fivefold classification of rulings—obligatory, recommended, permissible, disliked, and prohibited—followed by the threefold hierarchy of necessities, needs, and refinements, and the fivefold framework of preserving religion, life, intellect, lineage, and wealth {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 134}. Al-Izz ibn Abd al-Salam describes the entire Sharia as consisting of interests, either warding off harms or securing benefits {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 134}.

## Rules for Resolving Conflicts
When interests conflict and cannot all be combined, they are ranked and the one yielding the greatest benefit is secured; when harms conflict, the greater harm is avoided; and when benefit and harm are mixed and the benefit prevails, it is secured while bearing the lesser harm {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 131}. Among the maxims he cites are: a general interest takes precedence over a particular one, warding off harms takes precedence over securing benefits, a certain benefit is not overridden by an imagined one, and private harm is borne to ward off public harm {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 132}.

## Time, Money, and Judgment
The author quotes al-Abbas ibn al-Hasan as saying that judgment cannot encompass everything, so it must be reserved for what is important; wealth cannot enrich everyone, so it is directed to those entitled to it; and night and day cannot accommodate every need {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 135}. He quotes Ibn al-Qayyim that desire either brings pain, cuts off a fuller pleasure, wastes time, or squanders wealth that is better kept {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 136}. He also quotes al-Shatibi that every task entails a proportionate degree of toil, and that shifting from one task to another carries a cost {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 136}.

## Giving Priority to the Superior Option
The author quotes al-Juwayni's definitive ruling prohibiting the appointment of a less qualified person when a more qualified one is available, applying it to leadership, public office, and employment {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 137}.



## Opportunity Cost in Jurisprudence
The author observes that the principle of opportunity cost among jurists appears in favoring the more advantageous option: preferring a deferred payment over an immediate one when the two are equal is a waste of wealth, because it forfeits the difference in present value {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 204}.

The author cites al-Izz ibn Abd al-Salam's statement on abandoning the most beneficial course when one is able to pursue it {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 205}, and considers "opportunity cost" an acceptable term that needs no alteration {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 205}.
