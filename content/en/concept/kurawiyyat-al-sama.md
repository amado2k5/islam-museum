## Summary
In chapter two al-Farghani rules that the heaven is like a sphere, and that all the spheres revolve in the sphere's motion about two fixed poles, one toward the north and the other toward the south {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 139}.

## Proof from the planets' rising
The planets appear in the east rising little by little in one order of their motions and sizes until they reach mid-heaven, then descend to the west in the same order; their motion is seen in parallel circles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 139}.

## Proof from the polar planets
Al-Farghani names the planets near the north pole — Capricornus (al-Jady), the Farqadân and Banat Na'sh — revolving in parallel circles about one point, the nearer to the point the smaller its circle and the slower its motion {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 140}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 124}. This difference of circles is what makes the heaven spherical rather than a flat surface {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 141}.

## Proof from parallax at the horizon
Al-Farghani shows that the sun and moon appear larger at rising and setting than at mid-heaven, and that this goes back to the vapour rising from the earth between the eye and the horizon, not to the body's nearness {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 142}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 122}. He compares it to what a viewer sees of an object set at the bottom of clear water {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 142}.

## Perspectives
- **The author (al-Farghani):** the heaven is a sphere; his proofs are observational, and in this chapter he voices no dissent except describing "some people" who hold the heaven flat {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 123}.
