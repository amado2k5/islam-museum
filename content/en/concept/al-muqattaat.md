## Summary
Some surahs open with disjoined letters. At the start of his commentary on Surah Al-A'raf, [[person/rashid-rida|Muhammad Rashid Rida]] states that his preferred view is that their wisdom is to alert the listener to what follows, so that nothing is missed. {cite:tafsir-manar-v08 p.261} {cite:tafsir-manar-v08 p.262} [[passage/quran-7-1|Al-A'raf 1]]

## The surahs that open with them
Rida notes that the Prophet recited these surahs to the polytheists of Mecca to call them to Islam and to establish revelation and prophethood. All of them are Meccan except al-Baqara and Al 'Imran, and most of them open by mentioning the Book. {cite:tafsir-manar-v08 p.262}

## The themes of these surahs
Rida shows that each surah of this kind has a theme connected with establishing the message and the Book. He notes that Surah al-'Ankabut speaks of trials in religion, and that Surah al-Rum reported the victory of the Romans over the Persians after the Persians had defeated them, which was one of the miracles of the Qur'an. {cite:tafsir-manar-v08 p.262} {cite:tafsir-manar-v08 p.263}

## Author's note
This is Rida's choice and his reasoning, and it is attributed to him. The other views on the meaning of the disjoined letters, and what the scholars of the Qur'anic sciences said about them, have not yet been presented.

## Summary of the lecture
The disjoined letters are letters with which some surahs open, such as Alif Lam Mim and Ha Mim; the Sheikh says that Taha and Yasin are among them and are not names of the Prophet {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep03 00:19:00}.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 3)

The Sheikh says that Taha and Yasin are not among the names of the Prophet, and that Mustafa and Mukhtar are attributes rather than names {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep03 00:19:00}. He says they belong to the disjoined letters with which some surahs open, like Alif Lam Mim and Ha Mim {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep03 00:19:00}. He criticises naming a boy Taha on the understanding that it is one of the Prophet's names {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep03 00:19:00}.

## In al-Zarkashi's al-Burhan
In type 7, 'on the secrets of the openings of the suras', [[person/badr-al-din-al-zarkashi|al-Zarkashi]] says God opened the suras with ten kinds of speech, none falling outside them, the second being openings with letters of the alphabet, found in twenty-nine suras. {cite:burhan-dar-al-hadith-2006 p.117} {cite:burhan-dar-al-hadith-2006 p.118} He cites [[person/al-zamakhshari|al-Zamakhshari]]: these letters are half the names of the letters of the alphabet, fourteen, covering half of each class of letters — voiceless and voiced, plosive, emphatic and others — and are those that recur most in speech; al-Zarkashi corrects him on the count of the 'echoing' letters. {cite:burhan-dar-al-hadith-2006 p.118} He cites Qadi Abu Bakr that they come as half the alphabet as if to say to whoever claims the Qur'an is no sign: take the other half and compose with it something to rival it. {cite:burhan-dar-al-hadith-2006 p.119} He counts seventy-eight letters with repetition, gives how they are spread over the suras, and notes that without repetition they are fourteen, gathered in the phrase 'nass hakim qati' lahu sirr', with [[person/al-suhayli|al-Suhayli]]'s and others' mnemonic phrases. {cite:burhan-dar-al-hadith-2006 p.119}

## In al-Baghawi
In the Tahdhib of his commentary, [[person/al-baghawi-al-husayn-ibn-muhammad|al-Baghawi]] reports from al-Sha'bi and others that 'Alif Lam Mim' and the other letters opening the suras are among the ambiguous whose knowledge God kept to Himself — the secret of the Qur'an — so one believes in them as they stand and leaves their knowledge to God, the point of mentioning them being to call for belief in them. {cite:tahdhib-tafsir-baghawi-tayba-2009 p.11}

## In al-Tabari's commentary: the views on "alif-lam-mim"
Al-Tabari sets out the views of the exegetes on the disjoined letters: that they are a name of the Qur'an; that they are openings with which God begins His speech; that they are names of the surahs by which each is known; that they are an oath God swore by, and one of His names; that each letter is a part of a word with a full sense; and that they are letters of the alphabet used as names. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 205} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 206} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 207} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 208}
From Ibn Abbas he reports that "alif-lam-mim" means "I am God, the Knowing"; from Rabi' ibn Anas, that each letter points to many meanings and not one. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 207} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 208} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 209}
He gives a report of Jewish rabbis who count the letters of "alif-lam-mim" by the abjad reckoning to reach the years of the community's rule. The editor grades the chain as weak, notes that al-Kalbi, who transmits it, is discarded in narration, and that Ibn Kathir rejected using such reckoning to know the periods. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 216} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 217} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 218} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 219}
He concludes that each disjoined letter holds the meanings that Rabi' ibn Anas and the other exegetes give, because God meant them to signify many things and not one, and that the abjad reckoning is not sound. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 220}

## In al-Tabari's commentary: rejecting the view that they mean "bal"
Al-Tabari reports the view of some grammarians that "alif-lam-mim" and its like are like "bal" in a line of verse, meaning nothing and serving only as filler. He rejects it on four grounds: it ascribes to God an address in a language neither the Arabs nor any human speak; the Arabs did not begin speech with these letters in the sense of "bal"; it ascribes futility to God; and "bal" has a known meaning in Arabic, a return from a sentence already completed. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 223} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 224}
He argues from God's description of the Qur'an as "a clear Arabic tongue", holding that what no one understands cannot be clear, and points to [[passage/quran-26-195|al-Shu'ara 195]]. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 223}

## In al-Tabari's commentary: each letter carries many meanings
Al-Tabari compares the disjoined letters with a single word that carries several meanings, such as "umma" or "din", and holds that whoever claims one meaning for them must prove it and will be met with the claim of his opponent. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 221} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 223}
He argues that if God had meant only one of these meanings, the Prophet would have made it plain; that his not doing so shows that all their possible meanings are intended, since none of them is impossible as an interpretation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 222}
