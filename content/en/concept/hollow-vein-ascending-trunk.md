## Summary
In the third chapter Ibn Sina describes the hollow vein: its root spreads in the liver like hairs to draw food from the branches of the gate vein, its stem then rises at the convexity and divides into rising and descending parts, and the rising part pierces the diaphragm and reaches the heart at its right auricle {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.88}.

## In the liver and at the diaphragm
The root of the hollow vein first spreads in the liver itself into hair-like parts to draw food from the branches of the gate vein, which themselves branch like hairs; the hollow vein's branches come into the liver's interior from its convexity, and the gate's from its concavity. Its stem then rises at the convexity and divides in two, rising and descending. The rising part pierces the diaphragm, passes through it, and leaves two vessels in it that feed it; it then faces the sac of the heart and sends it large branches that spread like hairs and feed it, then divides in two {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.88-89}.

## At the heart
A great part goes to the heart and enters it at its right auricle; this is the greatest of the heart's vessels, because the others are for breathing in air while this is for food, and food is coarser than breeze, so its passage must be wider and its vessel greater. As it enters the heart, three membranes were set up for it so that the heart, as it widens, draws food through them and the food does not return when it relaxes; its membranes are the hardest of membranes {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.89}. The second part circles the heart and is then spread inside it to feed it, just where the hollow vein is about to dive into the right auricle. The third inclines, in humans in particular, to the left side, then heads for the fifth thoracic vertebra, rests on it, and spreads in the lower eight ribs and the muscles and other bodies near them {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.89}.
