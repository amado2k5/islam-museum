## Summary
Anatomy is knowledge of the structure, forms and functions of the body's organs. In Ibn Sina's scheme it is one of the topics of [[concept/medicine|medicine]]. [[person/ibn-al-nafis|Ibn al-Nafis]] presented its topics in his commentary on the "Canon" {cite:sharh-tashrih-qanun-print p.1}.

## Reliance on earlier authorities
Ibn al-Nafis says that he did not perform dissection himself, for religious reasons and because compassion prevented him. For the forms of the inner organs he relied on what earlier writers described, especially [[person/jalinus|Galen]], and for their functions he relied on observation {cite:sharh-tashrih-qanun-print p.1}.

## In the Canon: the nature of the organ and its divisions
In the fifth teaching of the first part of Book I of the "Canon", [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]] defines the organ as a body generated from the first temperament of the humours, and divides it into simple and composite: the simple is what shares the whole in name and definition in any part taken from it, like flesh, bone and nerve — these are called organs of similar parts — while the composite is what does not, like the hand and the face, and is called an instrumental organ because it is an instrument of the soul in movements and actions {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.36-37}.

He counts among the similar-part organs first the bone, because it is the body's foundation and the support of movements; then cartilage, to stand between the hard and the soft and to serve as support for joints and the sinews of muscles; then the nerve, by which sensation and movement are completed; then sinews, ligaments and 'aqib; then the hollow arteries that stir the heart, carry off its smoky vapour and distribute the spirit; then the veins that supply the organs with blood; then the fine membranous sheets that cover the surfaces of bodies, preserve their forms and provide a sensitive surface for what has no sensation in its substance; and finally flesh, as stuffing for the body and a power of nutrition for it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.36-38}.

He then treats the organs in respect of receiving and giving powers: some only receive without giving, like flesh; some receive and give, like the heart, brain and liver, on which physicians and philosophers differed; the principal organs by the standard of the individual's survival are three — the heart, source of the power of life, the brain, source of sensation and movement, and the liver, source of nutrition — and by the standard of the species's survival the generative organs are added; serving organs are divided into preparatory and effective. He answers Galen's distinction of act from benefit {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.38-39}. He notes that what is formed from the two seeds does not regenerate after severance except in a few cases, such as bone in youth, while what is formed from blood regenerates after breaking, like flesh, and that strong organs push their waste to weak neighbours {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.40-41}.
