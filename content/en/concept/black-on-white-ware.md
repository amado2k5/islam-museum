## Summary
Black on white ware is the third of the three largest groups of glazed earthenware at [[place/nishapur|Nishapur]]: decorated in black only on a white engobe and covered with a clear, usually colourless lead glaze. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 132} [[work/nishapur-pottery-of-the-early-islamic-period|Wilkinson]] counts it among the most attractive Nishapur wares, the clarity of its simple, lively designs striking the eye after the confusion of colour and scratched lines in colour-splashed ware. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 132}

## Discovery and distribution
Modern knowledge of it began at the end of the nineteenth century, when it was found in quantity in Transoxiana: fragments from Afrasiyab were given to the Victoria and Albert Museum in 1898, and the Berlin museum acquired a large collection of sherds and wasters in 1906, proving manufacture at Afrasiyab. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 132} It has also been found at Tashkent, Gurgan, [[place/merv|Merv]] and Lashkari Bazar, showing its use in all the important cities of the eastern Islamic lands from the ninth century to the eleventh. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 132} Wasters at Nishapur show it was made there; some pieces carry designs unlike those of other centres, while others seem imported. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 133}

## Making
The Nishapur body is usually reddish; the engobe normally covers the whole vessel including the base, applied so thickly that the glaze cannot bond with the body, so that glaze and engobe often flake off together. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 133} Wilkinson notes that pieces with a pure white engobe and a colourless glaze also excel in potting and decoration, showing that the whiteness was sought. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 133} Arabic inscriptions in many forms dominate its decoration, including letters with large triangular or foliated heads. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 172}
