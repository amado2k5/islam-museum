## Summary
The sixth pair grows from the back of the brain and divides in three; the greatest part descends to the viscera and from it comes the recurrent nerve to the larynx {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.80-81}.

## Its parts
It grows from the back of the brain joined to the fifth, bound with it by membranes and ligaments as if one nerve, then parts and leaves by the hole at the end of the lambdoid suture, already divided in three. One part goes to the muscles of the throat and the root of the tongue to help the seventh pair; one to the muscles of the shoulder, mostly spreading in the broad muscle upon it; and the greatest part descends to the viscera along the rise of the carotid, bound to it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.80}.

## The recurrent nerve
When this part reaches the larynx branches leave it to the laryngeal muscles whose heads are above; passing them, branches rise from it to the bowing muscles whose heads are below, which are needed to close and open the arytenoid, for that needs a pull downward; so it is called the returning nerve. It was sent down from the brain because, were a spinal nerve sent up, it would rise obliquely and could not pull downward firmly {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.80}. He says that the one rising on the left meets the great artery straight and thick and turns on it like a pulley without much fastening, while the one on the right does not meet that artery in its first state and needs ligaments to bind its branches {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.80}. The strongest of the returning nerve spreads through the two layers of laryngeal muscle; the rest descends to the membranes and muscles of the diaphragm and chest and to the heart, lung, veins and arteries there, and what is left passes into the diaphragm {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.81}.
