## Summary
This page sets out what a research summary supplied to the museum says about Arabic in Niger: its legal status, the varieties spoken and the official figures, each as the summary gives it. In Niger the legal place of Arabic changed with the transitional charter of 2025, according to the summary, and no official count of speakers was found. The museum did not open the pages the summary names, and the founder approved using it as supplied on 21 November 2026. Where the summary says that no official source or figure was found, this page says exactly that and supplies no number of its own.

## What this page is
This page is an article on the subject "Arabic in Niger", in the world chapter of the Arabic browser, one of the pages that treat Arabic outside the states counted as Arab. It answers three questions: what is the legal status of Arabic there, which varieties of it are spoken, and what official figures have been published on speakers, if any. The museum keeps here to what it keeps to on all its pages: a describing voice, not a commanding one, every statement ascribed to its page, and no verdict on a disputed question. All the material comes from one research summary, so the page describes what the summary said and does not claim to have read the sources.

## Background and dates
The summary gives one sequence here: the 1999 and 2010 constitutions, which counted Arabic among ten national languages, then the transitional Charte de la refondation of March 2025, which lists it among the spoken languages. This is all the history the summary tells; it does not tell the earlier history of Arabic in the region. For the general frame see [[concept/intishar-al-arabiyya-baada-al-fath|The spread of Arabic after the conquests]].

## Legal status
According to the summary, Article 12 of the transitional Charte de la refondation of March 2025 makes Hausa the national language and English and French working languages, and lists Arabic among the spoken languages of Niger {cite:ghanafact-niger-hausa-official-language} {cite:tv5monde-niger-haoussa-langue-nationale} {cite:cefan-niger}. The summary says this replaces its earlier status as one of ten national languages under the 1999 and 2010 constitutions. The change it describes is a move of Arabic from a national language to a named spoken language; the museum did not read the charter or the three pages and passes no judgement on the accuracy of the description.

## Varieties spoken
The summary names Chadian (Shuwa) Arabic in the Diffa region near Lake Chad, Hassaniya Arabic, and Classical and Modern Standard Arabic in religious and educational contexts {cite:wikipedia-chadian-arabic} {cite:wikipedia-hassaniya-arabic} {cite:cefan-niger}. In this description Niger is where two kinds of Arabic meet: an eastern kind that links it with Chad and a western kind that links it with Mauritania and Mali. For comparison see [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-mauritania|Arabic in Mauritania]] and [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-mali|Arabic in Mali]].

## Official figures
The summary found no official government census, from the national statistics institute (INS), and no UN or UNESCO count of speakers {cite:cefan-niger}. That is what the summary found; the museum supplies no number of its own, estimates no share of speakers of Arabic in Niger, and takes no figure from any page the summary did not name.

## How the sources differ
The pages named for this record are of different kinds. Reference, academic and press pages: Has Niger adopted Hausa as a new official language? (fact check) {cite:ghanafact-niger-hausa-official-language}, Au Niger, le haoussa devient langue nationale, le français relégué langue de travail {cite:tv5monde-niger-haoussa-langue-nationale} and Université Laval (CEFAN) page on Niger {cite:cefan-niger}; Wikipedia pages: Chadian Arabic {cite:wikipedia-chadian-arabic} and Hassaniya Arabic {cite:wikipedia-hassaniya-arabic}. The official texts and statistics speak for the state or the statistical office; the reference, academic and press pages describe and interpret; Wikipedia supports only names, places and dates. Where the kinds differ, the museum reports each in its own voice without choosing between them, and it opened none of them.
