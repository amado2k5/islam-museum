## Summary
Is a translation of the Qur'an called the Qur'an? And does it have the standing of the mushaf in touching and respect? This page gives the answer of the four Sunni schools as it appears in their books and in the fatwa houses: a translation is not called the Qur'an by any of them, though the Hanafis and the majority differ in how they explain it and in the ruling on touching books that contain Arabic verses. It rests on a research summary supplied to the museum; the books and fatwas it points to were not opened. Each view is given under its holders, and the page does not judge between them.

## The question and its background
This question lies behind two others, reciting a translation in prayer, in [[concept/qiraat-al-quran-bil-tarjama-fi-al-salat|Reciting the Qur'an in translation in prayer]], and the sermon in another language, in [[concept/khutbat-al-jumua-bi-ghayr-al-arabiyya|The Friday sermon in a language other than Arabic]]: every ruling in those places assumes an answer to what is called the Qur'an and whether a translation falls under its name. For the jurists the weight rests on the revealed Arabic wording, so the question touches inimitability, in [[concept/ijaz-al-quran|The inimitability of the Qur'an]], and the naming of a translation as tafsir, in [[concept/limadha-tusamma-al-tarjama-tafsiran|Why a translation is called an interpretation]].

## The Hanafis: an expression of the meaning
Manna' al-Qattan in Mabahith fi Ulum al-Qur'an, as the IslamWeb library gives it, reports that the Hanafis who allowed recitation in translation upon incapacity agree with the others that a translation is not called the Qur'an and suffices only as an expression of the meaning for the sake of prayer; compare al-Kasani's Bada'i al-Sana'i, p. 113 {cite:islamweb-qattan-mabahith-reading-prayer-non-arabic on Manna' al-Qattan} {cite:ablibrary-badai-sanai-kasani-p113 p.113}. On touching books, the Kuwaiti Mawsu'a Fiqhiyya, vol. 13, p. 97, via the Mosannefat site, states that the Hanafis still require wudu to touch tafsir works that contain Arabic verses {cite:mosannefat-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-v13-p97 p.97}.

## The Malikis, Shafi'is and Hanbalis: a translation is tafsir
Dar al-Ifta al-Misriyya's Fatwa 12699 on the ruling on reciting and writing the Qur'an in other than Arabic says that for the Malikis, Shafi'is and Hanbalis the Qur'an is the revealed Arabic wording with its arrangement, and a translation is not the Qur'an but tafsir {cite:dar-alifta-fatwa-12699 on Fatwa 12699}. Al-Nawawi in al-Majmu', vol. 3, pp. 342-343, supports this by treating a translation as other than the Qur'an {cite:islamweb-majmu-nawawi-v3-quran-non-arabic p.342-343}.

## The ruling on touching
The Kuwaiti Mawsu'a is quoted for the point that the majority of the Malikis, Shafi'is and Hanbalis, because a translation is tafsir, permit touching a pure translation, or a tafsir work in which commentary outweighs the Arabic text, without wudu {cite:mosannefat-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-v13-p97 p.97} {cite:ketabonline-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-v38-p9 p.9}. So the Hanafis and the majority agree that a translation is not the Qur'an and part company over touching what contains Arabic verses, according to whether the Arabic text or the commentary predominates.

## A short comparison of the two views
The above can be summed up thus: for the Hanafis a translation is an expression of the meaning that can stand in for the Arabic recitation in prayer when one is unable, and it is not called the Qur'an, while books that carry Arabic verses keep the mushaf's rule for touching. For the majority a translation is tafsir, because the Qur'an is the revealed wording and arrangement; a pure translation needs no wudu, and its wording does not stand in for the Qur'an's wording in prayer, as in [[concept/qiraat-al-quran-bil-tarjama-fi-al-salat|Reciting the Qur'an in translation in prayer]]. It appears that the two sides differ in the effect that follows from the naming, not in the naming itself, and that all acknowledge that the Arabic text has what a translation does not.

## How the sources differ
The first source type is an early juristic book, such as Bada'i al-Sana'i and al-Majmu'; the second is the contemporary fatwas of Dar al-Ifta; the third is a contemporary juristic encyclopaedia. A reader will therefore sometimes meet the same wording twice, once from the book and once from the fatwa that quoted it.

## What this page does not reach
The pages of Bada'i al-Sana'i, al-Majmu', al-Qattan's Mabahith, the Kuwaiti Mawsu'a and the Dar al-Ifta fatwa were not opened; their statements come from a research summary supplied to the museum, and the founder approved using it on 21 November 2026 without re-verification. Page and volume numbers are the summary's and unchecked, and the detail of disagreement inside each school over touching books with Arabic verses was not examined.
