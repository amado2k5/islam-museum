## Summary
Ibn Sina opens the second lesson, on causes, by dividing the causes of the body's states into antecedent, evident and conjoined, showing how they differ, then causes by themselves and by accident, the conditions for a cause to act, surviving and non-surviving causes, and the six necessary genera {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.112-113}.

## Antecedent, evident and conjoined
The causes of the body's states, health, disease and the state between them, are three: antecedent, evident and conjoined. The antecedent and the conjoined share being bodily matters, of humour, temperament or composition. The evident come from things outside the body's substance, either from outside bodies, such as a blow, the heat of the air, hot or cold food, or from the soul, such as anger and fear. The antecedent and evident share that something may stand between them and the state; the evident and conjoined share that nothing may stand between; but the antecedent certainly has an intermediary and the conjoined never has {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.112}.

## Examples
An example of the antecedent: fullness of the eye's vessels for the descent of water in it. Of the conjoined: blockage for fever. Of the evident: the sun's heat and severe heat, grief, sleeplessness or taking something heating like garlic, all for fever, or a blow for dispersal and the descent of water in the eye {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.112-113}.

## By itself and by accident
Every cause is either a cause by itself, as pepper heats and opium cools, or by accident, as cold water when warmed by condensation and the shutting in of heat, hot water when cooled by dissolving, and scammony when it cools by draining the heating humour {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.113}.

## Conditions for acting
Not every cause that reaches the body acts on it; three things are needed: a power from its acting power, a power from the body's preparedness, and a chance for the two to meet for a time in which that act can arise. The state of causes may differ in what they bring about: one cause may bring on different diseases in different bodies or at different times, and its action differs in the weak and the strong and in the keen-sensed and the dull {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.113}.

## Surviving causes and the necessary six
Some causes are surviving, those whose effect remains after they leave, and some non-surviving, those whose departure brings recovery. The causes that change or keep the body's states are either necessary, ones a person cannot be rid of in life, or unnecessary. The necessary are six genera: the surrounding air; what is eaten and drunk; bodily movement and rest; movements of the soul; sleep and waking; evacuation and retention {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.113}.
