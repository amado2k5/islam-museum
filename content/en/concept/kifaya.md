## Summary
In the lexicon kifaya is what brings independence from others; one says he was sufficed by a thing, and kafaf is the measure of need with no excess or lack {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13376} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13377}. In the terminology it has three senses: the important acts the lawgiver wants done without regard to who does them, which are collective obligations; a person's fitness for public duties; and meeting one's essential needs to the level of one's condition {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13376}.

## Collective obligations and recommended collective acts
A collective obligation is an important general matter with religious or worldly benefit, meant to be done by enough of the people, not each; once some have done it the rest are excused. It differs from a personal obligation, whose benefit repeats with each performance, and it ends when some perform it {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13377} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13378}. Recommended collective acts include the greeting of a group and the blessing of a sneezer {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13378}.

## The benefits met by sufficiency
Religious benefits include learning, memorizing Qur'an and Sunna, public worship, jihad and enjoining good; worldly ones include learning crafts and farming; shared ones include bearing and giving testimony, taking in a foundling, visiting the sick, washing, shrouding, praying over and burying the dead, and holding public offices {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13379} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13380} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13381}. Bearing testimony is a collective obligation when many witnesses are available and a personal one when there is only one {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13379} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13380}.

## Appointing the imam and public offices
Appointing the imam is a collective obligation by agreement of the schools; those who choose the ruler, and those qualified for the post, discharge it for the community, and the same applies to other public offices {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13382} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13383}.

## Sufficiency of individuals
A person's sufficiency is his essential needs; it is owed first by himself, then by his kin, then by the Muslims {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13382}. A rich man or one able to work must meet his own needs and is not given zakat {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13383}. The maintenance owed to a relative is sufficiency suited to his condition; a wife's maintenance is measured by sufficiency, and the Shafi'is set measures of food by wealth and poverty {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13383} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13384}.

## Ways of providing sufficiency
Zakat may provide it: the Hanafis give a poor man less than the nisab, while the Malikis and one Hanbali narration give enough for a year. Poor people who may not take zakat are paid from the public treasury, and the ruler may levy taxes on the rich for public needs {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13384}.

## The level of sufficiency in al-Tahir Qana's al-Dawr al-Tawzi'i li-l-Milkiyya

[[person/al-tahir-qana|Al-Tahir Qana]] makes the provision of the level of sufficiency (hadd al-kifaya), not bare subsistence (hadd al-kafaf), the aim of redistribution in the Islamic economy. He grounds needs in the division of the aims of the law into necessities, needs and refinements, and treats the first two as the basic needs on which maintenance is assessed: food, drink and medical care, clothing, housing, a servant for the disabled and the sick, marriage, the maintenance of wife and children, and the needs of students of knowledge. {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.151} {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.152} He contrasts the subsistence level used by capitalist and socialist economics — what keeps a person alive and meets bare necessities — with the level of sufficiency, which for him means a dignified life and moderate comfort, up to the level of wealth customary in one's society; he cites the hadith of al-Mustawrid ibn Shaddad that whoever works for the state may acquire a wife, a servant and a house. {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.153} He quotes al-Mawardi that a zakat collector is given what lifts him out of poverty to the lowest rank of wealth according to his circumstances, and reports the jurists' disagreement on how much is given: sufficiency for a lifetime, for a year — from the report that the Prophet set aside a year's provision for his family — or for a day. {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.154} The burden of securing sufficiency, he holds, is shared among private, public and state property, each within its own sphere. {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.155}
