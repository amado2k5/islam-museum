## Summary
This page sets out three Moroccan texts on language as a research file supplied to the museum summarises them: the Constitution of 2011, Article 5; framework law No. 51-17 of 2019 on the system of education, training and scientific research; and organic law No. 26-16 implementing the official status of Amazigh. The museum opened neither the pages cited nor the texts, and what is here is ascribed to the summary.
## The Constitution of 2011, Article 5
The body, by the summary, is the Parliament and the Monarchy, the Constitution having been approved by referendum. Article 5 states that Arabic remains the official language of the State, which works to protect, develop and promote its use, while also recognising Amazigh as an official language and providing for an organic law on its implementation {cite:axl-cefan-ulaval-language-legislation}. The text thus joins the official status of Arabic and that of Amazigh in one article.
## Framework law 51-17 of 2019
This is the framework law on the system of education, training and scientific research, and by the summary it adopts Arabic as the principal language of teaching, develops Amazigh's position in schools within a clear national framework consistent with the Constitution, and supports progressive balanced plurilingualism {cite:axl-cefan-ulaval-language-legislation}. It shows that the Constitution did not leave language an abstract text but carried it down into education policy.
## Organic law 26-16
The summary says a related 2019 organic law, No. 26-16, implements Amazigh's official status, the law to which Article 5 referred {cite:adala-justice-gov-ma}. The summary does not detail its articles, so the museum adds nothing about them.
## Where the texts are
The summary says the Constitution text is in the Bulletin Officiel, and details of the framework law are in the Universite Laval archive and in Moroccan government and justice sites such as Adala (adala.justice.gov.ma) {cite:adala-justice-gov-ma} {cite:axl-cefan-ulaval-language-legislation}. For comparison with Algeria see [[concept/qanun-al-taarib-al-jazairi|Algeria's Arabisation law]].
## Place in the record of Arabic
These texts are read with [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-morocco|Arabic in Morocco]], [[place/morocco|Morocco]] and [[language/arabic|Arabic]]. The summary says nothing of application or of the views of different parties on the text, so the museum says nothing of it. At its close the summary notes that such instruments treat Arabic as a core marker of national and Arab identity while navigating multilingual realities, among them the recognition of Amazigh in Algeria and Morocco and the influence of English and French.

## How to read this page
The summary does not say which institutions oversee the application of these texts, nor the timetables for generalising Amazigh in education, nor the content of the article in detail beyond what it transmitted. This page gives the general lines of the texts as the summary put them. Whoever wants the provisions in detail should go to the texts in the Official Bulletin or the Moroccan legislation portal, from which the exact wording is known, which is not to be carried over by meaning. Reading it beside Arabic in Algeria shows two different methods: a constitutional text and a framework law here, a law specially for generalising use there.
## The Constitution, education and the organic law
The three texts show an order: the Constitution states the principle, the framework law carries it down into education, and the organic law fixes the implementation of Amazigh. That order is understood from the summary and not from the texts themselves. Whether Arabic as the principal language of teaching admits exceptions in particular subjects beyond what the summary says is a question that needs a reading of the text. The summary also does not give the number of years for generalising Amazigh or its extent.
