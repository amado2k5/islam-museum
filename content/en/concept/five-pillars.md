## Summary
"The five pillars of Islam" are the deeds on which Islam was built in the Prophet's hadith narrated by [[person/abdullah-ibn-umar|Abdullah ibn Umar]] {cite:sahih-al-bukhari-bugha p.14} {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.192}. In the version of [[person/al-bukhari|al-Bukhari]] they are the [[concept/shahada|testimony]] that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is God's Messenger, establishing [[practice/salah|the prayer]], giving [[practice/zakat|the zakat]], [[practice/hajj|the hajj]], and [[practice/sawm-ramadan|the fast of Ramadan]] {cite:sahih-al-bukhari-bugha p.14}. Their order differs between versions: in one version in [[person/ibn-khuzayma|Ibn Khuzayma]] the fast of Ramadan precedes the pilgrimage to the House, and elsewhere in his book the hajj precedes the fast {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.192} {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.906}. In the first, Ibn Umar cites the hadith to a man who asked him about his not going on campaign {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.192}.

The editor of the al-Bugha edition notes that Muslim recorded the hadith in the chapter "the pillars of Islam and its great supports", as number 16, and explains that these five deeds are to Islam as supports to a building, which cannot exist without them {cite:sahih-al-bukhari-bugha p.14}. [[person/ibn-rajab|Ibn Rajab]] makes it the third hadith of Jami' al-'Ulum wa-l-Hikam and states that al-Bukhari and Muslim recorded it in their Sahihs {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.121}. He explains that Islam is likened to a building whose supports are these five, without which it does not stand, while the other traits of Islam complete it {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.122}. Commenting on the hadith of Jibril, he divides these deeds into a deed of the tongue, the testimony; bodily deeds such as prayer and fasting; a deed of wealth, giving zakat; and one compounded of both, such as the hajj for someone far from Mecca {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.75}.

## The pillars in detail
- [[concept/shahada|The two testimonies]]: testifying that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is God's messenger {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.192}.
- [[practice/salah|The prayer]]: establishing the prayer {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.192}.
- [[practice/zakat|The zakat]]: giving the zakat {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.192}.
- [[practice/hajj|The hajj]] {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.192}.
- [[practice/sawm-ramadan|Fasting Ramadan]] {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.192}.

The order of the pillars differs between accounts: in another place in Ibn Khuzayma the hajj comes before the fast {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.906}.

## The hadith's source
Al-Bukhari recorded this hadith as number 8 (volume 1, page 12 of the al-Bugha edition); the editor notes that Muslim also recorded it in the chapter "the pillars of Islam and its great supports" as number 16 {cite:sahih-al-bukhari-bugha p.14}. The editor explains "supports" as being to the building in the place of pillars — it does not stand without them {cite:sahih-al-bukhari-bugha p.14}.

Ibn Khuzayma narrates it from Ibn Umar with the wording: "Islam is built on five: the testimony that there is no god but God, establishing the prayer, giving zakat, fasting Ramadan, and the Hajj to the House" {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.192}. Elsewhere in his book the Hajj comes before the fast {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.906}, which confirms that the order differs between the accounts.

## The pillars in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam treats the word behind "pillars" under the entry rukn. The plural, arkan, comes from a word meaning a corner, as in the corner of the Kaaba called the Yemeni corner, and by extension a support or pillar {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 642}. The singular occurs twice in the Qur'an, once for the strong support that Lot seeks and once for the retinue of Pharaoh {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 642}.

In religious and legal usage the article says the term appears in the phrases arkan al-din and arkan al-ibada, the basic pillars of religion and of religious observance. It lists the pillars of Islam as the profession of faith, the [[practice/hajj|pilgrimage]], the [[practice/salah|prayer]], [[practice/sawm-ramadan|fasting]] and [[practice/zakat|almsgiving]], and notes that some authorities add a sixth, [[concept/jihad|jihad]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 642}. The order of this list differs from the order of the hadith given above, which is one more instance of the variation among accounts. The article's bibliography also mentions a study by W. C. Smith which contends that arkan originally meant parts of the body {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 642}.

The same word has other uses in medieval science. In writings attributed to Balinus and to Jabir ibn Hayyan, rukn names a side or direction of the world, and in the Jabirian corpus it names the four natures, hot, cold, moist and dry, as the first simple elements; the article says it more often means an element in alchemical and cosmological writing {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 642} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 643}.

## Din, ibada and the pillars
The Encyclopaedia's entry on din gives three senses for the word: judgment or retribution, custom, and religion. It follows a French scholar in doubting the derivation from the Pahlavi word den, and describes din as the body of obligatory prescriptions given by God, to which a person submits {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 318}. It adds that the first of those obligations is submission, which links din to [[concept/islam-din|Islam]] in the sense of surrender to God {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 318}.

The article reports a well-known hadith in al-Bukhari that brings together three things under the teaching of religion: the contents of faith, [[concept/iman|iman]], the practice of Islam, and [[concept/ihsan|ihsan]], the inward dimension of faith; it says later authors often defined din by these three {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 319}. It then sets out how the schools put the emphasis differently. The Maturidis, it says, make faith part of religion, the Ashari school stresses the prescriptions to be observed, and for the Hanbali school the Qur'an and authentic tradition are the foundation {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 319}. Ibn Taymiyya is reported as quoting traditions that call the prayer the basis of religion, and the modern writer [[person/rashid-rida|Rashid Rida]] is reported as describing religion as worship together with avoidance of wrongdoing and just social relations {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 319}.

A separate entry, on ibadat, the plural of [[concept/ibada|ibada]], explains that the word means submissive obedience to a master and so religious practice. In books of [[concept/fiqh|fiqh]] it corresponds roughly to the ritual part of Islamic law, as opposed to mu'amalat, the dealings between people {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 679}. Its author cautions against translating it as "cult", arguing that fiqh is a statement of duties laid on the believer, and he adds that in practice and in feeling Islam clearly knows forms of worship {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 679}.

According to that entry, the books of fiqh place ibadat at the beginning, starting with ritual purity, then prayer, zakat, fasting and pilgrimage in that order. The first pillar, the [[concept/shahada|shahada]], is not given a chapter because it is pronounced at prayer and is so simple {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 679}. On [[concept/jihad|jihad]], the author says it is not an obligation on each individual but one that falls on the community, and is not one of the five fundamental obligations, though it clearly has a religious character; al-Shirazi, a Shafii, treats it at the end of his book, while the Maliki Khalil places it among the ibadat {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 679}. The article adds that the provisions on ibadat are in outline the same in the four Sunni schools and in the Shii and Ibadi schools {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 679}.

## For Sprouts
The pillars of Islam are five: the testimony of faith, prayer, zakat, fasting and the Hajj.

## For Explorers
The pillars of Islam are five: to testify that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is God's messenger, then prayer, zakat, fasting in Ramadan, and the Hajj for those who are able.

## For Scholars
The five acts on which Islam is built according to the sound hadith: the testimony that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is God's messenger, establishing prayer, paying zakat, fasting Ramadan, and the pilgrimage to the House for those able to make the journey. The pillars join the heart, the tongue and the body and the wealth. This order follows the well-known hadith of Ibn Umar in the two Sahihs. The details of each pillar differ among the schools of law, so each has its own entry. This entry gives what the hadith says without the detailed rulings.
