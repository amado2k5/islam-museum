## Summary
Ibn Sina closes the section on bones with the foot, an instrument of stability, whose bones are twenty-six, and then states that all the bones number two hundred and forty-eight apart from the sesamoids and the lam-shaped bone {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.59-60}.

## The foot
The foot was made an instrument for stability. Its form is lengthened forward to help standing upright, and it has an arch on the inner side so that its leaning toward standing, especially in walking, is toward the side opposite the raised leg, which steadies the stance, and so that stepping on things that stick up need not hurt greatly. It is made of many bones, one use of which is good gripping and enclosing of what it treads on, for the foot can hold what it treads on as the palm holds what it grasps {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.59}.

## Its bones
The foot has twenty-six bones: the ankle bone, which completes the joint with the shank; the heel bone, the mainstay of stability; the boat-like bone, which forms the arch; four tarsal bones joining the metatarsus, one of them the die-shaped bone, hexagonal, set on the outer side; and five metatarsal bones. The human ankle bone is more cube-like than in other animals, the noblest of the foot bones for movement, as the heel is for stability {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.59}. The heel is hard and rounded behind to resist blows and smooth below so the tread lies even. The five metatarsals join each of the five toes, firmness being needed there more than gripping {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.60}.

## The count of bones
Ibn Sina says he has said enough about bones, and that all of them, when counted, come to two hundred and forty-eight, apart from the sesamoids and the bone shaped like the Greek lambda. With this the first group ends and the second begins, on muscles, in thirty chapters {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.60}.
