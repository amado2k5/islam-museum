## Summary
The page presents four books on the cause of the Qur'an's inimitability: al-Nukat by al-Rummani, Bayan I'jaz al-Qur'an by al-Khattabi, I'jaz al-Qur'an by al-Baqillani and Dala'il al-I'jaz by Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani. Each author answers one question: what was it that the Arabs could not do? Then comes the sarfa view and the replies of al-Baqillani and al-Suyuti to it. The page gives each view under its author with his examples and does not rank them.

## Al-Rummani: seven aspects and ten parts
[[person/ali-ibn-isa-al-rummani|Al-Rummani]] says in al-Nukat that the aspects of the Qur'an's inimitability appear from seven sides: the leaving of the challenge unanswered although motives were strong and need great; the challenge to everyone; al-sarfa; eloquence; true reports about future matters; the breaking of custom; and its comparison with every other miracle. He then divides eloquence into three tiers: the highest is miraculous and is the Qur'an's, the lowest and the middle are within human reach, and eloquence is neither mere conveying of the meaning nor verbal exactness to the meaning {cite:nukat-rummani-1976 p.75}.

He divides eloquence into ten parts: brevity, simile, metaphor, euphony, rhyme-endings, assonance, inflection, inclusion, hyperbole and beauty of expression. Brevity has two kinds, omission and concision, and for omission he gives verses including [[passage/quran-12-82|Yusuf 82]], [[passage/quran-2-177|al-Baqara 177]], [[passage/quran-9-1|al-Tawba 1]] and [[passage/quran-47-21|Muhammad 21]]; he holds that omitting the answer is more eloquent than stating it, as in [[passage/quran-13-31|al-Ra’d 31]] and [[passage/quran-39-73|al-Zumar 73]] {cite:nukat-rummani-1976 p.76}.

## Al-Khattabi: the word, the meaning and the bond
[[person/abu-sulayman-al-khattabi|Al-Khattabi]] says that after reviewing and rejecting other proposed causes he reached the view that kinds of speech differ in eloquence: some is solid and strong, some easy, some free-flowing and fluent. The Qur'an's rhetoric takes a share from each, so it combines majesty and sweetness, which are normally opposed {cite:bayan-ijaz-al-quran-khattabi-1976 p.26}.

He then says humans cannot produce its like because their knowledge does not encompass all the names and words of the language, which are the vessels and carriers of meanings, nor all the meanings, nor all the ways of composition. Speech stands on three things: "a carrying word, a meaning that rests on it, and a bond that arranges them". These may be found separately in kinds of speech but come together only in the speech of the All-Knowing. His conclusion is that the Qur'an brought the most eloquent words in the best-arranged composition, containing the soundest meanings: tawhid, tanzih, the call to obedience, rulings, exhortation and ethics {cite:bayan-ijaz-al-quran-khattabi-1976 p.27}.

## Al-Baqillani: three aspects and the example of Imru' al-Qays
In his chapter on the sum of the aspects of inimitability, [[person/al-baqillani|al-Baqillani]] names three. The first is information about the unseen, which includes God's promise to make His religion prevail over all religions in [[passage/quran-9-33|al-Tawba 33]] and the lands conquered in the days of Abu Bakr and Umar. The second is that the Prophet was unlettered, neither writing nor reading nor knowing earlier books, yet brought the accounts of nations from Adam to his own mission; he cites [[passage/quran-29-48|al-Ankabut 48]]. The third is that it is wondrous in composition and marvellous in arrangement, reaching in eloquence a limit at which the incapacity of creation is known {cite:ijaz-al-quran-baqillani-saqr-1997 p.33} {cite:ijaz-al-quran-baqillani-saqr-1997 p.35}. He then says its composition lies outside the familiar forms of their speech, from the metres of verse to rhymed prose to free speech, and is distinct from their ways {cite:ijaz-al-quran-baqillani-saqr-1997 p.35}.

To show this he says the Qur'anic nazm is a distinct genus and a special style, and that whoever wishes to see its greatness should consider the faults of the best of Imru' al-Qays's poetry, verse by verse. He takes the opening, "Halt, you two, let us weep at the memory of a beloved and an abode", and reports that the poet's supporters call it wondrous because he stopped and made others stop, wept and made others weep, and mentioned the abode and the beloved. He faults it: asking another to weep at the mention of a beloved who is the speaker's own; naming places adds nothing and is a kind of inarticulacy; and saying that the trace has not effaced is a flaw, since had it effaced they would have rested {cite:ijaz-al-quran-baqillani-saqr-1997 p.159} {cite:ijaz-al-quran-baqillani-saqr-1997 p.160}.

## Al-Jurjani: nazm
[[person/abd-al-qahir-al-jurjani|Abd al-Qahir]] says in Dala'il al-I'jaz: "Nazm is only that you put your speech in the position that the science of grammar requires." He explains that the composer seeks only to look at the aspects and differences of each chapter of [[concept/ilm-al-nahw|grammar]], such as the variants of the predicate in "Zayd is departing", "Zayd departs", "departs Zayd" and "Zayd is the departing one", and the variants of conditional sentences {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992 1 p.81}.

On where the inimitability lies, he says that if the quality that made them unable is not in anything he has refuted, single words, their sound and the like, nothing remains but nazm, and then only nazm and metaphor remain; metaphor cannot be the basis, since it would confine the miracle to a few verses within the long suras, so it is in nazm and composition {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992 1 p.391}.

He analyses [[passage/quran-11-44|Hud 44]] as his example, and finds the inimitability in the connection of the words to one another, not in any single word. He lists that the earth is addressed and then commanded; that the address uses "ya" and not "ayyuha"; that the water is attributed to the earth by a pronoun; that the sky's command matches the earth's; that the verb for the water's subsiding is in the passive, signalling a commander and power behind it; that the settling of the matter is a confirmation; that the ark is referred to by a pronoun before it is named; and that the closing of the speech matches its opening. He concludes that words do not excel as single words, but by the fit of one word's meaning to the next {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992 1 p.45} {cite:dalail-al-ijaz-shakir-1992 1 p.46}.

## The view of sarfa
In al-Itqan, al-Suyuti reports that al-Nazzam claimed the inimitability is by sarfa, that is, God turned the Arabs away from opposing it and took away their minds; it was within their power, but an external matter hindered them, so it became like other miracles. Al-Suyuti calls this "a corrupt view", arguing that [[passage/quran-17-88|al-Isra 88]] shows their incapacity while their ability remained, and had ability been removed, their gathering would be like the gathering of the dead {cite:itqan-abu-al-fadl-1974 4 p.7}. He adds that the consensus holds the Qur'an itself to be the miracle, that sarfa puts the miracle in the removal of ability, and that it implies the inimitability ended with the age of the challenge; he quotes al-Qadi Abu Bakr that if opposition were possible and only sarfa prevented it, the speech would not be miraculous and the prevention would be {cite:itqan-abu-al-fadl-1974 4 p.8}.

Al-Baqillani gives an imagined objection: why not say that God turned them away? He answers that whoever could compose eloquent words could, by that logic, compose a whole speech, and that the people of the Jahiliyya, who were not challenged, never produced its like, so the claim of sarfa is plainly false {cite:ijaz-al-quran-baqillani-saqr-1997 p.29} {cite:ijaz-al-quran-baqillani-saqr-1997 p.30}. Al-Rummani counts sarfa among the seven aspects he lists {cite:nukat-rummani-1976 p.75}. The editor of al-Baqillani says al-Jahiz wrote a refutation of al-Nazzam in a lost book called Nazm al-Qur'an {cite:ijaz-al-quran-baqillani-saqr-1997 p.8}.

## What this page does not reach
Al-Zarkashi's al-Burhan was not read, nor al-Nazzam's own writings; the view of sarfa is attributed to him as al-Suyuti reports it. Of these authors' books only the pages cited were read. The verse numbers of the examples the authors give were identified from their wording and were not read printed on the pages.
