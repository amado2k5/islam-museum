## Who the vanished Arabs are
The Arab historians call the oldest Arab nations al-'Arab al-'ariba, and some call them al-ba'ida, the vanished, because they perished and none is left. Al-Tabari reports from Ibn Ishaq that 'Ad, Thamud, the 'Amaliq, Umaym, Jasim, Jadis and Tasm were Arab peoples whose native tongue was Arabic, and that the Arabs called them al-'ariba, while the sons of Ishmael were called al-muta'arriba, the Arabised, since they spoke the tongue of those among whom they settled. {cite:tarikh-al-tabari on the dispersal after Babel} Ibn Khaldun says the 'ariba are 'Ad, Thamud, Tasm, Jadis, Umaym, 'Abil, 'Abd Dakhm, Jurhum, Hadramawt and Hadura, and that some count Yaqtan among them too; they are also called the vanished, and none of them is left on earth. He opens by saying this is the oldest nation after the people of Noah, and that the accounts of the ages before them cannot be known except what the Book relates and what is transmitted from the prophets by revelation, whereas anything else has a broken chain. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar pp. 333, 338-339} In Muruj al-Dhahab the 'ariba, 'Ad, Thamud, 'Abid, Tasm, Jadis, the 'Amaliq, Wabar and Jurhum, are said to have died out, leaving only those of 'Adnan and Qahtan; those left of the vanished were counted among them and their lineages were effaced and their traces gone. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab OpenITI text vol. 1 pp. 188-189} In Ibn Kathir the Arabs before Ishmael are the 'ariba, many tribes among them 'Ad, Thamud, Jurhum, Tasm, Jadis, Umaym, Madyan, 'Imliq, Jasim, Qahtan and the sons of Yaqtan. {cite:al-bidaya-wal-nihaya on 'Ad and the Arabs before Ishmael}

## Each nation in the sources
- **'Ad, the first:** al-Mas'udi counts them the first to rule after Noah, distinguishes first 'Ad from second 'Ad, ascribes to Shaddad ibn 'Ad the building of Iram of the pillars as he set out elsewhere, and places their land in al-Ahqaf of Hadramawt. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab OpenITI text vol. 1 pp. 183-184 and 189-190} Ibn Kathir says they were Arabs who lived in al-Ahqaf, the sand hills between 'Uman and Hadramawt on a coast called al-Shihr, their valley named Mughith, that they are the first 'Ad and the second 'Ad is later, and that they were the first to worship idols after the Flood. {cite:al-bidaya-wal-nihaya on 'Ad} In al-Tabari they reached al-Shihr and perished in a valley called Mughith. {cite:tarikh-al-tabari on the dispersal after Babel}
- **Thamud:** in al-Mas'udi their realm lay between al-Sham and the Hijaz to the shore of the Abyssinian sea and their dwellings were at Fajj al-Naqa, houses cut into the mountains at about the size of the houses of his own day, which he takes as showing that their bodies were not like those of 'Ad, whose remains at al-Shihr indicate far greater size. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab OpenITI text vol. 1 pp. 184-185} Ibn Kathir says they are a well-known tribe named for their ancestor Thamud, brother of Jadis, sons of 'Abir ibn Iram, who lived after 'Ad in al-Hijr between the Hijaz and Tabuk. {cite:al-bidaya-wal-nihaya on Thamud} In al-Tabari they are at al-Hijr between the Hijaz and al-Sham up to Wadi al-Qura. {cite:tarikh-al-tabari on the dispersal after Babel}
- **Tasm and Jadis:** al-Tabari has them joining one another in al-Yamama, then called Jaww, and perishing there. {cite:tarikh-al-tabari on the dispersal after Babel} Al-Mas'udi says they destroyed one another in about seventy years of rivalry and the contest for leadership, and the Arabs made them a proverb. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab OpenITI text vol. 1 p. 189} Ibn Khaldun relays Ibn al-Kalbi's account, through Ibn Ishaq, that they lived in al-Yamama, then among the most fertile lands, and that the king of Tasm was a tyrant. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar pp. 340-342}
- **The 'Amaliq:** in al-Tabari 'Imliq, who is 'Arib, with Tasm and Umaym, are sons of Lawidh ibn Sam, and it is said 'Imliq was the first to speak Arabic when the tongues were confounded. {cite:tarikh-al-tabari on the dispersal after Babel} Al-Mas'udi says they rebelled in the land and God set the kings of the earth over them to destroy them, and that the Arab scholars trace them to a different line from that of those who attach them to the sons of Esau, the better known view. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab OpenITI text vol. 1 p. 189} Ibn Khaldun relays that the 'Amaliq of al-Sham are of the sons of 'Imliq ibn al-Yafad ibn Esau, and distinguishes them from the 'Amaliq of the Hijaz. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p. 342}
- **Umaym and Wabar:** in al-Tabari Umaym went to the land of Abar and perished there, and no one reaches it today since the jinn have overcome it. {cite:tarikh-al-tabari on the dispersal after Babel} In al-Mas'udi Wabar ibn Umaym settled the land of Wabar in the sands of 'Alij and perished for his transgression; he relays the Arab chroniclers' claim that the jinn dwelt there and guarded it, and says that this goes beyond what is reasonable and customary. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab OpenITI text vol. 1 pp. 224-225}
- **Jasim:** in al-Tabari they settled 'Uman. {cite:tarikh-al-tabari on the dispersal after Babel} Ibn Khaldun, on Ibn Ishaq, says that among the 'Amaliq is the nation of Jasim. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p. 333}
- **Jurhum:** al-Mas'udi counts them among the vanished, the people whose men had charge of the House at Mecca and neighboured Ishmael as in-laws until Khuza'a expelled them; Ibn Khaldun says the genealogists derive them from Yaqtan, and counts them among the 'ariba who are sons of Qahtan. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab OpenITI text vol. 1 pp. 188-189} {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p. 333} I did not find the expression "the first Jurhum" in the texts I read, so I do not use it.

## Where they differ
First, genealogy: al-Tabari, from Ibn Ishaq, makes Thamud and Jadis sons of Ghathir ibn Iram, whereas Ibn Kathir and al-Mas'udi make them sons of 'Abir ibn Iram. {cite:tarikh-al-tabari on the dispersal after Babel} {cite:al-bidaya-wal-nihaya on Thamud} Ibn Khaldun relays from Ibn Hazm that Lawidh is the son of Iram and brother of 'Aws and Kathir, which makes Tasm and 'Imliq brothers and cousins of 'Ad, where Ibn Ishaq makes Lawidh a son of Sam. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p. 333} Second, the origin of the 'Amaliq: Arab in the view of Arab scholars as al-Mas'udi gives it, descended from Esau in the view of others. Third, Umaym and Wabar: a fertile land guarded by jinn in the tellers' account, a picture al-Mas'udi finds beyond what is reasonable.

:::perspectives
- **Sunni view, the historians:** these are ancient Arab nations that perished before Islam; their accounts are genealogy and the tellers' reports, which each historian relays and weighs differently. {cite:tarikh-al-tabari on the dispersal after Babel} {cite:muruj-al-dhahab OpenITI text vol. 1 pp. 188-189}
- **Ibn Khaldun's caution:** the accounts of ages before what the Book relates have a broken chain, so they cannot be relied on as one relies on what is established by revelation. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar pp. 338-339}
- **Academic view, the Encyclopaedia of Islam:** the article on 'Ad says it is still an unanswered question whether a nation called 'Ad really existed, that the Arab genealogies of the 'Adites are worthless, and that Wellhausen thought 'Ad may originally have been a common noun for ancient time, the mythical nation arising from a misreading. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 188} For Thamud, Irfan Shahid's article sets out early outside evidence: an Assyrian inscription of Sargon II in 715 BC, Ptolemy's Geography, a bilingual Greek-Aramaic inscription of the second century AD at Ruwafa in the northern Hijaz, and the Byzantine Notitia Dignitatum of the fifth century, which lists two Thamudi units in the army, one in Palestine and one in Egypt; after that the tribe disappears from the sources, the poets of the century before the Hijra and the genealogists treated it as a thing of the past, and the article places it with Tasm and Jadis in tribal limbo. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 461}
:::

## What the inscriptions and the sites say
The same article records that "Thamudic", the name given to many inscriptions found in northern and central Arabia, is a modern conventional label for texts in unclassified Ancient North Arabian scripts, with no evidence that they have anything to do with the tribe of Thamud. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 461} As for al-Hijr, the Madain Salih of the Thamud tradition, the Encyclopaedia of Islam's article says it is an archaeological site matched with Pliny's Hegra and with a place Strabo named, with rock-cut tombs like those of Petra, with inscriptions in Arabic, Aramaic, Thamudic, Nabataean and other scripts, second only to Petra in importance in the Nabataean period, and that at the time the article was written no excavation had been carried out there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 385} Nothing I read ties those sites and inscriptions to a precise identification of any of the vanished nations except the historical existence of Thamud noted above, and I attribute nothing more to archaeology than the articles say.

## What we do not know
The lists and genealogies of the vanished Arabs remain the chroniclers' reports. Of these nations the Qur'an names 'Ad and Thamud, and mentions beside them the Companions of al-Rass, the people of Tubba', the Companions of the Thicket and Madyan; I found no name in the Qur'an for Tasm, Jadis, 'Imliq, Umaym, Jasim or Wabar, and their lineages all come from the chroniclers. Anyone who tries to place al-Ahqaf, al-Shihr or Wabar on the ground must separate the transmitted account from field confirmation, and I did not read a verified study of that.
