## Overview
Adab is a word whose history, in the account of the Encyclopaedia of Islam, mirrors the evolution of Arab culture from its pre-Islamic roots to the present day, in some respects more fully than the histories of [[concept/ilm|ilm]] and din. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194} The article traces how it moved from meaning inherited custom, to courtesy and good upbringing, to the broad culture of the educated person, to the specialised knowledge of an office, to literature in the modern sense. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 195} The record below also keeps the sense of Prophetic adab, the whole body of good manners that the believer is commanded to observe in his words, his deeds, and his relationship with God and with His servants. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam}

## The oldest meaning
In its oldest sense the article treats adab as close to [[concept/sunna|sunna]]: a habit or hereditary norm of conduct, a custom taken from ancestors and from persons held up as models, just as the sunna of the Prophet served the community in the religious sense. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194} It reports the etymology put forward by Vollers and Nallino, who considered that the plural ādāb came from da'b, "custom, habit", with the singular formed from it afterwards. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194} Native lexicographers, it adds, connect the word with a root meaning a marvellous thing, or a preparation or feast. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194} On either account the early meaning is a practical norm of conduct, praiseworthy and inherited. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194}

## Ethical and social adab
The article says that this earliest sense developed in two directions. One stressed its ethical and practical content: adab came to mean high quality of soul, good upbringing, urbanity and courtesy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194} It corresponds, the article says, to the refining of bedouin ethics and customs through Islam and through contact with foreign cultures in the first two centuries of the Hijra. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194} At the start of the Abbasid age adab in this sense was the equivalent of the Latin urbanitas, the civility and refinement of the cities as against bedouin roughness. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194} The word kept this ethical and social meaning throughout medieval Muslim civilisation, and it appears in the titles of treatises on etiquette: eating, drinking and dress, the boon companion, as in the treatise of [[person/kushajim|Kushajim]], disputation, and study, including the duties of teacher and student. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194}

## Adab as culture and learning
From the first century of the Hijra, the article continues, adab also acquired an intellectual meaning, at first tied to the ethical one and then increasingly distinct. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194} It came to mean the sum of knowledge that makes a man courteous and urbane: profane culture, distinguished from ilm, which here means religious learning, namely the Qur'an, hadith and fiqh. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194} It rested first on poetry, the art of oratory, the historical and tribal traditions of the ancient Arabs, and the related sciences of rhetoric, grammar, lexicography and metrics. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194} This humanistic adab was at first strictly national; the perfect adib of the [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad]] period excelled in the old poets and the battle-days of the Arabs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194}

## Widening under the Abbasids
Contact with foreign cultures widened this Arab humanitas into humanitas without qualification. It took in the gnomic and technical literature of the Indian, Iranian and Hellenistic worlds with which Arab Muslims came into contact from the early Abbasid period. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 194} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 195} The article takes [[person/al-jahiz|al-Jahiz]] as the most perfect example of the adib of the third and ninth century: cultivated in Arabic poetry and prose, maxims, proverbs, genealogy and the tradition of the jahiliyya, and also interested in Iranian narrative and epic tradition, Indian fables and Greek ethics and economics. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 195} Out of this came, in the article's words, the great literature of adab, which is not pure scholarship, although it often touches scientific subjects, but is centred on man, his qualities and passions, his environment and the material and spiritual culture he has created. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 195}

Al-Jahiz and his followers, among them [[person/abu-hayyan-al-tawhidi|Abu Hayyan al-Tawhidi]] and al-Tanukhi, drew on and extended an inheritance left by the Iranian [[person/ibn-al-muqaffa|Ibn al-Muqaffa]]. The article calls Ibn al-Muqaffa the true creator of this enlarged conception of adab, through his versions of foreign works, the Khuday-namak and [[work/kalila-wa-dimna|Kalila wa-Dimna]], and his original ethical and didactic tracts, al-Adab al-Kabir and al-Adab al-Saghir, though it adds that the authenticity of the latter is very questionable. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 195} It describes the literature of adab as the backbone of high Abbasid culture. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 195}

## Narrowing of the term
The article reports that the richness of this concept was reduced already in the Abbasid epoch. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 195} From the general culture expected of a well-educated man, adab took the specific sense of the knowledge needed for a given function. One could speak of the adab of the secretary, the title of a treatise by [[person/ibn-qutayba|Ibn Qutayba]], or of the adab of viziers, meaning the special knowledge and experience of the office. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 195} Eventually, it says, the concept lost its wide humanistic sense and was restricted to belles-lettres: poetry, artistic prose, proverbs and anecdote. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 195} It names [[person/al-hariri|al-Hariri]], with his verbal virtuosity and formal, purist interests, as an adept of this kind of adab, and judges that from humanitas adab had become the literature of the academy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 195} The article describes the later period as a long decadence of Arabic letters; this is its own assessment, not a verdict of the museum. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 195}

## In the modern age
In the modern age, the article says, adab, and still more its plural ādāb, are synonyms of literature in the most specific sense. Tarikh al-Adab al-Arabiyya is the history of Arabic literature, and kulliyyat al-ādāb is the faculty of arts in universities organised on the European model. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 195} Beyond technical usage, it notes, certain writers, Taha Husayn among them, consciously try to give the word back something of its former elasticity and breadth. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 195}

## According to Ibn Rajab
Ibn Rajab draws from the phrase "whoever believes in God and the Last Day" in the hadith of Abu Hurayra that these qualities belong to [[concept/iman|faith]], and that deeds enter into faith. He divides the three commands of the hadith into what concerns the rights of God, which is speaking good or keeping silent, and what concerns the rights of people, which is honouring the neighbour, refraining from harming him, and honouring the guest. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam}

He relates from Aswad ibn Asram the Prophet's counsel that a person should say only what is good with his tongue and extend his hand only to what is good, and that uprightness of the tongue is one of the qualities of faith. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam}

## Foundations of adab
Abu Amr ibn al-Salah relates from Abu Muhammad ibn Abi Zayd, the leading Maliki scholar of his time, that the whole of the manners of goodness branches out from four hadiths: speaking good or keeping silent, leaving aside what does not concern one, not being angry, and loving for one's brother what one loves for oneself. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam}

The concept is linked to [[concept/husn-al-islam|the goodness of Islam]]. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam}

## For Sprouts
Prophetic adab is the conduct a believer is commanded in word and deed toward God and toward people. Ibn Rajab draws from 'whoever believes in God and the Last Day' that it includes speaking good or keeping silent, honoring the neighbor, and honoring the guest.

## For Explorers
Prophetic adab is the sum of conduct a believer is commanded in word and deed and in relation to God and His servants. Ibn Rajab derives from 'whoever believes in God and the Last Day' that these qualities are of the qualities of faith, and divides the hadith's three commands into what concerns God's right — speaking good or keeping silent — and what concerns the servants' rights — honoring the neighbor, withholding harm, honoring the guest. He reports from Ibn Abi Zayd that the sum of good conduct branches from four hadiths: speak good or be silent, leave what does not concern you, do not become angry, and love for your brother what you love for yourself.

## For Scholars
Prophetic adab is the sum of conduct a believer is commanded in word and deed and in relation to God and His servants, as Ibn Rajab treats it in Jami' al-Ulum wa-l-Hikam. He derives from 'whoever believes in God and the Last Day' that these qualities are of the qualities of faith and that deeds enter into it; he divides the hadith's three commands into what concerns God's rights — speaking good and silence on all else — and what concerns the servants' rights — honoring the neighbor, withholding harm, honoring the guest. He transmits through Aswad ibn Asram the Prophet's counsel that a man should say with his tongue only what is known good and stretch his hand only to good. Abu Amr ibn al-Salah reports from Ibn Abi Zayd, the Maliki imam of his time, that the sum of good conduct branches from four hadiths: speak good or be silent, leave what does not concern you, do not become angry, and love for one's brother what one loves for oneself — the concept linking to husn al-islam.

## From Omar Abdelkafy's programme al-Munjiyat
