## Overview
Scrupulousness (*wara'*), in Ibn Rajab's commentary, is standing back from doubtful matters and guarding against them, leaving what causes doubt for what does not. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.261}

## The basis in the hadiths
Ibn Rajab finds this meaning in two of the Forty hadiths. The first is the hadith of al-Nu'man ibn Bashir that the lawful is clear and the unlawful is clear, and between them are doubtful matters. The second is the hadith of al-Hasan ibn Ali on leaving what causes doubt for what does not; he holds that its meaning returns to standing back from doubtful matters. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.172-192}

## Reports and sayings
Ibn Rajab cites a weak report from Wathila ibn al-Asqa' that the scrupulous person is the one who stands back from doubt, and another weak report that the scrupulous Muslim leaves the small sin for fear of the great one. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.260-267}

Among the sayings of the early generations in his view is the saying of Ibn Mas'ud on leaving what causes you doubt, and the saying of Umar, "Leave usury and what causes doubt," that is, what you doubt, even if you have not established that it is usury. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.260-261}

## In the fiqh literature

In fiqh: the lexical sense of wara' is restraint. Ibn al-Humam and al-Nawawi define it as avoiding doubtful matters; al-Dusuqi as leaving some permitted things for fear of falling into doubt; al-Ghazali and Ibn al-Qayyim add leaving what is known to be forbidden, out of fear of God. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17154} Jurists separate it from piety (taqwa), which is wider, from precaution (ihtiyat), which is wider still, and from asceticism (zuhd), which is about the heart rather than conduct. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17154}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17155} Al-Qarafi holds wara' recommended; it applies where a ruling is unclear because the evidence is hidden or conflicting, where the cause of prohibition is doubtful, or, as far as possible, to leave a scholarly disagreement. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17155}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17156} Ibn al-Shatt and al-Shatibi deny that leaving every disagreement is wara', since it would lead to hardship. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17158}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17159} Ibn Taymiyya lists three ways scrupulosity goes wrong: restricting it to avoiding the forbidden and neglecting duties, treating a natural distaste as a legal ruling, and weighing the harm of an act while ignoring a greater good it serves. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17159}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17160} Al-Ghazali sets four grades: the wara' of the upright, of the righteous, of the pious and of the truthful. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17161}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17162} In leading prayer the Hanafis put the more scrupulous after the most learned in its rules and the best reciter; the Shafi'is have two views on placing him before the most learned in jurisprudence; the Hanbalis put him after the ruler, the householder and the regular imam. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17163}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17164} Scrupulousness is recommended for rulers, judges and the market inspector; Ibn Taymiyya prefers, for judgeship, the most learned and most scrupulous, depending on whether the matter is plain or delicate. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17164}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17165}
