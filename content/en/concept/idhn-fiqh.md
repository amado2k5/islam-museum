## Meaning and related terms
In the lexicon idhn (permission) means releasing an act and allowing it, and the jurists do not depart from this sense. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2025}
Ibaha (permissibility) is the choice between doing and not doing without reward or punishment. The usulis list it among the kinds of the legal ruling, and the jurists explain ibaha in the same sense. They use idhn and ibaha as one, since permission is the root of permissibility: without something showing permission the act could not occur. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2025} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2026}
Ijaza is the approval of a thing, making it lawful and binding; idhn is permission to do an act and comes before the act, while ijaza comes after it. Amr (command) is a demand for an act from a higher, and every command carries permission by priority. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2026}
## Kinds of permission
Permission is general or specific. Generality and specificity may concern the person permitted, the transaction, the time or the place. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2026} General permission to a person is like one who throws something and says "whoever takes it may have it", or who puts water at his door so that any passer-by may drink, or who lets the Muslims stand their animals in a place, and the open invitation to a wedding feast. Specific permission is like one who says "this is alms for so-and-so", or who singles out one guest for a particular food. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2026} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2027}
A master's permission to his slave to trade is, for the Hanafis and the relied-on Maliki view, general over all trade except gifts, so that once given it is unlimited until he is restrained. The Shafi'is, Hanbalis, some Malikis and Zufar of the Hanafis restrict it to the kind, time or place the master named. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2027} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2028}
## Who grants permission: the Lawgiver
The Lawgiver's permission is either by text or by the ruler's ijtihad on matters of the people's welfare, observing the general principles of the law in bringing benefits and averting harms. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2027} It may be for ease and widening: sale, purchase, hire and pledge; the good things of food, drink, dwelling and clothing; marriage; hunting outside the state of ihram; reviving dead land; and the use of the public road and the public watercourse. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2028} It may be for worship and nearness to God, such as using mosques, graves and ribats. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2028} All of this is limited by the rule that there is no harm and no harming in Islam, and each act has rules and conditions whose breach voids it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2028}
The Lawgiver's permission may also lift hardship: the traveller and the sick may break the fast in Ramadan. The Prophet ordered the one who had vowed to fast standing in the sun to speak, to shade himself and to complete his fast when he learned of it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2028} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2029}
Permission may make lawful what is forbidden in itself under necessity, as carrion, pork or wine to clear a blocked throat when death or loss is feared, and what is forbidden for another reason, such as looking at a woman to marry her, or at a private part for treatment. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2029} The principles are that necessity makes forbidden things lawful, hardship brings ease, and harm is removed. Defence against an attacker is permitted, and there is no liability for killing him. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2029}
## The owner's permission
Ownership is a lawful tie between a person and a thing which makes it his to dispose of and bars others from disposing of it. Whatever belongs to someone may not be disposed of by another without his permission, except out of need, as a father or son may buy a sick person's necessities from his property. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2029}
The owner's permission to others takes several forms: permission to dispose, as in agency, the profit-sharing partnership and guardianship and the administration of endowments; permission to transfer ownership, as in sale, gift and endowment; permission to consume, as in food at feasts, coins scattered at celebrations, and a loan with a return; and permission to use, as in lending, hire and a bequest of usufruct. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2030} For permission to use, the owner need not own the thing itself; owning the usufruct suffices. It must be for a lawful purpose, and use must be as permitted or less harmful, or else it is trespass. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2030}
## The permission of the right holder
A right is a settled interest of a person under the law, financial or not. A transaction that touches another's right is not valid without that holder's permission. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2030} Examples: a husband's right to keep his wife from leaving the house without his leave, excepting the stronger rights such as the obligatory pilgrimage, treatment and visiting parents, on the schools' differences; a pledgee's right to hold the pledge, so the pledgor may not sell it without his leave, and according to the Hanafis such a sale stands on the pledgee's consent or on payment of the debt; the donor's right before delivery, so the recipient may not take the gift without the donor's leave, according to the Shafi'is; and the wife's right to intercourse, so a husband may not practise withdrawal with a free wife without her permission. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2030} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2031}
A woman has a right over her own marriage. A previously married woman's consent is required by consensus, and for a virgin the jurists differ between obligation and recommendation. The owner of a house has the right to bar entry without his leave, on the command in al-Nur (verse 27) about asking leave before entering houses. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2031}
## The judge's permission
Judgeship is a general office whose purpose is to establish justice and deliver rights to their owners. Some transactions therefore need the judge's permission to take effect, to prevent injustice and dispute. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2031} If a husband cannot pay maintenance, the Hanafis do not separate them; the judge fixes the maintenance and orders the wife to borrow, and the debt then falls on the husband if she borrowed on the judge's order. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2031} For the Malikis zakat is due on a minor's property, and the later scholars of the school hold that a guardian should pay it only with the ruler's permission, to leave the disagreement. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2031}
## The judge's permission in maintaining the absent
The Hanbalis say that a man who is away and leaves a deposit or the like and dependants may have them maintained from it only with the judge's permission; the maintenance of a foundling needs no judge's permission, though the judge's leave is the safer course. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2032}
## The guardian's permission
A guardian (wali) has power to act for another in person or property because of minority, foolishness, slavery or some other cause, and the interdiction lasts until its cause ends. Because the acts of one under interdiction may harm him or his master, the guardian's oversight and permission are needed to prevent harm. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2032}
Transactions of the foolish, the discerning minor and the slave in property and marriage need the guardian's permission according to the majority of jurists; Abu Hanifa does not place the foolish under interdiction. The Shafi'is, and one Hanbali view, hold that a minor's acts are invalid even with the guardian's permission, and the sounder Shafi'i view is the same for the foolish, though his marriage is valid. An insane person's acts are invalid even with permission. If a discerning person, a foolish one or a slave acts without the guardian's leave, the jurists differ between ratification, rejection and nullity. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2032} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2033}
A mature free woman needs her guardian's permission to marry, whether virgin or previously married, according to the majority, on the Prophet's words that "any woman who marries without her guardian's permission, her marriage is void". Abu Hanifa and Abu Yusuf, on the apparent narration, hold that a sane adult free woman's marriage is valid with her own consent, since she disposes of her own right; the guardian is asked to marry her so that she is not ascribed to shamelessness. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2032}
## The trustee's permission
The trustee (mutawalli or nazir) of an endowment looks after it, keeps and repairs it, and carries out the founder's condition. He acts only for the good of the endowment. Those to whom it is endowed, or anyone else, may not build or plant on it without his leave where he sees benefit. Collecting and distributing its revenue is his duty, and a collector, agent or teacher may not do so without his permission. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2033}
The usufruct of an endowment belongs to its beneficiaries, who may take it in person or through others by loan or hire, according to the Shafi'is, but may do so only with the trustee's permission. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2033}
## The licensed slave's permission
Jurists use the term licensed person (ma'dhun) mostly for a slave whose master has licensed him to trade, and they devote a special chapter to him. They differ on whether he may license another to trade. The Hanafis and Malikis allow it, since permission to trade is itself trade. The Shafi'is and Hanbalis do not allow it without the master's permission, which then makes it valid; for a general act they require the master's leave, while for a specific act such as buying a garment it is allowed. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2033} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2034}
A profit-sharing (qirad) agent is licensed by the capital owner to trade. Most jurists hold that he may not give his share to another without the owner's permission, which then makes it valid. The Hanafis hold that if the owner leaves the matter to him, saying "act as you see", he may do so without permission, but not if he has restricted him. The sounder Shafi'i view forbids it even with permission, while a second view allows it with permission, a view al-Subki strengthened and said most jurists hold. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2033}
## Conflicting permissions
When two or more people with a right to permit the marriage of the same woman stand in the same rank, such as brothers or paternal uncles, and dispute the matter, the Hanbalis and Shafi'is draw lots to end the dispute, since they are equal in right and cannot all act. The Malikis have the judge choose whoever he judges best among the guardians. The Hanafis let each marry her separately, if the suitor is a suitable match with a full dowry. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2034} If there is more than one suitor, her consent is taken, and if she names none, the judge considers the best choice. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2034} If two guardians of equal rank marry her to two men, the earlier contract stands and the later is void if the earlier is known; if the two contracts are made at the same time, or the earlier is unknown, both are void. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2034}
In a bequest to two executors, neither acts alone. If they disagree, the Malikis have the judge decide what is best. The Hanafis, except Abu Yusuf, let one act alone only when they were appointed by two judges of different towns; Abu Yusuf lets each act alone in all matters. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2034}
## How permission is given
Permission may be expressed in several ways. The explicit word is one, as when a father says to his discerning son, "I have permitted you to trade". {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2034} It may be by sign, writing or messenger; a dumb person's known sign is valid, and an invitation to a feast by letter or messenger counts as permission to enter and eat, on the report of Abu Hurayra that when one of you is invited and comes with the messenger, that is permission. Appointing an agent by writing or message is also permission. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2034} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2035}
## Silence and indication
Silence is not in principle permission, on the maxim that no word is attributed to one who is silent. There are exceptions. A virgin's silence before her guardian counts as permission, by consensus, on the report that women are to be asked about their marriages, and that a virgin's silence is her consent. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2035} The jurists differ on whether a guardian's silence when he sees his ward buying and selling is permission: the Hanafis and one Maliki view say it is, while the Shafi'is, the Hanbalis, Zufar and another Maliki view say it is not, since permission is a condition and silence may mean either consent or displeasure. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2035}
Permission may also be given by indication: serving food to guests, a master's buying goods for his slave and placing them in his shop and ordering him to sit there, and the building of water-points and inns for Muslims and travellers. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2035}
## Permission limited by safety
A rule holds that what results from a permitted act is not an injury and is not guaranteed, except where the permission is conditioned on the safety of the outcome. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2035} The Hanafis divide the rights of a licensed person into obligatory ones, such as the imam's right to carry out the prescribed penalty, retaliation and discipline, and the rights of a craft, such as phlebotomy, cupping and circumcision, which require safety of the outcome only beyond the usual limit; and permitted ones, such as a guardian's right to discipline. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2035}
