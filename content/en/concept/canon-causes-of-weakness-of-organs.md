## Summary
In chapter 29 Ibn Sina classes weakness of the organs by where the cause falls: on the organ's body, on the spirit that bears the power, or on the power itself, and then names remote causes such as spoiled air, water and food {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.152}.

## Three seats
The cause of weakness falls either on the substance of the organ, or on the spirit that carries the faculty that manages the organ, or on the faculty itself {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.152}.

## What belongs to the organ
Where the cause belongs to the organ, it is either a settled bad temperament, above all cold, though heat may do what weakens cold's action in numbing by spoiling the spirit's temperament, as happens to someone who stays long in the bath, even to one who faints; dryness blocks the powers from passing by condensing, and wetness by slackening and obstructing; or it is a disease of composition. Natural and voluntary acts are completed by the fibre and its arrangement, and digestion too needs good holding, which is by the fibre {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.152}.

## What belongs to the spirit and to the power
Where the cause belongs to the spirit it is either a bad temperament, or dissolving through an evacuation of its own or as following the evacuation of another thing. What belongs to the power is the multitude and repetition of acts, which weaken it, even if the dissolving of spirit accompanies it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.152}.

## Remote causes
Counting the causes another way, he brings in the remote causes, which are causes of the immediate ones: from these arise bad temperament, and also spoiling of air, water and food, and things that first frighten the spirit, such as stench, stagnant water and the spread of poisonous powers in the air or in the body {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.152}.
