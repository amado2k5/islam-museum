## Summary
Ibn Sina names the hot tumours by their humours and describes their four times and outcomes, then divides the non-hot by black bile, phlegm, water and wind into hardness, cancer, glands, wens, dropsy, puffiness and inflation {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.107-109}.

## The hot ones
It is their custom to call the purely sanguine phlegmon and the purely bilious carbuncle, and the mixture by a compound name, saying now a phlegmonous carbuncle, now a carbuncular phlegmon; when matter has gathered it is called an abscess; and when the abscess falls in soft flesh, the armpits, behind the ears or at the tip of the nose and is of a corrupt kind, it is called plague. Hot tumours have a beginning in which the humour is driven and the bulk appears, then increase and stretching, then a standstill at the greatest bulk, then decline and ripening, and the outcome is dissolution, collection of pus or change to hardness {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.107-108}.

## Those of black bile
Those arising from black bile are of three genera: hardness, cancer and the kinds of glands, among them scrofula and wens. Glands differ from the other two in beginning from what contains them or clinging only to its outside, like scrofula, while the other two mix with the organ's substance. Hardness is a still, hard tumour that abolishes or impairs sensation without pain; cancer is moving, growing and painful, with roots growing in the organs, and sensation need not be lost with it unless it lasts so long that it kills the organ; it is not unlikely that the difference between hardness and cancer lies in necessary accidents and not in essential differences. Glands and wens differ from a knotting of nerve in that the knotting is more tied to its place and feels nervous, returns when stretched by pressure, and does not return when dispersed by a strong drug; most come from fatigue and vanish with heavy things such as lead {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.108}.

## Phlegmatic, watery and windy
Phlegmatic tumours are of two kinds, the soft swelling and the soft wen, which differ in that wens are set apart in a sac and the soft swelling is mixed and not distinct; most winter tumours are phlegmatic, even the hot ones being white. They vary with the thickness, softness or thinness of the phlegm, so that now they resemble the black-bile kind and now the windy, and thin phlegm often comes down in catarrhs through the gaps in the nerve fibres as far as the lower muscles of the larynx and below. Watery ones are like dropsy, hydrocele and the watery swelling that arises in the skull. Windy ones are of two kinds, puffiness and inflation, which differ in consistency and mixing: in puffiness the wind mixes with the organ's substance, in inflation it gathers stretched and unmixed; puffiness yields softly to touch, while inflation resists the pressing hand much or little {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.108-109}.
