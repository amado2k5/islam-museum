## Summary
Ibn Sina completes chapter 8 with the effect of neighbouring mountains, seas, winds and the soil of a country on its air and water, and names the most temperate country and the most fitting winds {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.123-125}.

## Mountains
A neighbouring mountain acts in two ways: by returning the sun's rays to the country or screening them off, and by blocking the wind or helping it blow. A mountain to the north of a country takes the sun and its heat is reflected back; mountains in the west do this more than those in the east, since the sun draws nearer to them each hour. A mountain may keep out the cooling north wind or press in the heating south wind, and a country between two mountains and open to a wind has a stronger wind than an open plain, because air once drawn through a narrow passage keeps being drawn. The most temperate country, as to mountains, is open to the east and north and screened toward the west and south {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.123-124}.

## Seas
Seas add moisture to the lands beside them. A sea to the north helps to cool them as the north wind passes lightly over water that is cold by nature; a sea to the south adds to the thickness of the south wind; a sea in the east moistens more than one in the west, since the sun presses upon it with dissolving. When winds are many and no mountain opposes them the air is safer from putrefaction, and when they cannot blow the air is ready to rot and to make the humours rot {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.124}.

## Winds
The most fitting winds are the north, then the east, then the west; the most harmful is the south. The south wind in most lands is hot, moist and relaxing, since it comes from the side of the sun and from the seas. The north is cold and dry, having crossed mountains and cold lands of much snow. The east is moderate in heat and cold but drier than the west, and the west is a little moister for crossing seas and because the sun moves against it. These rulings vary with the country: a south wind may be colder if it crosses snowy mountains, and a north wind hotter if it crosses scorched plains {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.124}.

## Soil
The ancient scholars held that strong winds begin their movement from above even if their matter rises from below. Countries differ by soil: free clay, rock, sand, black mud, salt flats, and some in which a mineral strength prevails; all of this acts on their air and water {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.124-125}.
