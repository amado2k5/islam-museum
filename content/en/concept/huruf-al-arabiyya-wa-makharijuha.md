## Summary
This page sets two accounts of the letters of Arabic side by side: what the Kitab al-Ayn, attributed to [[person/al-khalil-ibn-ahmad-al-farahidi|al-Khalil ibn Ahmad]], says of the number of letters and their order by place of articulation, and what the Encyclopaedia Britannica says of the Arabic alphabet, its descent and its hands. Each statement is ascribed to its speaker, and the two numbers are not reconciled.

## Twenty-nine letters in al-Khalil
The [[work/kitab-al-ayn|Kitab al-Ayn]] opens its account with a chain in which al-Layth reports from al-Khalil: "Arabic has twenty-nine letters", twenty-five of them sound, with places and passages, and four hollow: waw, ya', the soft alif and the hamza. They are called hollow because they come out of the hollow, so they fall in no passage of the tongue, the throat or the uvula, and al-Khalil often said that alif, waw and ya' are airy, that is, in the air {cite:kitab-al-ayn-makhzumi 1 p.57}. Elsewhere it ascribes to him the saying that the Arabs have no structure, in nouns or in verbs, of more than five radical letters {cite:kitab-al-ayn-makhzumi 1 p.49}.

## The order of the letters by place
Al-Khalil orders the letters from the farthest in the throat to the lips. The farthest of all is the 'ayn, then the ha' and the ha', of which he says that, were it not for a roughness or a breathiness in one, it would resemble the one before it, and he sets the three in one place; then kha' and ghayn, all of them throat letters. Then qaf and kaf, uvular; jim, shin and dad, shajriyya; sad, sin and za', asaliyya; ta', da' and ta', nit'iyya; za', dhal and tha', lithawiyya; ra', lam and nun, dhalaqiyya; fa', ba' and mim, labial. Then alif, waw and ya' in one place, and the hamza in the air, without a place {cite:kitab-al-ayn-makhzumi 1 p.57} {cite:kitab-al-ayn-makhzumi 1 p.58}. The sound letters run, as in the text: ع ح هـ خ غ، ق ك، ج ش ض، ص س ز، ط د ت، ظ ذ ث، ر ل ن، ف ب م. A summary printed on the book's catalogue card differs from this order in the position of ha', kha' and ghayn; this page follows the text of the book, not the summary.

## Twenty-eight letters in the Britannica
The encyclopedia says the Arabic alphabet has 28 letters, all representing consonants, written from right to left; that it probably developed in the 4th century CE as a direct descendant of the Nabataean alphabet, its origins and early history being vague; that alif, waw and ya' mark long vowels; and that diacritics developed in the 8th century CE {cite:britannica-arabic-alphabet}. The page does not explain the difference between the encyclopedia's number and al-Khalil's. For the dots and vowel marks see [[concept/naqt-al-ijam|the dotting of letters]], and for the origin [[concept/khatt-al-arabi|the Arabic script]].

## The hands and the languages that took the script
The encyclopedia names two early types: [[concept/kufic-script|Kufic]], which appeared in the late 7th century in Kufa and was monumental, and [[concept/naskh-script|Naskh]], the flowing hand it calls the direct ancestor of modern Arabic writing and ties to Mecca and Medina; then later hands including [[concept/thuluth-script|Thuluth]], [[concept/maghribi-script|Maghribi]], Diwani, Ta'liq and Nasta'liq, the last being the primary hand for Persian, Dari, Pashto and Urdu. It says the alphabet was adapted to Persian, Turkish, Spanish and Swahili {cite:britannica-arabic-alphabet}. See [[language/persian|Persian]], [[language/turkish|Turkish]], [[language/urdu|Urdu]] and [[language/swahili|Swahili]].

## What this page does not reach
Ibn Khallikan reports that most scholars who know the language say the Kitab al-Ayn attributed to al-Khalil is not his composition: he began it and arranged its opening, then died, and his pupils, among them al-Nadr ibn Shumayl, completed it {cite:wafayat-ihsan-abbas 2 p.246}; the ascription of the order to al-Khalil should be read with that in mind. No modern works on phonetics were read, and the page does not set al-Khalil's places against the articulation of sounds in present-day phonetics. Only one encyclopedia article on the alphabet was read, a tertiary reference.
