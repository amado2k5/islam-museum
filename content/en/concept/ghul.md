## Overview
Al-Masudi, in *Muruj al-dhahab*, transmits what the Arabs claimed about the *ghul* (ghoul), a creature that appears to them in lonely places and takes various forms. This is a presentation of a belief that spread among them. It is not the museum's own account. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.469-472}

## The reports
They say that its legs are the legs of a she-goat. When the ghoul confronted them in the wilderness, they would recite rajaz verses, and it would flee from them into the bellies of the valleys. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.469-472}

Al-Masudi mentions that a group of Companions, among them Umar ibn al-Khattab, witnessed it. He reports that Umar saw it on a journey to Syria before Islam, and struck it with his sword. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.469-472}

## Other explanations
- Some philosophers related that it is an abnormal, deformed animal not governed by nature.
- A group in India held that they are persons who appear at the rising of the forty-eight constellations known as the bearers of the ghoul's head; Ptolemy and Abu Ma'shar mention them.
- The Arabs distinguished between the *sa'la* and the ghoul, and counted the *qutrub* and the *ghaddar* as mischievous types in Yemen, the Tihama, and the upper Egyptian Sa'id.

{cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.469-472}

Al-Masudi also gives, citing the historians, a lineage for the jinn and the places where they dwell. The details are in the book.

## In al-Jahiz's Kitab al-Hayawan
In the sixth part of [[work/kitab-al-hayawan|Kitab al-Hayawan]], [[person/al-jahiz|al-Jahiz]] gathers what Bedouins and poets say of ghouls. For him the ghul is a name for any of the jinn that waylays travellers and changes into all kinds of forms and clothing, male or female, though most of what they say treats it as female; the si'lat is the name for one of the jinn's women when she does not take on such shapes. {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v3 p. 397} He notes that the common people claim the ghoul takes the fairest of forms except that its foot must be a donkey's foot, cites a verse to this effect that a Bedouin recited to al-Khalil ibn Ahmad, and says: "I think they took this only from the Bedouins." {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v3 p. 426} In the chapter on fires he mentions "the ghoul's fire", which Bedouins claim it lights at night in sport and to lead wayfarers astray, citing verses of 'Ubayd ibn Ayyub al-'Anbari. {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v3 p. 67}

Al-Jahiz reports the explanation of these tales given by his teacher [[person/al-nazzam|Abu Ishaq al-Nazzam]]: when people settled in wild lands loneliness worked on them; whoever was alone and stayed long in empty places grew fearful, so that small things appeared large and he saw what is not seen and heard what is not heard; they then made this into poems they recited and stories they handed down, on which the young were raised, until one of them would say: I saw the ghouls and spoke to the si'lat — then, I killed her — then, I travelled with her — then, I married her. {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v3 p. 445} {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v3 p. 446} This is a Mu'tazili explanation that al-Jahiz reports from al-Nazzam.
