## Overview
In Manhaj al-Qur'an fi al-Iqtisad, Zaydan 'Abd al-Fattah Qa'dat holds that the Qur'an did not come with a detailed economic theory, but rather drew "broad outlines" that reason works to elaborate without excess or extremism {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 8}. For this reason, the book pauses first at the meaning of the word itself, viewing "economy" (iqtisad) in the Qur'an as a moral concept before it is a material science.

## Meaning of the Word
According to the author, the word iqtisad carries four interconnected meanings: balance, moderation, and the middle way in affairs—that is, choosing the optimal alternatives between opposites such as wealth and poverty or strength and weakness {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 24}. This meaning goes beyond transactions to encompass all relationships, for balance in his view lies between body and spirit, work and rest, and spending and withholding {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 23}.

## The First Lesson
The author reads the story of Adam in Paradise as "the first lesson in economics," since the first human was placed before a test among available alternatives, and the flaw occurred when he stepped outside the balanced, moderate circle {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 19}. He regards excess and extremism in behavior as the root of the fall, and holds that whoever follows their path has his flaw exposed before people {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 20}.

## Justice and the Three Existents
The author makes justice the pillar of the economy, because economy in his view means above all choosing ideal alternatives, and justice in word and deed is something neither the rightful claimant nor the debtor disputes {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 21}. He founds the just Islamic economy on three inseparable existents: God, matter, and the human being, which must be kept in balance {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 29}. Matter is an existence subjected to the human being by permission of the Creator {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 36}, and the human being stands midway between strength and weakness, owner and owned, containing good and evil, moderation and injustice, and must therefore balance what is within himself with what is in the universe {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 39}.

## The Moral Economy
The author calls what brings these dimensions together "the moral economy": if a thing is material, it must be used optimally without excess or negligence and distributed justly, and if it is non-material, one must adhere to moral value within the material work itself {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 25}. He states that the wealth of the universe belongs to humanity across its long journey, so any flaw in using it harms all relationships—with the Creator, with people, and with future generations {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 25}.

## The Author's View
The author writes with confidence that if the nation followed the Qur'anic paths in economics, it would become one of the strongest states {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 65}. This is his personal opinion and assessment rather than the result of measurement, and it is presented in the book as such. He holds that the Islamic economy serves both this world and the hereafter together, not material things alone {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 28}.
