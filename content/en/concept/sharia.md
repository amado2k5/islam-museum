## Overview
Sharia is the Islamic normative framework that takes in worship, dealings between people and ethics. Jurisprudence teaches it, and the schools and interpretations vary within it. {cite:wikidata}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam article on which this page draws explains that the word comes from a root whose main range of meaning relates to religion and religious law, and that the related word shar' is often a synonym. In the Arabic-speaking Middle East the word names a prophetic religion as a whole, which gives phrases for the law of Moses, the law of the Messiah, the Zoroastrian religion, or simply "our religion" said by the adherents of any monotheist faith. Within Muslim discourse it names the rules and regulations that govern Muslim life, derived in principle from the Qur'an and the hadith. In that sense it is closely tied to [[concept/fiqh|fiqh]], the scholarly discussion of divine law. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 334}

## In the Qur'an and the hadith
The article notes that the noun occurs only once in the Qur'an, where it means a divinely appointed way or path. A cognate noun appears once, beside a word for a road, and the verb occurs twice, once with God as the one who lays down religion and once in relation to those who set up rules of their own. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 334}

In the hadith corpus the singular noun is found once, in the Musnad of Ibn Hanbal, meaning a path on which the community remains. The plural occurs about a dozen times, mostly in phrases such as the rules of Islam, and the verb carries the sense of laying down law only once, in variants of one report about guidance for the Prophet. The article judges that this thin usage makes it unlikely that these sources mark the beginning of the term's development. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 334}

## Jewish and Christian usage
The article shows that the vocabulary was shared with other communities. In the Arabic translation of the Hebrew Bible attributed to Saadya Gaon, who died in 933, the word is the commonest rendering of the Hebrew torah, whether a single rule or the whole law given to Moses. In his theological work it names both individual laws and systems of law revealed through prophets, rational laws are set apart from revealed ones, and the question whether a later prophet's law abrogates an earlier one, naskh, brings the term up repeatedly. Christian writers used the same words, as in a tenth-century tract by the Jacobite Ibn Zur'a, where the Christian religion is called the law of the Messiah. The article holds that Arabic-speaking Jewish and Christian communities existed alongside or before early Islam, and suggests the shared vocabulary of the prophetic religions is best seen as the common achievement of several communities in polemical encounter between the seventh and ninth centuries. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 334} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 335}

## In Muslim literature
In Islamic religious writing the word and its relatives cover the same range: a rule of law, a system of laws, or the whole message of one prophet. As a system of laws it is a synonym of shar', which the article thinks is the commoner word in juristic writing for divine law. The one who lays down the law is usually Muhammad, as model and exemplar, and in a rare extension the term is applied to the jurists, drawing attention to the creative side of their interpretation, as in a passage of [[person/abu-ishaq-al-shatibi|al-Shatibi]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 335}

In theology, or [[concept/ilm-al-kalam|kalam]], the word comes up wherever the prophet's bearing of a message is discussed. [[person/al-baqillani|Al-Baqillani]], who died in 403/1013, asked whether a prophet confirms or abrogates the law of an earlier one, and used the adjective for revealed rules, probably to set them apart from rational ones. The article says Sunnis rejected a category of moral rules open to the intellect, but that their [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazili]] opponents forced the idea on them. [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|Al-Ghazali]], who died in 505/1111, describes Muhammad as having abrogated earlier laws with his own. In the Mu'tazili tradition the words kept their general meaning of a whole prophetic religion but were also used systematically to separate rational from revealed laws. The Shi'i scholar [[person/al-allama-al-hilli|al-Allama al-Hilli]], who died in 726/1325, counts it a benefit of prophecy that the prophet brings rules the intellect cannot reach, and holds that no period of time is without a prophetic law. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 335}

In Qur'anic commentary the cognate noun is usually said to mean the same as the main word. A comparison of two verses led commentators to distinguish the law, which differs from prophet to prophet, from din, the recognition of the one God, which is the same for all. The article cites [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]] reporting Qatada to the effect that the Torah, the Gospels and the Qur'an each have their own law, while the religion is one. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 335}

In juristic writing, the article says, several technical terms for a rule existed side by side, and the word sharia did not dominate in the earliest texts; general reference to the law is more likely to use shar'. The earliest work on the sources of law, the Risala of [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]], uses it little. A characteristic question was whether Muhammad was bound by the laws of earlier prophets, and al-Ghazali, in the Mustasfa, affirms one position current among jurists, that the law of the Prophet abrogated earlier systems. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 335}

The Hanafi jurist [[person/ibn-abidin|Ibn Abidin]], who died in 1252/1836, takes the word as meaning what is laid down or decreed. Calling the Prophet the law-giver is, on his account, a figure of speech, since in truth it is God who lays down the law. He treats the word as meaning the same as milla and din, though it may also apply strictly to the rules governing human acts. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 335}

## Sharia and fiqh
Fiqh is the scholarly discipline through which the jurists, the fuqaha, described and explored the law. It names a human activity and cannot be ascribed to God, and it is often attached to a scholar's name, as in the fiqh of Malik. The law contained in revelation, the Qur'an and hadith, is explained and elaborated by the interpretative work of these scholars. Since that is in practice the only way to reach the law, the two words are sometimes used as synonyms, though the first keeps the sense of the divine and the second that of the human. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 335}

Among the literature of fiqh, the article describes the genre of furu, the works that set out the detail of the law, as combining inventiveness with practical concern; it says the four major schools of law and the Shi'i tradition treat the genre in a broadly similar way. These works carry two kinds of justification: the relationship between the school tradition, the madhhab, and revelation, and the demonstration that later statements of the law derive from earlier ones in the same school. The article judges that the first dominates; jurists did not act as independent interpreters of revelation but submitted to the school and its eponymous founder, to which they were committed by an earlier loyalty, usually of birth or place. Their creative effort was called [[concept/ijtihad|ijtihad]], and the duty of submission [[concept/taqlid|taqlid]]. The founders' own ijtihad was absolute and independent, that of later jurists qualified and limited. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 337}

Two literary types answer the pull between expansion and concision: the mukhtasar, a concise epitome, for instance the [[work/mukhtasar-khalil|Mukhtasar]] of Khalil b. Ishaq, who died in 776/1374, and the mabsut, which multiplies detail and argument. The article reads the summarising and commenting as signs of loyalty to the tradition that also screened real change. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 337}

## Usul al-fiqh and ijtihad
Works of usul appeared in numbers from the early fifth/eleventh century, and the article singles out a group of Shafi'i scholars under the Seljuks: Ibrahim b. Ali [[person/abu-ishaq-al-shirazi|al-Shirazi]], [[person/imam-al-haramayn-al-juwayni|al-Juwayni]] and al-Ghazali, whose [[work/al-mustasfa|Mustasfa]] ordered the whole subject and decisively shaped the genre. The Risala of al-Shafi'i is called an apparently isolated early work with most of the features of a work of usul; contemporary scholars, the article says, judge it either a late school work, as Calder holds, or a work of limited achievement whose implications took time to discover, as Hallaq holds. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 337}

Such works usually have four parts: the categories of the law, its sources, the interpretative rules for drawing norms from the sources, and the theory of ijtihad. The categories include at least the five rulings, namely mandatory, preferred, permitted, disliked and forbidden. The sources always include the Qur'an, hadith and consensus, [[concept/ijma|ijma]], and may include the opinions of the Companions, juristic preference, [[concept/istihsan|istihsan]], and public welfare. The interpretative principles concern language and the working of analogy, [[concept/qiyas|qiyas]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 337}

Ijtihad literally means effort. Technically it is the utmost effort of a trained jurist, weighing all the relevant texts and principles, to find a rule for a particular situation. The article draws out what this implies: most details of the law are not known for certain but are a matter of skilled and pious deduction, which yields only opinion, yet the duty to search for and defend an opinion by argument is absolute. Because final decisions were still needed, the result of ijtihad was held binding on the mujtahid and on the untrained, the amma, who as muqallids follow the mujtahid. When a mujtahid answers a particular question he acts as a mufti, and his answer is a fatwa. The article says this body of thought belonged to both the Sunni and the Shi'i traditions and could be developed in varied, sometimes highly individual, ways. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 337} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 338}

## Sharia and practice
The article places the main social realisation of the law in education. With the rise of the [[concept/madrasa|madrasas]] in the fifth/eleventh century, fiqh was recognised as the chief aim of education, a position it kept until the traditional system declined in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and the Arabic curriculum helped form a pan-Islamic cultural elite. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 338}

The article cautions that a work of furu was never a set of rules for practice in the way statutes are: different jurists in one city wrote different works, and how the law was realised depended on family, quarter, city and the practice of a judge, governor or sultan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 338}

Some areas of law became administrative structures, chiefly the office of judge, the qadi, whose competence covered family law, the administration of endowments, [[concept/waqf|waqf]], the property of orphans and civil disputes. Political authority controlled his appointment, and the stringency of the rules was often thought to limit his efficiency, which led to parallel courts, called [[concept/mazalim|mazalim]] in early Abbasid times, that took a more pragmatic view and were close to government. Under the [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottomans]] the qadi was almost wholly integrated into government. Between law as theory and law in practice stood the mufti, a jurist who gave specific answers to specific questions; in many places and periods, notably under the Ottomans, the higher muftis were controlled and paid by the state. Their responses, the fatwas, are recognised as important evidence for the law in practice. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 338}

## Modern developments
The article names three factors, all at least partly due to Western influence, that affected the history of sharia from the mid-nineteenth century. First came secular education, aimed at first at the military and then at administrators and merchants, which reduced the numbers in the traditional system and marginalised its subject matter; the Shi'i centres, with more independent finance, survived better. Second, modern nation states produced law codes, constitutions and statute law. The Ottoman Majalla of 1876, a partial codification of Hanafi law, began a long history of selective codification of traditional law. The reformist ideas of [[person/muhammad-abduh|Muhammad Abduh]], who died in 1905, brought flexibility through renewed ijtihad, a loosening of school loyalty and a patchwork use of the tradition. A majority of Shi'i jurists supported the Persian Constitution of 1906, and the article says that throughout the twentieth century modern Muslim states built legal systems in which sharia has been one influence, Western systems being another and often dominant. The role of fiqh has varied, in how prominently it is invoked and in its real effect, which has always been greatest in family law. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 338}

Third, the article treats political opposition, whose ideology it says has been influenced by Western thought yet nearly always appeals to sharia as an ideal of social justice. In these contexts, it argues, the word is stripped of detail, complexity and tie to the fiqh tradition, and works as a demand for loyalty and unity, an ideal rather than a real system of government. It sees this pattern in the statements of the Muslim Brothers and of more recent fundamentalist groups, sometimes linked to the name of [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]], used for his argument for renewed ijtihad by return to the earliest generations, or to selected rules that carry a disproportionate ideological load. The article concludes that neither the practical nor the ideological history of sharia in the twentieth century draws on the cultural, philosophical and theological messages in the tradition, and that recovering them for the twenty-first century seems likely to fall to the modern universities of the Muslim world. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 338} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 339}

## The word in the dictionaries
The Arabic lexicographers, the article says, record two main non-religious fields. In pastoral poetry and hadith the verb relates to animals going to a permanent water-hole, and the noun is the area round it or the point of entry, a place rather than a road. A second field is stretching, extension and length: a fine string on a bow or lute, a ship's sail, a long neck. The article judges this second field, which is related to a Hebrew root meaning to stretch, the likely source of the sense of way, path and highway, from which the religious use came. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 339}

## Sharia in South-East Asia
A second part of the entry, by M. B. Hooker, treats South-East Asia, where Islam dates from the late fourteenth century. Pre-modern Malay texts usually combine fiqh with local custom, adat, in separate parts, one expressing human practice and the other God's will, and Javanese texts limit the Islamic element to promulgation by a Muslim ruler and the law of God. Major Arabic works, including those of al-Shafi'i and al-Nawawi, circulated widely and were used by judges, imams and in the pesantren. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 339}

In the colonial period, the article says, Islam lost its place at the centre of kingship and morality and sharia was narrowly limited. The Netherlands East Indies used separate legal regimes, Dutch law for Europeans and adat law for the native population, and from 1882 a Priests' Court for Java with limited competence, mainly family law. In the British territories sharia was recognised mainly in family law and religious matters, as a personal law dependent on the colonial authority. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 340}

Since the Second World War, the article reports, Islam could take an open political place in [[place/indonesia|Indonesia]] and [[place/malaysia|Malaysia]]. Indonesia kept and extended the religious courts and set up a Department of Religious Affairs, with no single Islamic code. [[place/singapore|Singapore]] and all the Malaysian states have enactments on the administration of Islamic law, with councils of scholars, Muslim courts and statements of substantive law, and Malaysia strengthened sharia by constitutional amendment in 1988. In the [[place/philippines|Philippines]] the Code of Muslim Personal Laws was proclaimed in 1977. The article sees a recent trend toward restating sharia in a more classically accurate form, which would bring the region's legal history full circle from Arabic to Malay and back to the Arabic sources, but it adds that sharia there depends on the authority of a secular state. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 340} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 341}
