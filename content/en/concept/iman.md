## Summary
"Faith" in Gabriel's hadith is, as the Prophet explained: "That you believe in God, His angels, His book, the meeting with Him, His messengers, and that you believe in the resurrection" {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.1070}. It is the first of the questions in the hadith, followed by [[concept/islam-din|Islam]] and then [[concept/ihsan|ihsan]].

## Its definition in Gabriel's hadith
In Abu Hurayra's account the questioner, who is Gabriel in the form of a man {cite:sahih-al-bukhari-bugha p.36}, asks "What is faith?" and the Prophet answers with the six things: God, the angels, the books, the meeting with God, the messengers, and the resurrection {cite:sahih-ibn-khuzayma p.1070}. The Qur'an has a verse that mentions some of these: [[passage/quran-2-285|al-Baqara 285]].

## Its definition in the 'Aqida al-Tahawiyya
Abu Ja'far al-Tahawi says: "Faith is affirmation by the tongue and assent in the heart" {cite:aqida-tahawiyya p.21}. For the detail see [[concept/ahl-al-qibla|the people of the qibla and no takfir for sin]].

## Its definition in Lum'at al-I'tiqad
Ibn Qudama al-Maqdisi says: "Faith is saying with the tongue, acting with the limbs, and conviction in the heart; it increases with obedience and decreases with disobedience" {cite:lumat-al-itiqad p.28}. He supports it with the verse of Surat al-Bayyina that worshipping God, sincerity of heart, establishing prayer and giving zakat are of the religion ([[passage/quran-98-5|al-Bayyina 5]]) {cite:lumat-al-itiqad p.28}, and with the hadith "Faith is some seventy branches — the highest the testimony that there is no god but God, the lowest removing harm from the path" {cite:lumat-al-itiqad p.28}. His wording is nearer to al-Bukhari's "speech and action" {cite:sahih-al-bukhari-bugha p.13}, and differs from al-Tahawi's, which confines faith to affirmation and assent.

## In Ibn Taymiyya's al-'Aqida al-Wasitiyya
Ibn Taymiyya says: "Among the principles of the people of the Sunna is that religion and faith are word and deed: the word of heart and tongue, the deed of heart, tongue and limbs; and that faith increases with obedience and decreases with sin" {cite:aqida-wasitiyya p.22}. Of the sinner among the people of the creed he says: "They do not strip the sinful believer of faith entirely, nor hold him in the Fire forever"; for him he is "a believer deficient in faith" or "a believer in his faith, a sinner by his major sin" — neither given the unqualified name nor stripped of the name unqualifiedly {cite:aqida-wasitiyya p.23}.

## In al-Ash'ari's Maqalat al-Islamiyyin
In "the account of the sum of the saying of the people of hadith and sunna" in Maqalat al-Islamiyyin, Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari presents what the people of hadith and sunna hold, ending: "And in all we have mentioned of their saying we speak, and to it we go" {cite:maqalat-islamiyyin p.350}. He relates there that faith for them "is belief in God, His angels, His books and His messengers, and in the decree — its good and evil, its sweet and bitter"; and that Islam is the testimony that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is God's messenger; and that "Islam, for them, is not faith" {cite:maqalat-islamiyyin p.347}. Then he says: "And they affirm that faith is word and deed, increasing and decreasing; they do not say created nor uncreated" {cite:maqalat-islamiyyin p.347}. This is near in wording to the definition of faith in Lum'at al-I'tiqad and al-Wasitiyya, differing from al-Tahawi's wording.

## In Ibn Abi al-Izz al-Hanafi's Sharh al-Tahawiyya
Ibn Abi al-Izz says: "People have differed much over what the name faith applies to": Malik, al-Shafi'i, Ahmad, al-Awza'i, Ishaq ibn Rahawayh and the rest of the people of hadith, of Medina and of the Zahiris, and a group of the mutakallimun held it is "conviction in the heart, affirmation by the tongue and action by the limbs"; "many of our companions" held what al-Tahawi stated; some of them say the tongue's affirmation is "an extra pillar, not original" — to which Abu Mansur al-Maturidi inclined {cite:sharh-tahawiyya-ibn-abi-al-izz p.271}. He mentions the Karramis' school, which holds affirmation by the tongue alone {cite:sharh-tahawiyya-ibn-abi-al-izz p.271}.

## In Ibn Batta al-Ukbari's al-Ibana al-Kubra
In the Book of Faith of *[[work/ibana-al-kubra-ibn-batta|al-Ibana al-Kubra]]*, the Hanbali [[person/ibn-batta-al-ukbari-ubayd-allah|Ibn Batta al-Ukbari]] has a chapter headed: faith is assent of the heart, affirmation of the tongue and action of the limbs, and no one is a believer without all three. {cite:ibana-kubra-muti-iman p. 760} He states that God made knowledge of Him and assent to Him, His messengers and His books obligatory on the heart, utterance and affirmation on the tongue, and action on the body. None of these suffices without the others, and he adds the condition of following the Sunna in word and deed. {cite:ibana-kubra-muti-iman p. 761} He follows this with chapters on the increase and decrease of faith and on [[question/may-one-say-i-am-a-believer-god-willing|the exception in faith]]. {cite:ibana-kubra-muti-iman p. 52}

## Iman in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam treats iman in one article that covers the word, the elements of the act of faith, its content, its value and several related questions. The article explains that the Arabic word is the verbal noun of the fourth form of a root whose senses are being secure, trusting and turning to. From the same root come good faith, fidelity and protection granted, and the fourth form means both to believe and, with a preposition, to protect and place in safety. The root is among the most frequent in the vocabulary of the Qur'an, where iman denotes sometimes the act of faith, sometimes its content and sometimes both together. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1203}

## The elements of the act of faith
According to the article, the schools of theology and of law asked very early what it means to believe, and kept returning to the question. Three elements come together in the act of faith: inner conviction, verbal expression and the performance of prescribed works. The solutions overlap, and the article stresses that each term in a definition must be read in relation to the school or tendency that uses it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1203}

## The Ash'ari position
The article says that the [[concept/ashariyya|Ash'ari school]] stresses conviction, or inner judgement. It finds two ideas of faith in [[person/al-ashari|al-Ash'ari]] himself. One appears in the creeds of the *[[work/al-ibana-ashari|Ibana]]* and the *[[work/maqalat-islamiyyin|Maqalat]]*, which define faith, in the Hanbali manner, as words and works. The other appears in the *[[work/al-luma-ashari|Luma]]*, which says that faith is tasdiq in God, tasdiq being an inner judgement of truthfulness that gives its adherence to God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1203}

The vocabulary varies. [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|Al-Ghazali]] speaks of a pact, an agreement of the heart, and [[person/al-sharif-al-jurjani-ali-ibn-muhammad|al-Jurjani]] prefers firm adherence. Yet the school as a whole takes the formal constituent of faith to be the conviction of the heart, or of the intellect. The verbal profession of it is the required condition unless it is impossible, and the actions of the limbs serve to perfect it. When al-Ghazali, in the *[[work/ihya-ulum-al-din|Ihya]]*, joins the three elements, defining faith as tasdiq plus speech plus action, the article judges that he cannot be said to depart from the Ash'ari position. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1203} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1204}

## The Hanafi and Maturidi position
In the Hanafi-Maturidi tendencies, the article says, the stress moves from conviction to speech, without dropping tasdiq, and the knowledge of the heart is added. It cites the first article of the Wasiyya attributed to [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]], which names confession by the tongue, inner conviction and knowledge of the heart, and article 18 of the Fiqh Akbar II, which names confession and tasdiq. The expressed profession then appears as the constituent of the act of faith, and the conviction given to it as its condition. Some manuscripts of the Wasiyya lack tasdiq, and more often lack knowledge. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1204}

The article calls the appeal to the knowledge of the heart characteristic of these tendencies. Al-Ash'ari, in the *Maqalat*, even saw it as the first element of faith for Abu Hanifa. Wensinck, the article notes, suggested a possible continuity with the definition of the [[group/murjia|Murji'a]], who made faith consist of knowledge of God, of the Prophet and of his teaching; the *Maqalat* mention in this connection [[person/jahm-ibn-safwan|Jahm ibn Safwan]] and the [[group/jahmiyya|Jahmiyya]]. Al-Ash'ari describes the Hanafiyya as a Murji'i sect, while granting that, unlike the Jahmiyya, they include speech in faith, after knowledge. The brief Fiqh Akbar II, naming only verbal confession and inner conviction, became the main theme of the Maturidi line, and the article says al-Jurjani seems to agree with it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1204}

## Works as part of faith
The article says Ash'ari texts mention the works of the limbs, in contrast to the Murji'a, for whom works are only ways of living the faith. The Ash'aris do not exclude works from faith, but do not make them a formal constituent or even an obligatory condition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1204}

It describes an earlier attitude, later challenged by Sunnism, in which the [[group/khawarij|Kharijites]], the Shia, the Qadaris and the [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazila]] joined: works, meaning the pillars of Islam including the profession of faith and the works the Qur'an prescribes, are an integral part of faith, even faith itself. On this view the unrepentant sinner is doomed to hell because his disobedience has made him abandon his faith. The article sets aside the differences between the Kharijites and the Mu'tazila, whose intermediate rank for the believing sinner is dealt with under the entry on the [[concept/fasiq|fasiq]], and says that for both, works are not merely a sign or a perfecting of faith but faith itself. For the Kharijites faith and works are interchangeable; for the Mu'tazila the works are the witness that constitutes faith, itself a witness rendered to God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1204}

It quotes the judge [[person/qadi-abd-al-jabbar|Abd al-Jabbar]] for the view that faith and religion are one and the same thing. [[person/al-jubbai|Al-Jubba'i]] and his son [[person/abu-hashim-al-jubai|Abu Hashim]] defined faith only as the fulfilment of the prescribed obediences, whereas [[person/abu-al-hudhayl-al-allaf|Abu al-Hudhayl al-Allaf]], followed by Abd al-Jabbar, included supererogatory works as well. To omit a prescribed duty deliberately is, in this scheme, to cease to bear witness to faith, while failing deliberately in a secondary commandment only tarnishes the purity of the witness. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1204}

## The Hanbali position and Ibn Taymiyya
The article says the [[group/hanbali-madhhab|Hanbali]] line insists on faith as something expressed. It is vehemently opposed to the Murji'a and, without making works the only pillar, gives them a place in its definitions. It cites [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]] for a definition that includes words, works, right intention and attachment to the Sunna, and [[person/ibn-batta-al-ukbari-ubayd-allah|Ibn Batta]] for one that includes stating the message with the tongue, adhering to it with the heart and fulfilling the pillars of Islam. Tasdiq is here, the article says, less a judgement of veracity than something close to right intention. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1204}

[[person/abu-bakr-al-kalabadhi|Al-Kalabadhi]] turned this Hanbali list into what he called the doctrine of the Sufis: faith is word, act and right intention. Many Hanbali texts, however, prefer to name only words and works, the same terms as the creeds of al-Ash'ari and the one used by the Wahhabi creed. The emphasis falls on visible and audible witness, which is valid before God only when rooted in the heart. This is the import of the *[[work/kitab-al-iman-ibn-taymiyya|Kitab al-Iman]]* of [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]], whose main argument is repeated in his *Kitab al-Furqan*. Faith must be expressed in words and in works of the limbs, but it must also arouse in the heart fear of God, trust in Him, humility and patient endurance. The article agrees with H. Laoust's summary that faith in Ibn Taymiyya's doctrine is all-embracing. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1204}

The article adds that Ibn Taymiyya may have accepted certain influences from Shii thought, which he opposed; if so, he separated them from their gnostic tendencies and set them in a Sunni context. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1204}

## The Ikhwan al-Safa
As a comparison, the article turns to the moderate Ismaili thought of the [[group/ikhwan-al-safa|Ikhwan al-Safa]] in their *[[work/rasail-ikhwan-al-safa|Rasa'il]]*. There it finds a distinction between external faith, which is verbal affirmation, and internal faith, which is the true faith: the innermost thoughts of the heart brought to bear, with experienced certainty, on the truths professed by the tongue. It is no longer adherence or a judgement of veracity but an intellectual conception that realises the certainty of faith's object. The article compares the certainty preached by al-Ghazali, who calls the reality of faith an experienced taste of inner evidence. The Ikhwan combine this certainty with trust, sincerity and patience. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1204}

## The content of faith
The article says the Hanbali school insisted, against the Murji'a and the Jahmiyya, on a faith that is expressed. Faith is not knowledge alone but must be made alive by the intention or tasdiq of the heart and give witness in words and works. Knowledge, understood as the object of faith, is not ignored, but knowledge alone does not constitute faith: the article draws a distinction between the act of faith and its content. Ibn Batta is quoted as saying that faith in God is adherence to all that God has said and ordered, to all the duties and prohibitions He has laid down, and to all that He instructed His prophets to transmit and revealed in His books. More briefly, the content of faith is the Qur'an itself, summed up in the two limbs of the [[concept/shahada|shahada]]: the unity of God and the mission of Muhammad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1205}

The manuals often distinguish the necessary beliefs from those a man may ignore without ceasing to be a Muslim. The essential beliefs are readily listed from a famous Qur'anic verse that names God, the angels, the books and the messengers, with the Last Day added from another verse, and from three hadiths. One is the hadith of Gabriel, reported by al-Bukhari, which lists belief in God, His angels, the future life, the prophets and the resurrection. A second adds belief in the divine decree, good and evil, sweet and bitter. A third says a man has no faith unless he bears witness that there is no divinity but God, believes that Muhammad is the messenger of God charged with teaching the truth, and believes in the resurrection after death and in the divine decree. The exact list varies somewhat between schools and sometimes between authors, but it always refers in essentials to the verse and these three hadiths. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1205}

The article also notes that the essential content of the Shii conception of faith remains close to the Sunni one, with doctrinal points peculiar to Shiism added. In Ismailism especially it adds an interpretation of the nature of angels and of prophecy that follows an emanatist and monist view of the order of the world. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1205}

## Faith, freedom and salvation
The article takes up three problems about the value of faith. The first is faith and freedom, which depends on the wider question of free human action, so that schools assess it differently. The Mu'tazila regard the act of faith as created by man through a power God created in him. The Ash'aris regard it as created directly by God in the human heart, through the tawfiq that is itself the creation in man of the power to obey. The article remarks that the definition of tawfiq it cites from [[person/saad-al-din-al-taftazani|al-Taftazani]] is more Ash'ari than Maturidi. The Maturidi Hanafis, in general, hold that the root of every human act is created by God while its qualification arises from man's judgement, an attempt to reconcile God's creation of actions with the free will the Qur'an recognises. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1205}

The second problem is faith and salvation. All the schools state that faith ensures salvation, and their differences over its conditions follow from their differences over the formal constituents of the act of faith. For the Ash'aris it is centred on inner tasdiq; for the Maturidi Hanafis on the expressed profession and the adherence of the heart; for the Mu'tazila on performing the prescribed duties; for the Hanbalis and the Wahhabis on the profession and the performance of the basic duties. As a common denominator the article offers the idea that iman, summed up in the shahada, is the witness made to God that He is Lord, according to the pact of pre-eternity. On that basis the schools can agree on the well-known hadiths that no one with an atom of faith in his heart will remain in hell. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1205}

The interpretations nevertheless diverge. For those who regard the performance of works as an integral part of faith, the sinner guilty of grave disobedience is no longer truly a believer. For those who regard works as only the perfection of faith, the believing sinner remains a believer, may be punished for a time in hell, and will in the end be among the guests of paradise. The first opinion is that of the Kharijites and, with the nuance of the intermediate state, of the Mu'tazila; the second is held, generally speaking, by the Sunnis. The article adds that the Maturidis consider that the believing sinner will certainly undergo temporary punishment, while the Ash'aris consider that he may be pardoned. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1205}

## Uncreated faith
The third problem arises from the insistence of tradition on an atom of faith. Schools that stress conviction or tasdiq generally make this immutable nucleus a created response to the uncreated faith of God. The article admits this may seem surprising, yet says Ash'ari authors insist on it. They take the uncreated faith of God to be the attestation He gives to Himself in the Qur'an. In the list of the ninety-nine beautiful names, [[person/adud-al-din-al-iji|al-Iji]] and al-Jurjani, among others, first explain the name al-Mu'min in this sense, that God adheres to Himself and to His prophet. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1205}

This meaning does not exclude the other sense of mu'min, one who gives security and protection, so that God is the source of security and the Protector. [[person/fakhr-al-din-al-razi|Fakhr al-Din al-Razi]], while mentioning the first, seems to prefer the second. For the majority of the Ash'aris, the atom of faith on which salvation depends is readily considered a created participation in God's uncreated faith, placed in the human heart at the time of the pact. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1205} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1206}

## Can faith increase and decrease?
The article lists further questions raised by faith: the status of the believer in this world and the next, the relation of faith to unbelief and to islam, whether a person may hide his faith, whether the proviso of God's will should be added to the statement that one is a believer, whether faith can increase and decrease, and its degrees. The first five, it says, are treated in separate entries, among them [[concept/mumin|mu'min]], [[concept/kufr|kufr]], [[concept/islam-din|islam]] and [[concept/taqiyya|taqiyya]]; it discusses only the last two. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1206}

On increase and decrease, the article notes that the Qur'an mentions an increase of faith several times, yet some opinions present faith as immutable. The Kharijites and the [[group/karramiyya|Karramiyya]] consider that faith is given whole and is kept or lost entirely, according to whether obedience to the law is kept or lost. The Murji'a and the Maturidi Hanafis also consider faith immutable, but not in the Kharijite sense. The Wasiyya of Abu Hanifa is quoted as saying faith cannot grow or diminish, since its weakening could only be conceived with a growth of unbelief, which would let a person be believer and unbeliever at once. Later Maturidi manuals defended this thesis against the Ash'aris. It implies a radical distinction between faith and works: omitting an obligatory work is disobedience but does not touch faith, either in itself or in its perfection. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1206}

A very early tradition, the article says, admitted variations of faith, in keeping with the Qur'an, and the majority of schools followed it. [[person/al-bukhari|Al-Bukhari]] and [[person/ibn-majah|Ibn Majah]], citing the Companions, stated this in their introductions to the chapter on faith. For the Mu'tazila, faith is the witness of works and therefore varies, rising with the performance of prescribed works and falling with their omission. Ibn Hanbal holds faith, defined by word and works, to be open to growth and decrease, and Hanbali thought is unanimous on it. Ibn Batta says inner faith grows with works and words of goodness and decreases with disobedience, and that it can progress without end. The article finds the same view in Ibn Taymiyya, among the Wahhabis, and in the creeds of the *Maqalat* and *Ibana* of al-Ash'ari. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1206}

The whole Ash'ari school upheld growth and decrease, stressing that this concerns the degree of perfection works bring. Following al-Bajuri, the article reports that God's uncreated faith and the faith of the angels can neither increase nor diminish; the faith of the prophets may grow as their mission is fulfilled but cannot decrease, because of their infallibility; only the faith of ordinary people can both increase and decrease. Since the school defines the formal constituent of faith as inner tasdiq, it distinguished at the root of faith an immutable nucleus, the atom of faith, created by God in the heart, which can be lost entirely by an act of unbelief but cannot vary. The Ash'aris thus admit both the immutability of the main nucleus, as the Maturidi Hanafis do, and variability in the degree of perfection, as the Hanbalis do. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1206}

## The degrees of faith
This second question, the article says, is related to the first but different. It concerns not higher and lower levels of faith within one person according to obedience and disobedience, but the degrees of faith according to its intrinsic nature in different people. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1206}

The Ash'ari and Shafi'i lines took [[concept/taqlid|taqlid]] to be unreasoning, passive acceptance and were severe on faith through taqlid, holding it valid only for someone unable to rise higher. The Hanbalis defined taqlid as intentional, conscious imitation of the Prophet, the Companions and their successors, and regarded it as a fundamental attitude of the believer. [[person/ibn-aqil|Ibn Aqil]], however, was wary of it, fearing that imitation would replace the search for proofs. Most manuals of [[concept/ilm-al-kalam|kalam]] rank far above it an enlightened faith based on knowledge that proves its object by the arguments of the theologians. That faith was attacked by both Hanbalis and philosophers. Al-Ghazali named a third, higher degree, the faith of certitude, which for him alone is true faith, and the article sees here probable influence of Shiism and Sufism, as with the inner faith of the Ikhwan al-Safa. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1206}

The article sees the same influences probably at work in Ibn Taymiyya. After defining faith through islam, meaning the proclamation of the shahada and the performance of the basic duties, and listing the feelings it produces in the believer's heart, he distinguishes in ascending order the faith of the friend of God, [[concept/wali|the wali]], that of the siddiq, the most truthful, and that of the prophet. Starting from what Laoust calls conformist ritualism, faith in Ibn Taymiyya's account ends in virtuous conduct, in sincerity and unadulterated purity, and in the annihilation of the created will in total submission to the divine command. The article closes by noting that a complete bibliography on faith would be immense, since it would include the professions of faith of the Sunni schools and the sects, almost all the manuals of kalam and of the principles of religion, and many Sufi works. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1206} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1207}

## What is not covered here
This entry presents what is documented from Sahih al-Bukhari, the 'Aqida al-Tahawiyya, Lum'at al-I'tiqad, al-Wasitiyya, Maqalat al-Islamiyyin and Ibn Abi al-Izz's Sharh al-Tahawiyya. Al-Maturidi's sayings from his own books and the rest of the Sunni and non-Sunni schools' sayings have not yet been added; no judgement between the two definitions is made here.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 4)

The Sheikh holds that God does not want from people a faith of mere witnessing and verbal testimony but a faith in the unseen {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:14:00}. For that reason he says that the faith of one who believes too late is not accepted: one who does not pray would not be accepted if he prayed then, and likewise those who associate partners with God, even if they became certain at that point that the Messiah is a human being and that Muhammad is the Messenger {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:14:00}.

The Sheikh warns against wavering faith and says that the servant who worships God on an edge gains nothing from it {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:18:30}.

The Sheikh gives a familiar example from families: a man who does not pray and a woman who refuses the headscarf, who are nonetheless of excellent character {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:41:30}. He asks whether their good character makes up for the prayer and the headscarf {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:41:30}.

The Sheikh compares this to a pupil expelled from school for refusing some subjects, and says the believer takes the religion whole {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:42:00}. He notes that Bani Israel were faulted for believing in part of the Book and rejecting part of it, as though one believed in two pages and rejected the third, whereas we believe in the whole Book {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:42:00}.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Sharh Ketab Al Fawaed lectures (episode 3)

The Sheikh says that faith has an outward and an inward part: the outward is speech of the tongue and deeds of the limbs, and the inward is the heart's assent {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep03 00:42:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep03 00:43:00}.

He says that every believer is a Muslim, but not every Muslim is a believer, and he quotes a verse about the desert Arabs, not named by him {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep03 00:41:30}.

The Sheikh holds that the inward faith shows itself only through the outward, except in some cases, such as coercion or fear for one's life {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep03 00:52:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep03 00:53:00}.

He holds that leaving the outward act when nothing prevents it is a sign of corruption within {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep03 00:53:30}.

## For Sprouts
Iman in Islam means truly believing in God, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day and God's decree. When the angel Gabriel asked the Prophet about iman, he answered with these pillars — they are the root of everything a Muslim does.

## For Explorers
In the famous hadith of Gabriel, the angel asked the Prophet about iman and he answered: 'to believe in God, His angels, His book, the meeting with Him, His messengers, and to believe in the resurrection.' Muslims thus count six pillars of faith named in the Qur'an and Sunna: belief in God, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and the decree, its good and its hard side. Early scholars differed on whether deeds are part of the word 'iman': most Sunnis held that faith is word and deed, increasing and decreasing; the Murji'a separated deeds from the name; the Khawarij were stricter. Iman is tied in theology to islam and ihsan — the three divisions of the religion in the same hadith.

## For Scholars
Iman is one of the three divisions of religion in the hadith of Gabriel — islam, iman, ihsan — whose six pillars the Prophet named: belief in God, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and the decree in its good and its hardship, as transmitted by al-Bukhari, Muslim and Ibn Khuzayma. The theological schools disputed its essence: the Murji'a made it heart-assent and deferred deeds, while scholars of hadith and fiqh answered that iman is statement, action and belief, increasing through obedience and decreasing through sin in the common Sunni formulation; the Khawarij and Mu'tazila drew harder conclusions for the grave sinner — exit from faith for the first, an in-between rank for the second. The question branched into disputes over istithna' (saying 'God willing' on one's faith), the complete believer, the hypocrite and the original unbeliever, filling the creed books — Tahawiyya, Wasitiyya, Lum'a. The 'branches of faith' hadiths, numbering them seventy-some, root daily practice: modesty, prayer and removing harm from the path are all branches of iman.

## In al-Tabari's commentary: "those who believe in the unseen"
Al-Tabari gives several readings of "yu'minun" (believe): Ibn Abbas takes it to mean "affirm as true", al-Rabi' ibn Anas "fear", al-Zuhri that belief is action, and Abdullah ibn Masud that belief is affirmation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 234} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 235}
He holds that in Arabic usage "iman" is affirmation: one who affirms a thing in words is called a believer in it, and one who affirms another's word by his deed is also called a believer. He notes that the fear of God may enter into the sense of iman, which is affirming speech by action, and cites a verse from the story of Yusuf in which the word means accepting what someone says. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 235}
He describes iman as a comprehensive term: acknowledging God, His books and His messengers, and confirming that acknowledgment by deed. He judges the more apt reading to be that the people are described as affirming the unseen in word, belief and action, since God did not restrict their belief to one of its senses. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 235}
The verse that follows, on believing in what was sent down to you, is [[passage/quran-2-4|Qur'an 2:4]], which al-Tabari takes up in the next section.
