## The word and the verse

The word *al-'alamin* occurs in the opening of al-Fatiha, where God is described as Lord of the worlds: [[passage/quran-1-2|Al-Fatiha 1:2]]. It recurs elsewhere in the Qur'an, for instance in Pharaoh's question about the Lord of the worlds in [[passage/quran-26-23|Ash-Shu'ara 26:23]]. It is the plural of *'alam*, and the commentators treat *'alam* as a collective noun with no singular from its own word; some derive it from *'alama*, a sign, since each world is a sign pointing to its Creator. They differ on what it covers: everything other than God, or particular classes of creation, and whether it means a plurality of worlds in the sense a reader may have in mind today. This record sets out their views by name, and leaves the numbers that were reported with the people they are attributed to.

## Al-Tabari: the world as the classes of peoples

Al-Tabari says *al-'alamin* is the plural of *'alam*, that *'alam* is a collective with no singular from its own word, like *al-anam*, *al-rahṭ* or *al-jaysh*, and that it names the classes of nations: each class is a world, and the people of each age within each class are the world of that age. People are a world, and the people of each period are the world of that period; the jinn are a world, and so are the other kinds of creation. He says this is the view of Ibn Abbas and Sa'id ibn Jubayr and the meaning of what most commentators said. {cite:tafsir-tabari-quran-com on 1:2}

Al-Tabari also reports from Ibn Abbas, through al-Dahhak, that the sense is: praise belongs to God to whom all creation belongs, all the heavens and those in them, all the earths and those in them and between them, of what is known and what is not. In other reports from Ibn Abbas, Sa'id ibn Jubayr, Mujahid and Ibn Jurayj the meaning is Lord of the jinn and people. From Qatada: every kind is a world. {cite:tafsir-tabari-quran-com on 1:2}

## The reported numbers and whom they are attributed to

Al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir report from Abu al-'Aliya: people are a world, the jinn are a world, and beyond them are eighteen thousand worlds, or fourteen thousand (he was unsure), of angels on the earth; the earth has four corners, in each corner three thousand five hundred worlds God created to worship Him. Ibn Kathir comments: "a strange statement that needs sound evidence." {cite:tafsir-tabari-quran-com on 1:2} {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir on 1:2}

Ibn Kathir gives the other reports:

- From Tubay' al-Himyari: the worlds are a thousand nations, six hundred in the sea and four hundred on land; the same is reported from Sa'id ibn al-Musayyab.
- Abu Ya'la's Musnad has, from Jabir, that in a year of Umar's caliphate locusts became scarce; when a handful was brought from Yemen, Umar said he had heard the Messenger of God say that God created a thousand nations, six hundred in the sea and four hundred on land, and the first to perish of them is the locust. Ibn Kathir says of this chain that Muhammad ibn Isa, al-Hilali, is weak.
- Al-Baghawi reported from Wahb ibn Munabbih eighteen thousand worlds, this world being one of them; from Muqatil eighty thousand; and from Ka'b al-Ahbar that none knows the number of the worlds but God.
- Al-Qurtubi reported from Abu Sa'id al-Khudri that God has forty thousand worlds, this world from its east to its west being one of them.
- Ibn Asakir, in the biography of Marwan ibn Muhammad, the last Umayyad caliph, reports that he said God created seventeen thousand worlds, the people of the heavens and the people of the earth being one world, and the rest known to God alone.

{cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir on 1:2}

These numbers are **reports attributed** to their narrators, or to chains whose full transmission the museum did not read; Ibn Kathir calls Abu al-'Aliya's report one that needs sound evidence, and weakens the chain of the locust hadith. Ka'b al-Ahbar's report is that the number is known only to God.

## A general sense: everything other than God

Ibn Kathir says *al-'alamin* is the plural of *'alam*, which is everything that exists other than God; the worlds are the kinds of creatures in the heavens and the earth, on land and sea, and each age and generation of them is also called a world. He reports Abu 'Ubayda that the world means those with reason, that is people, jinn, angels and devils, and that animals are not called a world; Zayd ibn Aslam and Abu 'Amr ibn al-'Ala' that it is everything with a spirit that is provided for; and al-Zajjaj that it is everything God created in this world and the next. He reports that al-Qurtubi called the last "the sound one", because it includes all the worlds. {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir on 1:2}

## Al-Razi: all that exists besides God, and the possibility of other worlds

Al-Razi divides the worlds, which for him are everything that exists besides God, into bodies, separate beings and attributes; among the bodies are the simple ones (the spheres, the stars, the elements) and the compounds (the three kingdoms of minerals, plants and animals). He then says no proof limits bodies to these: it is proven to him that outside the world there is an unending void, and that God is able to do everything possible, so He is able to create "a thousand thousand worlds" outside the world, each larger than this one and each containing what this one has: a Throne, a Footstool, heavens, earths, a sun and a moon. He calls the philosophers' arguments for a single world weak and flimsy. {cite:mafatih-al-ghayb-quran-tafsir-net on 1:2} This is a statement about the scope of God's power, not a report that those worlds exist.

:::perspectives
**Sunni: the worlds are the kinds and nations of creation.** Al-Tabari, from Ibn Abbas and Sa'id ibn Jubayr, and as the meaning of what most commentators said: the world is the classes of nations, each class a world, and the people of each age the world of that age; Qatada: every kind is a world. {cite:tafsir-tabari-quran-com on 1:2}

**Sunni: the worlds are the jinn and people.** Reported from Ibn Abbas, Ikrima, Sa'id ibn Jubayr, Mujahid and Ibn Jurayj. {cite:tafsir-tabari-quran-com on 1:2}

**Sunni: many worlds, with reported numbers.** Abu al-'Aliya, Tubay' al-Himyari, Sa'id ibn al-Musayyab, Wahb, Muqatil and, in al-Qurtubi's report, Abu Sa'id al-Khudri, each with a number; with Ibn Kathir's comments that Abu al-'Aliya's report needs sound evidence and that the locust hadith has a weak chain; and Ka'b al-Ahbar's saying that only God knows the number. {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir on 1:2}

**Sunni, theological: the possibility of worlds beyond this world.** Al-Razi: God is able to create a thousand thousand worlds outside this world, and the philosophers' arguments for the unity of the world are weak. {cite:mafatih-al-ghayb-quran-tafsir-net on 1:2}
:::

## What is not claimed

- No text read by the museum names or describes any of these worlds, or makes the worlds planets inhabited by people.
- The commentaries read give no exact number as known from the text itself: the figures are sayings attributed to Successors and others, and to Abu Sa'id al-Khudri in al-Qurtubi's report, and one is a hadith with a weak chain according to Ibn Kathir.
- Al-Razi speaks of rational possibility and divine power, not of worlds known to exist.
- The Qurtubi text of al-Fatiha as served by Quran.com is cut short, so his view is given here as Ibn Kathir reports it.
