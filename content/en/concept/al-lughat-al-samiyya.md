## Summary
This page sets out what the Encyclopaedia Britannica says about the place of [[language/arabic|Arabic]] in the Semitic family: its membership of Afro-Asiatic, the clusters on which scholars agree, the debate over its classification, what it shares with its sister languages, and then Maltese. All of it is ascribed to the encyclopedia articles read, and the classification, by the encyclopedia's own account, remains a matter of debate.

## The Semitic family
In its article on the Semitic languages the encyclopedia says they are a branch of the Afro-Asiatic phylum, spread through North Africa and Southwest Asia for more than 4,000 years, and that the most important of them by number of speakers in the early 21st century was Arabic {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}. Of other languages it gives Modern Hebrew with some 6 to 7 million speakers, Amharic with 17 million and Tigrinya with 5.8 million, as the page states. For the figures on Arabic see [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-al-alam-al-yawm|Arabic at the United Nations and UNESCO, and the speaker figures]].

## The classification of Arabic
In its Classification section the encyclopedia says the classification of the Semitic languages remains a matter of debate, and that scholars largely agree on these clusters: Akkadian; Northwest Semitic, with the Canaanite and Aramaic groups along with Ugaritic and Amorite; Arabic; Old South Arabian; Modern South Arabian, which is not descended from the Old; and Ethiopic. It sets Akkadian as an East Semitic branch distinct from West Semitic. Many researchers link Arabic with Northwest Semitic to form a Central Semitic branch; others view it as a separate branch or as a branch within South Semitic {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}. The article on the Arabic language says its relationship to the other Semitic languages is debated, and that the most widely accepted theory places it in Central Semitic, close to Aramaic and the Canaanite languages, on the basis of verb structure {cite:britannica-arabic-language}. On the [[place/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]] the Semitic article says Arabic was closely akin to the North Arabian languages Lihyanite, Safaitic and Thamudic {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}.

## What Arabic shares with its sisters
The encyclopedia notes that broken plurals are shared by Arabic, Ethiopic and Old and Modern South Arabian, and that the sound f, which descends from an early Semitic p, is shared by Arabic, Ethiopic and Modern South Arabian {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}. The article on Arabic describes pharyngeal and uvular fricatives and velarised consonants, three short and three long vowels, and the typical root of three consonants on which patterns are built, so that k, t and b with patterns give kitab and katib; two tenses, three persons, three numbers, the singular, dual and plural, two genders, and three cases that the spoken dialects have largely lost {cite:britannica-arabic-language}. For the root and pattern as the classical authors saw them see [[concept/khasais-al-arabiyya|the qualities of Arabic according to Ibn Faris and Ibn Jinni]].

## Maltese
The article on Maltese says it is a Semitic language of the Southern Central group spoken on the island of [[place/malta|Malta]], that it developed from a dialect of Arabic and is closely related to the western Arabic dialects of Algeria and Tunisia, that it was strongly influenced by Sicilian, and that it is the only form of Arabic written in the Latin alphabet {cite:britannica-maltese-language}. The Semitic article says it originated as such a dialect and gives some 370,000 speakers {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}.

## What this page does not reach
Only the Encyclopaedia Britannica articles were read here, which are tertiary references; the studies behind the debate on Semitic classification were not read, nor the specialist works on Lihyanite, Safaitic and Thamudic. This page does not set out the arguments on which scholars build each view of the classification. What the classical Arab linguists said about the relation of Arabic to Syriac and Hebrew was not read.
