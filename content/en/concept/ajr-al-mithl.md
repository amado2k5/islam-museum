## Summary
The customary wage (ajr al-mithl) is the market wage paid to a worker's peers, not a general rule for every hire contract, as the default is a mutually agreed-upon named wage {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 256}.

## Cases in Which It Applies
The author mentions four cases cited by jurists for the customary wage: first, the invalidity of a hire or profit-sharing partnership (mudarabah) contract due to an unspecified wage, in which case the worker receives his customary wage, or according to some jurists, his customary profit-sharing rate (qirad al-mithl) {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 255}. Second, the invalidity of a hire contract for a reason other than an unspecified wage, where the majority require the customary wage, while Abu Hanifa and his two companions require the customary wage provided it does not exceed the named wage {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 255}. Third, when the ruler compels certain craftsmen to perform work needed by the public {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 255}. Fourth, gross unfairness in hiring a worker {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 255}.

## Who Determines It
The customary wage is determined by the ruler in consultation with experts according to average market wages, {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 256}. The author considers the assumption that the customary wage is the fair wage for every contract to be incorrect {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 255}.

## Its Relation to Price Fixing
Ibn al-Qayyim divides price fixing into two categories: unjust and forbidden, and just and permissible, noting that setting wages when workers collude to inflate them is a required act of justice {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 256}. If the public needs the craft of a particular group, the ruler may compel them to work for their customary wage {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 257} (see [[concept/wage-in-islam|Wage for Work]]).
