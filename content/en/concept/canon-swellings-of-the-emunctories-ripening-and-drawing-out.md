## Summary
Ibn Sina gives a different rule for swellings in the glandular places that serve as drains for the principal organs, so that nothing repelling is brought near them, and then turns to ripening, incision and drawing out a deep swelling {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.304-305}.

## The emunctories
When the swollen organ is a drain for a principal organ, such as the glandular places in the neck about the ears for the brain, the armpit for the heart and the two groins for the liver, nothing that repels may be brought near it at all. This is not because repelling is not a treatment for their swellings, for it is their treatment, but because we prefer not to treat their swellings and strive to increase them and draw the matter to them, careless of the harm growing in the organ for the sake of the principal organ, for fear that if we repel the matter it will turn toward the principal organ {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.304}.

## Drawing to the lesser organ
We choose harm to the lesser organ where it benefits the principal organ, so that we strive to draw the matter to it and swell it, even with cupping glasses and sharp drawing plasters {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.304}.

## Ripening and incision
When swellings of this sort gather, especially in empty places, they may open by themselves or with the help of ripening, and sometimes ripening and incision are both needed. Ripening is done with what holds, along with heat, some blocking and sealing by which the heat is held in. Whoever tries ripening with such drugs must watch: if he finds the innate heat weak and sees the organ tending to corruption, he sets the sealing and blocking drugs aside and uses openers and deep scarification, then drugs that dissolve and dry {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.304}.

## A deep swelling
A swelling is often deep and then needs to be drawn toward the skin, even by cupping glasses or by fire {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.304-305}.
