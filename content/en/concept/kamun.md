## Nature and choice
Cumin has many kinds: a black Kirmani, a yellow Persian, a Syrian, and a Nabataean; the Persian is stronger than the Syrian, and the Nabataean is the one found everywhere. All of them come wild and cultivated; the wild is sharper, and some wild kinds have seed like that of the iris. The book holds the Kirmani to be stronger than the Persian, and the Persian stronger than the rest. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.523}

## Dioscorides on cumin
Dioscorides says that the garden cumin is pleasant in taste, especially the Kirmani, and after it the Egyptian, and that it grows in many lands. A kind called cuminum agrion, the wild cumin, grows much in a city the book names Khalqidun; its seed is sharper than the garden seed and it grows on mounds. Another wild kind resembles the garden one, and its seed, when drunk, helps against the bites of venomous creatures. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.523}

## Temperament and properties
Hot in the second degree and dry in the third. It has a warming force that expels wind and resolves; it cuts and dries, and is said to be somewhat astringent. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.523}

## Cosmetic use and the eye
Washing the face with its water is said to clear the complexion, and taking it in large amounts yellows it. Chewed and mixed with oil and dripped on the eyelid film and on the bruise under the eye, it helps; the wild juice clears sight and draws tears, and is called in Greek kaiyus, meaning smoke. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.523}

## Swellings and wounds
Used with oil on swellings of the testicles, and sometimes with a cerate, or with oil and bean flour; it heals wounds, especially the wild kind whose seed resembles iris seed, when the wounds are dusted with it. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.523}

## Organs of the head and chest
Crushed with vinegar and smelled, cumin stops nosebleed. Given in vinegar mixed with water, it helps difficult breathing; Galen adds that it helps breathlessness on standing and is good for cold palpitation. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.523} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.524}

## Excretory organs and poisons
Used with oil on swelling of the testicle, and sometimes with a cerate. It breaks up stone, especially the wild kind, and helps dripping urine, bloody urine, colic and wind; the wild juice brings the bowels to move. Rufus says the Nabataean kind loosens the belly, while the Kirmani does not loosen but binds, and that the wild herb brings on urine repeatedly. For poisons it is given in wine against the bites of venomous creatures, especially the wild kind. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.524}
