## Overview
Tying up a principal asset and devoting its revenue to charitable purposes. It is the economic institution through which mosques, schools and hospitals were built in Islamic history. {cite:wikidata}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, describes the waqf, spelled wakf there, as the act of founding a charitable trust in Islamic law and, by extension, the trust itself. [[group/maliki-madhhab|Maliki]] jurists mainly use a synonym, habs, also hubus, which French writing renders as habous. The essential elements are that a person, intending a pious deed, declares part of their property unalienable from then on and names persons or public utilities as beneficiaries of its yield. The article notes that the [[group/imamiyya|Imami Shia]] separate waqf from habs, the second being a precarious kind in which the founder keeps the right to dispose of the property. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 67}

The article treats the subject in sections: classical law, then the Arab lands, Persia, the Ottoman empire, Central Asia, India, Southeast Asia and sub-Saharan Africa. This page follows its classical-law section and, from the Arab lands, its accounts of Egypt and North Africa. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 67}

## Basis and early forms
The article says the waqf is not mentioned in the Qur'an and draws its legitimacy mainly from hadiths. The first is reported on the authority of Ibn Umar. [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] had acquired land at [[place/khaybar|Khaybar]] and asked the Prophet's advice. He was told to make the land itself unalienable and give its yield away as alms. Umar did so: the land was not to be sold, inherited or donated, and its yield went to the poor, relatives, slaves, jihad, travellers and guests, while the person administering it could consume part of the yield in an appropriate manner. A second hadith, in the collection of Muslim, says that when a person dies three things survive, among them continuing alms. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 67}

In the legal discussion of the second and early third centuries after the Hijra, the article finds several institutions that later doctrine gathered under the heading of waqf. One was the dedication of horses, weapons and slaves for jihad, or of houses sheltering frontier warriors. A second was a temporary endowment for a limited group that reverted to the founder or his heirs when they died out; it survived in Maliki doctrine. A third was the permanent endowment, called sadaqa muharrama by early Shafiis, usually for the poor, for relatives and descendants or for freed clients, and then for the poor. Its validity was contested, notably by [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]]. A fourth was the permanent endowment for mosques or public utilities. The article judges that family endowments, wakf ahli, seem to have been very common early and those for mosques and public utilities, wakf khayri, secondary. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 67} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 68}

## The question of Byzantine influence
The article reports that C. H. Becker argued early in the twentieth century that charitable waqf was shaped by the pious causes of Byzantine law, and that many scholars shared his view. His arguments rested on structural similarities and on al-Maqrizi's remark that Egypt had only urban waqfs in the first three centuries of Islam, as did Coptic foundations. Claude Cahen, the article says, showed the evidence unconvincing. In his reading the remark, if reliable, applies only to Egypt, where the rural population was then mostly Christian; elsewhere waqfs of rural land are attested; and early waqfs favoured relatives and descendants rather than religious institutions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 68}

## Purposes
The article explains the quick spread of the waqf by the social and economic needs it met. It protected an estate from confiscation by the state or from division under the rules of succession, and it could keep property within the agnatic group while securing a regular income for relatives and descendants. For mosques and public utilities there were two types: waqfs consisting of the mosque or utility itself, such as a school, bridge or fountain, and waqfs producing income to maintain and run it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 68}

## Founder, property and beneficiaries
The founder must have full capacity to act: an adult, of sound mind, free, able to manage finances and not under interdiction. Being a Muslim is not required, and in principle waqfs founded by non-Muslims under protection are valid. A waqf founded during a last illness is subject to the limits on bequests, so its value may not exceed one third of the estate. In Maliki law a married woman needs her husband's authorisation if the waqf exceeds one third of her property. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 68}

The property must be capable of a valid contract: under the founder's control, lawful in use and not otherwise excluded from trade. Only clearly defined physical goods existing at the time of founding qualify, and the founder must be entitled to dispose of them. Waqfs must be perpetual, but jurists argue over what that means for the object. Goods consumed by use cannot be dedicated, yet most schools allow movables that wear out gradually, and some jurists even admit coins. The [[group/hanafi-madhhab|Hanafis]] are stricter: their basic rule limits waqf to immovable property, with three exceptions. These are movables that follow immovable property, such as animals on a rural estate; movables named in hadiths, such as horses and weapons for jihad, an opinion of [[person/abu-yusuf-al-qadi|Abu Yusuf]]; and movables people customarily dedicate, such as tools for graveyards or copies of the Qur'an for mosques, a view ascribed to [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-hasan-al-shaybani|al-Shaybani]] that became authoritative among the Hanafis. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 68}

Beneficiaries may be persons or public utilities such as mosques, schools, bridges, graveyards and fountains. Classical doctrine does not separate charitable from family waqf, though modern legislation does. A founder commonly names successive classes, first sons and unmarried daughters, then agnatic grandchildren, and so on, and no class takes before the previous one is extinct. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 68} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 69}

The article lists conditions of a valid dedication. The immediate beneficiaries must exist and be identifiable, so naming an unborn child is invalid, though the Malikis allow future beneficiaries and keep the proceeds until birth. Beneficiaries must be capable of acquiring property, which excludes slaves and residents of lands at war but not non-Muslims under protection. The purpose must be lawful, so a waqf to build churches, synagogues or monasteries is void even if founded by non-Muslims, although some jurists accept gifts to such bodies for functions unrelated to worship, such as hospitality to travellers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 69}

Jurists disagree on a waqf in favour of the founder. Most hold it invalid, since founding transfers the rights of disposal and use. The Hanafis, except al-Shaybani, hold it valid, citing Umar's stipulation that the administrator could eat from the yield, and Umar himself was the first administrator. The [[group/hanbali-madhhab|Hanbalis]] read the report more narrowly, accepting that the founder may use the yield in his lifetime but not that he be named a beneficiary. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 69}

## Legal effects
The act is irrevocable. For all schools except the Malikis, who keep the founder as owner without the powers of ownership, the founder ceases to be owner. Because Islamic law gives legal personality only to natural persons, jurists had to say who becomes owner, and they used two analogies. Most schools compare the waqf with a gift, so the beneficiaries become owners with restricted ownership, and the founder's right ends when possession is taken. Others compare it with freeing a slave and say ownership passes at once to God. Both views are found in each school except the Maliki. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 70}

Since a waqf is perpetual, its goods need upkeep. Where goods are rented out, maintenance is paid before the beneficiaries; where beneficiaries only use them, they bear it, and the qadi may intervene if they neglect it. When goods are damaged or destroyed without a tort, jurists differ. The [[group/shafii-madhhab|Shafiis]] and Imami Shia hold the waqf continues while anything usable remains. The other schools accept, under conditions, selling the remains and buying new waqf property, called istibdal. The Malikis allow this only for unfit movables; the Hanbalis allow it when the goods can no longer serve their purpose. The Hanafis first ask whether the founder gave himself or the administrators the power of istibdal; Abu Yusuf's view that such a clause is valid, except for mosques, prevailed over al-Shaybani's. If there is no clause, the qadi may still allow it, but only with the authorisation of the caliph or sultan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 70} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 71}

## Administration and ending
The founder usually appoints an administrator, called nazir, mutawalli or qayyim. Most schools let the founder administer during his life; the Malikis do not, since they require handing the property over. Where he made no arrangement, Abu Yusuf gives the right to the founder and his executor, whereas other jurists give it to the qadi. The Malikis, most Hanbalis and the Imami Shia let a limited, identifiable group of beneficiaries administer. All schools except the Hanafis require the administrator to be Muslim and male; he must be trustworthy and competent. His tasks are maintenance, exploitation and distribution of the proceeds, for which he may take remuneration, and the qadi supervises him as he would a guardian. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 71}

A waqf is meant to last forever, yet the jurists foresee ending when its goods perish, where al-Shaybani held that the remains revert to the founder or heirs while the others demand that every alternative use be explored; when the qadi declares it void for failing the conditions; when the founder apostatises; and, for the Malikis, when the last beneficiary of a limited waqf dies or its term expires. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 71}

## In Egypt
The article says the earliest pious foundations in Muslim Egypt look like charitable gifts rather than later waqfs, for example the first mosque built by [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn al-As]]. The earliest known document for a genuine charitable waqf of farmland dates from the Abbasid period, 307/919. Under the Umayyads a diwan for waqf administration already existed, and Abbasid judges of Fustat inspected waqf properties monthly. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 71} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 72}

The [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimids]] changed the system. [[person/al-muizz-li-din-allah|Al-Muizz]] ordered the funds moved to the treasury, and the waqfs were later run by tax-farming. Under the [[polity/ayyubid-dynasty|Ayyubids]], [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Salah al-Din]] used treasury land to support religious foundations. Muhammad Amin, the article says, calls this irsad rather than waqf, because waqf alienates private property while irsad uses public funds. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 72}

Under the [[polity/mamluk-sultanate|Mamluks]] much of what was called waqf was irsad. In 789/1387 Sultan [[person/barquq|Barquq]] tried to liquidate some great waqfs as illicit, but the jurists did not authorise him. The article describes a division under [[person/baybars|Baybars]] between ahbas and awqaf, the latter split into hukmiyya waqfs, supervised by the Shafii chief qadi, and ahliyya waqfs, the great foundations of sultans and amirs that combined charity with income for the founder's clan. Beneficiaries included madrasas, hospitals, the Holy Cities, public kitchens and the kiswa. Chroniclers criticised abuses, and founders wrote strict conditions, such as no rental beyond three years. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 72} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 73} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 75}

The [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottomans]] found almost half of Egypt's farmland alienated as waqf. They gave primacy to the Hanafi school and stressed the distinction between waqf and irsad. Governors of the tenth and eleventh centuries created charitable waqfs, and [[person/selim-i-uthmani|Selim I]] and [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Suleiman]] enlarged the endowments for the Holy Cities. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 74} The article adds that Muhammad Ali's son Ibrahim confiscated waqf land in Upper Egypt, and that after [[person/muhammad-ali-pasha|Muhammad Ali]]'s confiscations primary education was left without income. In 1913 Lord Kitchener reinstated the Waqf Ministry. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 74} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 75}

## In North Africa
In the account of North Africa to 1914, the [[polity/marinid-dynasty|Marinid]] sultans created public waqfs for mosques, zawiyas, hospitals and especially madrasas, with at least six madrasas built between 721/1321 and 756/1356. Their assets included shops, houses, baths, mills and farmland. The article reads this as part of a policy to foster a new religious elite, and says waqf inscriptions served Marinid propaganda. Books were endowed too, and Abu Inan built a library at the [[building/qarawiyyin|Qarawiyyin]] mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 77}

For family endowments the article cites Maliki practice, where the founder cannot be the first beneficiary. A deed names a first generation, then descendants, and its wording decides whether several generations draw together or one after another. When the line dies out, the revenue goes to a charitable purpose, often a mosque or the poor. The article adds that family waqf let a proprietor remove property from the effect of inheritance law, and that in a study of 101 disputes fathers founded six times as often as mothers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 78} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 79}

Disputes were frequent, and deeds were kept in family archives. The jurists treated the founder's words as binding, one Andalusi jurist comparing them to those of the lawgiver. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 80}

## From the book Usul al-Iqtisad al-Islami: part two
The types of waqf projects in the author's account are: family waqf, whose yield returns first to the founder, then to his descendants, then to charitable causes; public waqfs for Muslims in general; and charitable waqfs for groups such as mosques, hospitals and the poor. {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 145} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 146}.

The author holds that jurisprudence recognised a legal personality in the waqf, so that when the administrator borrows, the waqf is the debtor. He holds that a person's legal liability continues to be reckoned after death until the rights are settled. {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 148}.

## In Ragheb El-Sergani's programme What Did Muslims Bring to the World
In the fourteenth episode [[person/ragheb-al-sergani|Ragheb El-Sergani]] speaks of endowments the Muslims set up to care for animals, saying that Damascus had endowments, paid for by the rich, for cats their owners had neglected, so that there was no such thing there as a street cat, and that Damascus was a city resting entirely on endowments. {cite:assabile-ragheb-el-sergani what-did-muslims-bring-to-the-world/ep14 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-ragheb-el-sergani what-did-muslims-bring-to-the-world/ep14 00:20:00} {cite:assabile-ragheb-el-sergani what-did-muslims-bring-to-the-world/ep14 00:21:00} He says Ibn Battuta passed by these endowments on his journey and described the fat cats he saw there (needs_source: the attribution to Ibn Battuta's travels was not checked here). {cite:assabile-ragheb-el-sergani what-did-muslims-bring-to-the-world/ep14 00:21:00} He mentions another endowment in Damascus for aged horses no longer able to work, with land, staff, fodder and a veterinarian who treated them until the end of their lives. {cite:assabile-ragheb-el-sergani what-did-muslims-bring-to-the-world/ep14 00:21:30} {cite:assabile-ragheb-el-sergani what-did-muslims-bring-to-the-world/ep14 00:22:00} These are his accounts.

## The waqf in Mamluk Cairo's architecture, in Yeomans
Yeomans defines the waqf as land or property endowed in perpetuity for charity and, in his own view, traces it to the Byzantine pious foundations known as piae causae. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.201} The founder drew up a deed (waqfiyya) setting out how the property was run and its income; Yeomans cites Michael Rogers that the founder's descendants drew benefits in perpetuity and that the success of this "legalized self-interest" explains the number of such institutions and the excellence of Mamluk architecture. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.201} [[person/barsbay|Barsbay]], for example, had the waqf's conditions carved in the tiraz of his al-Mu'izz street madrasa so that later rulers could not tamper with it. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.210}
