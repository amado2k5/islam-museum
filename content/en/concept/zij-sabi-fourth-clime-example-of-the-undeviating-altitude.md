## Summary
Al-Battani gives an example in the fourth clime with the Sun at the first of Cancer, showing that the Sun's altitude at midnight below the northern horizon equals the altitude of the opposite degree above the earth on the midheaven line, and giving a second way by doubling the altitude of the first of Aries and subtracting from it the altitude of the first of Cancer. He then draws the circle of midheaven and the horizon to show where the Sun stands when its azimuth is the azimuth of the equinoctial rising. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.41}.

## Two altitudes of the Sun, two ways
He says: let us give an example and place it in the fourth clime, where the altitude of the pole is a stated amount, and let the Sun's place be the first of Cancer; its altitude at midday is then one amount and its altitude at midnight from the northern horizon another. It is known that this equals the altitude of the opposite degree above the earth on the line of midheaven. You can also know it another way: double the altitude of the first of Aries in that clime and subtract from the result the altitude of the first of Cancer at midheaven. The numbers of the example are garbled in the scan and are not given. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.41}.

## The azimuth of the Cancer rising and the figure of the midheaven circle
He adds that the azimuth of the first of Cancer at its rising in this clime lies to the north of the rising of Aries by a number of degrees, and then says: if that is as we have described, draw a circle for midheaven, with a diameter that is half the horizon, the point of the zenith, the points of the rising of the first of Aries and of the first of Cancer, and the points of the Sun's altitude at noon and midnight. The place common to a line drawn from one point and another line is the place which, when the Sun has risen to it, puts it on the azimuth of the rising point of the first of Aries; so it then has no deviation from the azimuth of the equinoctial rising. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.41}.
