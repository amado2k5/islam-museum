## Summary
For al-Aqqad, chivalry (nakhwah) is "the ethics of knighthood": courage paired with magnanimity, refraining from aggression, and abstaining from treachery, and in his view it is the key to the character of Ali ibn Abi Talib {cite:abqariyyat-ali-aqqad p.33}.

## The Meaning of Chivalry in the Book
Al-Aqqad sums up the ethics of knighthood in a single word—chivalry (nakhwah)—and sees it as innate in Ali, an ethos of the Hashimite family, and a habit of practical chivalry cultivated by the brave knight {cite:abqariyyat-ali-aqqad p.33}. Consequently, in his view it requires no affectation and is inseparable from the honor a knight upholds in private and in public {cite:abqariyyat-ali-aqqad p.33}.

## Manifestations of Chivalry in Ali's Actions
- **Refraining from striking an exposed foe:** Al-Aqqad notes that Ali repeatedly had an opening to deliver a fatal blow to his adversary yet passed up the chance, because he wished to defeat his opponent with the triumph of a noble man {cite:abqariyyat-ali-aqqad p.33}.
- **Magnanimity toward an adversary:** He never finished off a wounded fighter, never violated anyone's privacy, never reached for plunder, and restrained his troops from killing retreating foes even when they were enraged {cite:abqariyyat-ali-aqqad p.24}.
- **Refraining from initiating combat:** He used to say, "I will not fight them until they fight me," and he called for peace before every battle {cite:abqariyyat-ali-aqqad p.23}.
- **Abstaining from verbal abuse:** He did not make insults his habit, and al-Aqqad regards whatever harsh words escaped him as rare slips rather than a deliberate practice {cite:abqariyyat-ali-aqqad p.36}.

## Interpretation and Its Limits
Al-Aqqad reads chivalry as an explanation for Ali's stances and responds to those who faulted him for Sufism by arguing that Sufism, at its core, is a struggle for truth {cite:abqariyyat-ali-aqqad p.37}.
