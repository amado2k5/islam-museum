## Summary
Arabic speakers call both the old fusha of the Qur'an and the heritage and the contemporary standard "al-fusha", and a research summary supplied to the museum says their core morphology and grammar remain largely the same, while they differ in vocabulary, syntax and style. This page gives what the summary says, attributes each statement to its page, and says at the end what it did not reach. For the wider linguistic situation see [[concept/al-izdiwajiyya-al-lughawiyya-fi-al-arabiyya|Diglossia in Arabic]].

## The name and the shared core
The summary says that Classical Arabic and Modern Standard Arabic are both called al-fusha in the Arab world, and that their morphology and core grammar remain largely identical, citing Karin Ryding's A Reference Grammar of Modern Standard Arabic, 2005, and a Language Log post of 2022 {cite:ryding-2005-reference-grammar-msa} {cite:languagelog-arabic-and-the-vernaculars-2022}. This bears on what the sciences of [[concept/ilm-al-nahw|grammar]] and [[concept/ilm-al-sarf|morphology]] describe in the heritage, and on the page that gathers what is said of [[concept/al-arabiyya-lughat-al-quran|Arabic as the language of the Qur'an]]. The summary places the differences under three heads, vocabulary, syntax and style, which the table below sets side by side.

| Aspect | Classical and Qur'anic Arabic | Modern Standard Arabic |
|---|---|---|
| Vocabulary | pre-modern tribal, literary and theological vocabulary | a lexicon much enlarged by root-pattern derivation, calques and borrowing to name science, politics, media and technology |
| Syntax | the wider range of classical constructions, some rare, with traditional verb-subject-object order | a narrower, more standardised subset of constructions; a preference in journalism for subject-verb-object alongside the traditional order, avoidance of rare constructions, and frequent dropping of the case and mood vowel endings in speech |
| Style | rhetorical parallelism, rhymed prose (saj') and classical ellipsis | concise, linear sentences, nominalisation and impersonal reporting conventions, shaped by journalism, translation and international media |

## Vocabulary
The summary says Qur'anic and Classical Arabic preserve pre-modern vocabulary, tribal, literary and theological, whereas Modern Standard Arabic has greatly enlarged its lexicon through root-pattern derivation, calques from European languages and direct borrowing, to name what is new in science, politics, media and technology {cite:google-books-holes-modern-arabic} {cite:ryding-2005-reference-grammar-msa}.

## Syntax
It says Modern Standard Arabic keeps Classical morphology but uses a narrower, more standardised subset of constructions: in journalism it leans more to subject-verb-object order alongside the traditional order, avoids rare classical constructions, and often drops the case and mood vowel endings in speech {cite:languagelog-arabic-and-the-vernaculars-2022} {cite:google-books-holes-modern-arabic}.

## Style
The summary credits Werner Diem, in Hochsprache und Dialekt im Arabischen, 1974, and Clive Holes, 2004, with the view that style is where the two diverge most sharply: Classical and Qur'anic texts, it says, show rhetorical parallelism, rhymed prose (saj') and classical ellipsis, whereas modern standard prose, shaped by journalism, translation and international media, favours concise linear sentences, nominalisations and an impersonal reporting convention {cite:diem-1974-hochsprache-und-dialekt} {cite:languagelog-arabic-and-the-vernaculars-2022} {cite:google-books-holes-modern-arabic}. This is the summary's description of the style of texts. Whether the rhythm of the Qur'an is to be called saj' or not was a matter on which the scholars of inimitability differed, and this page does not enter it; it refers to the pages on [[concept/ijaz-al-quran|the inimitability of the Qur'an]].

## What this page does not reach
The museum did not open the pages of Ryding, Holes, Diem or Language Log and did not read what they say, so everything above is attributed to them as the summary reported it. The summary gives no textual examples from the Qur'an or from journalism for the three differences, so none is given here, and it does not estimate how many constructions are rare or dropped. The old books of grammar on this question were not read; they are reported in other pages. This page does not decide between those who regard the two fushas as one language and those who regard them as two distinct varieties.
