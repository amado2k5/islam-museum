## Summary
For al-Battani the day arc is the amount of the day's equator that rises from sunrise to sunset, and the night arc is what rises from sunset to the next sunrise. The first is found by subtracting the risings of the Sun's degree in the clime from the risings of the degree opposite it there, the remainder being the day arc; if the Sun's risings are greater, a full circle is added to the opposite degree's risings before subtracting. Subtracting the day arc from a revolution leaves the night arc. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion pp.45-46}.

## Defining the two arcs and working them out
He says: if you wish to know the day arc and night arc by the table, that is, the amount of the equator that rises from the time of sunrise to the time of sunset, or from the time the Sun sets to its rising next day, find the degree in which the Sun is on the day you want and take what stands opposite it of the rising times in the given clime whose latitude equals that city's or is nearer to it than any other clime. Subtract it from the risings opposite the degree that faces the Sun's degree in that clime; the remainder is the day arc. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion pp.45-46}.

## The revolution and the night arc
Then he says: if the risings of the Sun's degree are greater than those of the opposite degree, which is the counterpart of the Sun's degree, add a revolution to the risings of the Sun's counterpart and subtract from the total the risings of the Sun's degree; the remainder is the day arc. Once you know the day arc, subtract it from a revolution and the remainder is the night arc. The revolution here is three hundred and sixty parts, and the text calls it a revolution. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.46}.
