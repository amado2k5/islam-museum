## Summary
In the early centuries a group of scholars disliked anything not Qur'an being added to the codex: dots and vowels, tens and fives, the openings and endings of suras, the number of verses and the names of suras. Then matters changed and dots and vowels, and later the other signs, became common. Al-Suyuti and al-Sabt set out the views of both groups and the readings of 'keep the Qur'an bare'. {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2246} {cite:manahil-irfan-sabt p. 331}

## The dislike in the first age
Abu Ubayd relays from Ibn Mas'ud: 'keep the Qur'an bare and do not mix anything with it.' From al-Nakha'i, dislike of dotting; from Ibn Sirin, dislike of dots, openings and endings; from Ibn Mas'ud and Mujahid, dislike of tens. Ibn Abi Dawud relays that al-Nakha'i was brought a codex with 'sura such and such, so many verses' written in it and said: erase this, for Ibn Mas'ud disliked it. {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2246} {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2247} Al-Sabt says the scholars of the first age held dotting and vowelling the codex disliked out of excess of care to keep delivery of the Qur'an as the codex wrote it, and fear it would lead to alteration. {cite:manahil-irfan-sabt p. 331}

## Permission after them
Ibn Abi Dawud then relays from al-Hasan and Ibn Sirin: no harm in dotting codices, and from Rabi'a ibn Abi Abd al-Rahman: no harm in vowelling them. Malik said: no harm in dots in the codices from which boys learn, but not in the mothers. {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2247} {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2248} Al-Nawawi resolved it by calling it recommended. {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2249} Al-Halimi distinguished: writing tens, fives, sura names and verse counts is disliked on the strength of 'keep the Qur'an bare', whereas dots are allowed because they have no form. {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2247} {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2248}

## What 'bare' means
Al-Sabt offers interpretations of Ibn Mas'ud's saying: bareness may be in the script, from dots, vowels and tens, or from explanation; or in recitation, not busying oneself with earlier books, or not being busied away from it by hadith. He notes that in one version of Ibn Mas'ud's report it says 'its being bare means no division, no tens and no "sura such and such" is written in the codex', and that he could not tell whether this is Ibn Mas'ud's own words or inserted in them. {cite:manahil-irfan-sabt p. 333} Abu Ubayd says al-Nakha'i took the saying to refer to dotting codices out of fear that a generation would grow up meeting dotted codices and see the dots as part of the Qur'an. {cite:manahil-irfan-sabt p. 332} Ibn al-Jazari records that they sometimes put explanation into the recitation for clarity, being sure of what they had received from the Prophet as Qur'an and so safe from confusion, but Ibn Mas'ud disliked and forbade it. {cite:manahil-irfan-sabt p. 333}

## In sum
The reports agree that the early dislike was out of fear that the Qur'an be mixed with something else, not an objection to explaining pronunciation itself; after error appeared and Islam's domain widened, marking became a safeguard.
