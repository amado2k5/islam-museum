## Summary
What a research summary supplied to the museum says about Arabic at the western edge of the Mediterranean Islamic world: al-Andalus from 711, Sicily from 827, and Malta, which alone carries a descendant of Siculo-Arabic in Maltese. The page follows the end of Arabic in Spain after 1492, then with the Pragmática Sanción of 1567, then with the expulsion of the Moriscos between 1609 and 1614, and the decisions of the rulers of Sicily who dropped Arabic from government in 1198. The museum did not open the pages named, and the founder approved using the summary on 21 November 2026.

## What this page is
This page gathers three places into one story: what Arabic was in the Iberian Peninsula, what it was in Sicily, and how a living trace of it remained in Malta. It distinguishes the names and dates supported by Wikipedia from what the summary ascribes to an academic work on Siculo-Arabic and to a chapter in a book on Spanish dress and sumptuary legislation. The page does not claim that the museum read any of this, and it passes no judgement on why Arabic ended in Spain, a question whose statements are ascribed to those who make them.

## Al-Andalus, 711 to 1492
The summary says Arabic reached the Iberian Peninsula with the Umayyad conquest of 711 and developed into Andalusi Arabic alongside Andalusi Romance, called Mozarabic, Latin, Hebrew and Berber {cite:wikipedia-al-andalus} {cite:wikipedia-andalusi-arabic}. According to the summary, conversion to Islam, whose converts are called Muwalladun, and cultural assimilation among Christians, the Mozarabs, made Arabic the prestige literary and colloquial language, and strophic poetic forms arose in it such as the muwashshah and the vernacular zajal {cite:wikipedia-andalusi-arabic} {cite:wikipedia-literature-of-al-andalus}. For the place see [[place/al-andalus|al-Andalus]] and [[place/spain|Spain]]; for the conquest [[event/conquest-of-al-andalus|Conquest of al-Andalus]]. The statement on the language's standing is the summary's and not a measurement by the museum.

## The end of Arabic in Spain
The summary says that after the fall of Granada in 1492 Spanish royal decrees banned Arabic, culminating in the Pragmática Sanción that Philip II issued in 1567, and that Andalusi Arabic disappeared from Iberia after the expulsion of the Moriscos in 1609-1614, leaving a deep lexical legacy in Spanish, Portuguese and Catalan and influencing urban Maghrebi dialects {cite:wikipedia-andalusi-arabic} {cite:wikipedia-al-andalus}. Philip II has a [[person/philip-ii-spain|record]]; for the expulsion see [[event/morisco-expulsion-1017|Expulsion of the Moriscos from al-Andalus]], an event that al-Maqqari dates to the year 1017 of the Hijra. The museum also holds a source record on the influence of Arabic on Spanish, not reviewed here.

## Sicily from 827 to the 13th century
The summary says that after the Aghlabid and Abbasid conquest of Byzantine Sicily, beginning in 827 and completed in 962, Siculo-Arabic, a pre-Hilalian Maghrebi-type variety, became established across the Emirate of Sicily {cite:wikipedia-siculo-arabic} {cite:wikipedia-muslim-conquest-of-sicily}. After the Norman conquest in the 11th century the Norman royal chancery continued to work in Arabic alongside Greek and Latin, producing works such as al-Idrisi's Tabula Rogeriana of 1154, until the Hohenstaufen dropped Arabic from government in 1198 and expelled Sicily's remaining Muslims in the 13th century {cite:wikipedia-siculo-arabic} {cite:wikipedia-norman-arab-byzantine-culture}. For the place see [[place/muslim-sicily|Muslim Sicily]], for the king [[person/roger-of-sicily|Roger]], and for the geographer [[person/al-idrisi-muhammad-ibn-abd-allah|al-Idrisi]].

## Malta and Maltese
The summary says Siculo-Arabic died out in Sicily itself, leaving about 300 loanwords in Sicilian, but its offshoot in Malta evolved into Maltese, the only surviving descendant of Siculo-Arabic {cite:wikipedia-siculo-arabic} {cite:wikipedia-arabization}. For Maltese today see [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-malta|Arabic in Malta]], which sets out its legal place and figures as the summary relayed them, and for the place [[place/malta|Malta]]. The museum notes that the summary calls Maltese a descendant and does not decide whether it is Arabic or a language in its own right.

## How the sources differ
Wikipedia supports names, dates and places in six pages: al-Andalus, Andalusi Arabic, the literature of al-Andalus, Siculo-Arabic, the conquest of Sicily and Norman-Arab-Byzantine culture. The non-Wikipedia support the summary names is two works by Dionisius A. Agius: the book Siculo Arabic of 1996, from Kegan Paul International, and an article of 1994, Siculo Arabic: interferences, deletions, and additions, at the University of Malta repository {cite:agius-siculo-arabic-1996-google-books} {cite:agius-siculo-arabic-interferences-1994-oar-um}; each has a source record. For the 1567 Pragmática the support is a chapter by Amanda Wunder, 2019, in The Right to Dress, from Cambridge University Press, titled Spanish Fashion and Sumptuary Legislation from the Thirteenth to the Eighteenth Century {cite:wunder-spanish-fashion-and-sumptuary-legislation-2019}. The chapter's title concerns dress and sumptuary law; the summary names it as support for the report of the decree and the museum did not open it, so it does not know what the chapter says about the Arabic language. The museum records this difficulty plainly and does not close it.

## What this page did not reach
The museum opened none of the pages the summary names. The summary gives no number of speakers of Arabic in al-Andalus or Sicily in any century, so the museum estimates none. The Sicilian dates, 827, 962, 1154 and 1198, and the Spanish dates, 1492, 1567 and 1609 to 1614, are as the summary gave them; the museum adds no date and does not settle the scholars' disagreement over why Arabic disappeared.
