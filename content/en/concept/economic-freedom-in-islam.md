## Overview
The author distinguishes between freedom in Islamic thought, which scholars examined in theological works on human actions, and economic freedom, about which very little has been written {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 76} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 77} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 79}.

## Limits
The author states that a Muslim is free to own the means of production and enter into contracts, but must strive to submit to the public interest when a conflict arises, while not being permitted to abolish their private interest altogether {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 79} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 80}.

## From the book Usul al-Iqtisad al-Islami: Part Two (pp. 101–200 of the PDF file)
The author holds that creed and worship are established only by scriptural text, whereas transactions are founded on permissibility and do not require a specific text; that prohibitions are limited and detailed; and that the legal schools agree on freedom of contract and mutual consent, with the Hanbali school being the broadest in this regard {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 173} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 174} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 175}.
