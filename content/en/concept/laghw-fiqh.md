## Summary
Laghw, in lexicon, has many senses: a fall, what counts for nothing in speech or otherwise, what the heart does not bind itself to, as in saying no by God or yes by God; Aisha said idle speech is only in dispute, jest and banter in a talk the heart does not bind to; it also means sin, uproar, and utterance. In law it is speech that carries no weight for the ruling; idle is more general than void {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13652}.

## Idle oaths
Most jurists hold that an idle oath carries no expiation and no sin, on the verse of the surah of the Table (verse 89) cited without quoting, which denies blame for the idle {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13653}. Ahmad and al-Nakha'i held that one who swears of something he believes so, and it proves otherwise, owes expiation since it is not idle {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13653}. The idle oath may concern the past, the present or the future: swearing on what one believes which proves otherwise, or words that run on the tongue without the heart binding to them {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13653} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13654}. The Hanafis, al-Adawi and Ibn Arafa of the Malikis deny that a future oath is idle, since a future oath binds whether intended or not {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13654}.

## Idle speech during the Friday sermon
The jurists differ on listening to the sermon and the idle speech of one who does not listen, in the detailed entry on listening {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13654}. The Malikis hold that the sermon must be in Arabic, so in another tongue it is idle, that it must be audible, and that it must have substance {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13654}.
