## Summary
Sarf, which the early scholars call tasrif, is the science concerned with the form of the word. The page gives what [[person/ibn-jinni|Ibn Jinni]] says in his al-Munsif about the need of the people of Arabic for it and that it is their balance, its place between grammar and lexicon, then what Kitab al-Ayn, ascribed to [[person/al-khalil-ibn-ahmad-al-farahidi|al-Khalil]], says of the most letters a root reaches, then what the Encyclopaedia Britannica says of root and pattern. No early account of who founded this science was found.

## The balance of Arabic
Al-Munsif is a commentary on al-Mazini's Kitab al-Tasrif, and Ibn Jinni begins by saying he will explain it {cite:munsif-ibn-jinni-1954 p.1}. He says this kind of knowledge, that is tasrif, is needed by all the people of Arabic to the full, "for it is the balance of Arabic", and by it the origins of Arab speech are known from the additions that enter them, and knowledge of derivation is reached only through it {cite:munsif-ibn-jinni-1954 p.2}.

## Analogy and hearing
Ibn Jinni states that part of the language is taken by analogy through tasrif and not by hearing, so the imperfect of fa'ula is yaf'ulu, for example, and someone who said kuruma yakramu would be rejected, since that is not the speech of the Arabs {cite:munsif-ibn-jinni-1954 p.2}. He says that lexicographers often err in tasrif because they are occupied with transmission and hearing rather than analogy {cite:munsif-ibn-jinni-1954 p.3}.

## Between grammar and lexicon
Ibn Jinni holds that tasrif is intermediate between grammar and lexicon, that tasrif is for knowing the stable selves of words and grammar for their changing states. He gives the example of building from daraba a form on the pattern of ja'far, which yields darbab. He notes that books of grammar usually have tasrif at their end, which is why the reader is referred to [[concept/ilm-al-nahw|Arabic grammar]] {cite:munsif-ibn-jinni-1954 p.4}.

## Root and pattern
The Encyclopaedia Britannica describes the structure of Arabic as resting on root and pattern: a root is typically three consonants, and from the root k-t-b different forms come, kitab being a book and katib one who writes {cite:britannica-arabic-language}. In Kitab al-Ayn, ascribed to al-Khalil, it is said that the Arabs have no structure in nouns or in verbs of more than five letters {cite:kitab-al-ayn-makhzumi 1 p.49}. Ibn Khallikan reports that most scholars of the language say Kitab al-Ayn is not al-Khalil's own composition: he began it and his pupils finished it {cite:wafayat-ihsan-abbas 2 p.246}; the ascription to al-Khalil here is therefore the ascription as the book itself gives it.

## What the encyclopaedia says of the structure of the word
The Encyclopaedia Britannica states that Arabic has two tenses of the verb, perfect and imperfect, three persons, three numbers, singular, dual and plural, and two genders, masculine and feminine. It states that the three cases, nominative, genitive and accusative, are largely lost in the spoken dialects. It describes the sound system as including pharyngeal and uvular fricatives, emphatic consonants, and three short and three long vowels. This is a general description from a modern encyclopaedia, not an account of the science of sarf as its own scholars knew it, and the page read does not mention their term tasrif {cite:britannica-arabic-language}.

## A summary of what Ibn Jinni says
Ibn Jinni's words on tasrif come to three claims: that it is the balance of Arabic, by which the root is known from the addition and derivation is reached; that part of the language is known through it by analogy and not by hearing alone; and that it stands between grammar and lexicon, grammar being for the changing states of words and tasrif for their stable selves. These are the author's claims, and the page gives them as they are and passes no judgement on them {cite:munsif-ibn-jinni-1954 p.2} {cite:munsif-ibn-jinni-1954 p.4}.

## What this page does not reach
No early account in the works read was found of who first set down or wrote on the science of sarf, nothing but Ibn Jinni's description. Only what Ibn Khaldun ascribes in grammar to Abu al-Aswad, then al-Khalil, then Sibawayh was found. The line of Ibn Jinni that defines tasrif as one's coming to the root letters and acting on them by adding a letter or altering them was not read in al-Munsif; it appeared only as an excerpt in another book, so it is not given. Al-Mazini's book itself was not read.
