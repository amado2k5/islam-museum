## Summary
The Hejaz is the region in the west of [[place/arabian-peninsula|Arabia]]; it holds [[place/mecca|Mecca]] and [[place/medina|Medina]] and was the home of [[group/quraysh|Quraysh]]. This page gathers what was read of the speech of the Hejaz, mostly old descriptions in the books of language that al-Suyuti and Ibn Khaldun relay, since no description of the Hejazi dialect of today was found in the references read. The page ascribes every description to its author; the dialects of the tribes have their own page, [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|the dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]]. Sections on classification, main features and speaker numbers were later added from a research summary supplied to the museum, and they supersede the remark that none were found.

## The place
In its article on Saudi Arabia the Encyclopaedia Britannica places the Hejaz in the western highlands along the Red Sea and calls it the cradle of Islam, with its holiest cities, Mecca and Medina {cite:britannica-saudi-arabia}. In its article on the Arabic language it says the chief dialect groups in modern times are those of Arabia, Iraq, Syria, Egypt and North Africa {cite:britannica-arabic-language}, so the Hejaz falls for it within the Arabia group, and the article gives no separate description of it.

## The Hejaz and Tamim on the working of 'ma'
In al-Muzhir al-Suyuti relays from Ibn Jinni in al-Khasa'is that the dialects, differing as they do, are all authoritative, and gives as an example the language of the Hejaz in making 'ma' act on the noun and the language of Tamim in leaving it so; each, he says, is accepted by analogy, so none may reject one dialect by the other, and the most one may do is choose one and strengthen it over its fellow {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.204}. This is one of the clearest statements in the books of language on a grammatical feature of the Hejazis.

## Words and forms ascribed to the people of the Hejaz
Elsewhere al-Suyuti relays from al-Qali that the language of the Hejaz has dha'a al-baql yadh'a, whereas the people of Najd say dhawa yadhwi, and that the Kufans also report dhawiya, which is not fasih {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.170}. In the same place it is said that the people of the Hejaz say qatara yaqtiru, and that the verb has another form with damma on the ta' that is the least frequent of the forms {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.170}. In the chapter on loanwords he relays from Ibn Durustawayh, on jass, which he holds a Persian loan, that some say qass with fatha, and that it is the language of the people of the Hejaz {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.216}. These are scattered examples noted in a compendium, not a systematic description of a dialect.

## Quraysh and the settled people of the Hejaz
Al-Suyuti relates that Tha'lab said Quraysh rose in fasaha above the 'an'ana of Tamim, the kashkasha of Hawazin, the kaskasa of Rabi'a, the talta of Bahra' and others, and relays from al-Farabi that Quraysh were the best of the Arabs at choosing the most eloquent of words, the easiest on the tongue in utterance {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.167}. He relays from al-Farabi also that the language was not taken from the settled people of the Hejaz, because those who transmitted the language found them, when they began to transmit, already mixed with other nations and their tongues spoiled, and that those who transmitted it were the people of Basra and Kufa {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.168}. Ibn Khaldun says the language of Quraysh was the most eloquent and clearest of the Arabic dialects because of their distance from the lands of the non-Arabs on every side, then those around them, Thaqif, Hudhayl, Khuza'a, Kinana, Ghatafan, Asad and Tamim {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.765}. So the sources read take the tongue of Quraysh as a model and do not take the settled people of the Hejaz as a source for transmitting the language; each page ascribes its statement to its author.

## What this page does not reach
No description of the contemporary Hejazi dialect was found in the references read, whether of sound, grammar or vocabulary, nor a count of its speakers, and no modern dialect survey was read. Articles dividing the Hejaz into towns and Bedouin were not read. Everything set out here about the Hejazis is an old description of single words and forms, relayed through al-Suyuti from authors whose books were not themselves read, and some of it comes in the context of ranking the dialects, which the museum sets out ascribed to its authors. For the general picture see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]].

## Classification
Glottolog 5.4 places Hijazi Arabic (identifier hija1235, ISO 639-3 code acw) within Afro-Asiatic, Semitic, West Semitic, Central Semitic, Arabian, Arabic and Arabian Peninsula Arabic, with four subdivisions: Coastal Tihaamah, North Hijazi, South Hijazi and Valley Tihaamah {cite:glottolog-hijazi-arabic}. The free page of Ethnologue (29th edition, 2026) places it in Afro-Asiatic, Semitic, Central, South, Arabic, as a stable indigenous language of Saudi Arabia within the Arabic macrolanguage {cite:ethnologue-acw}.

Brill's Encyclopedia of Arabic Language and Linguistics, in its entry on Meccan Arabic, puts Meccan in the West Arabian (Ḥijāzī) group as Urban Ḥijāzī Arabic (Mecca, Jeddah, Medina), contrasts it with Bedouin Ḥijāzī and the Tihāma dialects, and notes close affinities with Egyptian, Sudanese and Levantine Arabic {cite:brill-eall-meccan-arabic}. Its entry on Saudi Arabia contrasts the levelled Urban Ḥijāzī with rural Ḥijāzī, which forms a continuum with Yemen and Najd {cite:brill-eall-saudi-arabia}, and its entry on Bedouin Arabic links Bedouin Hijazi with the Bedouin varieties of the Negev and Sinai {cite:brill-eall-bedouin-arabic}. The Encyclopaedia Britannica's article on Saudi Arabia mentions three main dialect groups, eastern, central and western, without naming Hijazi {cite:britannica-saudi-arabia-climate}. The research summary found no UNESCO source on the point.

## Main features
**Phonology.** Brill says both Urban and rural Ḥijāzī realise Classical q as g and k as k (without the Najdi affrication of k in Mecca and Jeddah), and j as j, except for ž among the Ḥarb tribe near Medina {cite:brill-eall-saudi-arabia} {cite:brill-eall-bedouin-arabic}. Rural Ḥijāzī keeps the interdentals th and dh, whereas Urban Ḥijāzī merges them into t and d and merges dad and emphatic dh into dad, with emphatic dh surviving in some Meccan words {cite:brill-eall-meccan-arabic} {cite:brill-eall-saudi-arabia}. The diphthongs ay and aw become ē and ō; Meccan deletes unstressed short high vowels in open syllables and inserts an epenthetic a before consonant suffixes on certain stem shapes {cite:brill-eall-meccan-arabic}. A paper on Bedouin Hijazi describes quantity-sensitive, rightward, non-final stress {cite:kwpl-bedouin-hijazi-stress}.

**Morphology.** Urban Ḥijāzī has lost the feminine plural in verbs and pronouns and marks gender only in the second and third person singular {cite:brill-eall-meccan-arabic} {cite:brill-eall-saudi-arabia}. Meccan uses hāda, hādi and hadōl for "this, these" and hādak(a), hadīk(a) and hadōlāk(a) for "that, those", geminates the applicative markers -l- and -b- after -t, uses Form VII (an-, at-) as the passive, and replaces Form IX with Form II {cite:brill-eall-meccan-arabic}.

**Syntax.** Meccan uses both subject-verb-object and verb-subject-object order (with a resumptive pronoun for a fronted object), ḥagg for analytic possession, bi-, gāʿid or ʿammāl for the progressive, ḥa- or rāḥ for the future, and mā, mū, lā or ʾiṣḥa for negation {cite:brill-eall-meccan-arabic}; urban and rural Ḥijāzī share a preverbal gid, gad or gīd {cite:brill-eall-saudi-arabia}. A page of the Georgetown linguistics department notes that non-human plurals trigger feminine singular agreement {cite:georgetown-ruth-kramer-research}.

**Lexicon.** Hijazi shares Arabian items such as baġa/yabġi, "to want", and ḥagg, "of" {cite:brill-eall-saudi-arabia}; Meccan has local terms such as mirakkab, "kitchen", and daḥḥīn, "now", beside Turkish and Persian loans (duġri) and European ones (kanaba, kundēšan) {cite:brill-eall-meccan-arabic}.

## Speaker numbers
The research summary found no speaker count for Hijazi Arabic. The free page of Ethnologue (29th edition, 2026) keeps exact counts behind a paywall {cite:ethnologue-acw}; Brill's entry on Meccan Arabic says an accurate count is difficult because of Mecca's cosmopolitan population {cite:brill-eall-meccan-arabic}; and Glottolog, Britannica, UNESCO and the university sites give no total. This page therefore gives no figure.

## Common phrases
A research summary supplied to the museum added a comparison of sixty expressions and words across six Arabic dialects, including greetings, asking how someone is, asking what someone wants, and the numbers one to five. The reference variety here is urban Hijazi Arabic, associated with Jeddah, Mecca and Medina. Questions are generally addressed to one male speaker. Transliteration is normalized for comparison, and Arabic spelling may be normalized where a source uses phonetic transcription rather than Arabic script. The MSA equivalents are supplied for comparison and are not necessarily quotations from the dialect sources. The museum did not open the pages named; the sources are as the summary reported them, and its labels are given in the last column. {cite:fsi-saudi-arabic-basic-course-urban-hijazi-dialect} {cite:openarabic-saudi-arabic-resources}
