## Their definition and what they breed
These two ailments share that each is a flowing of matter from the brain, though some people give the name nazla to what descends alone to the throat, and zukam to what descends by the way of the smelling nose, pouring toward the eye, the facial skin and generally the front parts of the face. The nazla may dart to the throat, lung, gullet and stomach, perhaps ulcerating them; it often stirs the canine appetite ⁦(the editor's footnote: that a man eats much after long hunger and vomits upon it)⁩; it may dart into the nerves to the furthest members, and from it may be bred quinsies, pneumonia, pleurisy and consumption above all — especially if the nazla is hot and sharp — sour or salty stomach pains, and also colic, especially from the raw phlegmy kind {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.240} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.241}
## Their causes
The cause of all this is either a special temperamental heat or an external one — sun, hot winds, or smelling heating drugs ⁦(their names are not carried)⁩ — or a special temperamental cold or one arriving from outside, from cold air and the north wind, especially if the head is bared to them, above all when the brain has been loosened by a bath, exercise, anger, thought or the like. Venesection may also produce a looseness preparing the body to accept heat and cold, bringing on the nazla, especially after a copious letting. When the temperamental cold is strong and entrenched, as in the aged, it is said their catarrhs ripen only when they reach the utmost soundness of temperament and warmth; when food reaches the cold brain in the aged and the weak-brained it does not digest what enters for its weakness, so it leaves a surplus that descends. What arises from cold is more than what arises from heat {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.241}
## Temperaments, seasons and the ancients' sayings
The hot-tempered are readier to accept the external causes that produce catarrh than the cold-tempered, yet are safer from its arising from bodily causes; for the cold brain neither ripens the food that reaches it nor resolves the vapours rising to it — the food's arrival is turned back and the vapours condense as on the alembic's head, so catarrhs keep recurring. The nazla may be thick, or thin and watery, hot-bitter, salty and ill-tasting, hot-stinging, or cold; the cold nazla is ripened by fever, the hot gains nothing from fever. Catarrhal diseases multiply when the north wind blows, especially after a south wind, and in winter — above all when the summer after was northerly and rainless and the autumn southerly and rainy — and also in southern lands from the fullness of heads. Hippocrates said: most of those the catarrhs befall are not befallen by the spleen; Galen said: because most of those with the illness — ⁦(the printed text is deficient here, carried as is)⁩. I say ⁦(Ibn Sina speaks)⁩: perhaps it is that the catarrh-prone has rarer humours, while he whose humours are thick is little prone to catarrhs; and a headache accompanying the nazla increases it by drawing ⁦(the sayings of Hippocrates and Galen are carried as attributed; the 'I say' is Ibn Sina's own)⁩ {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.241}
