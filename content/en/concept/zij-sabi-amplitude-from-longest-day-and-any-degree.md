## Summary
In chapter 7 al-Battani teaches how to find the distance on the horizon circle between the rising and setting of the first of Cancer and the equator of the day, from an assumed longest day. The half-arc of the longest day is taken, an arc is worked out from the chords and taken from ninety, and what is left is that distance. He then widens the method to any degree by its declination and its half day-arc. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.33}.

## Steps for the first of Cancer
He says: take the assumed excess of the longest day and find what it comes to in degrees by multiplying each hour by fifteen; take half and add it to ninety, which gives the half-arc of the longest day. Then take the whole obliquity and subtract it from ninety, find the chord of what is left, multiply it by the chord of the half-arc of the longest day and divide by the radius, find the arc of the result and subtract it from ninety: what is left is the distance between the rising and setting of the first of Cancer and the equator of the day on the horizon circle, to the north. At the first of Capricorn the working is the same, with the half-arc of the shortest day, to the south. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.33}.

## Summer and winter risings, and any degree
He calls the risings and settings on the north side summer ones and those on the south side winter ones, and says that to find the bearing of rising and setting for any other point he takes the declination of whichever degree he likes, subtracts it from ninety and finds the chord of the remainder, then finds, or assumes, the half day-arc of that degree, multiplies its chord by the chord of the complement of the degree's declination, divides by the radius, finds the arc and takes it from ninety: what remains is the bearing of that degree's rising and setting on the horizon circle, northward if the declination is north and southward if it is south. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.33}.
