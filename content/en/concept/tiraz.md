## Overview
Embroidered cloth with Arabic inscriptions, made in the workshops of the caliphal palaces for the caliph and the notables of the state. Some pieces carry the names of caliphs and their dates. {cite:wikidata}

## In Nancy Britton (1938)
- **The name**: Britton says all decorative inscriptions on textiles are called tiraz, from a Persian word for embroidery; originally any embroidered ornament on a garment, the word came to mean any textile inscription and then the workshop where inscribed garments were made, short for *dar al-tiraz*. {cite:britton-early-islamic-textiles-mfa-boston-1938 p. 20} {cite:britton-early-islamic-textiles-mfa-boston-1938 p. 21}
- **Two kinds of workshop**: public factories owned by merchants who sold openly or exported, and private ones in the ruler's palace making garments for the royal household and robes of honour, which high officials received on appointment and during service; she cautions that a caliph's name on a tiraz may show only his sovereignty at the time, not that it came from his private workshop. {cite:britton-early-islamic-textiles-mfa-boston-1938 p. 21}
- **The formula**: inscriptions usually begin with the *basmala*, then the caliph's name, regnal title, honorific titles and blessings, sometimes the vizier's name (a sign of a royal workshop), then "made in the private (or public) tiraz" with the city, and finally the Hijri date. {cite:britton-early-islamic-textiles-mfa-boston-1938 p. 22} This form lasted until the Fatimid al-Mustansir, after which tiraz became a short pious formula of victory, sovereignty or good fortune, endlessly repeated. {cite:britton-early-islamic-textiles-mfa-boston-1938 p. 23}
- **Tinnis**: she quotes Nasir-i Khusraw's *Safarnama*: what is woven in the sultan's workshops is neither sold nor given; the ruler of Fars sent twenty thousand dinars to buy a garment of it and his agents failed for years; and Tinnis alone made *buqalamun*, whose colour changes with the hours of the day. {cite:britton-early-islamic-textiles-mfa-boston-1938 p. 21}
- **Early pieces**: she cites a piece in the Arab Museum, Cairo, dated 707 and made as a turban, and says only one fragment is known bearing the name of Harun al-Rashid. {cite:britton-early-islamic-textiles-mfa-boston-1938 p. 28}
