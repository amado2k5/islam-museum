## Its kind and its danger
These swellings are of the kind occurring in soft fleshes, especially the glandular; called parotis, also the ear's growth. Sometimes its pain grows so severe it kills; confusion of mind often precedes it; it and the swelling in the meatus kill the young more than the old, for it is softer in the aged while the young are hotter in temperament and matter, their painful swellings keener, more hurting and less patient before they ripen {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.232}
## The safest, the worst, and its matters
Of the swellings below the ear's root the safest is the one coming as a crisis of good signs; one from a crisis with no ripening sign, or before the crisis's time, is bad. In general they arise from a hot bilious or sanguine matter, or a melancholic or phlegmy one: the sanguine is shown by redness, heaviness, resisting touch and narrowing of the passages; the bilious — and what is of thin blood — by a stinging, burning pain without heaviness or narrowing but with intense flaming; the phlegmy by flabbiness, softness and little redness; the melancholic by hardness and little pain {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.232}
## The rule of its treatment
It is of the kind where care goes mostly to cooling it and drawing to it, not driving it off — when the matter poured down is a principal organ's surplus, above all in the crises of lethargus diseases, as he pointed out in the General Book. So its treatment must not begin with what a swelling deserves of binding and driving off at the start, then compounding of management, then pure dissolving; rather — especially when it occurs in fevers and headaches — the matter is helped to be drawn into the swelling by every device; if strongly tending to gather and be drawn, it is left to nature lest it cause severe pain and the fever double. If its start is severe pain, fomenting with plain water suffices; if mild, the known suffices; if not very light and it shows a head, what combines softening, loosening and ripening is used; if it is surmised it will not dissolve but suppurate, the pus must be brought out by gentle dissolving if possible or forceful — even by incision and suction.
