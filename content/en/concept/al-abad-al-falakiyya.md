## Summary
In Chapter 21, al-Farghani presents the distances of the Moon, the Sun, the planets, and the sphere of the fixed stars from the center of the Earth, relying on an Earth diameter he estimates at 6,500 miles, i.e., a radius of 3,250 miles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 213}. He notes that Ptolemy specified the distances of the Sun and the Moon alone and did not determine the distances of the other planets {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 212}.

## Method of Calculation
Al-Farghani takes the Earth's radius as the unit of measurement and assumes that the center of each sphere lies at a distance from the next sphere such that no empty space remains between the spheres, ending with the sphere of the fixed stars, whose center he sets at the center of the Earth {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 212}.

## The Stated Distances
- **The Moon:** Its least distance according to him is about 33 and a half Earth radii; the book records 109,037 miles, which does not match 33.5 radii, so the fraction is considered doubtful {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 213}. The Moon's greatest distance is 64 and one-sixth radii, or 208,542 miles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 213}.
- **Mercury:** Mercury's farthest distance from the Earth, which lies next to Venus, is 167 radii, or 542,750 miles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 213}.
- **Venus:** Its greatest distance is 1,120 radii, or 3,640,000 miles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 213}.
- **The Sun:** Its least distance is 1,220 radii, or 3,965,000 miles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 214}.
- **Mars:** Its greatest distance is 8,876 radii, or 28,847,000 miles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 214}.
- **Jupiter:** Its greatest distance is 14,405 radii, or 46,816,250 miles, with the reading of the last digit on the page being doubtful {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 214}.
- **Saturn and the Sphere of the Fixed Stars:** Saturn's greatest distance is 20,110 radii, or 65,357,500 miles; the diameter of the sphere of the fixed stars is 130,715,000 miles; its circumference is 410,818,570 miles, obtained by multiplying the diameter by 33 divided by 7; and one degree of it is 1,141,162 miles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 214}.

## Reading of the Numbers
The distance figures in radii mostly match the result of multiplying 3,250 by the printed numbers, which verifies the accuracy of the OCR reading. However, the fraction concerning the Moon's least distance and the reading "2383" adjacent to the Moon's greatest distance remain doubtful and should be checked against the image of page 213.
