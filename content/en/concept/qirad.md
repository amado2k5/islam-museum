## Summary
For the author, qirad or mudarabah is a partnership between a provider of money capital and a managing worker for a share of net profit, in which profits are distributed as agreed and losses fall on the capital providers in proportion to each one's share {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 144}; it is the alternative the author proposes to an interest-bearing loan {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 116}.

## Overview
Qirad is a partnership contract combining capital and labor: the capital provider contributes the capital, the worker contributes their effort, they share the profit at the agreed ratio, and financial loss falls on the capital provider {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 116} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 144}.

## Qirad and Loans
The author distinguishes between a loan—in which the lender is guaranteed their principal along with a guaranteed interest, which he considers impermissible—and qirad, in which the capital provider shares in potential profit {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 116}. He explains that a lender transfers ownership of the money to the borrower, so the borrower bears the risk, whereas in qirad the capital provider remains the owner and shares in loss in proportion to their capital {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 117}.

## Multiple Parties
There may be multiple capital providers and workers in a qirad; profits are distributed according to the agreement, and losses are distributed according to each owner's share {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 144}.

## Limited Liability
The author holds that limited liability exists in qirad, as the capital provider is liable only up to the amount of capital contributed, and liability does not extend to their personal assets {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 148}.



## Qirad: The Worker's Share and Tiered Mudarabah
The author states that profit in qirad is distributed as agreed, financial loss falls on the capital provider, and the worker bears no loss other than the loss of their labor {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 297}.

The worker may not take anything from the profit before it is fully realized and settled, except with the capital provider's permission, because profit serves as a buffer protecting capital against loss, because the capital provider is a partner, and because the worker's ownership of the profit is not yet settled {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 206}. They may agree to divide profits annually or every six months, which is treated as a payment on account {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 206}.

Neither the worker nor the capital provider may receive a fixed lump sum from the profit, as that could sever the partnership; the author permits this for the worker alone, not the capital provider, because of the semblance of riba for the capital provider {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 251}.

The author permits the worker to combine a fixed wage with a share of profit, since whatever allows a wage also allows a partnership {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 252}. As for a second-tier mudarib, the first mudarib may not hand over the capital to them without explicit permission according to the Malikis and Shafi'is, whereas among the Hanafis it is permitted by delegation {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 253}, and he presents two methods for distributing profit in that case {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 254}.

The author likens the mudarib to the entrepreneur in modern economics—acting as its manager and innovator—which explains their share of the profit {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 281}.
