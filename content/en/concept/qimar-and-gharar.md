## Summary
The author regards gambling as forbidden in all religions, yet argues that capitalism legalized it in practice if not in theory, through lotteries, sweepstakes, and stock exchanges {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 352}. He notes that the stock exchange has come to be called a gambling club {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 352}.

## His View of the Stock Exchange
The author describes stock exchange operations as resting largely on price speculation, with contracts involving no actual delivery and settled instead on price differences, giving rise to a fictitious speculative economy divorced from the real economy {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 352}. He argues that the small investor's profit depends on luck and lottery rather than financial analysis, and that major players deceive small ones {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 352}.

## His Proposed Reform
The author calls for ridding the stock exchange of unlawful practices so that it becomes a market for liquidating shares and financing projects without speculation, betting, gambling, gharar, or deception {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 352} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 353}. The section heading in the book is "Gambling and Gharar," while the text itself focuses on gambling, with gharar appearing in the heading and in the analysis of speculation {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 352}.
