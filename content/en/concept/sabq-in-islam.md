## Summary
Al-sabaq (most likely with an open 'b') refers to the stake, wager, prize, or pledge—all denoting the wealth set aside for the winner over those they outstrip—while al-sabq (with a silent 'b') refers to the race itself {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 229}.

## The Hadith
The author cites the hadith "There is no prize (sabaq) except in racing camels (khuff), horses (hafir), or archery (nasl)," where khuff (footpad) alludes to camels, hafir (hoof) to horses, and nasl (arrowhead) to arrows {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 229}.

## Types of Competitions
The first type is permissible with a prize, namely what aids and prepares for jihad, such as camel and horse racing {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 229}. The second is permissible without a prize, such as foot-racing, wrestling, and running competitions {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 229}. The third is impermissible with or without a prize, such as backgammon according to all jurists, and chess according to the majority because it distracts the heart from remembrance of God and prayer {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 229}.

## The Prize (Ju'l)
In permissible competitions, the prize is provided by the ruler, his deputy, or a third party; if the competitors themselves put it up, there is juristic disagreement {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 230}. Al-sabaq does not fall under prohibited uncertainty (gharar) or gambling {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 230}.

## Its Place in Distribution
The author places al-sabaq under primary distribution because it resembles war spoils distributed to fighters as a result of struggle and skill {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 230} (see [[concept/distribution-in-islam|Distribution in Islam]]).
