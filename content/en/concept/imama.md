## Overview
In the Encyclopaedia of Islam, imama, the imamate, means the supreme leadership of the Muslim community after the death of the Prophet. The article followed here deals with the theological and legal theory, and sends the reader to its entry on the caliphate for the history of the institution. It sets the positions of the Sunnis, the Mu'tazila, the Zaydis, the Twelver Imamis, the Isma'ilis and the Kharijis side by side. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1196}

The imamate, as Ahmad Amin presents it in *Duha al-Islam*, is the most important question on which the discourse of the Imamiyya turns. It is the centre of their inquiries, most secondary questions return to it, and most of the disagreement between them and the people of the Sunna revolves around it. {cite:duha-islam p.850}

## Early development
The installation of [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] as Khalifat Rasul Allah, "Vicar of the Messenger of God", affirmed that the community stayed united under one leader. According to the article, it favoured a preferential right to the imamate for the early Qurayshi Companions and implicitly denied any right based on kinship with the Prophet. Broad theoretical discussion began with the crisis that opened with the rebellion against [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]]. When the civil war ended, leaving [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya]] as ruler in practice, the community was deeply divided over who held the rightful imamate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1196}

The Uthmaniyya, upholders of Uthman as a just caliph, rejected the revolt and the caliphate of [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] that came from it. They included Mu'awiya's partisans and the upholders of the early caliphate's principles, above all the families of the non-Hashimite early Companions. The partisans of Ali, his shi'a, held the revolt just, because in their view Uthman's unjust acts had cost him his title, and looked for leadership to the clan of Muhammad, especially Ali's sons. Their support for the ahl al-bayt did not mean rejecting Abu Bakr and [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]]. The article calls the belief in Ali as divinely appointed heir with supernatural powers, which sources attribute to the largely legendary [[person/abdullah-ibn-saba|Abd Allah ibn Saba]], marginal at most. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1196} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1197}

The [[group/khawarij|Kharijis]], who seceded from Ali's partisans, shared their view of Uthman and held Ali's caliphate legitimate at first, but repudiated him once he agreed to arbitration, and Mu'awiya as a rebel. After the second civil war and the failure of [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Ibn al-Zubayr]], the supporters of the early caliphate idealised the age of the first three caliphs. They accepted Umayyad rule and opposed revolt, yet did not count that rule as genuine caliphate, which in their view ended with Uthman. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1197}

After the death of [[person/husayn-ibn-ali|al-Husayn]], radical currents grew among the Shi'a. In the movement of [[person/al-mukhtar-ibn-abi-ubayd|al-Mukhtar]] they broke with the conservative wing, held that the community had erred in denying Ali's divine right, and expected [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-hanafiyya|Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya]] to be the Mahdi. The [[group/murjia|Murji'a]] tried to bridge moderate Shi'is and the Uthmaniyya: they affirmed the superiority of Abu Bakr and Umar, left the case of Uthman, Ali and the other participants to God's judgment, and accepted Umayyad rule as decreed by God without necessarily justifying its conduct. The Kharijis, failing to win Ibn al-Zubayr, declared war on the wider community and began choosing their own imams. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1197}

## The people of the Sunna
The summary of the data states that the people of the Sunna make the imamate a matter entrusted to the choice and consultation of the community. {cite:duha-islam p.850}

According to the article, Sunni doctrine was shaped by the aim, shared by the Uthmaniyya and the Murji'a, of defending the unity and internal peace of the community under the historical caliphate against opposition claims, without unqualified approval of that record. It drew a sharp line between the early caliphate of the Rashidun, the "vicariate of prophecy", and the later imamate, which had the character of worldly kingship and admittedly included unjust rulers. The traditionalist form of this view appears in canonical hadith, the Hanbali creeds and [[person/al-ashari|al-Ash'ari]]. By the fourth/tenth century the caliphate of the four Rashidun was undisputed Sunni dogma, after the Kufan view including Ali was eventually favoured by [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]], who had first held the Uthmani position. Hanbali creeds and al-Ash'ari held that only the most excellent, al-afdal, could be rightful caliph, and al-Ash'ari counted the imamate of a less excellent man as worldly kingship. Later caliphs such as [[person/umar-ibn-abd-al-aziz|Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz]] might come close to the ideal, but nobody expected the vicariate of prophecy to return after the thirty years a well-known hadith assigns it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1197}

For the later imamate the traditionalists set minimal conditions: the imam must be a Muslim and a Qurayshi, and the imamate could become binding without any act of recognition by the community. Ahmad ibn Hanbal affirmed the validity of an imamate seized by force, ghalaba. A Muslim should not join a civil war when there is no imam, and owes the established imam, just or unjust, obedience and support unless he violates the sharia. The imamate was forfeited only through apostasy or neglect of the communal prayer. Hanafi and early Maturidi doctrine stood close to this; [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]], of the Murji'a, stated no preference between Uthman and Ali but ranked both below Abu Bakr and Umar, and Hanafi creeds rarely mention the imamate after the Rashidun. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1197}

The Shafi'i school and the Kullabiyya kalam school took a different course, which increasingly influenced the Ash'ari school after [[person/al-baqillani|al-Baqillani]]. They did not confine the imamate to the most excellent and admitted that of a less excellent man, especially to avoid discord. The article says this let them treat the post-Rashidun caliphate as a sound imamate, to be judged by standards drawn from the ideal early caliphate, and that a legal system strongly influenced by Mu'tazili theory was built on the imam's qualifications, investiture and duties. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1197} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1198}

Its high point was [[person/al-mawardi|al-Mawardi]] and his [[work/al-ahkam-al-sultaniyya-mawardi|al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya]], written as the Abbasid caliphate revived in the late [[polity/buyid-dynasty|Buyid]] period, to ground the caliphate in divine law. It also treated the vizierate and the emirate, which had taken over much of the caliph's authority, and legitimised them while keeping the caliph sovereign. The Hanbali [[person/abu-yala-al-farra|Abu Ya'la al-Farra']] followed him, but reaffirmed against him the validity of imamate by usurpation and denied that immorality, injustice or heterodoxy forfeits it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1198}

## The developed Sunni doctrine
The article lists the main points of the fully developed doctrine. Appointing an imam is permanently obligatory for the community, by the common view because of his functions under revealed law and not on rational grounds. There can be only one imam at a time, and the Karramiyya doctrine that Ali and Mu'awiya were both imams at once was rejected. The qualifications were Qurayshi descent, legal knowledge sufficient for a judge, probity of a legal witness, majority, physical fitness and capacity for political and military duties. The imam may be invested by his predecessor's appointment or by election. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1198}

The common view held that Abu Bakr was elected; the view that the Prophet appointed him was held by [[person/al-hasan-al-basri|al-Hasan al-Basri]], [[person/ibn-hazm|Ibn Hazm]] and [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]]. Any Muslim of probity who understands the imamate and can judge a candidate qualifies as an elector, and the number of electors needed, the ahl al-hall wa l-aqd, ranged from one, in the common Ash'ari view, to the generality, in Abu Ya'la's. Election was understood as choosing the most excellent. Loss of mental or physical fitness or of liberty ended the imamate; many Shafi'is added loss of probity, which Hanbali and Hanafi doctrine denied. The imam's duties included guarding the faith, enforcing law between disputants, applying the hudud, keeping peace and defending the territory of Islam, leading jihad against those resisting Islam's supremacy, collecting and distributing the legal revenues, and appointing reliable delegates. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1198}

## The Mu'tazila
The [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazila]] arose in the late Umayyad age to reunite the community on a compromise among the politico-religious parties. Like the Kharijis they stressed the need for a just imam and the community's duty to remove an unjust one, if necessary by force, but they refused the Khariji condemnation of Uthman, Ali, Talha, al-Zubayr and their supporters. Early members commonly suspended judgment on the conflicts of Uthman's day and the [[event/battle-of-the-camel|Battle of the Camel]]; later doctrine cleared Uthman and Ali and condemned Mu'awiya almost unanimously. The Umayyad caliphate was generally abhorred but not rejected in principle, and Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz was commonly accepted as a rightful imam. Later Mu'tazili doctrine grew more pro-Alid: after [[person/al-jubbai|Abu Ali al-Jubba'i]] the imamate of al-Hasan was common doctrine, and [[person/abd-al-jabbar-al-hamadhani|Abd al-Jabbar]] argued in his *al-Mughni* for the imamate of several Alids. The Basran school first ranked Abu Bakr highest after the Prophet and restricted the imamate to the most excellent, whereas the Baghdad school ranked Ali higher and so admitted the less excellent. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1198} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1199}

Early Mu'tazilis generally did not confine the imamate to Quraysh, but later doctrine required a Qurayshi when a qualified one was available. Most rejected the view of [[person/al-jahiz|al-Jahiz]] and others that appointing an imam is obligatory on rational grounds, and only [[person/abu-bakr-al-asamm|Abu Bakr al-Asamm]] thought each town might choose its own. In other respects the article finds Mu'tazili doctrine close to the Sunni. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1199}

## The Zaydiyya
The [[group/zaydiyya|Zaydiyya]], supporters of [[person/zayd-ibn-ali|Zayd ibn Ali]]'s revolt, recognised no hereditary line of imams but supported any member of the ahl al-bayt who claimed the imamate by "rising", khuruj, against illegitimate rulers; the prevalent doctrine limited it to descendants of al-Hasan and al-Husayn. In the second/eighth century the school divided into the Batriyya, who accepted the imamate of Abu Bakr and Umar because Ali had pledged allegiance to them, and the [[group/jarudiyya|Jarudiyya]], who held with the Imamis that the Prophet had named Ali his executor, wasi, by designation, nass. From the third/ninth century the Jarudi tendency came to prevail. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1199}

The Zaydi doctrine of the fourth/tenth century held that Ali, al-Hasan and al-Husayn were designated by the Prophet in a way that was obscure and could be found only by investigation, which, unlike the Imami view, softened the early community's fault. Afterwards any qualified descendant of al-Hasan or al-Husayn becomes imam through the formal call, da'wa, and rising, not through election and contract. The qualifications besides descent resemble the Sunni and Mu'tazili ones, with stress on religious knowledge, independent judgment, piety and courage. Usually one imam is admitted at a time. Pretenders who fell short could be recognised as restricted imams or "callers". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1199}

## The Twelver Imamiyya
This account is from the *al-Kafi* of al-Kulayni as transmitted by Ahmad Amin, and it is the view of the Imamiyya, not the museum's position. *Al-Kafi* states that the imam has a spiritual connection with God of the same kind as that of the prophets and messengers; that the earth cannot remain without an imam, who is God's proof against His servants; and that faith in the imam is part of faith. {cite:duha-islam p.850-852}

*Al-Kafi* describes the imamate as "the rank of the prophets and the inheritance of the executors, the vicegerency of God and the vicegerency of the Messenger." {cite:duha-islam p.850-852}

According to the Encyclopaedia of Islam, the basic Imami conception was formed in the time of [[person/jafar-al-sadiq|Ja'far al-Sadiq]], who died in 148/765. It rests the imamate on mankind's permanent need for a divinely guided, infallible leader and teacher, raising it to the level of prophecy; the imam differs from the messenger only in transmitting no scripture. The imam must be immune from sin and error, ma'sum, though he may practise dissimulation, taqiyya, when he or his followers are in danger. He is entitled to political leadership, but his imamate does not depend on holding power. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1199}

Following the radical wing of the early Shi'a, the Imamiyya rejected the caliphates of Abu Bakr, Umar and Uthman and held that the Prophet had appointed Ali as wasi by designation, and that most Companions had turned away from the faith by ignoring it. The imamate then passes from father to son among the descendants of al-Husayn until the Mahdi. When [[person/muhammad-ibn-ali-al-jawad|Muhammad al-Jawad]] succeeded at seven years old in 203/818, the majority held that a minor can fulfil every function of the imamate. The crisis over the eleventh imam, who left no apparent son, was resolved by affirming a son's existence and the doctrine of the absence, ghayba. The twelfth imam, though hidden, is held to live and to fulfil the essential functions, and is identified with the Mahdi, whose return is expected before the world ends. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1200}

Authoritative Imami hadith holds that the world cannot exist for a moment without a hujja of God, and that there is one imam at a time. The imams are the "light of God", His witnesses and signs, and heirs of the Prophet's knowledge, with perfect knowledge of the Qur'an in both its outward and inward meaning. Imami theologians argued that the imamate is obligatory on rational grounds, that God must establish an imam out of benevolence, that Ali was clearly named, and that the imam must be sinless and the most excellent of his time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1200}

## The Isma'ilis
Isma'ilism, which branched off from the Imamiyya after Ja'far's death, kept the Imami conception of a permanent need for a sinless and infallible imam and added a cyclical view of history in which seven imams follow the speaker-prophet, natiq, in each era. In the sixth era the seventh imam was Muhammad ibn Isma'il ibn Ja'far, expected in pre-Fatimid Isma'ilism to return as the Mahdi. Fatimid doctrine altered this by recognising the [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimids]] as imams of the sixth era, which moved the eschatological expectations into the future. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1200}

After the Fatimids, Isma'ilism survived chiefly in two branches. One recognised al-Tayyib, infant son of the Fatimid [[person/al-amir-fatimid|al-Amir]], who died in 524/1130; it holds that the hidden imam stays in touch with his community and rejects the Imami ghayba. The Nizaris, who recognised Nizar, son of [[person/al-mustansir-fatimid|al-Mustansir]], who died in 487/1094, kept a line of living imams; after the resurrection, qiyama, proclaimed in 559/1164, the imam was ranked above the natiq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1201} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1202}

## The Kharijis
Khariji doctrine tied the legitimacy of the imam most radically to his justice. In this teaching any breach of divine law forfeits it, the unjust imam may be removed by force, and he and his supporters are regarded as unbelievers unless they repent. By this reasoning Uthman and Ali, legitimate at first, both ceased to be so. Establishing an imam is obligatory by the common view. He is elected, his contract needs the pledge of two just Muslims, and only the most excellent is entitled. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1200} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1201}

All Kharijis rejected a Qurayshi prerogative and admitted any qualified Muslim, even of slave origin. Special weight fell on the imam's austerity, on commanding the proper and forbidding the improper, and on jihad against non-Khariji Muslims. The [[group/ibadiyya|Ibadiyya]] is the only Khariji branch studied through its own writings. It distinguished four states of the believing community before its enemies, manifestation, defence, self-sacrifice and concealment, and held that only the imam of manifestation may exercise all the functions of the imamate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1201}

## Later developments
Sunni thought followed the fortunes of the Abbasid caliphate. [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|Al-Ghazali]], writing under the [[polity/seljuk-empire|Seljuk]] sultanate, viewed the caliph as the representative head of Islam, installed by the pledge of the effective ruler, whose rule the caliph legitimises by recognition, a move the article links to the need to keep the acts of governors and judges lawful. After the fall of the caliphate of Baghdad, [[person/saad-al-din-al-taftazani|al-Taftazani]] held that judicial acts could not depend on a qualified Qurayshi imam when none could be set up. The Abbasid shadow caliphate in Cairo under the [[polity/mamluk-sultanate|Mamluks]] was practically ignored even by Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn Jama'a, who, treating real power as essential, implicitly placed the imam's functions with the actual ruler. The classical qualifications could be set aside by the doctrine of necessity, darura, and the classical doctrine was left in abeyance rather than revised. Later scholars often placed the imamate among the derived matters, furu, not among the fundamentals of religion, in answer to the Shi'i view that puts it at the core. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1201}

Modernist Sunni attitudes vary. Some denied that religion defines an imamate, as in the tract endorsed by the Turkish Grand National Assembly in 1922 and in Ali Abd al-Raziq's treatise of 1925. Others, notably [[person/rashid-rida|Rashid Rida]] in his book of 1923, proposed restoring a universal imamate on the model of the Rashidun. Modernist thought stresses consultation, [[concept/consultation-shura|shura]], and election as the sole way to establish an imam. Imamism kept its classical concepts; from the seventh/thirteenth century its esoteric teaching was greatly elaborated, partly under Sufi and Isma'ili influence, and the imamate's eternal reality, called walaya, was defined as the inner side of prophecy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1201}

## The academic view
Ahmad Amin describes the imamate as the colouring of the Imamiyya belief. {cite:duha-islam p.850}
