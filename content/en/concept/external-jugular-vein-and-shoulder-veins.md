## Summary
Ibn Sina describes the external jugular vein and how it forms from two parts that meet, what branches from it as the scapular and cephalic veins, and how it divides into an inner part to the jaws and tongue and an outer part to the head and ears {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.90}.

## How it forms
As the external jugular rises from the collarbone it divides in two: one, as it parts, goes forward and to the side; the other goes first forward and descends, then rises and appears over the collarbone, circles it, and rises over the neck until it reaches the first and mixes with it, and from the two comes the known external jugular. Before they mix two parts part from it: one goes across and the two meet at the junction of the collarbones in the deep place; the other slants toward the outside of the neck and its two never meet again {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.90}.

## The scapular and cephalic veins
From these two pairs spider-web branches arise that escape the senses, but from the second pair in particular three perceptible veins of some size arise. One extends over the shoulder and is called the scapular, and from it comes the cephalic; two at its sides keep to it as far as the head of the shoulder, but one stops and spreads there, and the front one passes to the head of the upper arm and spreads; the scapular passes both and goes to the end of the hand {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.90}.

## After the mixing
After the two ends of the external jugular mix, it may divide in two: one part goes inward and gives small branches spreading in the upper jaw and far larger ones in the lower jaw, with parts of both kinds around the tongue and in the outer parts of the muscles there; the other part goes outward and spreads in the places by the head and ears {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.90}.
