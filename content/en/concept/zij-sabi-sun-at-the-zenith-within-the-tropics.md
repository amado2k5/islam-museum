## Summary
Al-Battani says that on every parallel whose distance from the equator is less than the obliquity the Sun passes the zenith twice a year, which is known from the declination table, together with the part of the zodiac where it happens. It occurs when the pole's altitude there equals the declination of the degree the Sun occupies, so an upright gnomon has no noon shadow that day, and the direction of the shadow changes before and after. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion pp.26-27}.

## The day with no shadow
In a line whose pole altitude equals the declination of the degree the Sun occupies, with the declination northward, the Sun agrees with the zenith of those living beneath its path, so an upright gnomon there casts no noon shadow. The distance of the equator from the zenith equals the declination, he adds. It follows that once the Sun turns away from them the noon shadows lean south until it returns and stands over their heads a second time, with no shadow again, and then moves off so that the shadows lean north. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion pp.26-27}.

## Two passages a year
He notes that the first of Aries and the first of Libra, where the declination is zero, are where the Sun passes the zenith on the equator line, and that on every other line within the tropics the passage happens twice a year in two parts of the zodiac that the declination table identifies. See [[concept/zij-sabi-declination-table-in-four-rows|the declination table]]. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.26}.
