## Definition
Ikhtilaf (disagreement). In the lexicon it is the verbal noun of ikhtalafa, the opposite of agreement; whatever is not equal has differed. Khilaf is opposition; one opposes another to a thing when he disobeys him after forbidding him. Jurists use ikhtilaf in its lexical sense, and khilaf likewise. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1940}
Related terms: some glossers distinguish khilaf from ikhtilaf: the latter is used of a view built on evidence, the former of what has no evidence; the distinction is not always kept, and the two words are sometimes used as one; khilaf is the wider, and is alone used of opposing a consensus. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1940} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1941}
Tanazu' (dispute) is sometimes used by the jurists in the sense of ikhtilaf. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1941}
Iftirak, tafarruq and firqa (separation) mean that each group of people stands apart by itself; these are narrower than ikhtilaf. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1941}
Disagreement in matters of ijtihad ('ilm al-khilaf): the jurist must establish the point of disagreement, since to report a disagreement on a point where there is none is an error. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1941}
Kinds of disagreement: not every conflict between two views is a true disagreement; it is either in expression, or in variety, or in contrariety, and the last is true disagreement. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1941}
Disagreement in expression: each of two disagreeing parties names the meaning in words other than the other's. The example: explaining the straight path as the Qur'an or as Islam, which agree since Islam is following the Qur'an. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1941}
Disagreement of variety: each names some kinds of a general term by way of example and warning, not as an exact definition. The example: the explanations of the three kinds of servants named in the verse of Fatir (32), by the times of prayer, or by almsgiving, selling and usury. It may be obligation in one case and recommendation in another, as the duty of holy war on some, almsgiving on others, and teaching on a third. Collective duties are sometimes, in some times and places, become personal for one who is not discharged by others. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1941} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1942}
Al-Shatibi restricted the disagreement that is not true to ten kinds, including: disagreement in expression; a disagreement not on the same point; the varying views of one imam due to a change of ijtihad; and disagreement in act, not ruling, as the readers' differences in the modes of recitation, which they did not deny to one another but allowed. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1942}
The ruling on disagreement by kind: the matters of religion are either its principles or its branches, and each is either proved by decisive evidence or not, so there are four kinds: {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1942}
First, principles proved by decisive evidence, such as God's existence and unity, the angels, the books, the mission of Muhammad and resurrection; there is no room for disagreement, and whoever misses the truth in them is an unbeliever. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1942}
Second, some matters of principle such as the vision of God in the hereafter, the creation of the Qur'an, and the exit of the monotheists from hell; some hold the one who disagrees is an unbeliever, al-Shafi'i among them; the condition of not declaring unbelief is that the dissenter accepts what the Messenger brought, and denial that declares unbelief is to deny what the Messenger reported and claim it is a lie. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1942} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1943}
Third, branches known from religion by necessity, such as the obligation of the five prayers and the prohibition of adultery; there is no room for disagreement, and whoever disagrees is an unbeliever. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1943}
Fourth, branches of ijtihad whose evidence may be hidden; disagreement occurs here, and the dissenter is excused for the hiddenness or conflict of the evidence, or disagreement over its establishment; this is the kind the jurists mean when they say there is disagreement on a matter. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1943}
If there is a sound, clear text which the jurist did not know of and therefore disagreed with it, he is excused after his effort, and those who follow him are excused in leaving his opinion for the sound evidence that proves he did not know it; but this is not counted as disagreement in legal matters, since it is ijtihad that did not meet its object. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1943}
Proof that disagreement in branch matters is permitted: first, what the Companions did at the Qurayza expedition, when the Prophet said on the day of the confederates: «let none pray 'asr except in Qurayza»; some reached 'asr on the road and said we will not pray until we reach it, and others said we will pray, since that was not what was meant; the Prophet was told and did not rebuke any of them. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1943} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1944}
Second, the agreement of the Companions, in matters on which they disagreed, that each party accepts the others' acting on their own ijtihad, in worship, marriage, inheritance, allowances and government and the rest. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1943}
Disagreement without use: Ibn Taymiyya said disagreement may arise in the wording of Qur'anic exegesis without any basis in transmission from the Prophet, or on reports whose sound cannot be told from the weak, with no sound reasoning; such disagreement has no benefit, and speech in it is idle; what Muslims need to know God has set a proof for. Examples are the disagreement about the People of the Cave, the part of the cow with which Moses was struck, and the size of Noah's ark; such things are known only by transmission, and what comes from the People of the Book, such as Ka'b and Wahb, may neither be believed nor denied without proof. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1943} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1944}
Whether permitted disagreement is itself a kind of agreement: al-Shatibi holds that the disagreement which counts in appearance goes back in reality to agreement: disagreement in some legal matters goes back either to a matter lying between two clear sides that conflict in the view of jurists, or to the hiddenness of some evidence, or not having seen the evidence; the last is not true disagreement, since if the jurist saw the hidden evidence he would retract. As for the first, his wavering between the two sides is a search for the Lawgiver's purpose, and following the evidence that guides to it; they agree on this purpose, so the mutual love and harmony of those who disagree in matters of ijtihad becomes clear, since they meet in seeking the purpose of the Lawgiver, and so they do not become sects nor split apart. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1944}
Al-Sha'rani took another way, carrying each view to a state of those obliged: whoever is strong is addressed by the firm rule, and whoever is weak is addressed by the concession. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1944} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1944}
Is disagreement a mercy? It is the well-known view that the disagreement of the community's jurists in branch matters is a mercy and a breadth for it; those who say so cite the report of Ibn Abbas, very weak in its chain, «my companions are like stars in the sky; whichever you follow, you are guided; and the disagreement of my companions is a mercy to you», and the hadith «the disagreement of my community was made a mercy, and among those before us it was a punishment». {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1944}
The jurists took support from sayings of some Successors: al-Qasim ibn Muhammad said God benefited by the disagreement of the Prophet's Companions in their practice, so that whoever acts on one of them sees himself in ease and sees that a better one has done it; Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz said he did not like the Companions to have had no disagreement, since if there were one view people would be in hardship; Yahya ibn Sa'id said the disagreement of the people of knowledge is an ease. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1945}
Ibn Abidin said that disagreement among the mujtahids in branch matters, not disagreement as such, is one of the signs of mercy, and the more disagreement, the fuller the mercy. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1945}
This rule is not agreed. Ibn Wahb reported from Malik that there is no ease in the disagreement of the Prophet's Companions; the truth is in one view. Al-Muzani, a disciple of al-Shafi'i, said God censured disagreement and ordered a return on it to the Book and the sunna. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1945}
Ibn Taymiyya took a middle way: disagreement may be a mercy and may be a punishment. A dispute over rulings is a mercy when it does not lead to grave evil; the truth in itself is one, and its being hidden from the obligated may be a mercy from God; likewise what is in the markets may in fact be usurped, and if a person does not know, all of it is lawful for him; hidden knowledge of what would bring hardship may be a mercy, as hidden knowledge of what would bring concession may be a punishment; the concession is a mercy; and what the soul dislikes may be more beneficial, as in holy war. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1945} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1946}
Causes of the jurists' disagreement: it either comes from desire or from ijtihad permitted by the law. Disagreement from desire is blameworthy, since the jurist follows the evidence; if he bends the evidence to what he desires, he makes the evidence follow his desire. Al-Shatibi said disagreement from desire is not true disagreement, and that desire leads to following the ambiguous, seeking victory and the excuse of disagreement, and to division and hatred; so the views of the people of desire are not counted in the disagreement the law recognises; they are mentioned only so that they may be refuted. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1946}
Permitted disagreement from ijtihad has causes, which the usulis touch on and which have been collected: early writers include Ibn al-Sayyid al-Batalyawsi in «al-Insaf fi asbab al-khilaf», Ibn Rushd in the introduction to «Bidayat al-mujtahid», and Ibn Hazm in «al-Ihkam». They go back either to the evidence itself or to the usuli rules concerning it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1946}
Among causes going back to the evidence, Ibn al-Sayyid lists: generality and ambiguity of words open to interpretation; a text that may or may not stand alone for a ruling; a text that may be general or particular, as in the saying «there is no compulsion in religion», whether it is general or limited to the People of the Book who accept the poll-tax; differences of the readings of the Qur'an and the reports of hadith; claims of abrogation or not; and a jurist's not having seen a hadith or having forgotten it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1946}
Causes going back to the usuli rules are hard to count, since every disputed rule of usul gives rise to disagreement in the branches built on it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1946}
Denunciation and taking account of disagreed matters. First, denunciation: al-Suyuti said «what is disputed is not denounced, but what is agreed is denounced», and he excepted cases where a disputed view is denounced: where its basis is remote, as the hadd on a pledgee who has intercourse with a pledged slave-woman; where it comes before a judge who rules by his own belief; and where the denouncer has a right in it, as a Muslim husband who prevents his dhimmi wife from drinking wine. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1946}
Ibn Taymiyya held that a jurist may believe that his opponent in matters disputed between the lawful and the forbidden has committed the forbidden, but the threat and the curse do not reach one who has made permitted ijtihad; he is excused and rewarded for his ijtihad, and so is one who follows him in a valid following. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1947}
Second, taking account of disagreement: whoever holds a thing permitted leaves it if another holds it forbidden; in the case of obligation, whoever holds it lawful is recommended to do it if some imam holds it obligatory: one who holds witr not obligatory is recommended to keep it, to leave the disagreement of those who make it obligatory. One who holds a thing obligatory cannot take account of the view of one who holds it forbidden, nor one who holds it forbidden of one who holds it obligatory. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1947}
The ruling on taking account of disagreement: al-Suyuti of the Shafi'is held leaving the disagreement is recommended, with conditions: that it does not cause taking account of another disagreement; that it does not oppose a established sunna, which is why raising the hands in prayer is a sunna and the Hanafi view that it invalidates prayer is disregarded, since it is established from fifty Companions; and that its ground is strong enough not to be a slip, which is why fasting on a journey is better for one who can bear it, and the view of Dawud that it is not valid is disregarded. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.1947}
