## Summary
Many find the unglazed earthenware of the early Islamic period less important than the glazed, lacking its variety of colour and decoration and being mostly a humble product for lowly uses; [[work/nishapur-pottery-of-the-early-islamic-period|Wilkinson]], however, sees its chief interest in revealing aspects of life neither described in early Islamic literature nor, given the scarcity of painting, pictured. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 332}

## Function
At [[place/nishapur|Nishapur]] the difference between unglazed and glazed was one of function, not merely of cost: unglazed ware served for cooking vessels and above all for storing water, its porosity unfitting it for milk, wine or oil. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 332} Through it potable water reached the city and was stored and drunk — storage jars, large pitchers holding water in every house, and small pitchers to drink from — and its pitchers kept water cool by evaporation in a hot, dry land. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 332} Most small drinking vessels had handles because of their wet surface, which explains why the tapered tumbler shape is rare in Iranian earthenware though common in Iranian glass. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 332}

## Other uses
It was also made into lanterns, lamps, candlesticks, flowerpots, coin containers and toys, parts of fireplaces, and moulds and dies for making pottery itself, glazed and unglazed. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 332} By analogy with E. W. Lane's account of Egypt, Wilkinson suggests that well-to-do Nishapuris used unglazed pitchers in the warm months and glazed ones in the cold. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 332}
