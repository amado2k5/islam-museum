## Summary
Ibn Sina opens chapter 25, on the treatment of swellings, with their kinds and causes, and sets the rule that dissolvers are not brought near at the beginning, but the organs are set right and repelling astringents used, then orders how to mix them with dissolvers by the stages {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.304}.

## Kinds and causes
Swellings are hot, cold, soft and cold-hard. Their causes are evident or antecedent: the antecedent is fullness, the evident a fall, a blow or a bite. For a swelling from an antecedent cause, or from an evident one that fits a fullness of the body, when it is not in organs adjoining the principal ones, no dissolver at all may be brought near at the beginning; rather the expelling organ, or the whole body, is set right, and everything that repels, draws to the opposite side and contracts is brought near {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.304}.

## Drawing to the opposite side
The matter may also be drawn to the opposite side of that organ by exercise or by a heavy load on it. Often matter is drawn away from a swollen hand when a heavy load is carried in the other and held for an hour {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.304}.

## Astringents and dissolvers
In purely hot swellings the repelling astringents are sought pure; in cold swellings they are mixed with something of hot power along with astringency, such as lemon grass and onycha. As the stages advance, the astringency is lessened and the dissolver strengthened until the peak, when they are mixed in equal parts, and at the decline only the dissolver and the relaxer are used. For a cold, soft swelling the dissolver must be a hot, drying thing, more so than for a hot swelling {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.304}.

## Evident cause without fullness
A swelling from an evident cause with no fullness of humours is treated at first by relaxing and dissolving {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.304}.
