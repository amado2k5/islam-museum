## Summary
Ibn Sina finds a musical nature in the pulse, likening the proportions of its times to rhythm and of its states to harmony, and then divides rhythm into good and bad {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.170-171}.

## The likeness to music
As the art of music is completed by composing notes in a proportion of acuteness and gravity and by cycles of rhythm in the times between strokes, so the proportion of the pulse's times in speed and frequency is rhythmical and the proportion of its states in strength, weakness and size is harmonic; these may agree or disagree {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.170-171}.

## Galen and touch
Galen held that the perceptible degree of rhythmic proportion follows one of the musical ratios, but Ibn Sina thinks it a great matter to fix these ratios by touch; it is easiest for one practised in rhythm and the proportion of notes who can measure the made against the known {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.171}.

## Good and bad rhythm
Rhythm measures the proportions of the four times of the two motions and two rests; where touch falls short, it measures the time of expansion against the time between two expansions. A pulse has good rhythm or bad, and bad rhythm is of three kinds: the shifted, which has the rhythm of the next age, as a child's pulse with a youth's rhythm; the opposed, as a child's with an old man's; and the out of rhythm, which resembles no age's. A great departure from rhythm points to a great change of state {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.171}.
