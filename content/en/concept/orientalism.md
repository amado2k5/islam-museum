## The Book's Definition

Zaqzuq defines Orientalism in its specific sense as Western studies concerned with the Islamic East in its languages, literatures, history, beliefs, laws, and civilization. {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 30} This is the definition the book adopts, and it is what comes to mind in the Arab and Islamic world whenever the term Orientalism is mentioned. {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 30}

## Origins

Scholars do not agree on a fixed date for the beginning of Orientalism. The book notes that some consider the Council of Vienne's decision in 1312 CE to establish chairs in Arabic the start of "official Orientalism," while Rudi Paret holds that the beginnings of Islamic and Arabic studies in Europe go back to the twelfth century. {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 31} {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 30}

## The Religious Motive

The book states that the religious motive was the earliest reason for the emergence of Orientalism, and that from the outset it proceeded in three directions: combating Islam and searching for its weak points, protecting Christians from Islam, and conducting missionary work among Muslims. {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 42} {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 99} It notes that the religious aim was not the whole story. {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 101} These are the author's judgments and are attributed to him.

## Aims and Categories

The book lists three aims: scholarly, commercial—tied to trade and raw materials prior to colonialism—and political, which grew alongside colonialism in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 101} {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 102} {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 103} It divides secular Orientalists into categories: those who sought curiosities, those recruited to serve Western interests, those overcome by prejudice, those who attacked Islam in the name of research while probing for its weak points, those who upheld fairness, and those who devoted themselves to language and literature. {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 103} {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 104}

## Positive and Negative Aspects

The author sees positive elements in Orientalism, including care for Arabic manuscripts in Western libraries, cataloging them, editing classical heritage texts, and compiling dictionaries and encyclopedias. {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 24} {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 25} Its negative elements lie in studies of the Quran, the Sunnah, and the prophetic biography (Sirah), which the author views as containing obvious errors. {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 25} He calls for scholarly dialogue with moderate Orientalists, while describing existing dialogue as largely a dialogue of the deaf. {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 25} {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 26}

## The Author's View of the Term

The book cites Edward Said, who views Orientalism as a network of interests present whenever the East is written about. {cite:al-istishraq-wal-khalfiyya-azhar p. 22} This passage is a quotation from Said within the book, and the book itself is not presented here as passing judgment on it.
