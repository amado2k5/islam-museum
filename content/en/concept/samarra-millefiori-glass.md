## Summary
Millefiori glass tiles are among the most famous glass finds of [[place/samarra|Samarra]], believed to have adorned the audience hall of the Dar al-Khilafa. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 146} Nadine Schibille assigns them to the Samarra 2 group of Mesopotamian [[concept/plant-ash-glass|plant-ash glass]], together with the cobalt-blue vessel fragments with scratched decoration. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 146}

## Samarra's craftsmen
[[person/al-yaqubi|Al-Ya'qubi]] (d. 897) relates that the caliph [[person/al-mutasim|al-Mu'tasim]] gathered groups of craftsmen, glassworkers among them, from Basra and sent them to his new capital Samarra, which Schibille thinks may explain the sophistication and selectivity of Samarra's glass. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 145} The systematic differences between the Samarra 1 and Samarra 2 groups and their correlation with object types point to deliberate selection of raw materials and a willingness to invest effort in near-colourless glass for the aesthetic of the Abbasid "glass palaces". {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 145} {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 146}

## Link to Sasanian tradition
Comparing the tiles with Sasanian glass from Ctesiphon and Merovingian beads from Belgium, Schibille finds all three within a common Mesopotamian tradition, but the tiles differ in base-glass characteristics, implying different local raw materials. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 146} In this Samarra differs from al-Raqqa, where no evidence of strongly coloured glass production was found. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 146}
