## Overview
Pottery with a metallic lustre developed in Iraq and Fatimid Egypt, then passed to Persia and Islamic Spain. It is among the most prized products of Islamic pottery. {cite:wikidata}

## At Kairouan and Tlemcen

[[person/rexford-newcomb|Newcomb]] notes that the mihrab wall of the [[building/jami-qayrawan|Great Mosque of Kairouan]] was faced with lustre tiles said to have been brought from Baghdad under the Aghlabids, attesting the ware in Tunisia by the ninth century, and that the lustre faience revetments and inscriptions of the Mechouar mosque at Tlemcen show, according to the Marçais brothers and Migeon, Andalusian manufacture. {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p.18} {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p.19} {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p.20}

## Technique and history in Arthur Lane
- **Technique**: Lane says the pigment consists essentially of sulphur in various forms with silver oxide, which stains glass or glaze yellow, and copper oxide, which gives the lustrous element; it is mixed with an earthy vehicle such as red or yellow ochre and painted, using vinegar or wine-lees, on a glazed vessel already fired once; the vessel is fired again lightly in a reducing kiln with little air and much smoke, and the ochre is rubbed away to leave a glittering metallic film imperceptible to the touch. {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 29}
- **Origin**: he holds it was first used in Egypt to decorate glass in the first two centuries of Islam, then applied to pottery in Iraq in the Samarra period (836–885). {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 29}
- **Iraq**: in the ninth century many lustre colours were combined on one vessel; about 860 a brown-and-yellow palette was adopted, and after Samarra was abandoned it became a single greenish or brownish tone. {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 30} Bichrome lustre tiles were sent to the Great Mosque of Kairouan about 862 with a Baghdad potter, and Baghdad lustre was exported to Samarkand, Brahminabad in Sind, Egypt and Madinat al-Zahra. {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 30} {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 31}
- **Fatimid Egypt**: Lane thinks Baghdad potters took their secrets to Cairo; the evidence includes the elephant bowl signed "the work of [I]brahim … in Misr", a fragment dedicated to al-Hakim (r. 996–1021), and the signatures "Muslim" and "Sa'd"; bowls in Sa'd's style were built into the walls of churches in Italy, probably brought back from the Crusades. {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 36} {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 37}
- **Persia and Raqqa**: the earliest dated Persian lustre piece is a Rayy bottle in the British Museum dated 575/1179; Lane sees the end of lustre in Egypt coinciding with its rise in Persia and at Raqqa, the secret being held by a very few families. {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 56} Kashan became famous for lustre tiles, so that *kashi* became a common word for a tile; most were made by one family whose signed and dated work runs from 1215 to 1334. {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 58}
