## Summary
This page sets out what the Encyclopaedia Britannica says of Modern Standard Arabic and of the spoken dialects today, and what [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]] says of the speech of the Bedouin and of the settled people of the cities in his time. For the old dialects it points to [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|the dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]]. The page is not a dialect survey, and it ascribes each description to its author.

## Modern Standard Arabic
In its article on the Arabic language the encyclopedia says Modern Standard Arabic, the written standard, differs only slightly from Classical Arabic in style and vocabulary and is used in most publications and formal broadcasts. It describes the language of the Qur'an as the ideal archetype, the fusha, and says Classical Arabic closely approaches it {cite:britannica-arabic-language}. For the history of these layers see [[concept/tarikh-al-lugha-al-arabiyya|the history of Arabic, from the inscriptions to Modern Standard Arabic]].

## The spoken varieties today
The encyclopedia says the spoken varieties differ significantly, that some are mutually unintelligible, and that such variation may precede the written language by centuries. It lists the chief dialect groups in modern times as those of [[place/arabian-peninsula|Arabia]], [[place/iraq|Iraq]], [[place/syria|Syria]], Egypt and North Africa, and notes that the three cases, nominative, genitive and accusative, have largely been lost in the spoken dialects {cite:britannica-arabic-language}. The page read gives no exact boundaries for each group and no example words.

## Ibn Khaldun on the Arabic of his time
In the Muqaddima Ibn Khaldun has a chapter on the point that the language of the Arabs of this period is independent of, and different from, the language of Mudar and Himyar. For him the Arabic of the Bedouin of his time lacks only the indication of agent and object by the vowel endings of words, replacing it with word order and contextual cues, yet clarity and eloquence are greater and more deeply rooted in the Mudar tongue {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.766}. A further chapter holds that the speech of the settled people of the cities is a language standing by itself, unlike Mudar's: for him it is neither the old Mudar language nor that of the present Arab tribe but another language, far from both, with things the grammarians would count as lahn. It varies from city to city, the Mashriq's differing somewhat from the Maghrib's and al-Andalus's; each conveys its purpose, and the loss of i'rab does not harm them. He ascribes its distance from the first language to mixing with the non-Arabs, so that the more they mixed, the further it moved. He adds that the language faculty is acquired by teaching and hearing {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.770}. These are Ibn Khaldun's descriptions of his own time, not a dialect survey of the present.

## The old dialects
The differences between the old tribes, such as the 'an'ana, the kashkasha and the kaskasa, are set out by Ibn Faris in his chapter on the differences between the Arabs' dialects {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.25}; they are treated in [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|the dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]] and this page does not repeat them.

## What this page does not reach
No modern dialect survey was read, nor studies on mutual unintelligibility among the spoken varieties; only the Encyclopaedia Britannica article, a tertiary reference. Ibn Khaldun's chapters on rhetoric and literature were read only at their openings. The page gives no speaker numbers for each dialect, nor the part played by media and schooling in bringing them closer. The museum does not rule here whether the standard and the spoken varieties are one language or two; it relays the descriptions of the sources read as they stand.
