## Summary
Ta'liq, meaning "hanging" or "suspended", evolved, Maryam Ekhtiar writes, over a long period in Iran from the late thirteenth century, later spreading to Ottoman Turkey and the Indian subcontinent. {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 26}

## Features
It shares with some proportional scripts, such as [[concept/riqa-script|riqa']] and [[concept/tawqi-script|tawqi']], exaggerated descending loops, connected letters and ligatures, and words sloping upward at the end of a line. It was used mainly for official decrees, documents, deeds, endowments, diplomatic letters and ziyaratnamas — letters issued by shrines to pilgrims — but seldom for poetry or religious texts. {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 26} Its fusion with naskh produced [[concept/nastaliq-script|nasta'liq]]. {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}

## Example
The Met holds a letter in ta'liq dated 911 AH/1505–6 CE, signed by Darvish Abdullah Munshi, a known secretary of the early Safavid period, in which the sender longs for [[place/shiraz|Shiraz]], left some seventeen years before, amid prayers and a Persian quatrain. The author thinks it was a gift for a friend, and that its reader had to turn the page several times to read it. {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 49}
