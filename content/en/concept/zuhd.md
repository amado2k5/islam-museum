## Overview
Asceticism (*zuhd*) in its linguistic sense is turning away from a thing because one finds it insignificant and one's aspiration rises above it. Ibn Rajab presents it in his commentary on the hadith "Be ascetic in this world and God will love you." {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.643-655}

## In the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, defines *zuhd* as the material and spiritual asceticism that eases closer association with the divine, and counts it among the spiritual virtues weighed not only by the mystics but by a large body of thinkers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 588} The opening of the article's account is badly damaged in the digitised text, so what follows keeps to the passages that can be read. A list in the damaged opening includes safeguarding the status of a servant, scrupulous piety, [[concept/wara|*wara*]], and vigilant piety, [[concept/taqwa|*taqwa*]]. Some writers added poverty, [[concept/faqr|*faqr*]], which covers outward deprivation as well as the absence of desire for riches, and in a spiritual sense the absence of desire even for the blessings of the other world. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 589}

## Who has spoken about it
The article stresses that giving an opinion on renunciation was never the exclusive preserve of the Sufis. It reports a study of al-Bayhaqi's large *Kitab al-Zuhd*, which found that a hadith scholar, Abu Abd Allah al-Hafiz, who died in 412/1021, and not a Sufi, supplies most of the information on the renunciation of ascetics, mystics and gnostics; on this basis the article credits the memorisers of hadith with an essential role in shaping this literature. It adds that in Twelver Shi'i circles *zuhd* was counted among the qualities required of a transmitter of Imami traditions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 589} It also notes that being an ascetic has never implied adherence to [[concept/tasawwuf|Sufism]], although the consensus among the mystics has always stressed the importance of the quality. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 589}

## The early literature and its models
The earliest works, such as that of [[person/abd-allah-ibn-al-mubarak|Ibn al-Mubarak]], who died in 181/797, deal chiefly with the actions and gestures of [[person/muhammad|the Prophet Muhammad]], who appears more and more, as mystical literature develops, as the most complete model of the renouncer. They also treat his Companions and certain later figures, the one most often cited being [[person/al-hasan-al-basri|al-Hasan al-Basri]]. The work of [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ibn Hanbal]], who died in 241/855, adds the renunciation practised by eleven sanctified individuals, from Adam to Jesus, and by the Umayyad caliph [[person/umar-ibn-abd-al-aziz|Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 589} From the fourth/tenth century onward the leading figures of this literature become the mystics of the two preceding centuries: [[person/dhu-al-nun-al-misri|Dhu al-Nun al-Misri]], [[person/ibn-adham-ibrahim|Ibn Adham]], [[person/al-fudayl-ibn-iyad|Fudayl ibn Iyad]], [[person/bishr-al-hafi|Bishr al-Hafi]], [[person/sari-al-saqati|Sari al-Saqati]], Yahya ibn Mu'adh, [[person/sahl-al-tustari|Sahl al-Tustari]], [[person/al-junayd-ibn-muhammad|al-Junayd]] and [[person/al-shibli|al-Shibli]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 589}

## Hadith, moderation and the academic debate
Ignaz Goldziher put forward the hypothesis that the earliest prophetic traditions, those with the strongest guarantee of authenticity, tend toward a rejection of *zuhd*, while only late forgeries endorsed or extolled it. The article presents this as a scholar's hypothesis. It adds that it is in any event certain that a large number of hadith, corroborated by a verse of the Qur'an, [[passage/quran-25-67|al-Furqan 67]], stressed the need to limit the practice of asceticism and to observe a high degree of moderation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 589}

For the first four centuries of the Hijra, the article says, it is difficult to draw a firm line between the work of the Hanbalis or other hadith scholars and that of the mystics proper. The distinction was sharpened under the Mamluks, when a Sufi or a saint received the epithet *zahid* only if he had distinguished himself by extreme bodily asceticism. Citing E. Geoffroy, it says that at the end of that period Hanbali authors, using the terms *zahid* and *abid* freely, drew a very fine line between adherence to Hanbalism and the attribution of the word Sufi, especially in Syria, while in the Ottoman period Hanbalism and Sufism drew generally closer. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 589}

## Two readings of its scope
The article says the greatest differences arise over the sphere to which *zuhd* applies. In the second/eighth and third/ninth centuries two readings took shape. For some it meant above all renunciation, not only of pleasant clothes, lodging and food, but also of comfort, sleep and all human relationships, sometimes including marriage. For others it was a more inward and subjective asceticism, the renunciation of intentions and desires, which led to the idea of [[concept/tawakkul|*tawakkul*]], trust in God. For many mystics, the article notes, the two aspects went hand in hand. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 589} Sahl al-Tustari, who died in 283/896, draws on a prophetic tradition to distinguish clearly between *zuhd* and *takashshuf*, the mortification of the flesh. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 589}

## Stages and degrees
Ibrahim ibn Adham, who died in 165/782, is generally credited with dividing *zuhd* into three stages, a scheme also found in al-Sarraj and one the article says could come from a later source: renunciation of the world, renunciation of the joy of having devoted oneself to renunciation, and the stage at which the world becomes so insignificant in the ascetic's eyes that he no longer takes any interest in it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 589} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 590}

His disciple [[person/shaqiq-al-balkhi|Shaqiq al-Balkhi]], reckoned the first to speak of mystical states, holds that *zuhd* is the most elementary stage of those who practise sincerity. It is followed by the stages of fear, of desire for Paradise and of love of God, and its beginning lies in training the body to bear the hunger that frees one from other worldly preoccupations. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 590}

[[person/abu-said-al-kharraz|Abu Said al-Kharraz]], who died in 286/899, adopted the same ordering and defined renunciation as the progressive detachment of the heart from every desire concerning this lower world. He distinguished three kinds of renouncer: those who free the heart for obedience, remembrance and service of God; those who wish to pass lightly over the bridge across Hell; and those who act from desire for Paradise. The highest degree, in his account as the article quotes it, is total consent to the love of God and unreserved acceptance of servitude through understanding His will. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 590}

## The Malamatiyya, al-Tirmidhi and the heart
The article says the Malamatiyya, like the Khurasani spiritual leader [[person/al-hakim-al-tirmidhi|al-Tirmidhi]], who died in 318/930, proposed a conception of *zuhd* that served as a model for many mystical systems from the third/ninth century. On this view genuine *zuhd*, that of the Prophet and his Companions, implies no visible, material practice of asceticism but a deep detachment, an attitude of the heart. Ascetic practices may be admitted as a preparatory stage if thought necessary, as al-Junayd affirms in another context. This form of renunciation is entirely spontaneous and linked to divine grace. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 590}

For [[person/ibn-ata-allah-al-iskandari|Ibn Ata Allah al-Iskandari]], who died in 709/1309, renunciation means freeing the heart from love of this lower world and from envy of what others enjoy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 590} The Persian [[person/jami-abd-al-rahman|Jami]], who died in 898/1492 and clearly separates *zuhd* from true Sufism, describes the ascetics as those who see the beauty of the next life in the light of faith and certitude and do not despise this world, yet remain veiled by the pleasure of contemplating Paradise; the true Sufi, by contrast, is veiled from both worlds by the vision of primordial Beauty and eternal love. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 589}

## Place among the mystical stations
The article says *zuhd* has gradually taken a place in the succession of mystical stations, with differences between thinkers, though all link it to the beginning of the way. In Twelver Shi'i traditions it appears as the least of the believer's virtues, its highest level matching the lowest level of contentment with little. Dhu al-Nun al-Misri says that the ladder of *wara* gives access to *zuhd*. For [[person/abu-sulayman-al-darani|al-Darani]], who died in 205/820 and is called the spiritual heir of al-Hasan al-Basri, the summit of *zuhd* is abandonment to trust in God, and the article says it would be the same for [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]]; the rest of that comparison is lost in the damaged text. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 590}

The same volume treats the poetry of renunciation, *zuhdiyya*, in a separate entry. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 591}

## Its meaning according to Ibn Rajab
Ibn Rajab notes that asceticism in this world is not forbidding what is lawful, nor squandering wealth. It is that the servant be more reliant on what is in God's hand than on what is in his own. When he suffers a calamity, he is more hopeful of its reward than of its removal. He says that this is the saying of Abu Muslim al-Khawlani in Ahmad's *Musnad*, and that it is the sound view in his opinion. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.643-655}

## Sayings of the early generations
- Al-Zuhri: the ascetic is one whose patience is not overcome by the unlawful, and whose gratitude is not occupied by the lawful.
- Sufyan ibn Uyayna: one who gives thanks when blessed, and is patient when tried.
- Sufyan al-Thawri and Ahmad: asceticism is the shortening of hope, not eating coarse food or wearing a cloak.
- Ibrahim ibn Adham: three kinds, an obligation regarding the unlawful, a virtue regarding the lawful, and safety regarding doubtful matters.
- Abu Sulayman al-Darani: leaving what occupies you from God, which Ibn Rajab regards as comprehensive of its meanings.

{cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.643-655}

He notes that people disagreed, in two views, over whether the name of ascetic belongs to one who abstains only from the unlawful. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.643-655}

## From al-Sallabi's book: Asceticism in the life of Ali
Al-Sallabi presents the asceticism of Ali as a prominent trait of his character. Ali gathered the definition of asceticism in his words: "O people, asceticism is shortness of hope, gratitude at blessings, and abstinence from what is forbidden" {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 210}. The book holds that asceticism gives a person strength of resistance and a sense of self-respect {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 211}.

### Examples of asceticism
- He used to sweep the treasury of the Muslims and pray in it {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 206}.
- He said: "It is not lawful for the caliph from God's wealth beyond two dishes," one for himself and his household, and one for the people {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 209}.
- He bought a shirt from a boy for three dirhams, and when his father learned that its price was two dirhams, he returned it with one dirham, because the traders had known him and so did not buy from him {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 208}.

The book holds that Ali's asceticism, although he had every means of comfort and wealth and the esteem of people, kept him from being criticised {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 210}, and that his asceticism was not accompanied by harshness or frowning but by warmth and cheerfulness {cite:sallabi-ali-vol1 p. 210}.

### Note
The anecdotes in this section are taken from biographical works such as the *Hilya* and from histories, and the book presents them as practical education. Some lines on pages 208-209 are garbled, so only the legible parts have been transmitted.

## From the book Principles of Islamic Economics: Part Two
The author sets out Ahmad ibn Hanbal's saying that asceticism has three degrees: leaving the unlawful for the common people, leaving the excess of the lawful for the elect, and leaving whatever distracts from God for the knowers of God. He cites al-Izz ibn Abd al-Salam that wealth does not contradict asceticism {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 121}.

The author holds that true asceticism does not keep a person from earning, working and producing, that asceticism applies to earning from the unlawful or spending on it, and that the trial of wealth is harder than the trial of poverty {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 120} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 122}.
