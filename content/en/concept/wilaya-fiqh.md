Wilaya, with a kasra, in the language comes from the root wali, nearness; he is near to him, and one who takes up a matter is said to have governed it. With a fatha it means help and love. The guardian (wali) of an orphan, of a slain person, or of a woman is one who takes charge of their affairs. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17849}

In the technical sense most jurists use wilaya as carrying out a word over another whether he wills it or not. It covers the supreme imamate, judgeship, the office of hisba (public order), the grievances court and the police, and the adult's management of the affairs of one who is still a minor. The word is also used for a husband's authority to discipline a rebellious wife, a father's to discipline a young son, and a teacher's over his pupils. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17850}

The Malikis use wilaya for a bond that causes inheritance, in five kinds: wilayat al-Islam, which gives inheritance only when no other kind exists; wilayat al-hilf (alliance); wilayat al-hijra (migration), by which inheritance was given at the start of Islam and then abrogated, as were the alliance; wilayat al-qaraba (kinship); and wilayat al-'itq (manumission), by which inheritance stands. Ibn Rushd explains that the wilaya of Islam is general, by the verse of believers as protectors of one another; the wilaya of alliance was abrogated by the verse of the kin; the wilaya of migration was likewise abrogated; kinship by the verse of shares; and manumission gives inheritance where lineage fails. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17850}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17851}

God's wilaya over believers means that He takes charge of them with love, guidance and help. Ibn al-Qayyim says it is general for every pious believer and special for one who keeps all God's rights. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17851}

Related words: deputising (niyaba), which is one person standing in place of another in an act; wilaya is wider than it, so every deputising is a wilaya, but not every wilaya is a deputising. 'Amala, the fee of the collector of charity; the wali is wider than the collector. Qiwama, the custody and care of a matter; the custodian (qayyim) is the manager and administrator of a minor, an insane or a spendthrift, or of an endowment's property. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17852}

Related words. Qiwama is narrower than wilaya and means keeping and preserving. Wasiya (guardianship after death) is the setting of another in one's place after death to look after the estate and the young children, and is narrower too. Wakala is the delegation of a disposal that admits delegation during one's life, and is narrower than wilaya. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17852}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17853}

General authority (wilaya 'amma) is power to compel and carry out an act over another without his delegation, in matters of religion and the world, of persons and property, governing the public affairs of life, to secure benefits and avert harms. It is a religious and worldly office, instituted for three things: enjoining good and forbidding wrong, delivering trusts to their owners, and judging among people with justice. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17853}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17854}

Its ranks run from the supreme imam down to his deputies and governors. By it armies are equipped, frontiers defended, taxes collected and spent where they belong, judges and governors appointed, the hudud and the discretionary penalties enforced, rebels suppressed, disputes settled, and guardians and custodians appointed and held to account. Ibn Taymiyya says the aim of all authority in Islam is that religion be wholly God's. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17854}

The law makes the authority over people one of the greatest duties of religion, since people's interests are not secured without gathering, and gathering needs a head; hence the saying that when three go on a journey they should appoint one of them as leader. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17854}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17855}

The common conditions of general authority. Islam is required by agreement, on the verses of Surat al-Nisa (verses 144 and 59, cited by number). Puberty is required by agreement, on the saying that the pen is lifted from three. Sanity is required without dispute. Freedom is required, so a slave does not hold it. Male sex is required by the majority, on the verse that men are guardians over women (Surat al-Nisa, verse 34) and the saying that a people who give their affairs to a woman will not prosper. Justice is required by the majority, and knowledge of the rulings by the majority; al-Samarqandi requires knowledge of the principles of religion and independent judgement in its branches, and reports that a scholar of his time held that this would mean that no imam could hold office, so that a man can rely on others for what he lacks. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17855}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17856}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17857}

Bodily soundness: the holder must hear, see and speak, since a defect in these senses prevents full action; al-Juwayni says that loss of sight, hearing or speech prevents rising to urgent matters. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17857}

Opinion and capacity. These differ with each office: what is needed in the supreme imamate (political, military and intellectual ability, firmness, compassion and shrewdness) is not required of lesser offices. The Prophet told Abu Dharr: 'you are weak, and it is a trust', and said: 'when trust is lost, await the hour'. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17857}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17858}

Choosing the best when the ideal is absent. When all the conditions cannot be met, the appointment of holders is not suspended; the most suitable is chosen, since necessity permits what is forbidden, and keeping some interests is better than losing all. Al-'Izz ibn 'Abd al-Salam says that if justice were absent from all people, the interests of judges, caliphs and governors could not be suspended, and the best of the corrupt would be chosen. Necessity also requires striving to reform matters until the conditions are completed. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17858}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17859}

The duties of the holder of general authority vary with the office. He must keep to the law of Islam in word, deed and conduct, since he is an example; and he must discharge trusts, as the verse of Surat al-Nisa (verse 58) commands. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17859}

The Prophet said: 'No servant whom God appoints over a flock dies on the day he dies while deceiving them without God forbidding him Paradise.' He also said: 'Whoever appoints a man from a group while in it is one more pleasing to God has betrayed God and His Messenger.' So whoever holds any part of the affairs of Muslims must appoint in each place the most suitable and most capable he can find. Ibn Taymiyya says that turning from the most entitled to another for kinship, clientage, friendship, shared town or school, race, bribery or grudge is betrayal of God, His Messenger and the believers. He holds that the discharge of trust rests on three things: fear of God, not selling His signs for a small price, and not fearing people. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17860}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17861}

Justice is God's balance on earth and the support of religion and the world. The jurists say that when authority is accompanied by justice it is among the best acts of obedience; a just imam, ruler or judge has a greater reward than all others, and unjust rulers bear the greatest burden. The sum of just governance is the delivery of trusts to their owners and judging with justice. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17861}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17862}

Enjoining good and forbidding wrong is the purpose of all authority. It is an obligation on each capable person, and becomes an individual obligation on the capable one when no one else undertakes it; those in power bear more of it. Offices differ: some are like a trusted witness, required to be truthful, and others like a trusted commander, required to be just. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17861}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17862}

Consulting people of knowledge, judgement and experience is obligatory for the holder of general authority, on the verse that he should consult them in the matter (Surat al-Shura, verse 38, cited by number). Ibn Khuwaz Mindad said that rulers must consult the scholars where they do not know or where religious matters are unclear, the commanders in war, the notables on public interests, and the scribes and governors on the good of the lands. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17862}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17863}

Caring for the needs of the community is an obligation in every office, on the saying that whoever God gives authority over Muslims and shuts himself off from their needs, poverty and wants, God shuts Himself off from his needs. Al-Qarafi says that authority is for securing pure or preponderant benefit and averting pure or preponderant harm. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17863}

The rights of the holder of authority include obedience in what is good, agreed upon, on the verse 'obey those in authority among you' and the saying 'hearing and obedience are due to the Muslim in what he likes and dislikes, unless he is ordered to sin'. Rebellion against a just ruler is agreed to be forbidden; rebellion against an unjust one is disputed. Advising him is obligatory, on the saying 'religion is sincere counsel... to the leaders of the Muslims and their common folk'; it should be gentle and private, not public censure. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17863}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17864}

The holder has a right to a stipend from the public treasury sufficient for him and his dependants, by analogy with the zakat collector, since he devotes himself to the interests of Muslims, and so that he is not driven to bribery or unlawful gain. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17864}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17865}

The kinds of general authority differ with regions, eras and customs, and the jurists count more than twenty. They include the supreme imamate, the great leadership in guarding religion and governing the world as deputy of the Prophet, called 'great' to distinguish it from leading the prayer; the vizierate, a legal authority the imam gives to a trusted man, in two kinds, delegated and executive; and the judgeship, whose reality is declaring the legal ruling with binding force and settling disputes, with partial kinds such as a supervisor of marriage contracts only or of orphans' affairs only. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17865}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17866}

The mazalim (grievances) authority. Al-Mawardi says it is the leading of wrongdoers to fairness through awe, and the restraint of disputants from denying one another's rights through dignity. Its holder must be of high rank, obeyed, of great presence, clearly chaste, little greedy and very pious. Those who already hold general powers, such as ministers and amirs, need no separate appointment; others need one. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17867}

The amirate (imara) has two kinds. The chosen amirate (istikfa') is made by the imam, general over a town or region or specific to the army's command, the protection of the realm and the defence of its honour, without judgement, taxes or alms. The seized amirate (istila') is made by the imam out of necessity, when a commander has taken a land by force and the caliph gives him its command. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17867}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17868}

The police (shurta) are the sultan's aides. Ibn al-Amin al-Qurtubi gives them two tasks: helping the judges in imprisonment and release, and investigating crimes and carrying out the hudud. Ibn Khaldun notes the title varies by land (hakim, sahib al-madina, wali). Taj al-Din al-Subki says the police may investigate public wrongs such as wine and hashish and stop their pretext, and cover those whom God has covered, but may not spy on people or raid their houses on rumour; they should send a trusted man in secret to forbid the wrong only as far as God has forbidden it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17868}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17869}

Ibn Taymiyya calls this the 'lesser war' (wilayat al-harb al-sughra), to distinguish it from the 'greater war', which he describes as the deputising of the sultanate. Its purpose is to prevent corruption on earth and suppress the wicked and aggressive. He says that in his time in the Levant and Egypt it covered the hudud involving bodily harm and judgement in disputes, while in the Maghrib the governor only carries out what the judge orders. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17869}

The hisba is enjoining good when it is abandoned and forbidding evil when it is committed. It is a religious office like prayer, giving fatwas, judgeship and jihad. It is of two kinds: an original office from the lawgiver, binding on everyone asked, and a delegated one, given by the caliph or amir, held by the muhtasib. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17869}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17870}

The amirate of jihad is only against polytheists, and is of two kinds: one limited to army policy and the conduct of war, and one that includes the division of booty and the making of treaties. If it is made for a single campaign, the amir may not undertake another; if it is general, he must renew campaigns year by year. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17870}

Authority over wars for the public good is against non-polytheists: apostates, rebels and highwaymen. The office of collecting charity is limited to zakat funds; its holder must know the rules, the amounts, the kinds of property and the payers, and be gentle and vigilant. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17870}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17871}

Special authority (wilaya khassa) has three forms: the compulsory deputising over a minor's person and property (guardianship over the person and over property), the authority of the administrator of an endowment over its property, and the authority the law gives the kin of a slain person to exact retaliation or to pardon it for blood-money, on the verses and sayings 'whoever is slain wrongly, We have given his heir authority' and 'the kin of the slain have the choice of two things'. The first is the common meaning in the jurists' usage. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17871}

Special authority passes to the general authority when its holders are absent, on the saying 'the sultan is the guardian of him who has no guardian'; the sultan exercises it himself or through his deputies, such as governors and judges, since the deputies stand in his place. Special authority prevails over general authority, since it is stronger. Thus the endowment administrator prevails over the judge, and the guardian of an orphan prevails over the judge; the judge may not dispose of an orphan's property while the father's executor exists, nor marry an orphan except when there is no guardian or the guardian refuses. The holder of special authority may pardon retaliation free of charge, and the imam may not. If the judge leases an endowed shop to one man and the administrator leases it to another, the administrator's lease stands. The general authority nonetheless supervises all, and may audit the executors, supervisors and administrators, and remove the dishonest among them, even if the donor or founder stipulated against his interference. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17871}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17872}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17873}

Hanafi exceptions: the administrator may not appoint officers for the endowed purposes unless the founder stipulated it, but the judge may; the judge may lend an orphan's property without the father or executor. Ibn al-Qasim of the Malikis says that if the sultan marries an adult woman while her guardian is present, the marriage stands and is not annulled. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17873}

Special authority over property is of two kinds: the authority over one's own property (qasira, proper to any fully capable person) and authority over another's property (muta'addiya), which is either original, given by the lawgiver (the father's or grandfather's authority over a minor's property), or deputising, such as the executor or agent. It is established over those under restraint: the minor, the insane, the imbecile, the spendthrift and the inattentive, and lasts while the cause remains. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17873}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17874}

The minor is one who has not reached puberty, male or female; he is discerning or not discerning. The insane is one whose reason is gone so that his words and acts do not go right, and it is permanent or intermittent; his acts while insane are void, and while sane are valid. The imbecile has little understanding and disordered speech but does not strike or curse; he may be like a non-discerning or a discerning minor. The spendthrift squanders his property against reason or the law, from a levity of joy or anger. The Shafi'is, Malikis, Hanbalis and the two companions of Abu Hanifa allow him to be placed under restraint, while Abu Hanifa does not. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17874}
