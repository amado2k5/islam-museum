## Summary
The two solstices are two points on the ecliptic at the greatest distance from the celestial equator: one northern, which is the summer solstice at the beginning of Cancer, and the other southern, which is the winter solstice at the beginning of Capricorn {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112}.

## Naming and Position
The northern point is called the "point of the summer solstice," which is the beginning of the sign of Cancer, and the southern is called the "point of the winter solstice," which is the beginning of Capricorn {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112}. The distance between each of them and the celestial equator is the obliquity of the ecliptic {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112}.

## Day and Night at the Solstices
When the Sun is at the beginning of Cancer, the day reaches its maximum length and the night its minimum, and when it is at the beginning of Capricorn, the opposite occurs {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 107}. Al-Farghani explains that the longest day equals the longest night in corresponding latitudes {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 106}.

## In Different Regions
From this rule, al-Farghani moves on to explain that the Sun does not pass through the zenith in latitudes where the pole's altitude is greater than the obliquity of the ecliptic, except in the south, and at a latitude where the pole equals the obliquity of the ecliptic, the summer solstice is at the zenith {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 105}. At a latitude equal to the obliquity of the ecliptic, the Sun rises at the beginning of Cancer without setting, and sets at the beginning of Capricorn without rising {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 158}.
