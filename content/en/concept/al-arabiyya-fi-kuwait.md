## Summary
What was found on Arabic in Kuwait: Articles 3 and 82 of the Constitution as the Constitute Project shows them, two passing remarks in the Library of Congress study on Farsi and on Asian workers who do not speak Arabic, and the name of the Kuwaiti Hadari variety in Glottolog's tree, with a statement of what was not found. For the place see [[place/kuwait|Kuwait]]. For the Arab states in general see [[concept/al-alam-al-arabi|The Arab states and the League of Arab States]].

## Official status
Article 3 of Kuwait's Constitution, in the translation on the Constitute Project titled "1962 (reinst. 1992)", says Arabic is the official language of the State, and Article 82 requires a member of the National Assembly to have a sound reading and writing knowledge of Arabic {cite:constituteproject-kuwait}. The article on the Semitic languages in the Encyclopaedia Britannica lists Kuwait among the states where Arabic is official {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}, and the article on the Arab League says Kuwait joined it in 1961 {cite:britannica-arab-league}.

## The spoken varieties
Glottolog's tree lists, under Gulf Arabic, "Kuwaiti Hadari Arabic" {cite:glottolog-arabic}; no description of it and no share of speakers was found. The Library of Congress page on the population, which is not a page about language, says some Kuwaitis of Iranian origin, who are Shia, "often speak Farsi as well as Arabic at home" in the page's words {cite:loc-kuwait-population}. Among the theses the University of Manchester site lists is a 2014 study of code-switching among Kuwaiti bilinguals, which was not itself read {cite:manchester-arabic-sociolinguistic}.

The same Manchester page divides the Arabic dialects into geographical units, one of them the dialects of the Arabian Peninsula; it does not name the states inside that unit, so placing this state in it is an inference from its location, and it says that in most Arab countries urban and rural dialects differ, as do those of settled and formerly nomadic (Bedouin) people {cite:manchester-arabic-structural-variation}. Glottolog's tree shows separately named varieties under "Arabian Peninsula Arabic" {cite:glottolog-arabic}; these are two different classifications, one geographical group at Manchester and distinct varieties at Glottolog, and the museum does not choose between them. The Ethnologue page was not read, so the SIL classification is not known here. For more on the dialects see [[concept/lahjat-al-khalij|The Gulf dialects]].

## Other languages
The same page says the Asian workers whom the state favours for their lower cost "are unable to speak Arabic" in its words {cite:loc-kuwait-population}; it most likely describes the 1980s, since the page gives 1985 for the make-up of foreign labour and carries no date itself, so it cannot stand as a description of today. No list of the languages of Kuwait and no shares for them were found.

## The standard and the spoken varieties together
The University of Manchester's page on the dialects of Arabic describes the general situation across the Arabic-speaking world as three-layered: Modern Standard Arabic, the language of the state, education, public services and the media; Classical Arabic, found in the Qur'an, religious texts and worship; and the colloquial dialects of everyday exchange, which the page says people usually regard as of little value even though they are common in song, theatre and film {cite:manchester-arabic-sociolinguistic}. This is a description of the Arab world in general, not of this state alone, and no study measuring it here was read. For the relation of the standard to the spoken varieties see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]], and for the differences among the tribes' speech before the standard [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|The dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]].
