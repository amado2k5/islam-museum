## Summary
What was found on Arabic in Lebanon: Article 11 of the Constitution on Arabic and French as the Constitute Project shows it, the Library of Congress study's account of the forms of Arabic, Lebanese colloquial, French, English, Armenian and Syriac and the proposal to write the colloquial, and the Levantine classification at Manchester and Glottolog, with what was not found. ## Official status
Article 11 of Lebanon's Constitution, in the translation on the Constitute Project titled "1926 (rev. 2004)", says Arabic is the official national language and that a law determines the cases in which French is to be used {cite:constituteproject-lebanon}. The Library of Congress study agrees Arabic is official and says French is not, though most government publications appear in French and Arabic {cite:loc-lebanon-languages}. The article on the Semitic languages in the Encyclopaedia Britannica lists Lebanon among the states where Arabic is official {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}.

## Constitutional text
The Lebanese Constitution of 1926, Article 11. The research summary supplied to the museum gives this Arabic wording: «اللغة العربية هي اللغة الوطنية الرسمية. أما اللغة الفرنسية فتحدد الأحوال التي تستعمل بها بموجب قانون.», which the museum translates: "The Arabic language is the official national language. As for the French language, the cases in which it is used are determined by law". The summary names the Presidency of the Lebanese Republic as the official source; the museum did not open that page, so the wording is taken from the summary as supplied, and the founder approved using it so on 21 November 2026 {cite:official-constitution-lebanon}. The summary dates the Constitution to 1926 and says Article 11 was amended in 1943.

## The spoken varieties
The Library of Congress study says Classical Arabic is uniform across the Arab world and "chiefly a written language", used in speeches, poetry and broadcasts; that colloquial Arabic is many dialects; that Arabs revere the classical as a sacred language and a symbol of unity and regard the spoken dialects as corruptions of it; that Lebanese colloquial grew from Syrian Arabic, was influenced by Aramaic and varies by region, the Druze dialect being distinctive {cite:loc-lebanon-languages}. It says colloquial dialects are seldom written except in some novels, plays and humorous writings, and that in the 1960s some Maronite intellectuals proposed making the spoken language the national one, a cause the poet Said Aql championed, which few took seriously beyond a handful of writers {cite:loc-lebanon-languages}. Manchester puts Lebanon in the Levantine group, and Glottolog lists Lebanese varieties by name: Beqaa, North Lebanese, South Lebanese, Iqlim al-Kharrub, Sunni Beiruti and others, and one it calls "Standard Lebanese Arabic" {cite:manchester-arabic-structural-variation} {cite:glottolog-arabic}. The old source speaks of its own time and is not to be carried over to today. For more on the dialects see [[concept/lahjat-al-sham|The Levantine dialect]].

## Other languages
The study says Armenian is an Indo-European language with much vocabulary from Arabic and Turkish, that Lebanon has an active Armenian printing and publishing industry and Armenians value the language as a core part of their identity, that Assyrian is a modern spoken form of Syriac and Assyrians increasingly speak Arabic while Syriac remains in religious use, that English has grown in importance since the Second World War, that fluency in French or English generally marks an educated person, and that the Beirut dialect has absorbed French words {cite:loc-lebanon-languages}. These are the descriptions of an old study, and no shares of speakers were found.

## The standard and the spoken varieties together
The University of Manchester's page on the dialects of Arabic describes the general situation across the Arabic-speaking world as three-layered: Modern Standard Arabic, the language of the state, education, public services and the media; Classical Arabic, found in the Qur'an, religious texts and worship; and the colloquial dialects of everyday exchange, which the page says people usually regard as of little value even though they are common in song, theatre and film {cite:manchester-arabic-sociolinguistic}. This is a description of the Arab world in general, not of this state alone, and no study measuring it here was read. For the relation of the standard to the spoken varieties see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]], and for the differences among the tribes' speech before the standard [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|The dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]].
