## Summary
Egypt is a country in north-eastern Africa with [[place/cairo|Cairo]] as capital, and Egyptian colloquial is among the best-known Arabic dialects. This page gathers what the Encyclopaedia Britannica's article on the languages of Egypt says, the fullest of the references read on dialects, and what the old books of language say of the Arabs on the edge of Egypt. The page ascribes every description to its author, and gives no figure for the number of speakers because the article read does not state one.

## Standard Arabic and the vernacular in Egypt
The Encyclopaedia says the official language of Egypt is Arabic and that most Egyptians speak one of several vernaculars of it, which differ greatly from the literary language. It describes Modern Standard Arabic as learned only in school and as the lingua franca of educated persons across the Arab world; it says its grammar and syntax have stayed largely unchanged since the 7th century, and that its style, word order and phraseology are simpler and more flexible than in Classical Arabic and often derive from English or French {cite:britannica-egypt-languages}.

## The groups of Egyptian colloquial
The Encyclopaedia says that alongside the written language there are regional vernaculars and dialects of Arabic, termed collectively al-'ammiyya, which differ widely from the literary variety and from one another; that within what it calls Egyptian colloquial a number of separate vernacular groups can be discerned, each fairly homogeneous with strata of variation within it; and that the differences from place to place are sometimes subtle and sometimes profound. It names the dialect of the Bedouin of the Eastern Desert and Sinai, a separate group for the Bedouin of the Western Desert, an Upper Egyptian vernacular markedly different from Cairo's, the Cairo dialect used with variations in the towns of the Delta, and the rural people's own vernacular {cite:britannica-egypt-languages}. It adds that long contact with foreigners put many loanwords into Cairene colloquial, and that Cairo's prominence in the Arab film industry has made its dialect widely understood in the Arab world {cite:britannica-egypt-languages}.

## Minority languages and Coptic
The Encyclopaedia lists small linguistic groups in Egypt: the Beja of the south of the Eastern Desert, with a Cushitic language known as To Bedawi, though some speak Tigre and many use Arabic; a group at Siwa Oasis whose language is related, but not closely, to the Berber languages; and Nubians who speak Eastern Sudanic languages with some Cushitic features. It says that at the Islamic conquest Coptic was the language of religious and everyday life for the mass of the population, and that by the 12th century Arabic had come into common use even among Christian Copts, whose former tongue continued only as the liturgical language of the Coptic Orthodox Church {cite:britannica-egypt-languages}.

## The edge of Egypt in al-Muzhir
Al-Suyuti relays from al-Farabi that the language was not taken from Lakhm and Judham because they adjoined the people of Egypt and the Copts {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.167}. Ibn Khaldun names Egypt among the realms that the Arabs took and in which they mixed with the non-Arabs {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.767}. Both are old statements made in the context of relying on language as evidence, and neither describes Egyptian colloquial.

## What this page does not reach

It does not state the phonetic or grammatical features of Egyptian colloquial, so they are not given here, and no count of its speakers was found, nor the share of speakers of each group.
