## Summary
Egypt is a country in north-eastern Africa with [[place/cairo|Cairo]] as capital, and Egyptian colloquial is among the best-known Arabic dialects. This page gathers what the Encyclopaedia Britannica's article on the languages of Egypt says, the fullest of the references read on dialects, and what the old books of language say of the Arabs on the edge of Egypt. The page ascribes every description to its author, and gives no figure for the number of speakers because the article read does not state one. Sections on classification, main features and speaker numbers were later added from a research summary supplied to the museum, and they supersede the remark that none were found.

## Standard Arabic and the vernacular in Egypt
The Encyclopaedia says the official language of Egypt is Arabic and that most Egyptians speak one of several vernaculars of it, which differ greatly from the literary language. It describes Modern Standard Arabic as learned only in school and as the lingua franca of educated persons across the Arab world; it says its grammar and syntax have stayed largely unchanged since the 7th century, and that its style, word order and phraseology are simpler and more flexible than in Classical Arabic and often derive from English or French {cite:britannica-egypt-languages}.

## The groups of Egyptian colloquial
The Encyclopaedia says that alongside the written language there are regional vernaculars and dialects of Arabic, termed collectively al-'ammiyya, which differ widely from the literary variety and from one another; that within what it calls Egyptian colloquial a number of separate vernacular groups can be discerned, each fairly homogeneous with strata of variation within it; and that the differences from place to place are sometimes subtle and sometimes profound. It names the dialect of the Bedouin of the Eastern Desert and Sinai, a separate group for the Bedouin of the Western Desert, an Upper Egyptian vernacular markedly different from Cairo's, the Cairo dialect used with variations in the towns of the Delta, and the rural people's own vernacular {cite:britannica-egypt-languages}. It adds that long contact with foreigners put many loanwords into Cairene colloquial, and that Cairo's prominence in the Arab film industry has made its dialect widely understood in the Arab world {cite:britannica-egypt-languages}.

## Minority languages and Coptic
The Encyclopaedia lists small linguistic groups in Egypt: the Beja of the south of the Eastern Desert, with a Cushitic language known as To Bedawi, though some speak Tigre and many use Arabic; a group at Siwa Oasis whose language is related, but not closely, to the Berber languages; and Nubians who speak Eastern Sudanic languages with some Cushitic features. It says that at the Islamic conquest Coptic was the language of religious and everyday life for the mass of the population, and that by the 12th century Arabic had come into common use even among Christian Copts, whose former tongue continued only as the liturgical language of the Coptic Orthodox Church {cite:britannica-egypt-languages}.

## The edge of Egypt in al-Muzhir
Al-Suyuti relays from al-Farabi that the language was not taken from Lakhm and Judham because they adjoined the people of Egypt and the Copts {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.167}. Ibn Khaldun names Egypt among the realms that the Arabs took and in which they mixed with the non-Arabs {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.767}. Both are old statements made in the context of relying on language as evidence, and neither describes Egyptian colloquial.

## Classification
Glottolog 5.4 places Egyptian Arabic (identifier egyp1253, ISO 639-3 code arz) within Afro-Asiatic, Semitic, West Semitic, Central Semitic, Arabian, Arabic, Egyptic Arabic and Egypto-Sudanic Arabic, and divides it into Cairene, Central Delta Nile, Eastern Egyptian, Northeast Nile Delta and Western Nile Delta Arabic, separate from its sibling branches Sa'idi Arabic (Upper Egypt) and Western Desert Egyptian Arabic {cite:glottolog-egyptian-arabic}. The free page of Ethnologue (29th edition, 2026) places arz within the Arabic macrolanguage as an institutional language of wider communication originating in the Nile Delta {cite:ethnologue-arz}.

Linguistic studies, the research summary says, typically equate "Egyptian Arabic" with Cairene Arabic {cite:ucla-xu-egyptian-arabic-vowels}, which the Encyclopaedia Britannica says is widely understood across the Arab world through Egyptian cinema and television {cite:britannica-cairo-arabic-widely-understood}. In prosodic terms, a Stony Brook University paper reports that Cairene and Lower Egyptian are "CV (onset)" dialects, in contrast with Upper Egyptian dialects, which allow word-internal CVVC syllables {cite:stonybrook-broselow-syllable-structure}. Bedouin varieties are spoken east and west of the Nile, with Sinai linking the Negev, north-eastern Egyptian and Hijazi Bedouin Arabic {cite:brill-eall-bedouin-arabic}. The research summary found no UNESCO source on the point.

## Main features
**Phonology.** A page reproducing the Coptic Encyclopedia says Cairene and much of Lower Egypt realise Classical q as the glottal stop and j as g, whereas Upper Egypt realises q as g {cite:claremont-coptic-loanwords-egyptian-arabic}. A UCLA thesis gives Egyptian Arabic three short vowels (i, u, a) and five long ones (ī, ū, ē, ō, ā), with emphatic alveolars spreading backing and lowering across the phonological word {cite:ucla-xu-egyptian-arabic-vowels}. According to the Stony Brook paper, Cairene bans complex onsets, shortens long vowels in non-final closed syllables (kitaab + na becomes kitabna), inserts an epenthetic vowel after the second consonant of a three-consonant cluster, and deletes unstressed i in some contexts {cite:stonybrook-broselow-syllable-structure}.

**Morphology.** Britannica notes that spoken Egyptian has lost the Classical case endings and verbal duals {cite:britannica-arabic-language} {cite:britannica-egyptian-colloquial-vs-msa}. Imperfective stems combine with bi- (present, indicative) and ha- (future); roots with an initial glide keep the glide (wasˤal, ji-wsˤal); and Cairene first-pattern perfectives allow both a and i whereas Alexandrian allows only a {cite:ucla-xu-egyptian-arabic-vowels}.

**Syntax.** A 1991 MIT thesis describes Egyptian Arabic as leaving question words in place in main and embedded clauses; fronted question arguments form reduced clefts with illi and an obligatory resumptive pronoun, while fronted question adjuncts omit illi. Relative clauses require illi plus a resumptive pronoun, and embedded declaratives use inn {cite:mit-typology-of-wh-questions}.

**Lexicon.** The Coptic Encyclopedia page and a Brill entry point to a substantial Coptic substrate in agriculture, Nile fauna and idioms (tamy, "Nile silt", timsah, "crocodile", tannish, "ignore") {cite:claremont-coptic-loanwords-egyptian-arabic} {cite:brill-eall-dialects-genesis}, alongside extensive Turkish and European loanwords {cite:claremont-coptic-loanwords-egyptian-arabic} {cite:britannica-egyptian-colloquial-vs-msa}.

## Speaker numbers
The research summary gives one figure. The Encyclopaedia Britannica's page "Languages by number of native speakers", dated 4 August 2023, gives 77,436,230 native speakers of Egyptian Arabic, citing Ethnologue data of the early 2020s {cite:britannica-languages-by-native-speakers}. The summary found no figure in Ethnologue's free page (29th edition, 2026; exact counts are behind a paywall), Glottolog, Brill or UNESCO {cite:ethnologue-arz}. The figure is a count of native speakers as of the early 2020s as reported by Britannica, not a current census.

## Common phrases
A research summary supplied to the museum added a comparison of sixty expressions and words across six Arabic dialects, including greetings, asking how someone is, asking what someone wants, and the numbers one to five. The reference variety here is Cairene Arabic. Questions are generally addressed to one male speaker. Transliteration is normalized for comparison, and Arabic spelling may be normalized where a source uses phonetic transcription rather than Arabic script. The MSA equivalents are supplied for comparison and are not necessarily quotations from the dialect sources. The museum did not open the pages named; the sources are as the summary reported them, and its labels are given in the last column. {cite:yalla-masry-egyptian-arabic-phrase-guide-greetings} {cite:northwestern-first-year-arabic-mikhaeel-2024} {cite:el-baz-say-it-in-arabic-egyptian-dialect-1968} {cite:lisaan-masry-numbers}
