## Summary
Yemen is the country in the south-western corner of [[place/arabian-peninsula|Arabia]], with [[place/sanaa|Sanaa]] as capital, and was the seat of the state of [[group/himyar|Himyar]]. This page gathers what was read of the speech of Yemen in two kinds of source: the Encyclopaedia Britannica in its brief modern description, and the old books of language on the tongue of Himyar and on what they ascribe to the language of Yemen, mostly old descriptions relayed second hand. The page ascribes every description to its author, and no detailed description of the dialects of Yemen today was found. Sections on classification, main features and speaker numbers were later added from a research summary supplied to the museum, and they supersede the remark that none were found.

## The modern description
The Encyclopaedia Britannica says in its article on Yemen that the people speak various dialects of Arabic {cite:britannica-yemen}; the part of the article read goes no further. In its article on the Arabic language it says the chief dialect groups in modern times are those of Arabia, Iraq, Syria, Egypt and North Africa {cite:britannica-arabic-language}, so Yemen falls under the Arabia group in this division.

## The tongue of Himyar
In al-Sahibi, in the chapter on the differences between the dialects of the Arabs, Ibn Faris gives among the differences of opposition Himyar's saying thib to one standing, meaning sit, and relates that Zayd ibn Abd Allah ibn Darim came as a delegate to one of the kings of Himyar and found him at a hunting place on a high mountain; the king said to him thib, and he took it for an order to leap off the mountain, and leapt and died, and the king said: we have no Arabic here; whoever enters Zafar should learn Himyari {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.27} {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.28}. Al-Suyuti gives the same difference in al-Muzhir {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.203}. Ibn Khaldun holds that the language of Himyar is another language, differing from that of Mudar in much of its forms, inflections and vowel endings, as the language of the Arabs of his own time differs from that of Mudar, and he rejects those who take them for one language {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.767} {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.768}.

## Features ascribed to the language of Yemen
Ibn Faris relays from Ibn Duraid that a sound between qaf, kaf and jim is a widespread dialect in Yemen, so that they say kamal for jamal when pressed, and that a sound between shin, jim and ya' shows in ghulamij for the masculine and ghulamish for the feminine {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.30}. Al-Suyuti relays from books on fiqh al-lugha, under the heading 'the blameworthy dialects', that among the features of the language of Yemen are watm, making sin into ta' as in al-nat for al-nas; shanshana, making kaf into shin as in labbaysh for labbayk; and changing ya' to jim in annexation, as in ghulamij {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.176} {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.177}. The heading of the chapter is its authors' judgement, and the museum does not adopt describing these features as blameworthy; it sets them out ascribed to their authors.

## Yemen, the Qur'an and descent
Al-Suyuti says in the chapter on knowing the eloquent that words from the dialects of the people of Yemen in the Qur'an are well known, and mentions that it is reported as a raised report that the Qur'an came down in the language of the two Ka'bs, Ka'b ibn Lu'ayy and Ka'b ibn Amr, who is the father of Khuza'a; the page read gives no judgement on this report {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.167}. He relays from al-Farabi that the language was not taken from the people of Yemen because of their mixing with India and Abyssinia {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.168}. Ibn Faris answers those who claim that the Himyari tongue of the Qahtanis shows they are not Arabs, saying that a difference of dialects does not impugn lineages {cite:sahibi-baydun-1997 p.31}.

## What this page does not reach
No description of the dialects of Yemen today was found in the references read, whether in Sanaa, Aden, Tihama or Hadramawt, nor a count of their speakers; no modern dialect survey was read, nor texts on the ancient South Arabian languages. What al-Muzhir and al-Sahibi say of the language of Yemen are old descriptions of features in the speech of some Arabs, and whether they belong to Yemen as a whole or to particular tribes is a question the museum leaves to its authors. For the general picture see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]] and [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|the dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]].

## Classification
Glottolog 5.4 classifies the Yemeni varieties, under Arabian Peninsula Arabic, as separate languoids: Ta'izzi-Adeni Arabic (taiz1242, with Adeni and Ta'izzi), Hadrami Arabic (hadr1236), and Judeo-Muslim Sanaani Arabic, which branches into Sanaani Arabic (sana1295) and Judeo-Yemeni Arabic (jude1267); it places Coastal and Valley Tihaamah under Hijazi Arabic {cite:glottolog-taizzi-adeni-arabic} {cite:glottolog-hadrami-arabic} {cite:glottolog-sanaani-arabic} {cite:glottolog-judeo-yemeni-arabic}. The Ethnologue country page for Yemen likewise lists Sanaani (ayn), Ta'izzi-Adeni (acq), Hadrami (ayh) and Judeo-Yemeni (jye) as distinct living indigenous languages of Yemen {cite:ethnologue-yemen}.

Brill's Encyclopedia of Arabic Language and Linguistics describes Yemeni Arabic as "a zone of discontinuities", leaving out Mahra and Soqotra, where the Modern South Arabian languages are native; it reports a division by Diem and Behnstedt into 11 (or 10) northern and western types, among them Tihāmah, the -k dialects, Ṣanʿānī and Ṣaʿdah, to which Vanhove added at least five southern and eastern zones (Lahej, Aden, Ġayl Ḥabbān, Abyan and Wādi Ḥaḍramawt) {cite:brill-eall-yemen}. The Encyclopaedia Britannica's article on Yemen notes the strong Ancient South Arabian influence on the rural Arabic of the south {cite:britannica-yemen-land}. The research summary found no UNESCO source on the point.

## Main features
**Phonology.** According to Brill, a south-western enclave (Tihāmah to Abyan, Lahej, Aden, Yāfiʿ and Ḏ̣ālaʿ) keeps Classical j as g, whereas Ḥaḍramawt shifts it to y and Ḥabbān has j; q is q (or a fricative [ɣ, ʁ] in central Yemen), or g in Mukeyras, Ḥabbān and Ḥaḍramawt. Abyan and Ġayl Ḥabbān keep a lateralised ḷ for dad (shifting to ṛ at Mudia and Lawdar); the interdentals are generally preserved except in al-Ḥudaydah and Aden; and kaškaša, k becoming š in the feminine "you", is widespread outside Tihāmah, Ḥugariyyah, the southern -k dialects, Ḏ̣ālaʿ and Aden {cite:brill-eall-yemen}. A 1999 MIT article describes Ṣanʿānī as spreading pharyngealisation leftwards and labialisation rightwards {cite:mit-watson-emphasis-spread}, and a 2023 conference paper reports that Hajjah Tihāmi allows moraic word-initial geminates arising from assimilation of the definite article {cite:lsa-amp-tihami-definite-determiners}.

**Morphology.** The high-mountain -k dialects use -k instead of -t in the first and second person perfect {cite:brill-eall-yemen}. Beside al-, Tihāmah, al-Bayḏ̣āʾ, Mukeyras and Abyan use nasal or labial definite articles (am-, um-, m-, b-) {cite:brill-eall-yemen} {cite:leeds-arabic-morphology}. Western highland dialects distinguish gender in the first person singular pronoun (ana masculine, ani feminine), most dialects keep gender in the second and third person plural, internal passives of Form I (gutil) remain common, and Ṣanʿānī marks continuous aspect with bi- and the future with ša-, ʕad- or ʕa- {cite:brill-eall-yemen} {cite:leeds-arabic-morphology}.

**Syntax.** Relative pronouns vary: ḏi in the Southern High Plateau, di in Ġayl Ḥabbān and Ḏ̣ālaʿ, allaḏi in Ṣanʿāʾ {cite:brill-eall-yemen}. Ṣanʿānī forms negative imperatives with lā plus the second-person imperfect and often switches from the feminine plural to the masculine plural after the first verb in a series {cite:leeds-arabic-morphology}.

**Lexicon.** Brill points to Ḥimyaritic or Old South Arabian vocabulary (for example Tihāmah dawʾ, "no"), regional terms (tqambas, "sit down", and ḥusan, "cat", in Yāfiʿ), and Ṣanʿānī truncations such as kamsayta, "good evening" {cite:brill-eall-yemen} {cite:brill-eall-truncation}.

## Speaker numbers
The research summary gives figures from two sources and a statement from a third, and flags the first as old. A Stanford University course-archive page of 2003, reproducing figures of 1995 and 1996, gives 7,600,000 speakers of Sanaani Arabic (1996); 6,760,000 in Yemen and 6,840,000 in total for Ta'izzi-Adeni Arabic (1996); 300,000 in Yemen and 410,000 in total for Hadrami Arabic (1995); and 1,000 in Yemen for Judeo-Yemeni Arabic (1995) {cite:stanford-yemen-variability-immigration}. These are 25 to 30 year old figures taken from a course page, and the museum gives them as such, not as present counts. The Encyclopaedia Britannica's article on Yemen (updated 20 November 2026) says more than nine-tenths of Yemenis speak an Arabic dialect as their first language {cite:britannica-yemen-land}. The Ethnologue country page (29th edition, 2026) gives Yemen's total population as 35,220,000, but keeps per-language speaker counts behind a paywall {cite:ethnologue-yemen}. The summary found no figure in Glottolog, Brill or UNESCO.

## Common phrases
A research summary supplied to the museum added a comparison of sixty expressions and words across six Arabic dialects, including greetings, asking how someone is, asking what someone wants, and the numbers one to five. The reference variety here is Yemeni Arabic, with regional qualifications. Questions are generally addressed to one male speaker. Transliteration is normalized for comparison, and Arabic spelling may be normalized where a source uses phonetic transcription rather than Arabic script. The MSA equivalents are supplied for comparison and are not necessarily quotations from the dialect sources. The examples in this table should not be interpreted as exclusive to, or universally pronounced identically throughout, Yemen.
