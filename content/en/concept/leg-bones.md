## Summary
Ibn Sina sums up the use of the leg in two things: stability and support, which is the foot's work, and moving on level ground, up and down, which is the work of the thigh and shank; he then describes the thigh and the shank {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.58}.

## Use of the leg
If the foot is harmed, standing and stability become hard, though walking is not, except for the extra stability walking needs in one leg; if the muscles of the thigh and shank are harmed, standing is easy and walking hard {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.58}.

## The thigh
The thigh bone is the first of the leg bones and the largest bone in the body, since it carries what is above it and transmits to what is below. Its upper end is domed to fit the hip socket. It is convex toward the outer side and concave toward the inner side and back, because set straight and parallel to the socket it would cause a kind of bow-leggedness. At its lower end are two processes for the knee joint {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.58}.

## The shank
The shank, like the forearm, is made of two bones: the larger and longer, on the inner side, called the great reed (the tibia in the editor's gloss), and a smaller, shorter one that does not reach the thigh, called the small reed (the fibula). The shank bows outward and then, near its lower end, inward, for good balance. The great reed was made smaller than the thigh bone because the reason for smallness, lightness for movement, matters more to the shank, and the reason for size, steadiness and carrying, matters more to the thigh. Were it larger, movement would be hard, as in elephantiasis and varicose veins; were it smaller, there would be weakness and failure to carry, as in the thin-shanked. The small reed supports the great one, shelters the nerve and vessels between them, and shares in the ankle joint so that bending and straightening are secured {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.58-59}.
