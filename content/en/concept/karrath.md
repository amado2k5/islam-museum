## Summary
Karrath is a known plant of foul smell. It is distinct from garlic, onion and radish; each is a different kind of herb {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13242} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13243}.

## Eating it and attending the congregation
The jurists agree that eating a strong-smelling plant such as onion, garlic, leek or radish is an excuse to stay from the congregation when the smell cannot be removed, on the hadith of Jabir: "whoever eats of this plant, let him not come near our mosque" {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13243}. Eating it without intending to attend the mosque is disliked, according to Ibn Qudama; the Malikis hold that it is forbidden if one intends the mosque, and al-Qalyubi holds that it is not disliked for one who can remove the smell, while eating it to escape an obligation such as Friday prayer is forbidden. Al-Nawawi reports the agreement of those whose opinion counts that these herbs are lawful {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13243} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13244}.

## The husband's right
The jurists state that a husband may stop his wife from eating what has a foul smell, such as garlic, onion or leek, since it prevents kissing and full enjoyment. The Hanbalis have a view that he may not, since it does not prevent intercourse {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13244} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13245}.

## Salam and sale
The jurists differ on salam in herbs, including leeks. The Hanafis and the preferred Hanbali view refuse it, since herbs are alike and cannot be measured by bundles; the Malikis, the Shafi'is and one Hanbali narration allow it {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13244}. The jurists in general accept the sale of leek once it has ripened, on the general hadith forbidding the sale of fruit before its ripeness {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13244} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13245}.
