## Summary
What was found on Arabic in Jordan: Article 2 of the Constitution as the Constitute Project shows it, the Library of Congress study's account of the forms of Arabic, the Bedouin, the dialect shared with neighbours and the small communities, and the Levantine classification at Manchester and Glottolog, with what was not found. For the place see [[place/jordan|Jordan]]. For the Arab states in general see [[concept/al-alam-al-arabi|The Arab states and the League of Arab States]]. The constitutional text is added in its original wording, as supplied in a research summary that the museum did not check against the page.

## Official status
Article 2 of Jordan's Constitution, in the translation on the Constitute Project titled "1952 (rev. 2016)", says Islam is the religion of the State and Arabic its official language {cite:constituteproject-jordan}. The article on the Semitic languages in the Encyclopaedia Britannica lists Jordan among the states where Arabic is official {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}. The two sources agree.

## Constitutional text
The Constitution of the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan, 1952, as revised in 2022, Article 2. The research summary supplied to the museum gives this Arabic wording: «الإسلام دين الدولة واللغة العربية لغتها الرسمية.», which the museum translates: "Islam is the religion of the State and the Arabic language is its official language". The summary names the Social Security Investment Fund of Jordan, an official PDF as the official source; the museum did not open that page, so the wording is taken from the summary as supplied, and the founder approved using it so on 21 November 2026 {cite:official-constitution-jordan}.

## The spoken varieties
The Library of Congress study says every Jordanian, whatever his ethnicity or religion, speaks Arabic, in three forms: Classical, Modern Standard and local spoken dialects; that educated Arabs usually know Modern Standard and their own dialect, and that many people have memorised Qur'anic phrases but cannot speak Classical {cite:loc-jordan-ethnicity-language}. It says most Jordanians speak a dialect shared with Syria, Lebanon and parts of Iraq, that there are differences among townspeople, villagers and nomads and even among neighbourhoods of one village, that Arabs generally believe Bedouin speech is closest to the purer classical form, and that a few nomads crossing from Saudi Arabia may speak a dialect like those of Saudi Arabia {cite:loc-jordan-ethnicity-language}. Manchester puts Jordan in the Levantine group, and Glottolog lists under South Levantine Arabic two varieties, Fellahi and Madani {cite:manchester-arabic-structural-variation} {cite:glottolog-arabic}, without linking them to Jordan by name in the tree. Among the theses on the Manchester site is a 2015 study of loanwords in Jordanian Arabic, which was not read {cite:manchester-arabic-sociolinguistic}. The Jordan page says its dialect is shared in part with Iraq, whereas the other classifications set Iraq apart, as the Syria page has already noted. For more on the dialects see [[concept/lahjat-al-sham|The Levantine dialect]].

## Other languages
The study gives about 25,000 Circassians, Sunni Muslims, and says they all spoke Arabic and intermarriage with Arab Jordanians was common; it gives about 2,000 Chechens, and Armenians at about 1 percent, mainly in Irbid and Amman; it does not name their own languages or the Armenians' language, and it does not mention English in this section {cite:loc-jordan-ethnicity-language}. So the languages of Jordan other than Arabic, and their shares, were not found, and the numbers are from an old study.

## The standard and the spoken varieties together
The University of Manchester's page on the dialects of Arabic describes the general situation across the Arabic-speaking world as three-layered: Modern Standard Arabic, the language of the state, education, public services and the media; Classical Arabic, found in the Qur'an, religious texts and worship; and the colloquial dialects of everyday exchange, which the page says people usually regard as of little value even though they are common in song, theatre and film {cite:manchester-arabic-sociolinguistic}. This is a description of the Arab world in general, not of this state alone, and no study measuring it here was read. For the relation of the standard to the spoken varieties see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]], and for the differences among the tribes' speech before the standard [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|The dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]].
