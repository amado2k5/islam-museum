## Summary
The Maghrib here is the west of the Arab world, from [[place/libya|Libya]], [[place/tunisia|Tunisia]], [[place/algeria|Algeria]] and [[place/morocco|Morocco]] to [[place/mauritania|Mauritania]], and in old usage it takes in [[place/al-andalus|al-Andalus]]. This page gathers what was read of the speech of its people in five references: the Encyclopaedia Britannica's general article on the Arabic language and its articles on Morocco, the Maghrib and Mauritania, and Ibn Khaldun's chapters on the speech of the cities. The page ascribes every description to its author and gives no description of the features of the Maghribi darija, since the references read do not state them. Sections on classification, main features and speaker numbers were later added from a research summary supplied to the museum, and they supersede the remark that none were found.

## The Encyclopaedia's description
In its article on the Arabic language the Encyclopaedia says North Africa is one of the chief dialect groups in modern times {cite:britannica-arabic-language}. In its article on the Maghrib it says the Arabs conquered the Maghrib in the 7th and 8th centuries, that they imposed on the native peoples Islam and Arabic, the language of the Qur'an, and that most North African societies nonetheless kept their cultural identity {cite:britannica-maghrib}. In its article on Morocco it says Arabic is one of the country's two national and official languages and is spoken by two-thirds of the population, that Tamazight, the language of the Imazighen, is spoken by one-third and became official in 2011, that French is an important secondary language and Spanish and English are widely spoken {cite:britannica-morocco}. The page sets these statements out as they stand and does not reconcile them.

## Hassaniyya in Mauritania
The Encyclopaedia says in its article on Mauritania that Arabic is the official language and that Fula, Soninke and Wolof are national languages. It says the Moors speak Hassaniyya Arabic, a dialect based chiefly on Arabic grammar with a vocabulary of both Arabic and Arabized Amazigh words, and that many of its speakers understand Egyptian and Syrian Arabic because of Middle Eastern television and radio {cite:britannica-mauritania}. This is one of the few passages read that names a particular western dialect and says something of its character.

## The cities in Ibn Khaldun
Ibn Khaldun says the language of the cities is a language standing by itself, differing from that of Mudar, and that it varies from city to city: the tongue of the people of the east differs somewhat from that of the people of the west, and likewise the people of al-Andalus, each conveying his purpose by his own speech, and the loss of i'rab does not harm them {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.770}. He says the Arabs in Ifriqiya and the Maghrib mixed with the Berbers, among the non-Arabs, who filled the land with their numbers, so that non-Arab speech overcame the Arabic tongue they had and it became another, mixed language, and that al-Andalus was likewise with the non-Arabs of the Galicians and the Franks {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.770} {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.771}. This is his explanation of what he saw in his own time, not a description of sound or grammar.

## What this page does not reach
No description of the features of Moroccan, Algerian, Tunisian or Libyan darija was found in the references read, whether in sounds, inflection or vocabulary, nor a count of the Maghrib's speakers of Arabic, nor the effect on them of Amazigh, French and Spanish; and no modern dialect survey was read. The two-thirds said of Morocco is the Encyclopaedia's statement about that country alone, not about the whole Maghrib, and no matching shares for Algeria, Tunisia and Libya appear in the references read. The Encyclopaedia's word for the imposition of Arabic on the people of the Maghrib is its own, and historians have other statements that were not read. For the general picture see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]].

## Classification
Glottolog 5.4 places Maghrebi under Afro-Asiatic, Semitic, West Semitic, Central Semitic, Arabian, Arabic, North African Arabic and Western Middle Arabic, separates it from Libyan Arabic, Judeo-Tripolitanian Arabic and Hassaniyya, and subdivides Maghrebi into Algerian, Algerian Saharan, Andalusian, Judeo-Moroccan, Malta-Tunisian (Maltese and Tunisian Arabic) and Moroccan Arabic {cite:glottolog-arabic} {cite:glottolog-libyan-arabic}. The free pages of Ethnologue list Moroccan (ary), Algerian (arq), Algerian Saharan (aao), Tunisian (aeb), Libyan (ayl), Hassaniyya (mey), Judeo-Moroccan (aju), Judeo-Tripolitanian (yud) and Maltese (mlt) separately under the Arabic subgroup, rather than under a single Maghrebi node {cite:ethnologue-arabic-subgroup}. Glottolog and Ethnologue therefore differ on whether "Maghrebi" is a unit.

The Encyclopaedia Britannica treats North Africa as one of five main dialect regions {cite:britannica-arabic-language}. Scholars traditionally contrast an older pre-Hilalian sedentary and urban stratum (7th to 8th centuries) with the later Hilalian and Sulaym Bedouin dialects (9th to 14th centuries) {cite:brill-eall-bedouin-arabic} {cite:uri-arabic-historical-dialectology} {cite:univie-bedouin-type-dialects-tunisia}. Recent work, as the summary reports it, highlights polygenetic koines in Morocco {cite:brill-eall-polygenesis} and an east-west cline of innovation that cuts across the binary {cite:uri-arabic-historical-dialectology}.

## Main features
**Phonology.** Sedentary, pre-Hilalian dialects typically keep q and merge the interdentals (th, dh and emphatic dh become t, d and emphatic d), whereas Hilalian Bedouin dialects have g and keep the interdentals {cite:brill-eall-bedouin-arabic} {cite:uri-arabic-historical-dialectology}. Maghrebi dialects drop short vowels in open syllables (producing initial consonant clusters and the neutralisation of vowel length in Morocco), show unconditioned imāla and monophthongisation, affricate t to ts in Morocco and Algeria, and admit p and v in loanwords {cite:uri-arabic-historical-dialectology} {cite:yale-family-agreement-moroccan-arabic} {cite:brill-eall-bedouin-arabic}.

**Morphology.** Nearly all North African dialects share the imperfective pattern with n- for "I" and n-...-u for "we" (niktub, niktubu) {cite:uri-arabic-historical-dialectology}. The analytic genitive uses dyāl or d in Morocco and (n)tāʕ or mtāʕ from north-eastern Morocco to Libya; Western Bedouin shows Berber-influenced l- and -ya pronouns (leḥna, "we"); and Mauritanian Ḥassāniyya has causatives of the shape saCCaC {cite:brill-eall-bedouin-arabic} {cite:yale-family-agreement-moroccan-arabic}.

**Syntax.** Moroccan Arabic prefers subject-verb-object order, with verb-subject-object available, is pro-drop, neutralises feminine plural verb agreement, and uses dyāl in possessives and partitives {cite:yale-family-agreement-moroccan-arabic}. Sedentary dialects use two-part negation mā...-š {cite:yale-family-agreement-moroccan-arabic} {cite:vicav-tunico-fi-progressive-tunisian}, and Tunisian marks the progressive with fī on transitive verbs and qāʕid on intransitive ones {cite:vicav-tunico-fi-progressive-tunisian}.

**Lexicon.** The dialects have substantial Amazigh (Berber), French and Spanish borrowings {cite:soas-maghrebi-arabic-beginners} {cite:yale-family-agreement-moroccan-arabic}, and characteristic items such as škūn, "who", fayn, "where", waqtāš, "when", and žūž or zōz, "two" {cite:brill-eall-bedouin-arabic} {cite:uri-arabic-historical-dialectology}.

## Speaker numbers
The research summary gives one figure for the Maghrib: 24 million speakers of Moroccan Arabic in Morocco, about 90 per cent of the population, a figure taken from a May 2017 Yale linguistics paper that cites Morocco's 2004 census {cite:yale-family-agreement-moroccan-arabic}. This is a statement of that paper, about one country and tied to a 2004 census, and it differs from the Encyclopaedia Britannica's statement, set out earlier on this page, that Arabic is spoken by two-thirds of Morocco's population; the museum records both with their sources and does not reconcile them. For aggregate Maghrebi figures and for the other national varieties the summary found no source: the free Ethnologue page for Moroccan Arabic (29th edition, 2026) keeps exact counts behind a paywall {cite:ethnologue-ary}, UNESCO's Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger (3rd edition, 2010) notes that North African censuses lack linguistic breakdowns and covers only endangered minority varieties {cite:unesco-atlas-north-africa-middle-east}, and Glottolog, Britannica and Brill report no aggregate totals.

## Common phrases
A research summary supplied to the museum added a comparison of sixty expressions and words across six Arabic dialects, including greetings, asking how someone is, asking what someone wants, and the numbers one to five. The reference variety here is Moroccan Darija. Questions are generally addressed to one male speaker. Transliteration is normalized for comparison, and Arabic spelling may be normalized where a source uses phonetic transcription rather than Arabic script. The MSA equivalents are supplied for comparison and are not necessarily quotations from the dialect sources. The museum did not open the pages named; the sources are as the summary reported them, and its labels are given in the last column. {cite:peace-corps-morocco-moroccan-arabic-textbook-2011}
