## Summary
This page sets out what a research summary supplied to the museum says about Arabic in Cameroon: its legal status, the varieties spoken and the official figures, each as the summary gives it. In Cameroon English and French are official, and the summary says Shuwa Arabic serves wider communication in the north without standardisation. The museum did not open the pages the summary names, and the founder approved using it as supplied on 21 November 2026. Where the summary says that no official source or figure was found, this page says exactly that and supplies no number of its own.

## What this page is
This page is an article on the subject "Arabic in Cameroon", in the world chapter of the Arabic browser, one of the pages that treat Arabic outside the states counted as Arab. It answers three questions: what is the legal status of Arabic there, which varieties of it are spoken, and what official figures have been published on speakers, if any. The museum keeps here to what it keeps to on all its pages: a describing voice, not a commanding one, every statement ascribed to its page, and no verdict on a disputed question. All the material comes from one research summary, so the page describes what the summary said and does not claim to have read the sources.

## Background and dates
For Cameroon the summary mentions only the 1996 constitution and tells no history of Shuwa Arabic in the Far North. It describes a standing situation: two official languages of foreign origin, a northern language of communication without standardisation, and Standard Arabic taught as a foreign language. To trace the distant origin of the kind in the Lake Chad basin the reader is pointed to [[concept/intishar-al-arabiyya-baada-al-fath|The spread of Arabic after the conquests]], without claiming a direct connection.

## Legal status
According to the summary, Article 1(3) of the 1996 Constitution makes English and French the official languages, and the State undertakes to protect and promote national languages without naming them {cite:wikipedia-list-of-linguistic-rights-in-african-constitutions}. In practice, the summary says, Shuwa or Chowa Arabic serves as an indigenous national language of wider communication in the north without formal standardisation, while Standard Arabic is taught as a foreign language in northern secondary schools and in Franco-Arabic and Qur'anic schools {cite:brill-eall-cameroon-arabic}. This is ascribed to two pages the museum did not read.

## Varieties spoken
The summary names Shuwa or Chowa Arabic, arabe choa, that is Chadian Arabic, in the Far North Region and especially Logone-et-Chari, and Classical and Modern Standard Arabic {cite:brill-eall-cameroon-arabic}. In this description Cameroon is one edge of the Lake Chad area, where a single closely related kind is spoken; see the neighbouring pages [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-chad|Arabic in Chad]] and [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-nigeria|Arabic in Nigeria]]. The museum does not break this kind down into dialects, since the summary does not.

## Official figures
The summary found no official Cameroonian census, from BUCREP or the INS, and no UN or UNESCO count of speakers, and says so. There is no number here; the museum does not estimate the share of speakers of Shuwa Arabic in the north and borrows no figure from a source the summary did not name.

## How the sources differ
The pages named for this record are of different kinds. Reference, academic and press pages: Cameroon Arabic (Encyclopedia of Arabic Language and Linguistics) {cite:brill-eall-cameroon-arabic}; Wikipedia pages: Chadian Arabic {cite:wikipedia-chadian-arabic} and List of linguistic rights in African constitutions {cite:wikipedia-list-of-linguistic-rights-in-african-constitutions}. The official texts and statistics speak for the state or the statistical office; the reference, academic and press pages describe and interpret; Wikipedia supports only names, places and dates. Where the kinds differ, the museum reports each in its own voice without choosing between them, and it opened none of them.
