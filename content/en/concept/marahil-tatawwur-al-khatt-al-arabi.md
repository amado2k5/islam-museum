## Summary
This page sets out five stages in the development of the Arabic script in chronological order, as a research summary supplied to the museum gives them: Kufic from the late seventh to the tenth century, then the dots and vowel marks of the late seventh and eighth, then Naskh, then Thuluth in the tenth century, then Ruq'ah in the nineteenth. The order follows the start of each stage, and some stages overlap; everything here is the summary's account, as the museum attributes it.

## First: Kufic
Kufic emerged at Kufa in Iraq, according to the summary, and is the oldest calligraphic form of Arabic, marked by angular, rectilinear strokes and a horizontal emphasis. It began without consonant dots or vowel marks and remained the main script of the early parchment Qur'ans, of coins, textiles and architectural inscriptions into the tenth and eleventh centuries, later giving rise to ornamental forms such as floriated, knotted and Square Kufic {cite:met-development-spread-calligraphic-scripts} {cite:britannica-kufic-script} {cite:wikipedia-kufic-en}. An early example is the [[object/dome-of-the-rock-inscription|Dome of the Rock inscription]]. The museum has records on [[concept/kufic-script|Kufic script]] and [[concept/floriated-kufic|floriated Kufic]]; the Kufic record, following Ekhtiar, says the name comes from [[place/kufa|Kufa]] but names a family of scripts, not a place of making {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 23}. The two accounts are set side by side.

## Second: dots and vowel marks
The dots and vowel marks arose at Basra to resolve ambiguity in unpointed writing. Abu al-Aswad al-Du'ali (d. 688 or 689 CE) introduced coloured dots for short vowels; Nasr ibn 'Asim (d. 708 or 709 CE) and Yahya ibn Ya'mar introduced marks, dots or dashes, that tell similar consonants apart, called i'jam; and al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi (718 to 786 or 791 CE) replaced the coloured dots with the familiar stroke signs, fatha, kasra, damma and shadda, written in the ink of the text {cite:wikipedia-arabic-diacritics-en} {cite:wikipedia-abu-al-aswad-al-duali-en} {cite:wikipedia-al-khalil-ibn-ahmad-al-farahidi-en}. See [[person/abu-al-aswad-al-duali|Abu al-Aswad al-Du'ali]], [[person/nasr-ibn-asim|Nasr ibn 'Asim]], [[person/al-khalil-ibn-ahmad-al-farahidi|al-Khalil]], [[place/basra|Basra]], [[concept/naqt-al-irab|the dotting for vowels]] and [[concept/naqt-al-ijam|the dotting of letters]], where the story is told in the words of other sources.

## Third: Naskh
Naskh is a small, rounded, highly legible cursive that began in the seventh century for everyday writing, letters and administration; [[person/ibn-muqla|Ibn Muqla]] (d. 940 CE) and [[person/ibn-al-bawwab|Ibn al-Bawwab]] (d. 1022 or 1031 CE) standardised it in the tenth century, and the Ottoman calligrapher Shaykh [[person/shaykh-hamdullah|Hamdullah]] refined it later. From the eleventh century it largely replaced Kufic for copying books and the Qur'an, and it became the basis of modern Arabic print and digital type {cite:britannica-naskhi-script} {cite:wikipedia-naskh-script-en}. The museum's own record of Ibn al-Bawwab gives his death as 423 AH, 1032 CE {cite:wafayat-ayan}, so the difference from the summary's date is stated. Naskh has its record at [[concept/naskh-script|Naskh script]].

## Fourth: Thuluth
Ibn Muqla codified Thuluth in the tenth century as one of the Six Pens; with its sweeping curved and oblique strokes it replaced the angularity of Kufic, one third, the thuluth, of each letter sloping, which gives the script its name, and it is paired with decorative vowel signs. Ottoman refinements ran from the fifteenth to the nineteenth century. It became the premier monumental and display script for mosque inscriptions, sura headings and architectural panels {cite:britannica-thuluth-script} {cite:wikipedia-thuluth-en}. See [[concept/thuluth-script|Thuluth script]] and [[concept/al-aqlam-al-sitta|the six pens]].

## Fifth: Ruq'ah
Ruq'ah developed in the Ottoman Empire and was standardised in the nineteenth century by Mumtaz Efendi (1810 to 1872) and Mustafa Izzet Efendi (1801 to 1876) from Thuluth and Naskh. It is built from clipped letters, short straight strokes and even horizontal baselines, for speed and legibility, and according to the summary it remains the most widely used everyday handwriting style in the Arab world {cite:wikipedia-ruqah-script-en}. See [[person/kazasker-mustafa-izzet|Kazasker Mustafa Izzet Efendi]]. The museum does not make this Ruq'ah the same thing as the classical pen [[concept/riqa-script|Riqa']] of the six pens; each has its own record and description.

## What the order shows and where the stages overlap
The five stages are ordered by their beginnings and do not follow one another strictly: Kufic lasted into the tenth and eleventh centuries; the dots and vowel marks overlap with Kufic because they are not a script but signs added to the writing; Naskh had an everyday written origin in the seventh century before its standardisation; Naskh and Thuluth are both tied to Ibn Muqla; and Ruq'ah alone comes centuries later. Other museum records differ on points of detail and can be read through the linked records.
