## Summary
In the fourth chapter on bones Ibn Sina describes the limits and sutures of the upper jaw, then lists the three uses of the nose and the make-up of its bones, and then speaks briefly of the lower jaw {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.45-46}.

## The upper jaw
The upper jaw is bounded above by a suture shared with the forehead, running under the brow from temple to temple, below by the roots of the teeth, and at the sides by a suture coming from the region of the ear, shared with the sphenoid bone. Within it a suture cuts the roof of the palate lengthwise, another starts between the eyebrows and runs toward the gap between the two front teeth, and two more descend toward the space between the lateral incisor and the canine on right and left, so that triangular bones are marked off between them {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.45}.

## The uses of the nose
He gives the nose three uses. Its cavity helps in breathing in, so that more air is held and tempered before it reaches the brain, and good air is gathered in one place in front of the organ of smell. It helps cut the letters in speech and ease their emission, so that the air does not crowd at the places where letters are formed. And it is a screen for the residues driven out of the head, a cover from the eyes, and a tool to expel them by blowing {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.46}.

## The nose bones and the lower jaw
The bones of the nose are two triangular bones; at their lower ends are two soft cartilages, and along the middle suture a cartilage harder than the two. The middle cartilage divides the nose into two nostrils, so that when a residue comes down from the brain it leans to one and does not block the whole way of breathing. The nasal bones are thin and light, because lightness is needed there more than firmness. The lower jaw is two bones joined under the chin by a secured joint; their other ends rise hooked and fit with a process of the bone where they end, tied by ligaments {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.46}.
