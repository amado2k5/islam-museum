## Summary
Opaque white ware is earthenware covered with an opaque white glaze containing lead, its opacity depending on the amount of tin; [[work/nishapur-pottery-of-the-early-islamic-period|Wilkinson]] notes it was widely made in the Near East in the ninth and tenth centuries. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 221}

## Ancient background
The glaze was not new in the ninth century: artificially prepared tin oxide was found in Tutankhamun's tomb, and opaque white glaze was used on tiles of a palace of Ramesses II, on Nebuchadnezzar's glazed bricks at Babylon, and on the Achaemenid archers' frieze from Susa. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 221} Its use in the Sasanian period has not been shown; at Ctesiphon it appeared only in the early Islamic areas. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 221}

## In the Islamic period
Iraqi potters of the ninth century used the glaze to great effect; analysis of good Iraqi fragments found at [[place/nishapur|Nishapur]] shows their opaque glaze has less lead than clear lead glazes. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 221} Wilkinson thinks it was first used to imitate the white or cream Chinese ware imported in the eighth and ninth centuries — high-fired and porcellanous — which Iraqi potters, lacking kaolin, could not or chose not to copy exactly. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 221} His chapter covers the imported ware and its local imitations at Nishapur. {cite:wilkinson-nishapur-pottery p. 8}
