## Summary
Mashq in writing means drawing the letters out and extending them; it is sometimes used for speed in writing, as the editor of the Itqan explains. {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2240} Sheila Blair calls the principle mashq or madd in Arabic and kashida in Persian, from the verbs for extending and pulling. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 137}

## In mushafs
Ibn al-Nadim puts mashq in the list of the scripts of the mushafs and says the warraqs who copy mushafs write them in muhaqqaq and mashq and the like, naming several of them. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim-rahmaniyya-cairo p. 21} {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim-rahmaniyya-cairo p. 22} He names Khashnam al-Basri and Mahdi al-Kufi in the days of al-Rashid and says Khashnam's alifs were a cubit long. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim-rahmaniyya-cairo p. 22}

## The early dislike
Al-Suyuti reports that Ibn Sirin disliked a mushaf being written mashq because it brings a defect, that 'Umar disliked a mushaf in a fine hand, and that 'Ali disliked mushafs being made small. {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2240} {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2243} Among the etiquette of writing it, he says, is to beautify, clarify and verify the hand of the mushaf, without mashq or ta'liq. {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2240}
