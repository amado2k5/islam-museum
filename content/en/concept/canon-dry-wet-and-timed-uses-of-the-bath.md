## Summary
Ibn Sina sets out how the effect of the bath changes with its use: dry it dries and helps dropsy and soft swelling; wet it moistens; a long stay dries by dissolving and sweating, a short one moistens; on an empty stomach it dries and emaciates; just after a meal it fattens but risks blockages; at the end of the first digestion it helps in moderation {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.141}.

## The uses
The bath may be used dry, when it dries and helps people with dropsy or flaccid swelling, or wet, when it moistens. Sitting long in it dries through dissolving and sweating; sitting a little moistens, because the body absorbs from it before sweating begins. On an empty fasting body it dries severely, emaciates and weakens. Shortly after satiety it fattens, by drawing matter to the surface of the body, but it causes blockages through the undigested food drawn from the stomach and liver. At the end of the first digestion, before evacuation, it helps and fattens in moderation {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.141}.

## Regimen for hectic fever
Those who use the bath to moisten, as sufferers of hectic fever do, should soak in the water while their strength holds, then rub themselves with oil to increase the moistening and to hold the watery moisture that has entered the pores inside the skin. They should not stay long, should choose a temperate place, pour much water on the floor so that vapour fills and moistens the air, be carried out on a litter, be perfumed with a cooling scent on leaving, be left an hour in the changing room until the breath is even, and be given moistening drinks such as barley water and ass's milk. A long stay risks fainting from the relaxing of the heart, and nausea comes first {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.141}.
