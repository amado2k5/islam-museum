## Summary
With Islam the Arabic script passed to many languages other than Arabic, and each adjusted it to write sounds the original lacked. This page sets out eleven languages as a table in a research file supplied to the museum gives them: the number of letters in each, what each added, and whether it stayed in the Arabic script or had another put in its place, and when. All of it is ascribed to the summary, and the museum did not open the pages it cites; what is attributed to Wikipedia or others is what the summary says of them.

## Persian and what branched from it
For the summary Persian is the root of a whole group: its four letters, پ چ ژ گ, with the distinct yeh and kaf, pass to Urdu, Pashto, Sorani Kurdish and Uyghur, and each language adds its own {cite:wikipedia-persian-alphabet-en} {cite:openarabic-perso-arabic-alphabet}. In Soviet [[place/tajikistan|Tajikistan]] the Tajiks left the Arabic script for Latin in the late 1920s and then for Cyrillic in 1939 and 1940 {cite:wikipedia-tajik-alphabets-en}. Urdu has between 39 and 40 letters, with the retroflex letters, the nasal sign and the aspirate heh {cite:wikipedia-urdu-alphabet-en}. Pashto, at forty-five letters, has the most of these four. In Kurdish, Sorani is written in the Arabic script while Kurmanji in Turkey and Syria is written in the Latin Hawar alphabet {cite:wikipedia-kurdish-alphabets-en}. The languages have records at [[language/persian|Persian]], [[language/urdu|Urdu]], [[language/pashto|Pashto]] and [[language/kurdish|Kurdish]].
## Uyghur, Malay and Ottoman Turkish
Uyghur requires mandatory vowel letters, so it does not leave vowels optional as Arabic does, and its script was replaced in Soviet Central Asia by Latin Yanalif in the 1920s and 1930s, then by Cyrillic in the 1930s and 1940s, while it stayed official in [[place/xinjiang|Xinjiang]] {cite:wikipedia-uyghur-arabic-alphabet-en} {cite:wikipedia-yanalif-en}. Jawi Malay has six extra consonants and was gradually overtaken by Latin Rumi while remaining co-official in [[place/brunei|Brunei]] and in religious contexts {cite:academickids-jawi}. Ottoman Turkish has five added letters, and its Arabic script ended in the reform of 1928 {cite:wikipedia-ottoman-turkish-alphabet-en} {cite:wikipedia-arabic-script-en}; see [[language/turkish|Turkish]] and [[language/malay|Malay]].
## African Ajami: Swahili, Hausa and Wolof
African writing in the Arabic script is called Ajami. For Swahili the summary names two standardisations, by Mwalimu Sikujua and Yahya Ali Omar; for Hausa letters in a Maghrebi style and mandatory vowels; and for Wolof, Wolofal with nine vowels {cite:wikipedia-swahili-ajami-en} {cite:wikipedia-limba-hausa-ro} {cite:wikipedia-wolofal-alphabet-en} {cite:wikipedia-wolofal-fr}. In all three Latin replaced Arabic in administration and schooling, under colonial rule for Swahili and Hausa and by official decision in Senegal, while Arabic stayed in religious schools and Islamic literature. See [[language/swahili|Swahili]] and [[language/hausa|Hausa]].
## Sindhi
For Sindhi the summary gives fifty-two letters after its standardisation in 1853, with many dotted letters for implosive, retroflex and aspirated sounds, and its continuing as the main script in Sindh, Pakistan, alongside Devanagari in India {cite:britannica-sindhi-language} {cite:wikipedia-sindhis-en}. It has more letters than Persian or Urdu, which is why its figure is the largest in the table.
## The pattern of keeping and replacing
Two patterns come out of the table: languages that kept the Arabic script for political and religious reasons, namely Persian in Iran and Afghanistan, Urdu, Pashto, Sorani Kurdish, Uyghur in Xinjiang and Sindhi; and languages where another script took its place, by state decision, as with Turkish in 1928 and Tajik and Soviet Uyghur, or by colonial rule or gradual change, as with Swahili, Hausa and Malay. In the second cases Arabic stayed the script of religion and religious teaching.
## Where the sources differ
The pages count letters in different ways, so Urdu comes between 39 and 40, Kurdish 34, Persian 32, Uyghur 32, Jawi 37, Sindhi 52 and Pashto 45, which depends on whether some shapes count as separate letters or as forms. The summary gives no number for Ottoman Turkish, Swahili, Hausa or Wolof. It also describes Kurdish by its Sorani script and not Kurmanji, and Hausa as Maghrebi in style without counting its letters.
## What the museum has not reached
The museum did not open the pages named, and has not checked the added letters, the dates, or the claim that Ajami was replaced in a given period. The summary gives some dates as a year and some as a decade, and they stand as they came.
