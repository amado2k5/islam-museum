## Summary
Planetary retrogradation is the apparent westward motion of the five wandering planets along the ecliptic, even though they move eastward along their epicycles. Al-Farghani explains it through the combination of two motions in opposite directions {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 193}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 194}.

## Cause
When a planet is in the upper part of its epicycle, its motion along the epicycle is in the same direction as the motion of the epicycle's center, increasing its apparent speed. When it is in the lower part, however, its motion on the epicycle opposes the motion of the center, so if its westward motion prevails, the planet appears to move backward {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 193}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 194}. Retrogradation is most pronounced at perigee {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 194}.

## Why the Moon Does Not Retrograde
Al-Farghani asks why the Moon does not retrograde even though it moves on an epicycle like the five planets, and answers that the distance the Moon covers on its epicycle adds only a slight amount to its motion along the ecliptic relative to the motion of the epicycle's center {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 195}.

## Limits of Retrogradation
Al-Farghani states the limits of retrogradation in degrees for the five planets, and notes that the maximum elongation of Venus and Mercury from the Sun is about 48 degrees for Venus and 28 degrees for Mercury {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 195}.
