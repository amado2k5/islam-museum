## Summary
This page compares the two great schools of Arabic grammar, Basra and Kufa, on what each accepts as a witness and which principle of reasoning it puts first, analogy or transmission. It passes on what a research summary supplied to the museum set down in a table, and adds what the summary says of Ibn al-Anbari's gathering of the points of dispute between them. The two schools have a general page, [[concept/madrasat-al-nahw-al-basriyya-kufiyya|The Basran and Kufan school]] {cite:pageplace-baalbaki-early-islamic-grammatical-tradition-preview} {cite:al-kindi-publisher-ijllt-nahwu-kufah-2023} {cite:qaf-min-tarikh-al-nahw-al-arabi}.

## The figures of the two schools
For Basra the summary names [[person/al-khalil-ibn-ahmad-al-farahidi|al-Khalil ibn Ahmad]], [[person/sibawayh|Sibawayh]] and [[person/al-mubarrad|al-Mubarrad]]; for Kufa [[person/al-ruasi|al-Ru'asi]], [[person/al-kisai|al-Kisa'i]], [[person/al-farra|al-Farra]] and [[person/ahmad-ibn-yahya-tha-lab|Tha'lab]] {cite:pageplace-baalbaki-early-islamic-grammatical-tradition-preview} {cite:al-kindi-publisher-ijllt-nahwu-kufah-2023} {cite:qaf-min-tarikh-al-nahw-al-arabi}. The list stands as the summary has it; the page does not claim it is complete, and no work of any of them was read.

## The Basran method
The summary ascribes to the Basrans a preference for analogy, that is reasoning by strict likeness, and for systematic rules and causal explanation. They restrict witnesses, according to the summary, to the pre-Islamic poets and the early mukhadram poets and to informants who were pure Bedouin, and set aside an anomalous line, or one attested once, as poetic necessity, so that no rule is built on it {cite:pageplace-baalbaki-early-islamic-grammatical-tradition-preview} {cite:al-kindi-publisher-ijllt-nahwu-kufah-2023} {cite:qaf-min-tarikh-al-nahw-al-arabi}.

## The Kufan method
The summary ascribes to the Kufans a preference for transmission, that is what is firmly reported from the Arabs, over rigid analogy. They accept a wider body of poetic witnesses, including rare and dialectal lines and those with a single attestation, and accept non-canonical Qur'anic readings as a sound basis for a rule. This difference over witnesses is what ties to the page [[concept/al-shir-al-jahili-ka-shahid-lughawi|Pre-Islamic poetry as linguistic evidence]] {cite:pageplace-baalbaki-early-islamic-grammatical-tradition-preview} {cite:al-kindi-publisher-ijllt-nahwu-kufah-2023} {cite:qaf-min-tarikh-al-nahw-al-arabi}.

## The comparison table
The table has four rows: the figures, the core method, the use of witnesses, and the synthesis that the tradition reached. Basra's method is analogy and causal reasoning, Kufa's is transmission; Basra's witnesses are narrower, Kufa's wider. For the classical synthesis the summary names Ibn al-Anbari, who died in 577 AH, 1181 CE, and who gathered in his al-Insaf fi masa'il al-khilaf 121 points of grammatical dispute between the schools; the museum's record for him is [[person/al-anbari-abd-al-rahman|al-Anbari, Abd al-Rahman]] {cite:pageplace-baalbaki-early-islamic-grammatical-tradition-preview} {cite:al-kindi-publisher-ijllt-nahwu-kufah-2023} {cite:qaf-min-tarikh-al-nahw-al-arabi}.

## Analogy, transmission and necessity
In the grammarians' usage, analogy is to carry one word to its like in ruling when they share a ground, so that from the regular a rule is made to apply to what was never heard. Transmission is to keep to what is firmly reported from Arabs trusted for their eloquence, so that a ruling rests only on a text handed down. The summary lets one understand that the Basrans put the first foremost and so had to limit the heard material to what is regular, counting the anomalous, or the line attested alone, as poetic necessity, that is what is allowed to a poet alone to keep the metre; and that the Kufans put the second foremost and so bore with what is not regular. This is an explanation of terms, not a report from the summary, meant only to ease reading of the table {cite:pageplace-baalbaki-early-islamic-grammatical-tradition-preview} {cite:al-kindi-publisher-ijllt-nahwu-kufah-2023} {cite:qaf-min-tarikh-al-nahw-al-arabi}.

## Ibn al-Anbari and the points of dispute
The summary sees in Ibn al-Anbari the author of the classical synthesis of the dispute, since in one book he gathered 121 questions on which Basrans and Kufans differed. That means the quarrel between the schools no longer remained a quarrel over method alone but became a list of definite questions of grammar on which one view could be set against another. The summary names neither the questions nor what the author preferred on them, so the page passes on nothing of them and keeps to the number as given {cite:pageplace-baalbaki-early-islamic-grammatical-tradition-preview} {cite:al-kindi-publisher-ijllt-nahwu-kufah-2023} {cite:qaf-min-tarikh-al-nahw-al-arabi}.
