## Summary
Ibn Sina closes chapter 7 by noting that the exercisers of ancient times fled from mixing foods by limiting themselves to meat at the midday meal and bread at supper, and that the best time to eat in summer is the coolest; he then offers notes on roasted meat, shurbaj, grapes on roasted heads, partridge, chicken, chicken broth and sweets, and says undigested bread does more harm than undigested meat {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.235-236}.

## The ancients' habit
The exercisers of ancient times fled from this by limiting themselves to meat at the midday meal and bread at supper; the best time to eat in summer is the coolest, and holding off hunger may fill the stomach with bad watery humours {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.235-236}.

## Kabab and shurbaj
Roast meat, once digested, is the most nourishing food but descends slowly and stays in the caecum; shurbaj is a good food, which with onion drives out wind and without onion stirs it. For the hot-tempered shurbaj should be without saffron, for the cold-tempered with saffron {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.236}.

## Birds
The partridge is dry and binds, the young chicken moist and loosening; the best roast fowl is one roasted in the belly of a kid or lamb, which keeps its moisture. Broth of young chicken balances the humours more, though fowl broth is more nourishing; kid is better cold for the stillness of its vapour, lamb better hot for the melting of its greasiness {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.236}.

## Grapes, sweets and bread
He holds grapes over roasted heads to be very bad, whatever some suppose; sweets, even made with sugar like faludhaj, are bad because they block and make thirsty; and undigested bread does much harm, undigested meat less {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.236}.
