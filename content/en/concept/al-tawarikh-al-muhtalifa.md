## Summary
At the beginning of his book, al-Farghani lists five eras from which years are reckoned, and states the interval in years and days between each era and the next {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. This material is presented in the first chapter of the book in the Latin translation {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 138}.

## Order and Intervals
The eras begin with the Era of Bukhtanassar, then the Era of Philip, then the Era of Alexander, then the Era of the Hijrah, then the Era of Yazdegerd {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. Between the Era of Bukhtanassar and the Era of Yazdegerd there are 1,379 Persian years and three months; between the Era of Philip and the Era of Yazdegerd, 955 years and three months; and between the Hijrah and the Era of Yazdegerd, 3,624 days {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 126}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 139}. Between the Era of Alexander and the Era of Yazdegerd there are about 942 Roman years and 59 days; the Arabic figure here is uncertain due to damage on the page, so the Latin figure is relied upon {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 139}.

## Starting Days
Al-Farghani states the first day of each era: Thursday for the Arabs from the Hijrah; Tuesday for the Persians from Yazdegerd; Monday for the Romans and Syrians from Alexander; Wednesday for the Copts in the Almagest from the beginning of the reign of Bukhtanassar; and Sunday for the Copts in Ptolemy's Zij from Philip {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 126}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 138}.

## Note on the Name
The Arabic text calls the first era "Bukhtanassar," while the Latin translation calls it Nabonassar, who is the king of Babylon known as Nabonassar, not Bukhtanassar (Nebuchadnezzar), whom the encyclopedia identifies in another entry {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 125}. The name here thus refers to a chronological era rather than the king known by the same name, and the matter remains open in other sources.
