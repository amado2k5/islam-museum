## Summary
For the author, profit is the return on risk and innovation, and what remains of revenue after expenses {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 280}. Because Islam prohibits fixed interest, the return on money capital is a share of profit {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 262}.

## Definition of Profit
Among economists, profit is the residual return to the entrepreneur who bears risk, after paying fixed returns to the other factors of production {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 203}. In Islamic economics, profit means the increase over commercial capital, not loan capital, since on a loan it would be riba (usury/interest) {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 289}. Innovation refers to creating a new method, good, production technique, or market; its profit is temporary and diminishes as other entrepreneurs imitate it {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 280}.

## Economic Profit
Economic profit is net profit after deducting the entrepreneur's imputed wage (what they could have earned at a fixed salary), the rent of real estate if owned by the enterprise, and the cost of capital if provided by its owner, even when these costs are not actually paid out {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 280} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 281}. In Islam, what remains to the business owner after paying wages, rent, materials, and fixed and variable expenses is their net profit {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 281}. Workers may participate with a share of profit, as may the capital provider in a mudarabah (profit-sharing partnership), or the owners of production assets {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 281}.

## Liability and Profit
The hadith "Entitlement to revenue follows liability for loss" (al-kharaj bi-l-daman) indicates that return is tied to liability, and that risk is a dependent rather than an independent factor of production {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 202} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 283}. However, mere liability does not merit profit; the recognized liability is liability of ownership or liability of work, as in a credit partnership (sharikah al-wujuh) where the partner owns the goods upon purchase and thus bears owner's liability {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 284}. The prohibition against "profiting from what one has not guaranteed" is interpreted in this sense {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 202} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 286}.

## Prohibition on Profiting from What Is Not Guaranteed
The hadiths state that the Prophet "prohibited profiting from what has not been guaranteed," and the author cites Ahmad Shakir's authentication of its chain of transmission {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 234}. The author applies this prohibition to forbid profiting on food before taking possession of it and moving it, as the hadith entails more than mere physical receipt {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 234}.

## Bearing Risk
The author states that risk is productive and plays a dependent role in earning, since the capital provider in mudarabah is entitled to profit by virtue of the capital put at risk {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 283}. He cites Ibn Khaldun's observation that transporting goods from a distant land or along dangerous routes magnifies profits because the goods become scarce and prized {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 283}.

## Partnership Through Assets
The author considers it permissible for a machine or anything eligible for leasing to participate for a share of profit, reasoning that if leasing is permitted then partnership is even more fitting, and noting that land participates in sharecropping (muzara'ah) {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 282}; he cites the hadith of Ruwayfi ibn Thabit concerning the camel in war booty {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 282}.

## Continuity of Ownership
The author criticizes al-Sadr's view that growth deriving from an owned material also belongs to its owner, arguing that profit in mudarabah is the joint product of capital and labor, that prior ownership does not preclude newly arising ownership, and that a worker's development of a material may make the added value greater than the original {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 287}.

## Trade and Utility
For the author, trade creates time or place utility, such as storage—which involves bearing cost and risk—and transporting goods to a place where their utility is greater {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 233} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 234}. In the hadith of Ibn Umar, food may not be sold until it is moved to the merchants' quarters {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 234}, which some jurists restricted to food {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 234}.
