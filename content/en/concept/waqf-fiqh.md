Waqf, lexically, is detention: to hold back a thing in God's way; it also means prevention and stillness, and the word names the thing held. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17579} Technically the jurists define it in several ways: for the Hanafi companions, the detention of a thing in God's ownership with its use given to whom the founder wishes; for Abu Hanifa, its detention in the founder's ownership with the use given as charity; for the Malikis, the grant of the use of a thing for as long as it exists, the ownership remaining with the grantor even notionally; for the Shafi'is, the detention of property whose use can be had with its substance kept, the disposal of its ownership cut off, for a lawful and existing purpose; for the Hanbalis, the detention by an owner of his property whose use is kept in its substance, with its income given to a charitable purpose seeking God's favour. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17579}

Related words: tabarru' (giving without return, wider than waqf), sadaqa (gift in life without return, which may also name waqf, as in 'Umar's land), hiba (gift without return), 'ariya (the permission to use a thing while its owner keeps it), and wasiyya. The difference from a gift is that waqf transfers the use while the substance stays in God's ownership and cannot be disposed of, whereas a gift transfers the thing itself. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17579}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17581}

Wasiyya: the Hanafis define it as a transfer of ownership attached to what comes after death, and the Shafi'is similarly. It differs from waqf: a bequest takes effect after death and may be of the thing or of its use, while waqf is a gift made in life and only of the use. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17581}

The legality of waqf: the majority of jurists hold it lawful, binding and a recommended act of devotion, citing the report of Ibn 'Umar that 'Umar got land at Khaybar and the Prophet told him, if he wished, to hold back its substance and give its produce in charity; the saying that when a man dies his deeds end except for three, among them continuing charity; and the report of Jabir that every wealthy man among the emigrants and the helpers held back property in endless charity. Ibn Qudama calls this consensus of the Companions. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17581}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17582} Ibn 'Abidin, quoting al-Is'af, says waqf is lawful according to Abu Hanifa and his companions; the disagreement is about its binding character: for Abu Hanifa it is like a loan of use, with the ownership remaining with the founder, binding only by a judge's ruling or when made as a bequest. Ibn Qudama reports Shurayh as not accepting it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17582} On its legal ruling: it is basically a recommended act of devotion; it becomes obligatory when vowed; it is permitted when not made for the sake of devotion, as a dhimmi's waqf, which has no reward; and forbidden when it serves sin, as an endowment for a church. The Malikis mention that an endowment only for sons and not daughters is forbidden as resembling the custom of the pre-Islamic age, but Ibn al-Qasim preferred disliking it and enforcing it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17582}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17583}

The pillars of waqf: the majority (Malikis, Shafi'is, Hanbalis) count four: the formula, the founder, the beneficiary and the thing endowed; the Hanafis count only the formula. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17583} Waqf is not valid without an offer, and the jurists differ on whether acceptance is required. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17583}

The formula of offer: whatever shows the founder's intent by word, a clear sign, writing or act. The express words are 'I endowed' (waqaftu), which the Malikis, Shafi'is and Hanbalis hold express, and 'I detained' (habastu), express for the Hanbalis, the Maliki view and the sounder Shafi'i view, and 'I made a way' (sabbaltu), express for the Shafi'is and Hanbalis in the sounder view, since custom has fixed their use and the Prophet's words to 'Umar use the same. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17584} The allusive words are 'I gave in charity' (tasaddaqtu), which the Hanbalis and Malikis count allusive, and which the Shafi'is count express when it is joined to a general beneficiary such as the poor, and otherwise allusive; also 'I forbade' and 'I made eternal', allusive for the Hanbalis and the sounder Shafi'i view; they make a waqf only when joined to a clear sign, such as 'a charity endowed', 'a charity never to be sold, given or inherited', or when intention is added. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17584}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17585}

When the administrator holds, from the endowment's income, a sum that may properly go to a work of charity, such as ransoming Muslim captives or aiding a stranded fighter, and the endowment needs repair, then: if delaying repair until next year's income causes no clear harm that threatens the endowment's ruin, the income goes to the charity and repair is postponed; if the delay causes clear harm, the income goes first to repair and only the surplus to the charity. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17661} Ibn Najim states that an administrator who pays the beneficiaries while repair that cannot be delayed is due becomes liable, because the upkeep the endowment needs is excluded from the poor's right. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17661}

For the Hanafis, where repair is necessary, such as raising a roof or building a wall, it takes precedence over all other uses, since it is not proper care to let a mosque fall into ruin for the sake of what is given to the imam and the muezzin; any surplus over the repair goes to the beneficiary nearest to the founder's purpose whose loss would cause clear harm. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17661} Where repair is not necessary, so that leaving it until the next year's income would not ruin the thing, the more important need comes first. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17661} The Hanafis separate the case where the founder stipulated that repair be given priority each year, when the administrator keeps the amount aside each year even if it is not yet needed, from the case of silence, when repair is paid when needed and nothing is saved for it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17661} When part of an endowed building or its materials has fallen, the judge spends it on repair; if the means of repair are not ready at once, he keeps it until they are. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17661}

For the Shafi'is, the surplus of a mosque's income above what its repair needs, reckoned by the cost of its rebuilding, is saved, and with the balance a property is bought and endowed; this is safer for the mosque, and it is not bought from the endowed thing whose upkeep it is, since the founder endowed that thing for upkeep. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17662}

The jurists differ on the source from which the endowed thing and its upkeep are paid. The Hanafis and Malikis hold that it comes from the endowment's income whether or not the founder stipulated this, since the endowment is a continuing charity in God's way and can continue only in this manner; the Malikis add that a stipulation to the contrary is void. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17662} For the Hanafis, if the founder gave a house for his son's residence, its repair falls on the one who lives in it, from his own money and not from the income, since the burden follows the benefit; if he refuses or cannot pay because he is poor, the judge lets the house, from him or from another, and repairs it from the rent, and the one who refuses is not compelled to repair. The Malikis agree. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17662} The Malikis say that a horse endowed for raiding is fed from the public treasury; if there is no treasury, it is sold and its price buys weapons and similar things that need no feeding. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17662}

The Shafi'is and Hanbalis follow the founder's terms. For the Shafi'is, the feeding, the equipping and the repair are paid from the source named; failing that, from the endowed thing's own benefits, such as a slave's earnings or a property's rent; if its benefits cease, the feeding and the equipping come from the public treasury, while repair is then owed by no one, because a property is like absolute ownership, unlike a living animal, whose life and sanctity must be preserved. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17662} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17663} For the Hanbalis, the founder's terms govern. If he named a source of feeding, whether the income or another, that is followed. If he did not name one and the endowed thing is a living being, such as a slave or horses, it is fed from its own income; if there is no income, the feeding falls on the named beneficiary. If the feeding is impossible, the thing is sold and its price is spent on another thing that becomes an endowment; if it can be let out, it is let out for enough to feed it. If the beneficiary is unnamed, as the poor are, the feeding comes from the public treasury. For property, such as buildings, weapons and goods, repair is owed by no one unless the founder made it a condition. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17663}

The jurists agree that whoever wrongfully damages an endowment, by demolition or otherwise, must pay. For the Hanafis, a man who lets an endowed house, and whose tenant uses its courtyard to tether animals and wrecks it, is liable, because he acted without permission. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17663} For the Malikis, whoever wrongfully demolishes an endowment must rebuild it as it was, even if what was demolished was decayed; but the preponderant view among them is that he owes its value, like any other destroyed property. Ibn 'Arafa accepted this view and 'Iyad made it well known. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17663} For the Shafi'is, no one is liable for a book endowed for the pursuit of learning that is lost in his hands without his fault; but whoever is at fault is liable, and using the book for a purpose other than the one endowed is a fault. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17663}

When an endowed thing needs repair or upkeep and its income does not suffice, may the administrator borrow on the endowment, and does he need permission? The Malikis, Hanbalis and, in one view, the Hanafis hold that the administrator may borrow for the endowment without the judge's permission, as with his other acts, since he is a trusted agent with full power to act. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17664} The Malikis say that if an administrator undertook, on taking up the office, to spend from his own money on the endowment when needed, he is not bound by that, but he may recover what he spent. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17664} For the Shafi'is, the administrator may not borrow without the founder's condition or the ruler's permission. The ruler may lend him money from the public treasury, permit him to borrow, or permit him to spend his own money on repair on condition that he may recover it. If he borrows without permission or condition, it is not allowed, and he cannot recover what he spent, since he transgressed. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17664}

The preferred Hanafi view is that borrowing on an endowment is not allowed unless it is needed for the endowment's good, such as repair or buying seed, and then only on two conditions: the judge's permission, and that letting the thing and spending its rent is not possible; if the judge is far away, the administrator may borrow on his own authority. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17664} Ibn 'Abidin says borrowing is not allowed without the founder's order, because a debt attaches only to a person's liability; an endowment has no liability, the poor have liability but they cannot in practice be pursued, so the debt falls on the administrator alone. Necessity overrides this analogy; Abu al-Layth's preferred view is that borrowing is allowed with the judge's permission when there is no alternative and the judge is not far from the administrator, because the judge's authority covers the public interests of Muslims more widely. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17664} Where there is an alternative, such as paying the beneficiaries, borrowing for that is not allowed, except for what is given to the imam, the preacher and the muezzin, and the mats and oil of a mosque, on the preferred view. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17664}

If the judge's permission is required and the administrator claims to have had it, the claim is apparently not accepted without evidence, because he wants to take from the income, and his word is accepted only about what is in his hand. If he did not obtain permission, it is forbidden for him to take from the income, since he is then a volunteer, until permission is established. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17665} Ibn 'Abidin says that if the administrator spent his own money to repair the endowment, he may recover it from the endowment's income; the fatwa of al-Hanuti holds that he may recover it in conscience, but his claim is not accepted unless he had it witnessed that he spent in order to recover. This applies only where the endowment has income; without income, the judge's permission is necessary. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17665}

Second, the sale of an endowment and its exchange. When an endowed thing is disabled and can no longer be used, the Hanafis, Malikis, Hanbalis and, in one view, the Shafi'is allow its sale and the spending of the price on a like thing. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17665} The Hanafis allow sale and exchange even when the endowment is not disabled, under special conditions; the Malikis, in general, distinguish between real estate and movable goods. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17665}

The Hanafis recognise three cases of exchange. In the first, the founder stipulates, at the endowment, that the endowed land may be exchanged for another land, either for himself or for another. This has two formulas; the first is to say: 'my land is an endowed charity to God forever, on condition that I sell it and buy with its price another land', and then the second land is endowed on the first's terms. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17665} Abu Yusuf, Hilal and al-Khassaf hold that the endowment and the condition are valid by juristic preference, which is the sound view according to the Fatawa Qadikhan; the condition does not undo the endowment, since an endowment can pass from one land to another, and the second stands in place of the first. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17665} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17666} For example, if an usurper floods an endowed land until it becomes a sea unfit for farming, he pays its value and buys another land which becomes an endowment on the first's terms; likewise, if its yield has fallen through blight and does not exceed its costs, the good of the endowment lies in exchange, so the founder may validly stipulate the power of exchange even when the need is not present now. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17666}

Muhammad and Yusuf ibn Khalid al-Samti hold that the endowment is valid and the condition void, which is the analogical view. Sarakhsi justifies Muhammad's view: the condition does not prevent the endowment from ending, so the endowment is completed, while the exchange stands as a void condition in itself, like a mosque on which an exchange is stipulated or where it is stipulated that only some people may pray. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17666} Some Hanafi jurists say the endowment and the condition are both void. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17666} Kamal ibn al-Humam reports from al-Ansari that the condition is valid but the sale may be made only with the judge's permission, and the judge should permit a sale when there is no benefit in the endowment and he sees it is better for its beneficiaries. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17666}

If the founder stipulated that the land be sold and another bought with its price, without adding a further endowment formula, the second land becomes an endowment on the first's terms without renewed formula, because the land was already fixed for the endowment and its price takes its place. By analogy the endowment would be void, since no other land was named to stand in the first's place. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17666} The second formula: if the founder stipulated the right of exchange for the administrator and not for himself, the administrator may exchange for himself, since giving power to another follows from owning the power oneself. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17666} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17667}

If the right of exchange is stipulated for another man together with the founder, the founder alone holds the right, and the other man does not hold it alone. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17667} Exchange under a stipulation is allowed even if the thing yields income and benefit, since the endowment does not thereby leave use, and neither the judge's direct action nor the absence of income for repair is required. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17667} The Hanafis limit exchange to what the founder stated in kind and type: if he stipulated exchange for land, it may not be made for a house; if he stipulated the land of a village, it may not be made for other land, since village lands differ in cost and yield; if he did not specify the replacement, he may exchange for any real estate, whether land, house or town, as he wishes, since the stipulation is general. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17667} If the stipulation covers one exchange, the founder may not exchange the second land for a third, since the condition was fulfilled only in the first, unless the words express that. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17667}

The second case: the founder did not stipulate exchange at the endowment, whether he stipulated its absence or was silent, but the endowment came to a state in which it gives no benefit at all, such as giving no yield or not covering its upkeep. Exchange in this case is allowed on the sounder view when the judge permits it and sees the good in it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17667} Ibn 'Abidin reports from al-Bahr that Qadikhan's words differ: in one place he allowed the judge to exchange without the founder's stipulation where he saw the good; in another he forbade it even when the land had come to a state where no benefit could be had. The sound view is that the judge may exchange on the following conditions, the first of which is that the endowed thing is wholly taken out of use. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17667}

Among the conditions for exchange, as the Hanafis state them: the sale must not be at a gross undervaluation; the endowment must have some yield with which it can be repaired; the exchanger must be a man of knowledge and action, so that the endowments of Muslims are not undermined; the replacement must be real estate and not cash; and the thing may not be sold to someone whose testimony cannot be accepted in his favour or to a creditor of the founder's heirs. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17668} The exchanged and the replacement should be of one kind. Ibn 'Abidin, quoting Qanali-zada, says that the sameness of kind is probably not required for an endowment held for its yield, since what counts is a large return and little upkeep: exchanging a shop for land that yields rent equal to the shop's rent is better, because land lasts longer and needs less repair; whereas an endowment held for residence is judged by the residence. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17668} In al-Qunya, a house endowment may be exchanged for another house only if the new one is in the same quarter or in a better quarter; the reverse is not allowed, even if the house held is larger in area, value and rent, because it may fall into ruin in the lesser quarter. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17668}

The Bahr al-Ra'iq, citing the commentary on Ibn Wahban's poem, asks whether exchange is allowed when the founder forbade it, or when the administrator is removed before exchanging, or when he is removed as soon as he decides to exchange. Tarsusi replies that there is no transmitted ruling, and that the rules of the school mean the judge may exchange when he sees the good in it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17668} The same work reports from Muhammad that if an endowed land has weakened in yield, and the administrator can buy with its price another land with higher yield, he may sell and buy that. Some shaykhs forbade sale and exchange whether or not the endowment had ceased to yield. Qadikhan says that an endowment without a stipulation of exchange may not be sold or exchanged even if its land is salty and unusable, because an endowment is meant to be perpetual and is not sold; the power of exchange arises only from a stipulation. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17668} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17669}

The third case: the founder did not stipulate exchange; the endowment has yield and is not disabled; but exchange would bring some general benefit, and the replacement is better in benefit and yield. Ibn 'Abidin says that this is not allowed on the preferred view, as Qanali-zada settled it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17669} Ibn 'Abidin also reports from al-Ashbah that a productive endowment may be exchanged only in four cases: first, when the founder stipulated exchange; second, when a usurper flooded the land into a sea, so that he pays its value and the administrator buys land with it; third, when the usurper denies the seizure and there is no evidence, so that the administrator takes the value to buy a replacement; and fourth, when someone desires the land and offers a replacement with more yield and a better location, which is allowed on Abu Yusuf's view and is the fatwa, as in the fatwas of Qari al-Hidaya. The author of al-Nahr says that Sadr al-Shari'a's rebuttal stands against this: we do not give fatwa on the view of Abu Yusuf. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17669}

Exchange of endowed property among the Malikis. The Malikis distinguish between real estate and movable goods in sale and in exchange. They allow exchange of movable goods when no party can maintain them and they are in danger of perishing, or when their benefit has ceased so that they cannot be used for the purpose for which they were detained. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17669} Thus a horse endowed in God's way, for raiding or frontier guard, is fed from the public treasury; if there is no treasury, it is sold and the price buys weapons and similar things that need no feeding. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17669} Any movable endowment that is not real estate and cannot be used is sold, such as a horse that has caught rabies, a cloth that wears out, a slave who grows old, or books that decay. Its price is spent on a like thing if possible, or on a share of such a thing if the whole cannot be bought, and if that is impossible the price is given in charity. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17669} Likewise male animals endowed for raiding or breeding, when there are more of them than needed, or females that have grown too old to be of use, are sold and young females bought with the price, so that milk and offspring continue and the endowment remains, as al-Dardir says of ostriches. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17669} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17670}

For real estate, the Maliki school holds that it may not be sold even if it is ruined and cannot be used, whether a house, shops or other buildings, and it may not be exchanged for a like unruined thing. Abu al-Faraj reports from Malik that the imam may sell it, if he sees the good in it, and spend the price on a like thing. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17670} Some Malikis allowed the exchange of a ruined dwelling. Al-Taj wal-Iklil reports the absolute prohibition of selling a ruined dwelling of an endowment; Ibn al-Jahm gives the reason that someone will be found to repair it by renting it for years, so that it returns to its former state. The exchange of a ruined dwelling for an unruined one is disputed. Ibn Rushd says that if such a piece of land has lost all use and cannot be repaired or let, there is no harm in exchanging it for a place that will be endowed in its stead, by the judge's ruling after the cause is proved and the advantage to the endowment is clear; this is to be recorded and witnessed. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17670}

The Malikis allow the sale of real estate for the necessary widening of a congregational mosque, whether the endowment is held for named or unnamed persons. Ibn Rushd says that the apparent report of Ibn al-Qasim's collection allows it for every mosque, as does Sahnun's view; al-Nawadir reports from Malik and others that it applies to congregational mosques when there is need, and not to neighbourhood mosques, since the necessity there is not the same. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17670} Endowed property may also be sold to widen a graveyard or a road used by people, even against the will of the beneficiaries or the administrator; the beneficiaries are bound to have the price put into another endowment, but the judge may not compel them to accept a different endowment in its place. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17670} Al-Sharh al-Saghir says that for property taken to widen a mosque, the price is paid if the endowment was held for named persons; if it was held for unnamed persons, such as the poor, no compensation is due, since no named person has a right attached to it, and the reward the founder gains when the property enters the mosque is greater than what he first intended. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17671}

The Malikis say that if the founder stipulated change and exchange, his condition is followed. If he stipulated that, should a desirable price be offered, the property be sold and another bought, al-Nawadir and al-Mutiyya report that this is not allowed; if it is done nevertheless, it stands and the founder's condition is applied. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17671} Fath al-'Ali al-Malik reports a case: an endowed land on a road used by Muslims, whose founder stipulated that it not be sold or exchanged, was exchanged by the administrator of the road for a state land, the administrator handing the endowed land to a farmer and taking state land in return. Dardir holds that where the founder stipulated no exchange and gave no exception, the exchange is void; the administrator must return the state land to its owner and take back the endowed land itself, and the ruler restrains anyone who refuses. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17671}

Exchange of endowed property among the Shafi'is. The Muhadhdhab says that if a mosque is endowed and then ruined so that prayer in it ceases, it does not return to ownership, and the owner may not dispose of it, because what left ownership for God's right does not return to ownership by ruin, as a man who frees a slave and then becomes disabled does not reclaim him. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17671} Al-Sharbini says that the sounder view allows selling the mats of an endowed mosque when they have worn out, and its beams when they have broken or nearly so and are fit for nothing but burning, lest they be lost and the space be crowded with them to no purpose; a small sum from their price returning to the endowment is better than losing them. Such things are not counted as a sale of the endowment, since they are treated as nonexistent; this is the view on which the two shaykhs, al-Rafi'i and al-Nawawi, relied. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17671} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17672} Al-Rafi'i says that the analogy is to buy a replacement mat with the price of the old one, and al-Adhra'i takes the mentioned case to be straw used in clay. Mats given or bought for a mosque are sold when needed. Beams that can be used for other things, such as planks and doors, are never sold. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17672} If a mosque is at risk of falling, the judge may strip it and use the material to build another mosque, if he sees fit, and building it nearby is better; but he may not build a well with the material of a mosque. If an endowment on a bridge is destroyed by a flood and the bridge is not needed where it stands, it may be moved to where it is needed; the income of an endowment on a frontier that has become secure is kept by the administrator, in case the frontier returns. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17672}

If an endowed date palm dries, an endowed animal is disabled, or the beams of a mosque break, there are two views, and the sounder is that they may not be sold, as with a mosque. The other view allows sale, since the thing is no longer hoped to yield benefit; the price then takes the rule of the value that comes from the destruction of endowed property. If the ownership of the endowed thing's substance is held to belong to the beneficiary, which is the lesser view, the price goes to him; if it belongs to God, which is the more apparent view, a like thing is bought with the price to become an endowment in its place. Abu Hamid al-Isfara'ini says that the price buys a like thing to become an endowment in its place, without dispute. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17672}

Exchange of endowed property among the Hanbalis. Among them exchange is allowed when the endowed thing is no longer fit for the purpose for which it was endowed and no longer serves to be used, whether it is movable or real estate, and whether it is a mosque or not. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17673} They hold that selling an endowment is forbidden and invalid, and that exchanging it, even for something better, is invalid by explicit text, except where its intended benefit is lost through ruin and its yield cannot repair it, in which case it is sold; or where its intended benefit is lost by a cause other than ruin, such as wood that has cracked and is feared to fall. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17673} If the endowment is a mosque whose intended use is lost because it is too narrow for its people and cannot be widened, or because its neighbourhood is ruined, or because the place is filthy and prevents prayer in it, then, according to al-Qadi, sale is valid and the price is spent on a like thing, because keeping it so is a waste of property. Since an endowment is perpetual, when that cannot be kept in the same thing its purpose is preserved in another, and a succession of replacements is treated like the things themselves. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17673} A dried endowed tree may be sold; so may a beam that has broken, worn out or is feared to break or fall. Al-Talkhis says that when an endowed beam is near breaking, or an endowed house near collapse, and it is known that delay would take it out of use, it is sold to protect its value. Schools, hospices and caravanserais that are endowed may, when ruined, be sold by one view without dispute. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17673} An endowed horse for raiding that is no longer fit is sold and another horse fit for it is bought; once the replacement is bought it becomes an endowment. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17673} A founder's stipulation that an unusable endowment not be sold is void, and it must be sold. The seller is the judge where the endowment is for a public good, such as the poor, mosques or bridges; where it is held for a named person or named group, or for the worshippers or muezzins of a mosque, the endowment's special administrator sells it. Even so, it is best done with the judge's permission, because such a sale binds those who come after the present holders, like a sale made against an absent party. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17673} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17674} Once the replacement is bought for the endowment's use it becomes an endowment; the prudent course is to re-endow it by a new formula, lest a later person, not seeing that the purchase made it an endowment, undo it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17674}

The return of an endowment to the founder's ownership. Muhammad ibn al-Hasan of the Hanafis holds that if what surrounds a mosque is ruined and no longer needed, even while the mosque remains maintained, or if the mosque itself is ruined with no means to repair it and people no longer need it because another mosque has been built, then it returns to the ownership of its builder if he is alive, or to his heirs if he is dead; Abu Yusuf disagrees. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17674} Muhammad's reason: the founder gave the endowment to a kind of devotion, and since that devotion has ended the endowment ends with it. It is like a mosque's mat and grass when no longer needed, or its lamp when the mosque is ruined, which return to the one who made them; like a shroud that a beast has torn, which returns to its owner; and like an offering made by one who is prevented from completing the pilgrimage, which he may dispose of once the hindrance is removed. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17674} Abu Yusuf says that it is moved to another mosque with the judge's permission, and its debris is sold with the judge's permission and the price spent on some mosques. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17674}

The Hanafi consequence is that if an endowment falls into ruin and has no income to repair it, it returns to the builder or his heirs in Muhammad's view, though not in Abu Yusuf's. But in Muhammad's view, only what has left the founder's intended use altogether returns to ownership, such as a shop that burned and cannot be let for anything, or a hostel or a neighbourhood cistern that is ruined with no means of repair; what was made for income returns to ownership only as its debris, while its open ground remains an endowment that is let, even for a small rent. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17674} Khulasa reports that Muhammad said of a horse that was made a detained endowment in God's way and became unfit to ride that it is sold and its price goes to its owner or his heirs, as with a mosque. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17674} The Shafi'is, in the view opposite to the sounder, say that if an endowed thing loses its benefit through a cause that is not chargeable, such as a tree drying or being uprooted by wind or flood before it can be replanted, the endowment ends and the thing returns to the founder or his heir. The sounder Shafi'i view is that it does not return to ownership but remains an endowment; the Hanbalis agree. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17674} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17675}

The administration of an endowment. The jurists agree that the administration of an endowment follows the founder's condition. If he assigns it to a named person, his condition is followed, as 'Umar ibn al-Khattab did when he left his endowment to his daughter Hafsa to manage for her life, and then to the people of sound judgement of her kin, as al-Bayhaqi reports. Ibn Qudama says that the disposal of an endowment follows the founder's condition, and so does its administrator. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17675} The jurists differ on whether the founder may assign administration to himself. The Hanafis, Shafi'is and Hanbalis allow it. The Malikis say that if the beneficiary has not taken possession of the endowment, the endowment is void by the founder's death, illness, or bankruptcy; if the beneficiary has taken possession and the founder stipulated administration for himself, the endowment stands, but the founder is compelled to assign administration to another, since he may not assign it to himself. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17675}

If the founder names no administrator, the Malikis and Hanbalis hold that where the endowment is for unnamed persons, such as the poor, the mosques, the ruler appoints whom he chooses, since he cannot himself administer it. Where the endowment is for a named beneficiary of sound mind, that beneficiary administers it, since it is his ownership and his benefit, so that his supervision is like absolute ownership. If he is not of sound judgement, his guardian administers it. There is a possibility in the Hanbali school, which Ibn Abi Musa chose, that the ruler administers it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17675}

On the administration of an endowment where the founder did not stipulate it, the Hanafi views differ. Abu Yusuf holds that the endowment belongs to God and the administration goes first to the founder, then to his executor, then to the judge. Hilal, the apparent view of the school, holds that the administration belongs to the founder, then to his executor or the judge, because an administrator derives his authority from the founder's stipulation, so it is impossible that he should have authority while another derives it from him; and the founder is the person nearest to the endowment. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17676} Muhammad holds that the founder has no administration unless he stipulated it, because for him handing the endowment over to a custodian is a condition of its validity, so that once it is handed over the founder has no authority over it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17676} If the founder dies without naming an administrator, the judge appoints a custodian from among the founder's family while a suitable person can be found there, either because such a person is the most solicitous or because the founder meant the endowment to be attributed to them; only if none is found does he appoint an outsider. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17676} The Shafi'is hold, on the school's view, that if the founder names no administrator, administration belongs to the judge, who has general authority over such matters. A second view gives it to the founder, who had it, and a third gives it to the beneficiary, since the income is his. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17676} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17677}

The qualifications of an administrator. The jurists set several conditions for an administrator's fitness; some are agreed and some are disputed. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17676}

The first condition is legal capacity. The jurists agree that an administrator must be adult and sane, since a minor or a mentally ill person lacks the capacity; this holds in general. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17676} The Malikis and Hanbalis add that if the founder names no administrator and the beneficiary is a named person, such as Zayd or 'Amr, the beneficiary administers it himself; if he is a minor or insane, his guardian stands in for him. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17676} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17677} The Hanbalis say that the ownership of an endowed thing passes to the beneficiary if he is a named human or a limited group, and he administers it if he is adult and sound of judgement, or his guardian does if he is a minor, insane or a spendthrift; Ibn Abi Musa says the judge administers it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17677}

The Hanafis: Ibn 'Abidin quotes al-Is'af that appointing a minor as administrator is void by analogy in every case, and by juristic preference void while he is a minor, so that the administration passes to him when he grows up; al-Shilbi holds that the assignment to a minor is never valid, since administration is a form of guardianship and a minor under guardianship cannot be given guardianship over others. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17677} Ibn 'Abidin cites the fatwas of Rashid al-Din that a judge who assigns administration to a minor who is able to keep the property does so validly, since it grants him authority to act; the two views can be reconciled by reading al-Is'af as concerning a minor unable to act. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17677} Insanity prevents appointment and also ends it once it occurs; Ibn 'Abidin quotes al-Fath that an administrator is removed by permanent insanity lasting at least a year, and that if he recovers, administration returns to him, though al-Nahr says this holds for one named by the founder and not for one appointed by the judge. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17677} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17678} The Shafi'is state that administration is stripped by insanity, and al-Shabramallisi says that when a sane person returns to a founder-named administrator, the office returns by recovery alone without a new appointment. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17678}

The second condition is justice (adala). The Hanafis differ whether it is a condition of validity or of preference. Al-Is'af says that no one is appointed except a trustworthy and capable person, whether acting himself or through a deputy, because removing a dishonest person from office is not part of administration. Ibn 'Abidin holds that justice is a condition of preference, not of validity, and that an administrator who turns to wrongdoing deserves removal but is not removed automatically, as a judge who turns to wrongdoing is not removed on the sound and fatwa view. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17678} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17679}

The Malikis treat justice as a condition when the administrator was appointed by the judge or by the founder. Al-Hattab says that administration of a detained endowment belongs to the one the founder chose, who must be one trusted in religion and honesty; if the founder is negligent, administration falls to the judge, who sets up someone he approves. If the administrator is badly managing and not to be trusted, the judge removes him, unless the beneficiary owns his own affairs and accepts him. Al-Qarafi says that a judge removes an administrator only for a fault, but the founder may remove him even without one. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17678}

The Shafi'is require apparent and inner justice in an administrator the judge appoints. Al-Subki says that for one appointed by the founder, apparent justice should suffice; al-Adhra'i requires both apparent and inner justice in that case too, and al-Sharbini prefers the first view. If the administrator becomes a wrongdoer he is removed, and then the administration passes to the judge. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17678} Al-Shafi'iyya say that if an administrator becomes a wrongdoer and then just again, his authority returns if it was made a condition in the endowment's terms and named exactly; otherwise it does not. Al-Nawawi gave this fatwa, and Ibn al-Rif'a and others agreed; al-Zarkashi says it is apparent. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17679}

The Hanbalis hold that if administration belongs to someone other than the beneficiary, and the appointment was by the judge or by an original administrator, justice is required, because it is authority over property, like guardianship over an orphan's property. If he is not just, his hand is removed from the endowment to preserve it, and if he returns to fitness his right returns. If the administrator was set by the founder, justice is not required, and a trustworthy person is added to the wrongdoer; his authority is removed only where its removal is needed to keep the endowment, since preserving the endowment matters more than leaving a wrongdoer in authority over it. Where administration belongs to the beneficiary himself, whether just or not, he has the better claim to it, since he owns the endowment and so administers for himself; some say a trustworthy person is added to him. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17679} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17680}

The third condition is capability (kifaya), meaning the strength and ability of the person to act in what he administers. The Shafi'is and Hanbalis require it, since preserving an endowment is a religious duty and someone without capability cannot preserve it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17679} If capability is lost, the Shafi'is say the judge removes administration from him even if it was assigned to him by the founder. Al-Nawawi says that when the defect ends, his administration returns if it was stipulated in the endowment and named exactly. The Hanbalis say that if capability is lost he is not removed, but a strong and trustworthy administrator is added to him, to achieve the purpose. The Hanafis say that the administrator must be removed where he is not trustworthy or is incapable (the OCR text reads "the founder" here, probably a slip for the administrator, flagged as a doubt). The Malikis' words also imply that capability is required. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17679} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17680}

The fourth condition is Islam. The Hanbalis hold that an administrator must be Muslim if the beneficiary is Muslim or the object is a mosque or the like, relying on Surat al-Nisa, verse 141, which they cite for this condition. If the endowment is for a named unbeliever, an unbeliever may be made administrator. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17680} The Hanafis allow a dhimmi to be administrator and do not require Islam; if the judge removes a dhimmi for some reason and he then converts, administration does not return to him. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17680} Al-Ramli holds that analogy with the will and marriage makes valid a dhimmi's administration of an endowment for a dhimmi beneficiary, provided he is upright in his religion, but this is rejected because the endowment requires real justice; al-Shabramallisi says the rejection is the sound view. He distinguishes a dhimmi's marriage of his ward, where the guardian has a natural restraint from wishing to protect his ward from shame, which does not apply to an endowment. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17681}

The administrator's wage. The subject covers his right to a wage, whether it is set by the founder or the judge, its amount, and whether he is due one where neither has named it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17681} On the right to a wage: the jurists hold that an administrator is due a wage for his work in running the endowment and caring for its affairs. The evidence they cite includes 'Umar's words when he endowed his land at Khaybar, that whoever administers it may eat in reasonable measure or feed a friend without making profit from it; 'Ali's making the upkeep of the slaves he endowed to work the land a charge on the income; the analogy with the collector of zakat; and the saying attributed to the Prophet that what remains after his wives' maintenance and his worker's wage is charity. Ibn Hajar says that the worker there means the custodian of the land, so the saying shows that his wage is lawful. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17681} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17682}

On the setting of the wage. If the founder set it, the administrator takes what the founder fixed even if it exceeds the usual wage; this is the view of the Hanafis, Shafi'is and Hanbalis. The Hanafis say that if the founder fixed less than the usual wage, the judge completes it on request. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17682} The Shafi'is say that if the founder made himself administrator and fixed a wage for himself, it may not exceed the usual wage; if he fixed more, the endowment is void, since it would be an endowment upon himself. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17682} The Hanbalis say that if the founder fixed a stated wage, and it equals the usual wage, the administrator takes it; if it is more, the costs of what the endowment needs, such as the trustees and others, are paid from the excess until only the usual wage remains, unless the founder gave it to him outright. The Malikis fixed no amount and left it to the founder or the judge. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17682}

If the founder set no wage, the judge fixes it. The Hanafis and Hanbalis hold that the judge's wage must not exceed the usual wage, and if he sets more, the excess is withheld. The Malikis leave the matter to the judge's discretion; Ibn Fattuh says the judge may give the appointed administrator a fixed monthly stipend according to his work, as the imams did. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17682} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17683} The Shafi'is say that if the founder names no wage, the administrator has no wage on the sound view and may not take anything from the endowment; if he does take it, he is liable and is discharged only by handing it to the judge. If the administrator asks the judge to fix a wage for him, he is like a guardian of an orphan's property who asks the judge to set his fee. Al-Iraqi, who reports al-Balqini's view, says that the administrator then takes the wage where there is need, either the amount of his maintenance, as al-Rafi'i preferred, or the lesser of his maintenance and the usual wage, as al-Nawawi preferred; another view is that he is due the usual wage even if it exceeds his maintenance. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17683}

If the founder set no wage. Al-Ramli of the Hanafis says that the administrator is due nothing unless the judge fixes a wage for his work. Ibn 'Abidin concludes that where the founder names a sum for the administrator, he has it, whether large or small, whether or not he works, provided it is not set in return for work; where the founder names none and the judge fixes the usual wage, that is valid; if he fixes more, the excess is withheld; and if the administrator does no work he is due no wage. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17683} If the judge appoints an administrator and names no wage, then, where the custom is that the work is not done without the usual wage, he is due the usual wage, since custom is like a stipulation; otherwise nothing. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17683} Ibn Najim, citing al-Qunya, reports two Hanafi views on a judge's appointee who is given no wage: the first is that he has nothing even if he works a year; the second is that the custodian is due a wage like the effort he gives, whether or not the judge or the residents of the quarter set a wage for him, since no one accepts custody publicly without a wage, and custom is like a stipulation. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17683}

Where the endowment has administrators, the Malikis and Hanbalis and the Shafi'is treat the wage in a particular way. The Shafi'is hold that if the founder assigned no wage to the administrator, he is not entitled to one on the sound view; if the matter is referred to the judge, he is given one where there is need, as explained above. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17684} The Hanbalis have three views. The first is that the administrator may eat from the endowment's income in reasonable measure, whether he is needy or not, by analogy with the collector of zakat; this is the view of Abu al-Khattab. The second is that he takes the lesser of the usual wage or his maintenance, by analogy with the guardian of an orphan, and is entitled to it only if he is poor. The third is that an administrator known to take a wage for his work has a right to the usual wage, which is the analogy of the school. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17684} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17685} The Malikis, as Ibn Fattuh says, let the judge give a set monthly stipend in endowments, fixed by his judgement of the work. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17684}

The source of the wage. The majority of jurists, namely the Hanafis, the Malikis except Ibn 'Attab, the Shafi'is and the Hanbalis, hold that the administrator's wage, whether set by the founder or by the judge, comes from the endowment's income, citing 'Umar's words on his endowment that whoever administers it may eat from it in reasonable measure without accumulating property. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17685} Ibn 'Attab, of the Malikis, held that the wage comes only from the public treasury; if it is taken from endowments it is returned and recovered from the treasury, and if not given, his reward is with God. Al-Hattab explains that nothing should be taken from endowments because they are for bequests. Ibn Warad gave a fatwa in his view, but al-Dasuqi weakened Ibn 'Attab's view. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17685}

The work that earns the wage is to preserve the endowment, repair it, let it, collect its yield (rent, crop or fruit), strive to develop it, and spend it on its purposes, such as repair, upkeep and the beneficiaries, since that is what is customary for such work. The wage runs from the time the administrator takes up the office, and is due in proportion to the work. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17685} The Hanbalis say that if an administrator neglects his duty, his fixed wage is reduced by the amount his neglect cost the endowment; what is set for him is divided between what he did and what he did not, and the share for what he did not do falls away. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17685} The Hanafis say that if the beneficiaries dispute with an administrator who says the founder fixed a wage for work while he does nothing, the judge does not impose tasks that the rulers themselves do not perform. If an impediment comes upon him that stops him ordering, forbidding, taking and giving, he has no wage; if not, he keeps it. If the founder fixed more than the usual wage, that is permitted. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17685} In one case, the owner of an endowed land died and the judge appointed a custodian and gave him a tenth of the income, while a mill in the endowment was held by a man on a lump-sum lease and needed no custodian, and the beneficiaries collected its yield from him; the custodian has no right to a tenth of that yield, since his wage is payment for work and there is no wage without work. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17685}

The accounting of an administrator. It is the administrator's duty to collect the endowment's yield, spend from it on what the endowment needs, and pay the beneficiaries. The jurists agree that he must account for what he spends, whether he is called to account by the judge or by the beneficiaries. They differ on whether his word about his spending is accepted without evidence, and, if there is no evidence, whether his oath is required. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17685}

The Hanafis say that the judge need not require a detailed account of every sum the administrator has spent from the endowment's income; a general account suffices if he is known to be trustworthy. If he is suspected, the judge makes him explain item by item, and does not imprison him, but summons him for two or three days and warns him; if he explains, well and good, otherwise the judge takes his oath. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17686} Al-Durr quotes al-Qunya that if the judge suspects him he makes him swear, even if he is trustworthy, as a depositary who claims that a deposit was lost must swear. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17686} The Hanafi views differ on the claim that the administrator paid the beneficiaries. Al-Durr says his word is accepted without oath. The Is'af says that if he says he received the wage and paid it to the beneficiaries, and they deny it, his word is accepted with his oath and he is not liable, as a depositary who claims to have returned a deposit when the depositor denies it. The lessee of the endowed property is discharged by the administrator's acknowledgement of receiving the rent. If he says the rent was lost or stolen after he received it, his word with oath is accepted, since he is trustworthy. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17686} Ibn 'Abidin reports from al-Khayr al-Ramli that the fatwa is that he swears in this age. The Hamidiyya quotes Abu al-Su'ud as ruling that if the administrator is a squanderer, his oath is not accepted on his spending of endowment property. Al-Shilbi holds that a wrongdoer whose conduct breaks the Law is not believed on what he spent unless he has proof. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17686}

Whether a trusted administrator's word after his removal is accepted: al-Hamawi says the apparent view is that it is, since removal does not take away his trustworthiness. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17686} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17687} Mulla Abu al-Su'ud draws a further distinction: if the administrator claims to have paid from an endowment's income to beneficiaries whom the founder named as his children or grandchildren, his word is accepted; if he claims to have paid holders of offices, such as the imam and the muezzin, it is not, just as a man who hired a builder for a known wage in a mosque and then claims to have paid him is not believed. Al-Tamartashi praised this distinction. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17687}

The Malikis hold that if the founder dies and the endowment deed is lost, the administrator's word is accepted about the purposes on which he spends, if he is trustworthy. If he claims to have spent the yield, it is accepted if he is trustworthy, unless the deed had witnesses at its making, in which case he spends only with their knowledge. If he claims to have spent his own money on the endowment, it is accepted without oath unless he is suspected, and then he swears. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17687} Al-Hattab reports a case put to al-Suri: an imam, a muezzin and the general administrator of a mosque were charged years later by a market inspector with a surplus from the rent of shops; the administrator denied any surplus and refused to state his spending, saying he was not bound to, and that without him the endowment would be lost. Al-Suri ruled that his word is accepted about what he says he spent, if it resembles what he said. Al-Barzali adds that this holds where no condition was set requiring a record of income and spending, except with witnesses. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17687}

The Shafi'is distinguish between beneficiaries who are named, such as Zayd and 'Amr, whose word on what is paid to them is accepted and who may demand accounts, and unnamed beneficiaries, such as the poor. Where the beneficiaries are unnamed, it is disputed whether the ruler may demand accounts; of two views, the more apparent is that he may. The administrator is believed about the amount he spent, where that is probable; if the judge suspects him, he makes him swear. Al-Azra'i explains that what is meant is spending in accordance with custom; the payments to the poor and other general purposes are included, but spending on a named beneficiary is not believed, since that beneficiary did not entrust him with it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17687} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17688}

The Hanbalis distinguish the administrator who serves without pay from one who takes a wage. The word of a volunteer administrator is accepted about a payment to a beneficiary; a paid administrator's word is not accepted without evidence. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17688} Al-Buhuti and al-Mirdawi say that the beneficiaries may not object to a trusted administrator whom the founder appointed; they may question him about what their endowment needs, so that their knowledge equals his; and the ruler may set up an office to audit endowment accounts when it is in the public interest. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17688}

The removal of an endowment's administrator, and who has the right to remove him. The conditions for an administrator and the rule when one fails were set out above; here is who may remove him. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17688}

First, the founder's right to remove his appointee. The Shafi'is and Hanbalis distinguish between a founder who at the start kept administration for himself and later assigned it to another, and a founder who gave it to another from the outset. In the first case he may remove and appoint someone else, since the assignee stands in for him, as a principal removes his agent; this is the sound view of the Shafi'is and the sound and correct view of the Hanbalis in the corrected text of al-Furu'. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17688} Al-Shirbini notes that this implies the founder may remove without cause, and al-Subki says so in his fatwas; but many later scholars, among them Ibn Razin, ruled that removal without cause is not permitted. A Shafi'i and Hanbali view denies him any removal, since his ownership has gone. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17689}

In the second case, where the founder names an administrator at the endowment's creation, the Shafi'is say that he may not remove him, even for a reason of advantage, since he has no authority after assigning it to another. Hence if the administrator so named removes himself or becomes a wrongdoer, the appointment of a replacement belongs to the judge, not the founder. The Hanbalis say that if the founder named an administrator and stipulated for himself the right to remove him, he may remove him; if not, he may not. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17689} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17690}

The Hanafis: Muhammad ibn al-Hasan holds that if the founder stipulated for himself and his children the right to appoint and remove custodians, and to exchange the endowment, and all such authority, and then handed the endowment over to the administrator, the founder may remove him. If he did not stipulate the right to remove, he may not do so after handing over, because the administrator stands in place of the beneficiaries. Al-Sadr al-Shahid says that the fatwa follows Muhammad's view. Ibn 'Abidin says this rests on whether handing over is a condition, which it is for Muhammad and is not for Abu Yusuf. For Abu Yusuf the founder has authority whether or not he stipulated it, since handing over is not a condition; if he appoints another he is a deputy and may remove him, whether he stipulated the right or not. Hilal, al-Fath and al-Bahr hold this to be the apparent view, and the shaykhs of Balkh gave fatwa on Abu Yusuf's view. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17689} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17690} Al-Ramli says that this is plain: removal is valid for cause or without cause, since he is a deputy, and a principal may remove his agent in every case. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17690}

The Malikis hold that the founder may not stipulate administration for himself; he follows his own condition in naming the administrator, and if he named a particular person he may not change to another. Al-Qarafi says that the founder may remove an administrator even without cause; al-Hattab says that one who has appointed someone to administer his endowment may remove him only for a cause that appears. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17690}

Second, the judge's right to remove. The judge has general authority, so he may remove an administrator whom the founder named if the administrator's dishonesty is proved or if he lacks one of the required conditions; he may not remove him without cause. Where the judge himself appointed the administrator, the Malikis, the Shafi'is and some Hanafis hold that he may not be removed except for a cause, such as embezzlement or some other fault; the Hanbalis and some Hanafis hold that he may be removed without embezzlement. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17690}

The plurality of administrators. An endowment may have one administrator or more, as the jurists state. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17690} If the founder assigns administration to two persons, the Shafi'is, the Hanbalis and Abu Hanifa hold that neither may act alone, since the founder did not accept either's judgement alone; but if the founder names each of them, each may act alone. Abu Yusuf allows each of two administrators to act alone. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17691} The Hanbali text adds that if the founder assigns the repair of the endowment to one man and the collection of its yield to another, each has his assignment. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17691} The Shafi'is say that if the founder stipulates that the administrator be the most upright of his children, and they all share that uprightness, they share the office; if one is manifestly more upright, he alone holds it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17691} The Hanafis hold that if the founder names a man and then names a guardian for a minor, they are both administrators, the guardian sharing the office; unless the founder specifies, saying, for example, that he endowed his land on a named beneficiary and gave its administration to one man, and named another as executor of his estate and all his affairs, in which case each acts alone within his own assignment. Ibn 'Abidin cites al-Is'af for this. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17691} If the founder gives administration to the most virtuous of his children and they are equal in virtue, they do not share it and it goes to the eldest. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17692} If the founder gave the administration to two men and one accepts and the other refuses, or dies, or is disqualified, the judge appoints another in his place; the Hanafis, Shafi'is and Hanbalis agree. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17692}

The delegation of administration by an administrator. Except where the founder allowed it, the jurists hold that an administrator may not delegate administration to whomever he wishes or bequeath it to another. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17692} The Hanafis say that if the founder's delegation to the administrator was general, meaning that he stood in the founder's place and could assign and bequeath administration to whomever he chose, he may delegate it in health and in his last illness; if it was not general, he may not delegate it in health, but only in his last illness, since he is like an executor, and an executor may appoint another. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17692} The Shafi'is say that if the founder says he has assigned administration to a named person, who may delegate it to whomever he wishes, the delegation is valid. Whether the first person's administration then ceases is disputed: the school's view is that it does, since delegation is like a transfer of ownership, so that neither the founder nor the delegate can remove the new administrator, and it does not return to the delegator after his death. Al-Subki's view is that the delegate is the delegator's agent, so if the delegator dies administration returns to the delegate, and if the delegate dies it returns to the delegator. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17692} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17693} The Hanbalis say that an administrator named by the founder, if he is himself a beneficiary, may appoint an agent and remove him, since his authority is original, as a person disposes of his own property. If he is not a beneficiary, he may not appoint an administrator or bequeath the office, unless the founder stipulated that he may appoint whomever he chooses. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17693}

The end of an endowment. One way an endowment ends is the return of the endowed thing to the founder's ownership: when the thing is ruined and no longer fit for use, when the endowment is for a class of beneficiaries that comes to an end, or in the case of a temporary endowment, which the Malikis allow. These cases have been discussed in the course of the article. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17693}
