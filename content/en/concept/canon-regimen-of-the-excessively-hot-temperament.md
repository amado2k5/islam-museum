## Summary
Ibn Sina opens the fourth teaching, the regimen of a body whose temperament is not excellent, in five chapters, with the first on correcting the temperament that is hotter than it should be; he distinguishes the hot temperament balanced in the two passive qualities from the hot and dry and the hot and moist, and sets its regimen on two aims: return to balance and keeping health {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.255}.

## Kinds of hot temperament
A hot ill-temperament is either with balance of the two passive qualities, or with dryness or moisture predominating. When the two are balanced, the excess of heat reaches only a limit and is not extreme, or it would dry. The hot and dry may remain as it is for a long time; the hot and moist cannot stay together long, for at times the moisture overcomes the heat and puts it out, and at times the heat overcomes the moisture and dries it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.255}.

## The two aims
If moisture prevails, its owner improves at the peak of youth and becomes balanced; when he declines, foreign moisture grows and the heat lessens. The whole regimen of the hot-tempered rests on two aims: to return them to balance, and to preserve their health as it is. The first is possible only for the calm and the self-sufficient who steel themselves to a long patience, and is done by degrees, since returning them without degrees makes the body ill; the second is done with foods like their temperament {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.255}.
