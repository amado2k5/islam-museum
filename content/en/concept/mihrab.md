## Overview
A niche in the qibla wall that shows worshippers the direction of the Kaaba. It has been a fixed feature of mosque architecture since the Umayyad period. {cite:wikidata}

## Function and origins in Yeomans
The mihrab is the niche from which the imam leads the prayer, with an honorific function recalling the place where the Prophet led the prayers. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.19} Yeomans writes that the oldest recorded mihrab in the form of a semi-circular niche appeared in the prayer hall that al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik built, employing Copts, at the [[building/prophets-mosque|Prophet's Mosque]] in Medina (707-709), and that the mihrab of the Dome of the Rock, in the cave below the rock, is a flat slab with a lobed arch on two twisted columns resembling a Coptic funerary stele, and may predate Medina's. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.19} A concave mihrab was installed in the [[building/masjid-amr-fustat|Mosque of Amr]] at Fustat in 710, almost certainly by Coptic craftsmen. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.19} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.21}

In Yeomans's view the origin and meaning of the mihrab remain uncertain: semi-circular niches belonged to the vocabulary of Coptic art in churches and monastic cells, but are found too in Graeco-Roman architecture housing statues of the gods and in synagogues such as Dura Europos holding the Torah scrolls; the evidence points to the widespread pre-Islamic use of the niche as a receptacle for honouring something sacred. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.20} The first Mosque of Amr had no niche in its qibla wall; al-Qalqashandi says four inserted columns marked the direction. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.20}
