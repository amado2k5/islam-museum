## Summary
Glass weights and stamps are stamped glass pieces made in Islamic Egypt; their great advantage is that they can be dated with a precision hardly any other glass object approaches, so Nadine Schibille takes them as a chronological and compositional benchmark for Egyptian [[practice/islamic-glassmaking|glassmaking]], while granting that how far they represent the whole industry is debated. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 23}

## Break and return
The last weight of "Egypt 2" [[concept/natron-glass|natron]] composition dates to the first year of [[person/ahmad-ibn-tulun|Ahmad ibn Tulun]]'s governorship (868), after which comes a hundred-year interlude from which virtually no Egyptian glass weight or stamp is known. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 57} Weights reappear after the Fatimid conquest of al-Fustat in 969, by then made of soda-rich [[concept/plant-ash-glass|plant-ash glass]]. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 57} In the late tenth century Fatimid weights were made of Tyre-type plant-ash glass, the trade in glass now running from the Levant to Egypt. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 233}

## Opaque weights
Schibille analysed 33 fully opaque weights from the second quarter of the twelfth to the first quarter of the thirteenth century and found their white and aqua opacity due to tin oxide, labelling the group "Celadon" for its resemblance to jade-green celadon. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 69} They contain up to 33% lead oxide and 27% tin oxide, and she thinks lead and tin were added together to a pre-formed glass at the working stage. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 69} {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 70} Some polychrome Fatimid weights have yellow and green patches resembling a lead-tin vitreous pigment known as anima. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 69}
