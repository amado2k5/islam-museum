## What this record is
An Arabic word is, for the most part, built on a root of three consonants that is cast into patterns carrying a functional meaning. This record gives what a research summary supplied to the museum says of the ten common verb forms, forms I to X, on the root k-t-b (to write). It serves the science of [[concept/ilm-al-sarf|morphology]], which the books of the heritage call tasrif, and it connects with the nouns derived from these forms in [[concept/al-asma-al-mushtaqqa|Derived nouns]]. The page attributes each form and each page number to the summary as given, because the museum did not open the two books.

## Background: root and pattern
The summary rests on two references: Karin C. Ryding's A Reference Grammar of Modern Standard Arabic (Cambridge University Press, 2005), chapters 22 to 31, pages 455 to 587 {cite:ryding-2005-reference-grammar-msa}, and William Wright's A Grammar of the Arabic Language, third edition, volume 1 (Cambridge University Press, 1896), pages 29 to 45 {cite:wright-grammar-of-the-arabic-language-vol1}. The constant example in the table is the root k-t-b, whose vowels change and to which letters are added before or between, so that the ten forms arise. For each form two patterns are given, the perfect and the imperfect.

## The detail
The summary gives form I as the base, with the meaning "to write", and forms II and IV as causatives: II meaning to make another write or to form squadrons, IV meaning to dictate or make him write. Form III is associative or directed at another, as in kataba, "to correspond with". Form V is the reflexive or passive-like counterpart of II, and VI is reciprocal, "to write to each other". VII and VIII are medio-passives, VIII giving the senses copy, register and subscribe. IX is for colours and physical defects and X for requesting and estimating, as in istaktaba, "to ask to write; to dictate". All this is the summary's statement of the usual meanings, not an exceptionless rule, and it does not say that every root takes every form.

## How the references differ
The page numbers in the last two columns differ because the two books are arranged differently: Ryding gives the verbs chapters 22 to 31 over about a hundred and thirty pages, and Wright gathers them in about seventeen. The numbers are as the summary gave them and the museum did not check them; anyone citing them should go to the books.

## What this page does not reach
The museum did not open Ryding or Wright, did not read the forms in them, and did not read the old Arabic books of morphology. The summary gives no examples from Qur'anic or poetic usage, and no forms beyond the ten. The museum did not check the vowels written in the table, which are carried as given. This record does not decide how the early morphologists named the forms or how some of them counted them.
