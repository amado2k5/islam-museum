## Summary
This page sets out the Arabic keyboards as a research file supplied to the museum summarises them: the ASMO 663 standard of 1987, the Arabic (101) layout in Windows, the Arabic (102) and Arabic (102) AZERTY layouts, the difference between Arabic and Arabic - PC on the Mac, and the phonetic layout. The museum did not open the pages it cites, so everything here is as the summary gives it.
## The ASMO 663 standard
The summary says ASMO 663 (1987) is a standard Arabic keyboard layout set by the Arab Organization for Standardization and Metrology to pair with ASMO 449 (1985), a seven-bit Arabic code corresponding to ISO 9036, and with ASMO 708, which is ISO 8859-6 {cite:researchgate-asmo-663-keyboard-figure} {cite:psu-symbolcodes-glossary}. The standard is thus one link in a chain: an encoding for the letters, then a keyboard to enter them.
## Arabic (101) in Windows
In the summary this is the default Windows and PC layout across Egypt, the Levant and the Gulf, placing the Arabic letters on a QWERTY base, with the short vowels and the shadda on the Shift layer {cite:microsoft-qa-arabic-keyboards} {cite:openarabic-arabic-keyboard-with-harakat}.
## Arabic (102) and Arabic (102) AZERTY
These two keep the Arabic letter positions, with an extra ISO key and different punctuation on the shifted layer, over a European or French AZERTY base, and are widely used in North Africa, according to the summary {cite:microsoft-qa-arabic-keyboards} {cite:psu-symbolcodes-glossary}.
## The Mac: Arabic and Arabic - PC
The summary says Arabic - PC matches the Windows Arabic (101), whereas Apple's native Arabic layout, rooted in ASMO 663, puts several letters, such as dal, dhal, ta marbuta and waw, on different keys {cite:arabictyping101-arabic-101-vs-102} {cite:researchgate-asmo-663-keyboard-figure}.
## The phonetic layout
The Arabic phonetic layout maps each Arabic letter to a Latin key close to it in sound, and aims to help those unused to the standard layout, according to the summary {cite:kromekeycaps-what-is-an-arabic-pc-keyboard}. It recalls from afar what [[concept/arabizi|Arabizi]] did where Arabic keyboards were lacking, though Arabizi is Latin writing with numerals, not a keyboard.
## What the summary says and does not say
The summary does not give the letter maps themselves, does not say which keys change on the Mac, and does not say when each Windows layout arose, so the museum gives no dates the summary did not give. This topic goes with the history of the encoding in [[concept/al-tibaa-wal-ahruf-wa-unicode-al-arabi|printing, type and Unicode]].
## Table of layouts as the summary gives them
| Layout | Characteristics | Region |
|---|---|---|
| ASMO 663 (1987) | A standard layout from the Arab Organization for Standardization and Metrology, paired with ASMO 449 (1985), ISO 9036 and ASMO 708 (ISO 8859-6) | Pan-Arab standard |
| Arabic (101) | Arabic letters on a QWERTY base, vowels and shadda on the Shift layer | Windows in Egypt, the Levant and the Gulf |
| Arabic (102) and AZERTY | The same letter positions with an extra key and different punctuation over a European or AZERTY base | North Africa |
| Mac Arabic and Arabic - PC | The second matches Windows (101); the first, Apple's own, rooted in ASMO 663, puts dal, dhal, ta marbuta and waw in different places | Apple devices |
| Phonetic | Maps an Arabic letter to a Latin letter close in sound | Learners |

The table is the summary's, and its pages were not opened.
## Why there are several layouts
It follows from the summary that the layouts are many because the Latin base under the Arabic letters differs: on the American QWERTY base arises layout (101); on the European base with an extra key, or the French AZERTY, arise (102) and AZERTY; and on Apple machines the native layout kept a different placing of some letters from the ASMO standard. So an Arabic typist can meet two layouts differing in the place of dal or waw while writing the same letters, and learns the letter positions twice on moving from one system to another. In layout (101) the vowel signs stay on the Shift layer, so one who does not know their place can write them only by consulting the layout.
