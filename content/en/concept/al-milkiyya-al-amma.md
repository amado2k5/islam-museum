## Definition
Zaydan Qa'dat defines public ownership as what the state, the nation or the people keep for themselves and place at their disposal to distribute fairly among people {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 153}. For him all land is public property: no individual may take it for himself or drive people off it, and the public authority distributes it fairly so that each may benefit according to his effort {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 153}.

## The origin of public ownership
In his view public ownership arises from a community's presence on a given land and is a gift from God {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 153}. It does not apply to one who seizes land by force, since expulsion and taking by force are rejected in the Qur'an's method {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 153}.

## Its items
For him public ownership covers waters, rivers and seas and what is in them, minerals such as gold, silver and copper, oil and gas, and public utilities such as airports, railway stations and electricity {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 154}.

## Pasture, water and fire
The author holds that pasture, water and fire were common property in the first Islamic state, and that these resources, which should remain guaranteed to all, were later subjected to individual monopoly, which he attributes to favouritism, bribery and injustice {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 154}.

## Public ownership in al-Tahir Qana's al-Dawr al-Tawzi'i li-l-Milkiyya

[[person/al-tahir-qana|Al-Tahir Qana]] makes public ownership one of three kinds of property, alongside [[concept/al-milkiyya-al-khassa|private]] and [[concept/milkiyyat-al-dawla|state]] ownership, and defines it as that whose owner is the whole community, or one of its groups as a group: when the use of a thing is tied to the group's need it may not be privately owned, and for him the community owns the thing itself while the individual owns only its use. {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.73} {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.74} He argues for it from the verse that mosques belong to God, and so to the community of Muslims ([[passage/quran-72-18|al-Jinn 18]]), the verse on the fifth of spoils ([[passage/quran-8-41|al-Anfal 41]]) and the verse on fay' ([[passage/quran-59-7|al-Hashr 7]]), and from Umar's keeping the Sawad of Iraq, Egypt and Syria for those who would come after the conquerors. {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.74} {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.75} From the Sunna he cites the Prophet's division of Khaybar into two halves, one for the Muslims' emergencies; his protection of al-Naqi' for the horses of jihad; the institution of endowment, as in Umar's endowment of his land at Khaybar; the hadith "Muslims are partners in three: water, pasture and fire"; and the report of al-Abyad ibn Hammal, to whom the Prophet granted the salt of Ma'rib and then took it back when told it was like a perennial spring. {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.75} {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.76} {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.77}

Among its domains he counts public utilities such as rivers, roads, pastures and forests; [[concept/hima|protected pasture]]; lands endowed for the Muslims' benefit, such as conquered land left undivided; minerals, following the Maliki view; and what by nature individuals do not own, such as roads, mosques and schools. {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.87} {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.88} Among the means of protecting it are the ban on seizing it, treated as embezzlement from the common stock (ghulul), and the restriction of the ruler to acting in the public interest: rivers and surface minerals may not, in his view, be turned into private holdings, though they may be granted for use (iqta' irfaq) but not as property (iqta' tamlik). {cite:dawr-tawzii-milkiyya-qana-2007 p.88}
