## Summary
Ibn Sina says lands have properties of nature and temperament beyond analogy, so experience is to outweigh analogy; every physique and temperament has a food that suits it, changed only by its opposite; some good foods harm some people, who should give them up; the loose-textured body is fed on moist, quickly digested food; and one with bad humours may be allowed a wider good diet {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.230}.

## Lands and experience
Lands have properties of natures and temperaments that lie beyond analogy, so this is to be kept in mind and experience given priority over analogy; a familiar food in a place may do less harm than a better but unfamiliar one {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.230}.

## Physique and temperament
Every physique and temperament has a fitting, kindred food, and if it is to be changed, it is changed only by the opposite. Some people are harmed by certain good, praised foods and should leave them; one who digests bad foods easily should not be deceived, for bad, disease-making and deadly humours will in time be generated from them {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.230}.

## Humours and porous bodies
One whose body holds bad humours is often permitted to be generous with praised foods, especially if weakness prevents him from bearing purging. A porous body that dissolves easily should be fed on moist, quickly digested food, though porous bodies bear coarse and varied foods better, are less harmed by internal causes and take more harm from external ones {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.230}.
