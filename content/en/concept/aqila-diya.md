## Summary
The 'aqilah are the killer's paternal male relatives—free, adult men of means and capability—called 'asabat, who bear the blood money (diyah) for an unintentional killing even if the killer is wealthy {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 331}. In the author's understanding, diyah carries both the sense of criminal penalty and financial compensation, since it goes not to the public treasury (bayt al-mal) but to the victim's estate {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 331}.

## Order of Liability When Absent
If the killer has no 'aqilah but has a patron by compact (mawla muwalah), the diyah falls upon him according to the Hanafis; if he has none, it falls upon the members of the registry (ahl al-diwan), a form of solidarity among members of the same profession; and if none exists, it falls upon the public treasury (bayt al-mal), for the public treasury is the 'aqilah of whoever has no 'aqilah and the heir of whoever has no heir {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 331}. The footnote refers to Ibn Taymiyyah regarding the members of the registry {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 331}.

## Amount of Diyah and Its Installments
The diyah is one hundred camels, or one thousand mithqals of gold, or twelve thousand dirhams, as assessed in the time of the Prophet, and is paid in installments over three years {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 331}. The author cites the hadith "For a life there are one hundred camels," narrated by Malik and al-Nasa'i {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 331}. The verse on unintentional killing in Surah al-Nisa' is cited here by number [[passage/quran-4-92|al-Nisa' 92]].

## Qasamah
If the killer is unknown, the victim's next of kin select fifty men from the place where the slain person was found, who swear that they do not know the killer and are not sheltering him; once they swear, the Lawgiver rules that the victim's diyah be paid from the public treasury (bayt al-mal) {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 332}.
