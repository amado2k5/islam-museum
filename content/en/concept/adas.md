## Nature
There is an edible kind of lentil, the well-known one, and a wild kind that is bad. The bitter lentil is evidently hot, with some dryness and a little astringency; according to Dioscorides it is a tall herb with many branches and tall rods, its leaf longer and narrower than that of the quince, somewhat rough and whitish, sown much in the mountains of Tabaristan, where they call it by the name of lentil and link it to the snake; its seed is like a small lentil, in long pods {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.617}

## Selection
The best is the one that ripens soonest, the white broad kind, which does not blacken the water it falls into, and it must be cooked until thoroughly done {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.618}

## Temperament
Galen is quoted that it is either moderate in heat and dryness or inclined slightly to heat, and so it does not cool when it is eaten, nor while in the stomach, nor as it passes down {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.618}

## Actions and properties
It causes wind and is composed of an astringent and a cleansing power; it shows bad dreams. Its husk is very astringent, and as a whole it has much wind; it thickens the blood so that it does not run in the vessels, and for that reason lessens urine, and it engenders a black-bile humour and diseases of black bile. Barley kashk may be its counter, since mixing the two gives a very good food; it is also better as food with chard, the two being opposed and moderate in their states, and barley and pennyroyal are added to it; the worst of it is what is cooked with salted meat {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.618}

## Swellings and pustules
Cooked in vinegar and used as a poultice it dissolves scrofulous swellings and hard swellings, and with its repelling power it gathers pus; taking much of it engenders cancer and the hard swellings called scirrhus {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.618}

## Wounds and ulcers
Cooked in vinegar it fills deep ulcers and removes the foulness of ulcers so that their filth lessens, and for large ones it is used with something more astringent such as pomegranate rind; with seawater it is used for the eating sore, erysipelas, herpes and the cracks caused by cold {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.618}

## Joints
Bad for the nerves; laid with parched barley meal as a poultice on gout it benefits, and taking much of it brings on leprosy {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.618}

## Ocular organs
Whoever eats much of it has his sight darkened by its strong drying, and when it is applied as a poultice with melilot, quince and rose oil it cures very hot swellings of the eye {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.618}

## Organs of the chest
Cooked in seawater it is applied as a poultice to swellings of the breast arising from a pooling of blood and milk {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.618}

## Digestion and the organs of nourishment
It is hard to digest, bad for the stomach, causing wind and heavy; it is said that when some of the grains are peeled and swallowed they benefit laxity of the stomach. Sweetness should not be mixed with lentils, for then it brings many obstructions in the liver, and among the rumours about it is that it is of use in dropsy, which seems to be because of its drying {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.618}

## Excretory organs
Cooked without its husk it restrains the bowels; cooked with its husk and the first water poured off, it does so too, and the first water loosens the bowels. What is cooked with the husk and its water poured off restrains the bowels more than the peeled, because of the strong astringency in the husk; its restraining grows stronger when it is cooked with chicory, plantain and purslane, or with rose or some astringent after being well boiled beforehand, otherwise it stirs the bowels. It is applied as a poultice with melilot, quince and rose oil to a swelling of the anus. The wild lentil, which is the bitter lentil, opens the bowels to blood and brings down and promotes urine, and used with vinegar it benefits difficulty in urinating and quiets dysentery and colic; the edible lentil lessens urine by thickening the blood, so one with a disorder of the urine should not go near it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.619}

## Womb, menses and desire (labelled book listing, held for the founder's ruling)
The book lists the edible lentil as lessening the menses by thickening the blood, and the wild, bitter lentil as bringing down and promoting the menses {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.618-619}
