## Summary
Ibn Sina opens the first group of the Canon's anatomy of the simple organs, in Book I, with the bones, which he treats in thirty chapters; the first is a general discourse on bones and joints {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.42}. He holds that the bones as a whole are the prop and the frame of the body, and that some are the foundation on which the body is built, such as the spinal vertebrae, some are what repels a blow or an injury, such as the spines, and some are filling between the gaps of the joints {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.42}.

## Kinds of bone and their uses
The text sorts bones by their use. Some stand to the body as its foundation, like the vertebrae of the back. Others, called the spines (sanasin), lie on the vertebrae like thorns and turn aside what strikes. Others fill the gaps in joints, such as the sesamoid bones; and one bone, shaped like the letter lam, serves the muscles of the larynx and the tongue {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.42}.

## Solid bone and hollow bone
A bone needed only as a prop and a shield, and not to move the limbs, was made solid, though it has the pores and gaps it cannot do without. A bone also needed for movement was given a larger cavity, and a single cavity in the middle, so that its substance does not require scattered places for nourishment, which would make it soft. It stays hard and its nourishment, the marrow, is gathered inside it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.42}. Ibn Sina lists the reasons: a larger cavity makes the bone lighter; one cavity keeps the substance harder; hardness keeps it from breaking in violent movement; and the marrow feeds and moistens it so that it does not crumble from the dryness of movement. The cavity is smaller where firmness is needed more and larger where lightness is needed more {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.42}.

## How bones lie together
All the bones lie next to one another, with little distance between a bone and the next. In some places a small gap is filled by cartilage or cartilage-like appendages; where that function is not needed the joint is made without an appendage, as in the lower jaw {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.42}. The kinds of contact are set out in the entry on [[concept/joints|joints]].

## What does not grow back
On the page before this chapter Ibn Sina says that parts formed from the seed, such as bone and nerve, do not grow back when a piece is lost, whereas flesh, formed from blood, grows back and joins its like. He allows an exception for the tooth in childhood, since the time of the seed is still near, so it may grow again {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.40}.
