## Summary
Ibn Sina makes touch the first genus of signs of temperament: it is measured against the touch of a healthy-tempered person in moderate lands and air, with a caution against letting heat or cold mislead the judgement of softness and hardness {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.158-159}.

## The method
The physician considers whether the touch equals that of a healthy person in temperate lands and air; if it does, it shows balance. If a healthy-tempered toucher is chilled or warmed by it, or finds it softer or harder and rougher than natural, and there is no cause from air or bathing that adds softness or roughness, the temperament is not balanced. The state of the nails of the hands, in softness, roughness and dryness, also shows the body's temperament unless a foreign cause explains it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.158}.

## Guarding against illusion
Judging by softness and hardness depends on first having read heat and cold correctly: a hot body may soften what is hard and rough by dissolving it and so seem soft and moist by nature, and a cold one may harden what is soft by freezing and condensing it and so seem dry, like ice and fat. Most cold-tempered people are soft in body, even if lean, because rawness abounds in them {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.158-159}.
