## Overview
Tatayyur is taking an omen of ill luck, the omen itself being tiyara; the pre-Islamic Arabs did it, and Islam abolished it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5346}

## Origin
An Arab who set out on an errand would startle a bird in its nest; if it flew to the right he went on, calling it the right-flier, and if to the left he turned back, calling it the left-flier. Islam abolished this and returned the matter to God's fixed ways and decree. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5347}

## Ruling
Whoever believes that what he sees of a bird's state causes or affects the outcome has committed unbelief, as it is sharing in God's management. Whoever knows that God alone manages but feels some dread of evil and seeks refuge in God and goes on in trust is not harmed; if he settles his mind on it, he has done wrong. The Prophet told Muawiya ibn al-Hakam that such things are felt in the breast and should not turn them back. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5347}

## Good omen
A good omen is lawful; it is hope in God, while tiyara is ill opinion of God, and the believer is ordered to think well of God. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5347} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5348}

## Reference
The detail is in the entry on evil omen (shu'm). {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.5348}

## Tatayyur in the speaker's account
The speaker describes the omens the Arabs took from birds before Islam, by the direction in which the bird flew, and says that Islam replaced them with the prayer of istikhara. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-naba-al-safi/ep10 00:04:30}

## The speaker's view: superstition and the renewal of the nation
The speaker says that Muslims should stop laughing at people and give up the superstitions that hold the nation back, while other nations build their societies and cure their diseases; religion, he says, addresses enlightened minds. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-naba-al-safi/ep01 00:31:00} He says that the nation's renewal is not achieved by sorcery, by asking about someone's mother, or by claims that an uncle of someone envies him, and that the Companions did not resort to any of these. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-naba-al-safi/ep01 00:33:00}

## From Omar Abdelkafy's series on the Hikam of Ibn Ata Allah
Omar Abdelkafy recalls the pre-Islamic custom of the sanih and the barih: when a Bedouin set out on a journey he would startle the first bird he met, taking it as a good sign if it flew to the right and a bad one if it flew to the left. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep01 00:15:30} He counts the taking of ill omens from the crow and from other signs as remnants of it, and warns against them. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep01 00:16:00} He holds that Islam put the sunna of [[practice/istikhara|istikhara]] in its place. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep01 00:16:30}

## From Omar Abdelkafy's programme Bustan al-Amal
The Sheikh explains the Arabs' sanih and barih: a traveller would scare up the first bird he saw; if it flew from right to left he took it as a good omen and travelled, and if from the left he took it as a bad omen and turned back; they took the crow and the hoopoe as bad omens, the latter because its name recalls demolition; he holds that the Prophet taught Muslims the prayer of seeking guidance instead, and that Islam forbade nothing without providing an alternative. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy bustan-el-amal/ep29 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy bustan-el-amal/ep29 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy bustan-el-amal/ep29 00:04:30} See: [[practice/istikhara|istikhara]].
