## What the Qur'an says
The Qur'an names Iram once in everything that has come down to us. In [[passage/quran-89-6|al-Fajr 6]] it asks whether the Prophet has not seen what his Lord did to 'Ad; [[passage/quran-89-7|al-Fajr 7]] then gives the name Iram, with the description of the pillars; and [[passage/quran-89-8|al-Fajr 8]] says that the like of it was not created in the lands. It does not say where it was, nor whether it is a land or a people. The grammatical ambiguity of the sentence is the root of the disagreement: is Iram in apposition to 'Ad, or a description of something else? And what does the qualifier in the eighth verse refer back to?

## The grammatical readings
Al-Qurtubi reports that the common reading has 'Ad with tanwin followed by Iram uninflected, making Iram the name of the tribe, in apposition to 'Ad; that al-Hasan and Abu al-'Aliya read it in the genitive annexation, 'Ad of Iram, so that Iram is the name of their mother or their city and the sense is the people of Iram; that it was also read with Iram annexed to dhat al-'imad; that Mujahid, al-Dahhak and Qatada read the hamza with a fatha, which Mujahid explained as likening them to the "arams", the waymarks; and that it was read in the sense that God made them crumbled bones. {cite:tafsir-qurtubi-quran-com on 89:6}

## What al-Tabari says
On al-Fajr 7 al-Tabari gives the views and their holders: Iram is a city, Alexandria on al-Qurazi's authority and Damascus on that of Sa'id al-Maqburi; or a nation, Mujahid; or the ancient, again Mujahid; or a tribe of 'Ad and the house of their kingship, Qatada and Ibn Ishaq, who makes 'Ad the son of Iram ibn 'Aws ibn Sam; or the destroyed, Ibn 'Abbas and al-Dahhak. He then judges that Iram is either a town where 'Ad lived or the name of a tribe, and, on the strength of the reading and of its being left uninflected, prefers that it is the name of a tribe of 'Ad, as Qatada said; he rejects Mujahid's "ancient" because, were it a description, it would carry tanwin. On dhat al-'imad he quotes: tallness, Ibn 'Abbas and Mujahid; pole-dwellers who do not stay put, that is, nomads, Mujahid and Qatada; a building that some of them built, Ibn Zayd; strength and might, al-Dahhak; and he chooses that they were nomads of the tent-pole, because what Arabic means by 'imad is what is used for tents, and no building of theirs on pillars is known by a sound report. {cite:tafsir-tabari-quran-com on 89:7}

## What Ibn Kathir says
Ibn Kathir takes Iram as an apposition to 'Ad, meaning the first 'Ad, the sons of 'Ad ibn Iram ibn 'Aws ibn Sam ibn Nuh on Ibn Ishaq's authority; they are called of the pillars because they lived in hair tents raised on strong poles, and were the mightiest of their time in build and strength. He reports Mujahid that Iram is an ancient nation, and Qatada and al-Suddi that it is the house of 'Ad's kingship, calling this view good, excellent and strong. {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir on 89:7} On the eighth verse he sides with Qatada and Ibn Jarir that the pronoun in the words about its like returns to the tribe, that is, none like that tribe was created in their lands in their time, and he calls Ibn Zayd's view, which returns it to the pillars as buildings raised in al-Ahqaf, weak. {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir on 89:8} He objects to those who say Iram is a city, whether Damascus, as reported from Sa'id ibn al-Musayyab and 'Ikrima, or Alexandria, as reported from al-Qurazi, since the sentence does not hold together if Iram is made an apposition on that reading. And he warns against being misled by what a group of exegetes say of a city called Iram of the pillars built of gold and silver bricks, its pebbles pearls and its soil musk, which moves between al-Sham, Yemen and Iraq; all of this, he says, is among the Israelite fables, set down by some of their heretics to test the minds of the ignorant. {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir on 89:8} Of the Bedouin 'Abd Allah ibn Qilaba who, in Mu'awiya's time, is said to have gone out after his lost camels, found a great city, and returned to tell people, only for them to find nothing, Ibn Kathir says that the story's chain is not sound and that, even if it were sound to that Bedouin, he may have invented it or been seized by a kind of delusion. {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir on 89:8} In al-Bidaya wa-l-Nihaya he says that whoever claims that Iram is a city moving about in the earth, now in al-Sham, now in Yemen, now in the Hijaz, has strayed far and says what has no proof. {cite:al-bidaya-wal-nihaya on 'Ad}

## What al-Qurtubi says
Al-Qurtubi gathers many views on al-Fajr 7: Iram is Sam ibn Nuh, on Ibn Ishaq; or Iram the father of 'Ad, from 'Ata through Ibn 'Abbas; or a nation, Mujahid; or a tribe of 'Ad, Qatada; or there are two 'Ads and the first is Iram; and Ma'mar thinks it is where 'Ad and Thamud gather. Khalid al-Rab'i says it is Damascus, the view of 'Ikrima and Sa'id al-Maqburi through Malik, and al-Qurazi says Alexandria. He reports from Malik that a book was found at Alexandria saying "I am Shaddad ibn 'Ad, who raised the pillars", then gives Ibn al-'Arabi's choice of Damascus and a book from Thawr ibn Yazid in which Shaddad says he buried a treasure that only the community of Muhammad could bring out. On the eighth verse he relates that 'Ad had two sons, Shaddad and Shadid, that Shaddad built Iram in the deserts of Aden over three hundred years, its palaces of gold and silver and its columns of chrysolite and sapphire, and that when he was a day and a night's journey away a cry from heaven seized them; he relays the story of Ibn Qilaba and Ka'b, who said it was Iram of the pillars and that a man of the Muslims in Mu'awiya's time with such and such marks would enter it, then turned and saw Ibn Qilaba and said this is that man. He then says that the first, that the pronoun returns to the tribe, is the clearer and is held by most. {cite:tafsir-qurtubi-quran-com on 89:7} {cite:tafsir-qurtubi-quran-com on 89:8}

## Al-Mas'udi and Ibn Khaldun
Al-Mas'udi says the 'Adite Jayrun ibn Sa'd settled Damascus, gathered marble columns to it and called it Iram of the pillars; that in 332 AH there was in Damascus at the gate of the great mosque a market called Jayrun on that spot; and that a different account on Iram is reported from Ka'b al-Ahbar. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab OpenITI text vol. 1 p. 221} Elsewhere he gives Ka'b's report to Mu'awiya, a city of gold, silver, musk and saffron which an Arab looking for two lost camels enters, and says that if the report from Ka'b is true about this city it is a fine thing, but it is a report into which corruption enters from several directions of transmission and the like, and it is the craft of the storytellers; he adds that people disputed over this city and where it is. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab OpenITI text vol. 1 p. 270} Ibn Khaldun counts the exegetes' account, that Shaddad built Iram in the deserts of Aden, set down by al-Tabari, al-Tha'labi and al-Zamakhshari and others, and the story of Ibn Qilaba, whom he calls a Companion, and Ka'b, among the most far-fetched errors, because nothing has been heard of such a city since in any part of the earth: the deserts of Aden lie in the middle of Yemen, whose settlement has been continuous and whose roads the guides describe from every direction, and if they had said that the city had disappeared with the ruins it would be likelier. He thinks what drove them to this was the grammar of dhat al-'imad as a description of Iram, so they took 'imad as pillars and so it had to be a building; he mentions Ibn al-Zubayr's annexed reading, which he explains as like saying Quraysh Kinana, and says the book of God is to be kept clear of such weak tales. {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun on the errors of historians}

:::perspectives
- **Sunni view, al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir and Qatada:** Iram is a tribe or an ancestor of 'Ad, and dhat al-'imad describes them as people of the tent-pole or of strength and height; the verses report the destruction of a tribe, not a city. {cite:tafsir-tabari-quran-com on 89:7} {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir on 89:8}
- **Another Sunni view, Sa'id al-Maqburi, 'Ikrima and Khalid al-Rab'i as al-Tabari and al-Qurtubi give them, and al-Qurazi:** Iram is a city, Damascus for the first three and Alexandria for al-Qurazi; Ibn al-'Arabi chose Damascus as al-Qurtubi relays. {cite:tafsir-tabari-quran-com on 89:7} {cite:tafsir-qurtubi-quran-com on 89:7}
- **A third strand in the Sunni tradition, the golden city of Shaddad and the Ka'b and Ibn Qilaba story:** al-Qurtubi relays it without criticism; Ibn Kathir says the chain of the Ibn Qilaba story is not sound and that all this belongs to the Israelite fables; al-Mas'udi calls the Ka'b report the storytellers' craft; Ibn Khaldun finds it closer to fables. {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir on 89:8} {cite:muruj-al-dhahab OpenITI text vol. 1 p. 270} {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun on the errors of historians}
- **Academic view, the Encyclopaedia of Islam:** W. Montgomery Watt's article says that Iram occurs in old poetry as a tribe, mostly coupled with 'Ad and sometimes with Thamud and Himyar; that Muslim scholars linking Arab genealogies to the Bible made Iram into Aram son of Shem; that the likeliest view is that Iram is the name of a tribe in apposition to 'Ad and that 'imad means the tent-pole or tallness, Iram perhaps a subdivision of 'Ad as al-Tabari suggests; that later scholars preferred a town; and that the later stories grow more elaborate, a city built by Shaddad near Aden in imitation of Paradise, destroyed by a tornado and buried in sand. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1304}
:::

## Iram in modern scholarship
Paul Neuenkirchen treated the question in an article in Arabica in 2013, titled in English Biblical Elements in Koran 89, 6-8 and Its Exegeses: A New Interpretation of "Iram of the Pillars". His abstract says that the meaning of the expression was long debated among the exegetes, that the ambiguity of the passage has seemingly led to many myths in classical Arabic literature and in modern Western writing, and that he gives a critical overview of the exegeses and of the history of the "Iram myth", then suggests by a Biblical and Midrashic comparison his own account of what was the original, forgotten meaning. {cite:neuenkirchen-arabica-2013-iram} I did not read the article itself, so I do not report its result. A chapter titled Southern Arabian Desert Trade Routes, Frankincense, Myrrh, and the Ubar Legend, by Blom, Crippen, Elachi, Clapp, Hedges and Zarins, appears in the book Remote Sensing in Archaeology; I read only its title and authors, so I attribute to it no site identification, and I neither assert nor deny that Iram is Ubar. {cite:blom-et-al-ubar-legend-remote-sensing}

## What the text does not say
The Qur'an does not say that Iram had a golden city, that it was buried in sand, or that it survives and moves; all of that is reported from Ka'b and others, and the major exegetes and historians count it among Israelite lore or the storytellers' tales. I found nothing in what I read that fixes a location for Iram or 'Ad on the ground on the basis of published excavation, so this record takes no stand on an identification with Ubar or any other site. The place record [[place/iram-dhat-al-imad|Iram dhat al-'imad]] carries the accounts of al-Mas'udi and Ibn Khaldun and approximate coordinates recorded at a geocoding stage, which are not a verification of a site.
