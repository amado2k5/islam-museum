## Nature and selection
Nature: a large tree found in some countries. Oil is pressed from unripe olives and also from ripe ones. The oil of unripe olives is called zayt al-anfaq, and oil is also pressed from a red olive midway between unripe and ripe, whose action is between the two. Oil comes from cultivated and from wild olives. Old oil in poultices has the strength of castor oil and of radish and nigella oil, but it is hotter and close in action to them. When olive branches and leaves are to be burnt, they should be smeared with honey. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.469}
Selection: the best oil for the healthy is zayt al-anfaq, and the best gum of the wild olive is the kind that stings the tongue. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.469}

## Temperament
Oil of unripe olives is cold and dry in the first degree; according to Rufus it has some moisture. Oil of ripe olives is hot in moderation and leans to moisture; when washed it is moderate in moisture and dryness and less hot. Ripe olive is hot and its oil leans to moisture; the unripe olive is moderately cold, and its wood and leaves are cold. When oil of unripe olives is very old it takes on the nature of sweet olive oil. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.469}

## Actions
All kinds of oil strengthen the body, stir movement and cleanse. Salted olive water is stronger than salt water at cleansing. Old oil does not reach the sharpness of the sting, and the olive gives little nourishment. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.469}

## Uses by organ
Adornment: wild olive leaves are good for the ringworm-like patch the book calls dahs, and they stop sweating. Wild olive oil keeps the hair and, used daily, delays greying. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.469}
Swellings and pustules: the wild kind dissolves redness, ant-like and nettle-like pustules and hot swellings; the liquid that runs from its wood when it burns is used for scabies and herpes; the dregs of the oil are a remedy for hot swellings in the glands, especially with the leaf. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.469}
Wounds and ulcers: oil of wild olives pressed from the unripe fruit helps moist and dry ulcers and scabies, especially in a steep of lupin. Wild olive leaves help redness, spreading and malignant ulcers, dirty ulcers and nettle-rash. Olive brine applied to burns from fire stops them from spreading and cleans foul ulcers. The gum of the wild olive helps ulcerating scabies and the sores called qawabi, and it enters salves for wounds. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.469}
Joints: brine of salted olives is injected for sciatica; washed oil suits pains of the sciatic and related nerve; old oil helps those with gout when they are anointed with it. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.470}
Organs of the head: olive leaves boiled with unripe-grape juice until they become like honey, and smeared on decayed teeth, pull them out. Wild olive oil is like rose oil for headache. Wild olive sap is dried, shaped into pastilles, and kept for a discharge from the ear. Wild olive oil used as a gargle helps bleeding gums and firms loose teeth. The gum of the wild olive, packed into a decayed tooth, helps toothache. A compound called zayt al-aqarib is among the best remedies for earache when dropped in. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.470}
Organs of the eye: old oil is used as a collyrium for dimness of the eye; its dregs enter eye remedies; the burnt leaf replaces tutia for the eye; the gum helps cataract, whiteness and thickening of the cornea; the juice of the leaf helps protrusion of the eye and ulcers and accidents of the cornea. The cultivated olive suits the eye better than the wild, and its gum clears the eye and the filth of its ulcers. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.470}
Organs of the chest: black olive with its stone is among the fumigations for asthma and diseases of the lung. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.470}
Organs of digestion: the dregs of the oil are laid on the belly of a dropsy patient. Olive as it is causes difficult digestion; salted thick olive stirs appetite, strengthens the stomach and produces a binding chyme; the dissolving olive is the easiest of all for digestion and the quickest. Oil of unripe olives is good for the stomach. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.470}
Excretory organs: eaten with preserve before a meal it softens the bowels; taken in nine ounces with hot water or barley water it purges; it is boiled with rue for gripes and worms; it helps the swelling colic, and it is given by enema for the dregs-type colic. It is given with barley flour for chronic diarrhoea, and the matured old oil with the juice of unripe grapes helps when given by enema for ulcers of the inner rectum. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.470}
Womb, menses and foetus (a labelled listing, not advice; for the founder's ruling): the juice of the olive leaf is used for flow and bleeding of the womb, and its gum brings the menses and expels the foetus. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.470}
Poisons: oil is drunk with hot water to make a person vomit, and this breaks the strength of poison. {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.470}
