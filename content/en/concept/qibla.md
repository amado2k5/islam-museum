## Overview
The qibla is the direction of the Kaaba which Muslims face in prayer — among the clearest markers of unity in the Islamic world. In the early Medinan period the Prophet and his companions prayed toward Bayt al-Maqdis , Jerusalem,; the turn to the Kaaba came in the verses of Surat al-Baqara {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir}.

The Encyclopaedia of Islam defines it as the direction of Mecca, or more exactly of the [[building/kaaba|Kaaba]], or of the point between its water-spout and its western corner, towards which the worshipper must turn for prayer. Its article treats the subject in two parts, one on ritual and legal aspects and one on the astronomical methods by which medieval Muslim scholars worked out the direction for any given place. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 100}

## Direction in worship before Islam
The article notes that among the Semitic peoples the direction of prayer was from very early times not a matter of free choice. It points to an allusion in the First Book of Kings and to the account of Daniel praying three times a day towards [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]], which has remained the Jewish direction ever since. The names of the quarters of the sky show a life oriented towards the east, the Essenes prayed towards the rising sun, and the Syriac Christians also faced east. From this the article finds it quite possible, in agreement with the tradition, that [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] appointed a qibla at the same time as he instituted the prayer. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 100}

## The change of direction in Medina
The article holds it certain that in the period just after the [[event/hijra|hijra]] the Muslims used the direction already used by the Jews. Tradition places the change of qibla sixteen or seventeen months after the hijra, in Rajab or Sha'ban of the second year, and the author judges this probably right, because that is the time of an important shift in Muhammad's attitude to the Jews. In the author's account, disappointed by the slight success of his preaching among the Jews of Yathrib, he turned increasingly to the old Arabian tradition and made the religion of [[person/ibrahim|Ibrahim]] the basis of all monotheistic religions; the Kaaba was brought forward as a religious centre, the pilgrimage began to be spoken of as a Muslim rite, and the expulsion of the Jewish tribes of Yathrib was begun. The article treats the change of qibla as a not unimportant part of this series of events. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 100}

It reads the verses of the second sura, beginning at the verse numbered 136 in its edition, as referring to the change, and takes from them the importance Muhammad himself gave to it. It does not accept the tradition that the change was brought on by scornful remarks of the Jews about his dependence on their rules, a version the article associates with al-Tabari. Other traditions present the new qibla as that of Ibrahim, and the author sees in this a glimmering of the real matter, namely the link with Muhammad's new political and religious attitude. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 100}

Reports differ on the moment of the revelation. According to one tradition, from al-Bukhari, the verses were announced to the believers during the morning prayer at [[place/quba|Quba]]. According to another, given by al-Baydawi, Muhammad had prayed two rak'as of the noon prayer with part of the community in a mosque of the [[group/banu-salima|Banu Salima]] when he turned towards Mecca, and the mosque received the name mosque of the two qiblas, [[building/masjid-al-qiblatayn|Masjid al-Qiblatayn]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 100}

## The direction before the hijra
Granting that Muhammad and his community faced Jerusalem in prayer during the early years of the hijra, the article asks what his qibla was before it. Tradition gives two answers and a third reached by harmonising them. In the first, he faced the Kaaba in Mecca; in the second, the qibla had always been Jerusalem; in the third, taken from Ibn Hisham, he took care in Mecca to keep the Kaaba and Jerusalem in one line in front of him. The article sees the first view as shaped by the theory of the religion of Ibrahim, since al-Tibrizi, in the Hamasa, has even 'Abd al-Muttalib already knowing that Ibrahim appointed the Kaaba. It finds the second the most probable, reasoning that tradition does not like to admit Muhammad's dependence on Jewish practice, so the view would be hard to explain had it no historical basis. It adds that [[person/al-bara-ibn-marur|al-Bara' ibn Ma'rur]] is remembered for refusing, even before the hijra, to turn his back on the Kaaba, a report that would lose its point if the old qibla had lain in that direction. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 100}

Modern scholars have put forward other views. Tor Andrae held that the original qibla was to the east, basing this not on tradition but on the general agreement between early Muslim and Christian usages. Schwally suggested that the Jerusalem qibla was introduced into Mecca, perhaps as a Jewish-Christian institution. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 100}

## Where the qibla is observed
The article lists the occasions in the hadith on which the direction is taken. Besides the prayer itself, with the points of the toes also turned that way, it is observed in supplication and after the stone-throwing at the central station in the pilgrimage rites. The head of an animal to be slaughtered is turned to the qibla, and the dead are buried facing Mecca. The hadith forbids facing Mecca while relieving nature; whether it is allowed to turn one's back to it, and so in parts of Arabia to face Jerusalem, is a matter on which there is no unanimity, and one should not spit in its direction. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 100}

Old traditions name keeping a qibla, together with performing the prayer and slaughtering in the Muslim manner, as a mark of the Muslim. One term for the community of the orthodox is [[concept/ahl-al-qibla|ahl al-qibla]] wa 'l-jama'a. In many Muslim lands the word itself became the name of a point of the compass according to where Mecca lies, so that it means south in Egypt and Palestine and east in the Maghrib. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 100}

## In fiqh and creed
Facing the qibla is a condition of valid prayer in fiqh, and Fiqh al-Sunna details the rulings for one unable to determine it and the acts to which facing extends {cite:sabiq-fiqh-al-sunnah}. The Tahawiyya creed names the Muslims 'the people of our qibla' {cite:aqida-tahawiyya §73}, making the qibla a banner of the community's identity itself.

## The rules in the Shafi'i school
The article gives the legal rules only briefly, following the Shafi'i school as set out in al-Shirazi's Kitab al-Tanbih. Taking a qibla is a necessary condition for the validity of the prayer. It may be neglected only in great danger and in a voluntary prayer on a journey, but a person on foot, or able to turn his mount, should face it at the opening of the prayer, the bowing and the prostration. One should face exactly towards the qibla, and a person near it can do so with certainty. According to others, a person at a distance need only face the general direction, called jiha. Outside Mecca one turns towards the [[concept/mihrab|mihrab]] inside a mosque; away from a mosque one follows the direction given by reliable people, and only a man in a deserted region may work it out for himself from certain indications. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 101}

## Marking the direction
In mosques the mihrab shows the direction. The article observes that the word does not occur in the classical hadith, where qibla is used for the wall of the mosque towards which one turns. For prayer outside a mosque a [[concept/sutrah|sutra]] marks the direction, and in Egypt small compasses made for the purpose were used to find the qibla. The article notes that many mosques are oriented only approximately, according to the jiha, and that the error has sometimes been corrected later by lines or stretched threads, as in many mosques of Indonesia where worshippers take their direction from such marks rather than from the mihrab. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 101}

## The astronomical problem
The second part of the article explains that finding the qibla was an important problem for the scientists of medieval Islam. It belongs to mathematical geography but can also be treated as a problem of spherical astronomy, and so most Islamic astronomical handbooks, the zijes, of which close to 200 were compiled in the thousand years from 750, contain a chapter on it, as do several dozen manuals on timekeeping. Treatises devoted only to the qibla are few. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 101}

The qibla at a place depends on its latitude, the latitude of Mecca and the difference in longitude from Mecca. The article calls the derivation from these three quantities the most complicated of the standard problems of medieval spherical astronomy, and sees in the solutions a record of how mathematical methods developed between the ninth and fourteenth centuries. In medieval Arabic the angle between the local meridian and the line to Mecca was called inhiraf al-qibla, and the complementary angle measured from the east-west line was samt al-qibla. The problem is mathematically the same as finding the azimuth of a celestial body, and some methods say simply that a person facing the sun, on the day and at the hour of the right declination and hour angle, is facing Mecca. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 101} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 102}

## Methods of the astronomers
Already in the ninth century Muslim scholars had derived exact solutions, either by the analemma, a Greek construction that folds the relevant planes into one working plane, or by the theorem of Menelaos. Later methods used projection or simpler corollaries of that theorem. Some astronomers were content with approximations adequate for practice. The article describes a popular approximate construction found in the zij of [[person/al-battani|al-Battani]] and in simpler works, a table-based approach linked to a treatise related to [[person/al-khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]] that survives in several manuscripts of Syrian, Yemeni and Turkish origin, and further approximate tables by Persian astronomers of the fourteenth century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 101} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 102}

Of the exact solutions the article outlines four, and presents al-Khalili's table as perhaps the most sophisticated trigonometric table of the medieval period. [[person/habash-al-hasib-al-marwazi|Habash al-Hasib]] used an analemma construction, and a formula equivalent to the modern one can be derived from the analemma proposed by [[person/ibn-al-haytham|Ibn al-Haytham]]. [[person/ali-ibn-yunus-al-munajjim|Ibn Yunus]] of Cairo offered a trigonometric solution without justification, which the article shows to follow from Habash's figure, and he also compiled a table of the sun's altitude in Cairo when it stands in the direction of Mecca. Tables of this kind belonged to the main timekeeping tables used in Cairo, Damascus, Jerusalem and Istanbul, and one was made for the observatory at [[place/maragha|Maragha]]. The solar altitude in the direction of Mecca is sometimes also shown on the backs of astrolabes. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 103}

[[person/al-nayrizi|Al-Nayrizi]] solved the problem by four successive uses of the theorem of Menelaos. [[person/al-biruni-muhammad-ibn-ahmad|Al-Biruni]] proposed several methods: in his work on mathematical geography he derived the longitude difference between Mecca and [[place/ghazna|Ghazna]] from distances between stages on the caravan routes and then found the qibla at Ghazna in four ways. In his major astronomical work, the Mas'udi canon, he gave a further solution which the article calls more elegant than his solution by spherical trigonometry and which he proved correct by that means. The article takes his work to show the progress in spherical trigonometry during the tenth century, since al-Nayrizi had laboured with the theorem of Menelaos where al-Biruni used its simpler corollaries. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 103} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 104}

## Tables and the question of accuracy
[[person/shams-al-din-al-khalili|Al-Khalili]] of Damascus, active around 766/1365, compiled a table based on an accurate formula, giving the qibla for each degree of latitude from 10° to 56° and each degree of longitude difference from 1° to 60°, so almost 3,000 entries computed to degrees and minutes. The article notes that nearly all entries are correct or out by one or two minutes, which it calls remarkable. He did not describe his method; the article suggests he may have followed a method of al-Marrakushi of Cairo, of which he expressed approval, and he also computed the qibla for 44 localities in Palestine, Syria and Iraq. Other qibla lists were compiled by medieval astronomers, and the geographical tables of late zijes often show the qibla beside the coordinates of each place. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 105}

A scholar might know an exact formula, but the result still depended on the geographical data he had. Medieval longitudes, derived from distances, were generally not very accurate, whereas latitudes from observations of the sun's altitude were generally better. Even so, the most popular values for the latitude of Mecca were 21°, 21° 30' and 21° 40', where the accurate figure is 21° 26'. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 106}

## Why mosques differ in orientation
The article says some mosques may be wrongly aligned even though their mihrabs were set in a direction computed by competent mathematicians, and that others were never computed from geographical data at all but were inspired by tradition. Mosques in the Maghrib and the Indian subcontinent generally face due east or due west respectively. In early Muslim Egypt the qibla adopted was the direction of sunrise at the winter solstice, which was favoured as the qibla of the Companions, and several mosques in Cairo face it; it lies about 10° off the qibla computed with medieval coordinates and about 20° off the true qibla for Cairo. The article adds that no survey of the orientation of medieval mosques has yet been made, and that one would interest the history of Islamic architecture as well as of science. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 106}
