## Summary
Ibn Sina denies that the art of preserving health can guarantee safety from death, from outside afflictions or a full span of life, and limits what it guarantees to two things: preventing putrefaction at the outset, and protecting the moisture so that dissolving does not hurry to it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.201-202}.

## No guarantee of immortality
Food is worked on by the faculty and used only to a limit; the art of preserving health is not an art that guarantees safety from death, rescues the body from outside afflictions, or brings every body to the longest life a person might wish for {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.201}.

## What it does guarantee
It guarantees two things: preventing putrefaction altogether, and protecting the moisture so that dissolving does not hasten to it, so that it can last for the time its first temperament calls for. This is done by sound management in replacing in the body, as far as possible, what dissolves {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.201}. That means management that stops causes which hasten drying from overcoming the causes that are due, and management that guards against putrefaction arising and protects the body from foreign heat, outside or inside {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.202}.

## Each body has its limit
Bodies are not equal in the strength of original moisture and original heat. Each has its own limit in resisting the unavoidable drying, set by its temperament, and the amount of its innate moisture does not go past it, though specific causes that help the drying, or that destroy in another way, may overtake it. Many people say natural terms of life are these and accidental terms the others, as if the art of preserving health were what carries a person's body to the age called the natural term {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.202}.
