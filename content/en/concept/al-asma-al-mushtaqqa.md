## What this record is
Derived nouns are nouns taken from the verb on set patterns, so that they denote the doer, the object, the place, the instrument or the act itself. This record gives what a research summary supplied to the museum says of them: five classes, the active participle, the passive participle, the noun of place, the noun of instrument and the verbal noun, on the root k-t-b. It continues the record on [[concept/awzan-al-fiil-al-ashara|the ten verb forms]] and serves the science of [[concept/ilm-al-sarf|morphology]]; the page attributes each pattern, example and page number to the summary as given.

## Background
The summary rests on two references: Ryding's A Reference Grammar of Modern Standard Arabic, 2005, chapter 5 pages 75 to 87 and chapter 6 pages 102 to 118 {cite:ryding-2005-reference-grammar-msa}, and Wright's A Grammar of the Arabic Language, third edition, volume 1, 1896, pages 110 to 130 {cite:wright-grammar-of-the-arabic-language-vol1}. The idea the table presents is that a derived noun is built from the root on a fixed pattern: form I has its own pattern for the doer, the object, the place and the instrument, and forms II to X have a general rule based on the prefix mu- with the imperfect stem, the vowel before the third root letter differing between the doer and the object.

## The detail
The summary says the active participle in form I is on the pattern fāʿil, as kātib, "writer", and in forms II to X is built by adding mu- to the imperfect stem with -i- before the third root letter, as muktatib from form VIII. The passive participle is on mafʿūl, as maktūb, "written; letter", and in the derived forms is built with mu- and the imperfect stem with -a- before the third root letter, as mukātab from form III. The noun of place is on mafʿal, mafʿil or mafʿala, as maktab, "office or desk", and maktaba, "library", and in the derived forms it matches the passive participle. The noun of instrument is on mifʿal, mifʿāl and mifʿala; the summary says there is no common instrument noun from k-t-b, gives miftāḥ, "key", as an example from another root, and adds that the modern language also uses faʿʿāla. Last, the verbal noun is lexical and not confined to one pattern: the summary gives it forty and more patterns in form I and says it is regular in the derived forms, tafʿīl for II, mufāʿala for III, istifʿāl for X and so on, with kitāba, "writing", from I and mukātaba, "correspondence", from III as examples.

## How the references differ
By the summary's account the classes of the table agree with what is in the two references, and the page numbers differ because the books are arranged differently: Ryding spreads the derived nouns over two chapters, and Wright gathers most of them in a continuous run of pages. The numbers are as given and the museum did not check them.

## What this page does not reach
The museum did not open Ryding or Wright and did not read the patterns in them. The summary names no further classes, such as the elative, the assimilated adjective or the intensive forms, so none is given here. It gives no examples from the Qur'an or poetry. The museum did not check the vowels written in the table, which are carried as given. The record does not decide how the early grammarians and morphologists named and counted these classes.
