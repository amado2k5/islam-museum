## Summary
Bilad al-Sham is the [[place/levant|Levant]], which includes [[place/syria|Syria]], [[place/lebanon|Lebanon]], [[place/jordan|Jordan]] and [[place/palestine|Palestine]], and among its cities [[place/damascus|Damascus]]. This page gathers what was read of the speech of its people in two sources: the Encyclopaedia Britannica's modern division of the dialect groups, and what the old books of language say of the Arabs on the edge adjoining the Levant and Ibn Khaldun's account of the speech of the cities. No detailed description of the Levantine dialect was found in the references read, so the page is short, as far as what was read goes. Sections on classification, main features and speaker numbers were later added from a research summary supplied to the museum, and they supersede the remark that none were found.

## The modern description
The Encyclopaedia Britannica says in its article on the Arabic language that the spoken varieties differ a great deal and that some are not understood by speakers of others, that the chief dialect groups in modern times are those of Arabia, Iraq, Syria, Egypt and North Africa, and that the three cases of inflection have mostly disappeared from the spoken varieties {cite:britannica-arabic-language}. For it the Levant is a group in itself, but the article does not detail its features or limits.

## The Arabs adjoining the Levant in al-Muzhir
Al-Suyuti relays from al-Farabi that the language was not taken from Quda'a, Ghassan and Iyad because they adjoined the people of the Levant, most of whom were Christians who read Hebrew {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.167}. Ibn Khaldun says the tongue of Mudar was spoiled by their mixing with the non-Arabs when they took the realms of Iraq, the Levant, Egypt and the Maghrib, its faculty no longer taking the first form, so that it turned into another language {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.767}. This is Ibn Khaldun's statement about the tongue in general, not about the Levant in particular, and he describes no Levantine dialect in it.

## The speech of the cities in Ibn Khaldun
Ibn Khaldun has a chapter that the language of the settled people and the cities is a language standing by itself, differing from that of Mudar, and that it varies from city to city: the tongue of the people of the east differs somewhat from that of the people of the west, and likewise the people of al-Andalus, each conveying his purpose by his own speech, and the loss of i'rab does not harm them {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.770}. He explains the distance from the first language by mixing with the non-Arabs: whoever mixed with them more has a language further from the original, since the faculty is gained by teaching {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.770}. He says in a nearby place that when the Arabs overcame the nations of the east, the Persians and the Turks, they mixed with them and their languages passed among them, so that their speech was spoiled with the spoiling of the faculty until it became another language {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.771}. He sets east against west and al-Andalus in his talk and gives the Levant no description of its own.

## What this page does not reach
No description of the features of the Levantine dialect was found in the references read, whether of its sounds, grammar or vocabulary, nor a count of its speakers, nor differences among the dialects of Damascus, Beirut, Amman and Jerusalem or between towns, villages and the desert; and no modern dialect survey was read. The Encyclopaedia's articles on the countries concerned were not read, so what is related here of the Levant comes from the general article on Arabic and from the old books of language. For the general picture see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]]. Other references on Levantine dialects, dialect surveys and studies of the speech of towns, villages and the desert, should be added once read and their content is clear. Until then the page records the limits of what was read, not a description of the dialect.

## Classification
Glottolog 5.4 places Levantine Arabic (identifier nort3139, ISO 639-3 code apc) within Afro-Asiatic, Semitic, West Semitic, Central Semitic, Arabian, Arabic and Levantine-Cypriot Arabic, taking in the Lebanese and Syrian varieties and South Levantine Arabic (sout3123, with Fellahi and Madani); it notes that ISO 639-3 merged the code ajp into apc in January 2023 {cite:glottolog-levantine-arabic} {cite:glottolog-south-levantine-arabic}. The free page of Ethnologue (29th edition, 2026) places apc, with its Arabic name al-shāmī, in Afro-Asiatic within the Arabic macrolanguage, across Syria, Israel, Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine and Turkey {cite:ethnologue-apc}.

Brill's Encyclopedia of Arabic Language and Linguistics treats Lebanon, Syria, Palestine/Israel and Jordan as one of five major dialect clusters, crosscut by the divide between nomadic (Bedouin) and sedentary (rural and urban) speech {cite:brill-eall-toponyms} {cite:brill-eall-palestinian-arabic}, and classifies urban varieties such as Damascus Arabic as polygenetic composites {cite:brill-eall-polygenesis}. The Encyclopaedia Britannica's articles on the Levant and on Jordan identify Levantine Arabic (al-ʿarabiyyah al-shāmiyyah) across Syria, Lebanon, Palestine and Jordan {cite:britannica-levant} {cite:britannica-jordan}, and a page of the City University of New York's Academic Commons notes the divide between the sedentary urban speech (Madinee), village speech (Falahi) and Bedouin speech {cite:cuny-levantine-arabic}. The research summary found no UNESCO source on the point.

## Main features
**Phonology.** Brill reports that urban dialects merge Classical q and the glottal stop into the glottal stop and lose the interdentals (th becomes t, dh becomes d or z, emphatic dh becomes emphatic d or z), whereas rural dialects have k or a back k and Bedouin dialects have g, both keeping the interdentals {cite:brill-eall-palestinian-arabic}. Imāla raises and fronts final and medial a towards [ɛ, e, i] unless emphatics or postvelars block it {cite:brill-eall-vowel-raising} {cite:brill-eall-palestinian-arabic}. The dialects allow initial two-consonant clusters, delete unstressed i and u in non-final open syllables, and insert an epenthetic i without moving the stress {cite:brill-eall-palestinian-arabic}.

**Morphology.** The imperfective takes the indicative prefix b-; the future is marked by raḥ (or ḥa- and ta- in Palestinian); and the pseudo-verb bidd-/badd-, "to want", is widespread {cite:brill-eall-palestinian-arabic} {cite:brill-eall-polygenesis}. Urban and northern varieties neutralise gender in the plural, whereas rural and Bedouin Palestinian dialects keep feminine plurals; negation uses ma- (urban, northern) or ma-...-š (rural), with miš for non-verbal predicates {cite:brill-eall-palestinian-arabic}.

**Syntax.** Word order allows a flexible subject-verb-object order beside verb-subject-object {cite:cuny-levantine-arabic} {cite:brill-eall-palestinian-arabic}. Analytic possession uses tabaʿ or la; fī(h) marks existence and ability (fiyyi, fīk); and object relative clauses require a resumptive pronoun {cite:brill-eall-palestinian-arabic} {cite:brill-eall-cypriot-maronite-arabic}.

**Lexicon.** Brill points to an Aramaic and Syriac substrate (for example šōb, "heat", and naṭar, "to guard") and, in Palestinian Arabic, loanwords from French, Italian, English, Turkish, Greek, Persian and Hebrew {cite:brill-eall-aramaic-syriac-loanwords} {cite:brill-eall-palestinian-arabic}; the CUNY page makes the same general point about borrowing {cite:cuny-levantine-arabic}.

## Speaker numbers
The research summary gives two figures. The CUNY Sociology of Language page (2024) says Levantine Arabic has over 30 million speakers across Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, Palestine and the diaspora {cite:cuny-levantine-arabic}. Brill's entry on Palestinian Arabic (last updated 25 March 2019) gives approximately 8.5 million native speakers of the Palestinian subset {cite:brill-eall-palestinian-arabic}. The summary found no figure in Ethnologue (29th edition, 2026; exact counts are behind a paywall), Glottolog, Britannica or UNESCO {cite:ethnologue-apc}. The two figures come from different years and different kinds of source and are not added together.
