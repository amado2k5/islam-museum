## Summary
The author outlines the various motives for saving, argues that attracting savings can be accomplished through qirad rather than interest-bearing loans, defines hoarding as withholding money and wealth from consumption, investment, and charitable spending, and considers investment to be the formation of fixed or circulating assets for the purpose of increasing production {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 191} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 193} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 197} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 198}.

## Overview
The author defines saving as what remains of income after consumption; it extends beyond surplus food to include goods and money, occurs among individuals, households, enterprises, and governments, and takes the path of either hoarding or investment {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 191}.

## Motives for Saving
The author lists the motives for saving: for old age, for emergencies and lean years, for one's descendants, for purchasing durable goods, and for improving one's financial position and acquiring productive assets {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 191} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 192}. He cites the hadith that leaving one's heirs wealthy is better than leaving them dependent on others {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 192}.

## Saving Without Riba
The author argues that whoever thinks people save only for interest is mistaken, that economists have shown that interest rates and their fluctuations have only a negligible effect on saving and investment, and that attracting savings is done through qirad, that is, participating for a share of profit {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 193}.

## Increasing Income and Reducing Consumption
The author holds that saving grows either by increasing income or by reducing consumption, and lists Islam's means of increasing income: recognizing the incentive of private property and inheritance, maximizing benefits, excellence in work, prohibiting illicit income such as riba, gambling, hoarding/monopoly, and bribery, prohibiting idleness and begging, granting land according to capacity, and keeping zakat and financial levies moderate so as not to dampen producers' resolve {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 193} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 194}.

## Hoarding
The author presents Ibn al-Arabi's seven opinions on the meaning of "treasure" (kanz), noting that the dispute centers on whether kanz means wealth on which zakat has not been paid, or any wealth withheld from spending even if zakat has been paid {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 194} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 195}. He views hoarding as having two degrees—hoarding by accumulation and hoarding by withholding—and defines it as withholding wealth from consumption, investment, and charitable spending, noting that the punishment for hoarded wealth on which zakat has not been paid is greater {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 196} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 197}.

## Investment
In his view, investment is seeking to obtain yield—that is, forming fixed or circulating assets for production or its growth—and it is not net investment unless gross investment exceeds depreciation {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 198} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 199}. He distinguishes between individual investment and societal investment, as transferring ownership from one person to another does not increase society's assets {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 199}.

## Combating Hoarding
To combat hoarding, the author proposes creating short-, medium-, and long-term halal investment opportunities and providing security, while rejecting combating it through inflationary monetary issuance {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 198} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 199}.



## Principles of Investment and Capital Not Offered Free of Charge
The author lists the principles of investment: encouragement, risk, the link between profit and risk, risk distribution, time preference, opportunity cost, profit as protection for capital, maximizing profits, and minimizing losses {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 202} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 208}.

He argues that savers do not provide capital to investors free of charge because it is a scarce economic resource with a cost, and that prohibited interest does not mean capital has no price—rather, its price is a share of expected profit {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 209}.
