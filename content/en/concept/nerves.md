## Summary
Ibn Sina opens the third group of the anatomy of the simple organs, the nerves, in six chapters, with a special discourse on the use and origin of the nerve, and then begins on the seven cranial pairs {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.77-78}.

## Use
The uses of the nerve are some in itself and some by accident. In itself, it is how the brain gives every other organ sensation and movement. By accident, it tightens the flesh and strengthens the body, and it gives notice of harms to organs that lack feeling, such as the liver, spleen and lung; a nervous wrapper runs over them and they are covered with a nervous membrane, so that when they swell or stretch they are felt {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.77}.

## Origin
The nerves begin in the brain and end in the skin. The brain is the origin of some directly and of some through the spinal cord. The nerves springing from the brain itself give sensation and movement only to the organs of the head and face and the inner viscera; the other organs receive them from the nerves of the cord {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.77}. Nerves for sensation go straight to their organ, and the softer they are the better they carry the power of sense; nerves for movement are led through windings so as to grow harder gradually away from the origin. Most of what gives sensation comes from the front of the brain, which is softer; most of what gives movement from the back, which is thicker {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.77-78}.
