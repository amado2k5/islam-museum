## Summary
This page treats the laqab, the fourth of the parts of [[concept/ilm-al-asma-wal-alqab|onomastics]], and a particular kind of it, the khitab, as a research file supplied to the museum sets them out. The museum did not open the pages cited, so everything here is ascribed to the summary alone.
## What a laqab is
A laqab is an additional name associated with a person's qualities, appearance, accomplishments, reputation or status. Some were complimentary, some descriptive, and others potentially unflattering. Political and religious honorifics became particularly prominent in the medieval Islamic period {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names}. The laqab has a detailed juristic definition at [[concept/laqab-fiqh|the laqab in jurisprudence]].

## Khitab
A specialised category of honorific laqab is sometimes classified separately as khitab, especially titles ending in al-Din, al-Dawla or al-Mulk. The summary gives Fakhr al-Din, pride of the religion; Sayf al-Dawla, sword of the state or dynasty; Izz al-Din, glory of the religion; Nizam al-Mulk, order of the realm; and Rukn al-Din, pillar of the religion {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names}. It says many of these medieval honorifics subsequently became ordinary given names.
## Laqabs, nisbas and names that overlap
The summary notes the overlap of functions: some occupational forms are classed as laqabs and not grammatical nisbas, and some laqabs prevail over the name and become the shuhra, as with al-Jahiz, Salah al-Din, Abu Nuwas and al-Mutanabbi {cite:bnf-kitcat-arabic-names}. That makes the distinction between laqab, nisba and kunya a matter of context for many figures.
## The summary's references
Among them are Schimmel's Islamic Names (1989), Ibn Khallikan's Wafayat al-A'yan and the entry Lakab in the Encyclopaedia of Islam {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names} {cite:wafayat-ayan} {cite:brill-reference-works-home}, and the guidance of the Bibliotheque nationale de France, Usages nationaux: noms arabes.
## The limits of the account
The summary does not explain the origin of each laqab, gives no dates for them beyond the above, and for some gives only a literal translation. It does not say whether a laqab was bestowed by caliphs or acquired by its bearer, so no such claim is made here. For how the laqab relates to the other names see [[concept/al-ism-wal-kunya|ism and kunya]] and [[concept/al-nasab-wal-nisba|nasab and nisba]].

In these titles the derivation from a possessed noun, al-Din, al-Dawla or al-Mulk, shows, which is what gathers them under the name khitab in the summary's classification; the classification is a difference of terminology and not of meaning, as laqab is the general term and khitab a kind of it.
## How to read epithets
Each laqab should be read in the light of its age and context: al-Rashid, al-Faruq and al-Siddiq are laudatory epithets tied to the lives of their bearers; al-Jahiz is a descriptive epithet that arose from an appearance; and Salah al-Din and Nur al-Din are political and religious honorifics of the medieval period. One cannot understand from the laqab alone what its bearer was, for some are praise, some description and some may not please. This is a reading from the summary's classification, and the museum ascribes to none of these men anything beyond what it said.
