## Summary
This page sets out the history of Arabic printing in eight milestones, as a research file supplied to the museum summarises them, in order of time: the first Arabic letters on wood in Europe in 1486, the first Arabic book in movable type in 1514, the first printed Qur'an between 1537 and 1538, the Medici Oriental Press in Rome from 1584, then the Qozhaya press in 1610 and the Shuwayr press in 1734 in Mount Lebanon, the Bulaq press in Cairo in 1820, and the Imprimerie Catholique in Beirut in 1848. Everything here is ascribed to the summary, and the museum did not open the pages it cites, so dates and names stand as the summary gives them.
## Arabic letters in European books
The summary says the first appearance of Arabic letters in a European book was in 1486, when an Arabic alphabet cut in wood appeared in Bernhard von Breydenbach's Peregrinatio in Terram Sanctam, printed at [[place/mainz|Mainz]] {cite:mashq-studio-type-design}. The first Arabic book in movable type is, by its account, Kitab Salat al-Sawa'i, a Christian book of hours whose imprint names Fano in Italy; the summary thinks it likely printed in Venice by Gregorio de' Gregori under Pope Julius II or Leo X, and dates it to 1514 {cite:princeton-century-millennium-pdf} {cite:mashq-studio-type-design}.
## The first printed Qur'an
The summary says the first printed Arabic Qur'an was produced in Venice by Paganino and Alessandro Paganini between 1537 and 1538, and that a single surviving copy was rediscovered in 1987 {cite:mashq-studio-type-design}. The museum takes nothing here from the text of the Qur'an: its text is never typed or corrected and is referred to its frozen records.
## The Medici Oriental Press in Rome
The press was founded in [[place/rome|Rome]] in 1584 by Cardinal Ferdinando de' Medici and run by Giovanni Battista Raimondi, with Arabic punches cut by Robert Granjon, according to the summary. It printed the Arabic Gospels in 1590 and 1591, al-Idrisi's geography and the grammars al-Kafiya and al-Ajurrumiyya in 1592, the Qanun of [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]] in 1593 and Euclid in 1594; its years run in the summary from 1584 to 1614 {cite:mashq-studio-type-design} {cite:wikipedia-medici-oriental-press-en} {cite:jhiblog-early-history-arabic-printing-europe}. The summary uses the Wikipedia page as a reference for the fact of the press itself, not for a judgement on its works.
## The first presses in Mount Lebanon
In 1610 the Qozhaya Psalter was printed in Syriac and Garshuni, that is Arabic in Syriac script, at the Monastery of Qozhaya in [[place/mount-lebanon|Mount Lebanon]] {cite:calameo-photographing-lebanons-heritage}. In 1734 Abd Allah Zakhir cast Arabic movable type at Dayr al-Shuwayr in the same mountain and printed Mizan al-Zaman, which the summary calls the first Arabic movable-type book printed in Lebanon {cite:researchgate-moukarzel-first-arabic-book-lebanon-1734}.
## The Bulaq press
The Amiriyya press was founded at Bulaq in [[place/cairo|Cairo]] in 1820 by [[person/muhammad-ali-pasha|Muhammad Ali Pasha]], after a mission to Milan in 1815 led by Niqula al-Masabiki; it opened in 1821 and in 1822 published its first book, Don Raphael's Italian and Arabic dictionary {cite:ijodh-0201-06} {cite:cambridge-ijmes-journeymen-middlemen-muslim-printing}. The three years, founding, opening and first publication, are all given as the summary has them.
## The Imprimerie Catholique in Beirut
The Jesuits founded the Imprimerie Catholique in Beirut in 1848, with full press operations from 1852 and 1853 according to the summary, and it became a major centre for Naskh book type, dictionaries and the publishing of the Nahda {cite:khatt-foundation-imprimerie-catholique} {cite:brill-jjs-2-2-p248} {cite:imarabe-photographing-lebanon-heritage-1864-1970}. Naskh has its own record at [[concept/naskh-script|Naskh script]] and in [[concept/marahil-tatawwur-al-khatt-al-arabi|the stages of development of the Arabic script]].
## Where the dates overlap
The milestones do not form a single line: some are European and some Eastern, and the first Lebanese presses precede Bulaq by roughly two centuries in the summary, yet it makes Bulaq the key to modern printing in Egypt after the Milan mission. The summary itself says the place of printing of Kitab Salat al-Sawa'i is likely and not certain, and the Imprimerie Catholique has two numbers, 1848 for the founding and 1852 and 1853 for full operation, so both questions are left open here.
