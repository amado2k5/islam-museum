## Summary
This page sets out the place of [[language/arabic|Arabic]] in [[place/misr|Egypt]] as read in the languages section of the Encyclopaedia Britannica article on Egypt and in the Egypt page of The World Factbook. It ascribes each statement to its page, estimates no number of speakers and judges no classification of dialects.

## Official status
The encyclopedia says the official language of Egypt is Arabic, that most Egyptians speak one of its several vernacular dialects, and that the spoken language differs greatly from the literary one, as in other Arab countries {cite:britannica-egypt-languages}. The Factbook's Languages entry reads: Arabic (official); English and French widely understood by educated classes {cite:cia-factbook-egypt}. The encyclopedia says the same of the foreign languages, that most educated Egyptians are fluent in English or French or both besides Arabic. The two sources agree.

## The standard and the spoken
The encyclopedia describes modern literary Arabic, also called al-fusha, as developed out of Classical or medieval Arabic, learned only in school, and the lingua franca of educated people across the Arab world. It says its grammar and syntax remain substantially unchanged since the 7th century, but its style, word order and phraseology have become simpler and more flexible and are often derived directly from English or French. The colloquial it describes as regional vernaculars that differ widely from the literary form and from one another: the Bedouin of the Eastern Desert and Sinai have one dialect, the Bedouin of the Western Desert a separate one; Upper Egypt has its own, markedly different from Cairo's; the Cairo dialect is used with variations across the towns of the Delta; and rural people have their own {cite:britannica-egypt-languages}. It says long contact with foreigners brought many loanwords into Cairene colloquial, and that Cairo's place in the Arab film industry has made its dialect widely understood; that is the encyclopedia's claim, not measured by the museum. For the duality in general see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Fusha and the dialects]].

## Other languages in the country
The encyclopedia names small groups: the Beja of the southern Eastern Desert, using a Cushitic language called To Bedawi, some speaking Tigre and many Arabic; groups at Siwa Oasis whose language is related, not closely, to the Berber languages; Nubians, who speak Eastern Sudanic languages; and Greek, Italian and Armenian minorities, much smaller than they were {cite:britannica-egypt-languages}. These names and classifications are as the encyclopedia gives them. Compare [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|the languages of the tribes]].

## From the history of the language
The encyclopedia says Coptic, a later form of the ancient Egyptian language, was at the Islamic conquest the medium of both religious and everyday life for the mass of the population; by the 12th century, however, Arabic was in common use even among Christian Copts, whose former tongue continued only as a liturgical language of the Coptic Orthodox Church {cite:britannica-egypt-languages}. For Egypt among the Arab states see [[concept/al-alam-al-arabi|the Arab states and the League]].
