## Summary
Haqq means, in Arabic usage, what is established, owed or certain, the opposite of falsehood. In the jurists' terms it has two senses: a judgement that matches reality, and a duty established by the law. It falls into the right of God and the right of the servant. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7174} Ibn al-Qayyim: God's right admits no compromise, as in the hudud, zakat and expiations, while the servant's right admits settlement, waiver and exchange. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7174} Usulis disagreed whether the right is the divine address itself or the act it binds. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7176} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7177} Al-Qarafi divides obligations into God's alone, the servant's alone, and the mixed case of slander. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7176} Ibn Rajab lists five kinds of the servant's right. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7177} Jurists also use the word for financial and non-financial entitlements, for contractual obligations, for state stipends and for easements. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7178} The source of every right is God, and the holder, the obliged party and the object of the right are its pillars; the object must be lawful. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7179} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7180} The article continues beyond this scan and is not complete.
