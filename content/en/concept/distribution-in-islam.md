## Summary
The author divides distribution in Islam into three types: the distribution of wealth based on capability, the distribution of income to factors of production based on mutual exchange, and redistribution based on need, kinship ties, and religious outreach {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 210}. He sets justice and benevolence as the standard, and formulates the principle "from each according to his capacity, and to each according to his benefit or need" {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 211}.

## The Three Types
The first type distributes wealth to those who demonstrate actual or expected capability, such as jihad, competition, and land revival, and covers land, minerals, water, pasture, fire, and all other natural resources {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 210} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 213}. The second type distributes income to the factors of production and is based on mutual exchange {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 210} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 231}. The third type redistributes income on the basis of need, such as charity; kinship ties, such as maintenance for relatives; or outreach, such as winning hearts—which the author sometimes calls "social transfers" {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 211}.

## Distortion and Drawing Lots
Distortion can occur when distribution is achieved through force, or through trickery, fraud, and theft, in any of the three types {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 211}. Drawing lots is used according to the majority of jurists when a decision is required, the claimants are equal in right, and distribution to all of them is not possible {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 211}.

## Connection to Production
The author presents al-Sadr's theory that Islam does not make distribution dependent on modes of production, and notes that al-Sadr later stated elsewhere that the link is not completely severed and is realized through sharia-compliant public policy {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 212}. The author holds that there is a link between production and distribution, though not all rules of distribution are explicitly laid down in scripture, so human jurisprudence in this area changes with time, place, and circumstance {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 211}. For example, modern mechanized production may enable a few to dominate vast sectors; if the rule "whoever reaches an unowned resource first is entitled to it" were applied without regulation, it would lead to injustice that the sharia rejects {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 212}.

## Distribution of Natural Resources
The author begins the section on the first type of distribution with land and subsurface resources, as they are among the most important forms of wealth, and suggests that al-Sadr was perhaps the first to bring back into focus the importance of distributing natural resources and wealth—a form of distribution neglected in conventional economics textbooks {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 213} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 214}. From this branch out the rulings on land (see [[concept/ihya-al-mawat|Revival of Barren Land]] and [[concept/kharaj-lands|Kharaj Lands]]), minerals ([[concept/minerals-ownership-in-islam|Ownership of Minerals]]), water ([[concept/water-distribution-in-islam|Water Distribution]]), pasture ([[concept/pasture-in-islam|Pasture]]), and [[concept/sabq-in-islam|Precedence]]. The author considers that land, minerals, water, and pasture are examined here from the standpoint of wealth distribution and complement what preceded in the chapter on ownership {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 213}.

## Functional Distribution of Income
Economists divide the factors of production into land, labor, capital, and enterprise, with the return on each being rent for land, wages for labor, interest or profit for capital, and profit for enterprise {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 231}. The author holds that Islam prohibits a fixed return on capital—namely riba—and permits a return as a share of profit {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 231}. The factors can be reduced to capital and labor, since capital is stored labor, enterprise is included under labor, and land is included under capital {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 231} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 232}. The author calls this functional distribution because it is based on the function each factor performs, unlike personal distribution based on relationships {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 232}. He argues that a contract of mutual exchange is necessary, because charitable donations are rare due to owners' reluctance to part with them and the burden of indebtedness felt by the recipient, as al-'Izz ibn 'Abd al-Salam noted {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 232}.

## Critique of the Expended Labor Theory
The author presents al-Sadr's theory that the basis of earnings, whether wages or profit, is expended labor—either direct living labor or previously stored labor such as capital {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 232}—and cites six examples of it in subcontracting labor, subleasing, sub-mudarabah, and selling pastureland {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 233}. Al-Sadr holds that the price difference is only lawful if the intermediary adds new labor or utility {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 233}. The author does not agree with him on every ruling in this regard and considers that it requires a comparative study {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 234}. Purchasing, storage, and transport may be regarded as a commercial craft that creates time or place utility, thereby making the price difference lawful overall {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 233} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 234}.

## Redistribution
Redistribution in Islam may be obligatory, such as zakah, maintenance of relatives, inheritance, expiations, and vows, or voluntary, such as supererogatory charity, gifts, and bequests {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 300}. What the state commits to is spent from the treasury of zakah and the treasury of public welfare; part of the treasury of public welfare goes to public interests such as salaries, roads, and mosques, and part to redistribution {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 300}. Redistribution is not based on need alone, but may also be based on kinship ties and affection, or on righteousness and need together, so that the more righteous among the needy are given priority {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 300}.

## Summary
In the author's conception, distribution consists of three interconnected layers: wealth distributed by capability, income distributed by mutual exchange, and redistribution based on need. The pursuit of justice in these layers is connected to production without following it in every case.
