## Summary
Ibn Sina reports that Galen's position, closely examined, makes separation of continuity the sole essential cause of pain; he then prefers to make change of temperament a genus that causes pain in itself, arguing that pain can be uniform in a part while separation of continuity never is {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.148-149}.

## Galen's position as Ibn Sina gives it
Ibn Sina says that, on close inspection, Galen's doctrine comes down to separation of continuity as the one essential cause of pain: heat hurts because it separates continuity, and cold hurts too, because in its strong condensing and gathering the parts are drawn toward where it condenses and are parted on the side they are drawn from. He says Galen went so far in some books as to suggest that all the objects of the senses harm by separation, or by gathering that brings separation with it: black among things seen hurts by its strong gathering, white by its strong separating; bitter, salty and sour among tastes by excess of separating, and astringent by excess of contracting; likewise for smell; and sounds by the violence of air movement on meeting the ear {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.148}.

## Ibn Sina's own view
The true statement, for him, is to make change of temperament a genus that causes pain in itself, even if separation of continuity may accompany it, and he holds that the full demonstration belongs not to medicine but to the natural part of philosophy, so he only points to it. Pain may be uniform in the painful organ, whereas separation of continuity is never uniform, so pain present in parts free of separation arises from bad temperament and not from it. Cold also hurts where it contracts and chills, yet the separation it causes is not where the chill is but at the edges of the chilled place {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.148-149}.

## The definition and its consequence
Pain, he then says, is the sensing of an unsuitable agent, suddenly and as unsuitable, so every sensed thing that is unsuitable as such hurts. If one sensed the cold that spoils the temperament, as spoiling it, and no separation of continuity came of it, that would still be the sensing of something unsuitable, and so pain. He adds that pain stirs heat and heat stirs pain after pain, and that something with the feel of pain may remain after pain without being real pain, only something that dissolves of itself, which the ignorant treat and so harm it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.149}.
