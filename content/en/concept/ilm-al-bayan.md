## Summary
The science of bayan is the second of the sciences of [[concept/ilm-al-balagha|rhetoric]], and its subject, for the authors of the Miftah and the Idah, is bringing one meaning in different ways of clarity, by simile, trope, metaphor and metonymy. This page sets out its definition in [[person/al-sakkaki|al-Sakkaki]], al-Qazwini and Ibn Khaldun; al-Sakkaki's reason why it is confined to intellectual indications; his kinds of simile; and then four examples from the Qur'an that the early books on badi' counted as metaphors, with what the exegetes say at their verses. Each statement is ascribed to its author, and verses are pointed to by their pages without writing out their text.

## The definition
Al-Sakkaki says bayan is knowing how to bring one meaning in different ways, with more clarity of indication or less, to guard by this knowledge against error in matching speech to the whole of what is meant; he holds it a branch of ma'ani that separates from it only by an added consideration, and for that reason puts it later {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.162}. Al-Qazwini makes what guards against obscurity of meaning the science of bayan {cite:idah-khafaji-dar-al-jil 1 p.50}. For Ibn Khaldun it is the kind that looks at indication of the necessary and what necessitates it, which is metaphor and metonymy; he gives 'Zayd is a lion', where one does not mean the real lion but his courage, which is necessarily linked, and attributes it to Zayd, and 'Zayd has many ashes', where one means what follows from it, generosity and feeding guests {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.760} {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.761}.

## Why it works only through intellectual indications
Al-Sakkaki prepares the ground with a rule: bringing one meaning in ways of differing clarity cannot be done through conventional indications. Whoever wishes to compare a cheek to a rose in redness and says 'a cheek resembling a rose' cannot find a sentence conveying that meaning by conventional indication more complete or less complete in clarity, because a hearer who knows the conventions understands from it as he would from its synonyms, and one who does not understands nothing {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.329}. It is possible only with intellectual indications: of congruence, of inclusion and of entailment. If the original meaning has a link to another meaning, the mind can pass from one to the other. From this al-Sakkaki concludes that bayan comes back to considering the entailments between meanings, and that the passage has two directions: from what entails to what is entailed, and from the entailed to what entails {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.330}.

## Simile in al-Sakkaki
Al-Sakkaki divides simile by its two sides and its ground. The two sides are sensory, or intellectual, or one of each; the ground is one or more than one, and may count as one {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.334} {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.335}. His examples of an intellectual ground include likening knowledge to life, a man to a lion in boldness, and justice to a balance, and he says the scholars of this art hold that a simile with an intellectual ground is more general than one with a sensory ground, since the mind can perceive from the sensible a certain aspect while the sense cannot perceive the non-sensible {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.335} {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.336}. He gives the likening of a compound to a compound, such as the shape formed by black dust and scattered white swords in it, to the shape formed by night with falling stars in it {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.337}.

## Metaphors in the Qur'an in Ibn al-Mu'tazz
Ibn al-Mu'tazz opens his al-Badi' by saying that among badi' speech is the verse of al-Zukhruf at [[passage/quran-43-4|al-Zukhruf 4]], and that it is the borrowing of a word for something not known by it from something known by it; he adds the example of lowering the wing for one's parents {cite:badi-ibn-al-mutazz-dar-al-jil-1990 p.75}. In the chapter on metaphor he then gives verses including [[passage/quran-3-7|Al Imran 7]], [[passage/quran-17-24|al-Isra' 24]], [[passage/quran-19-4|Maryam 4]] and [[passage/quran-36-37|Ya Sin 37]] {cite:badi-ibn-al-mutazz-dar-al-jil-1990 p.76} {cite:badi-ibn-al-mutazz-dar-al-jil-1990 p.77}. Ibn al-Mu'tazz counts metaphor the first chapter of badi', while Ibn Khaldun places metaphor and metonymy in bayan {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.761}; so their examples appear on this page and on [[concept/ilm-al-badi|the page of badi']] according to these two classifications.

## What the exegetes say
At [[passage/quran-36-37|Ya Sin 37]] al-Qurtubi notes that stripping is flaying and removing, and that the going of light and coming of darkness was made like stripping something and the exposing of what was stripped; he says: it is a metaphor {cite:tafsir-qurtubi-quran-com on 36:37}. Al-Baghawi explains that God removes day from night and peels it off, the origin being darkness with day entering upon it {cite:tafsir-baghawi-quran-com on 36:37}. At [[passage/quran-17-24|al-Isra' 24]] al-Qurtubi says the expression is a metaphor for tenderness and mercy toward the parents and for lowly deference to them, and that lowering and raising the wing is given as a likeness from a bird's wing when it is raised over its young {cite:tafsir-qurtubi-quran-com on 17:24}; in al-Baghawi the sense is to soften your side to them and submit {cite:tafsir-baghawi-quran-com on 17:24}. At [[passage/quran-19-4|Maryam 4]] al-Baghawi explains the head's kindling as the whitening of its hair {cite:tafsir-baghawi-quran-com on 19:4}, and Ibn Kathir says the sense is that grey blazed up in the black, and gives a line of Ibn Duraid likening it to the blazing of fire in glowing coals {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-quran-com on 19:4}. At [[passage/quran-43-4|al-Zukhruf 4]] al-Baghawi relays from Qatada that umm al-kitab is the origin of the book and that the mother of anything is its origin, and says it is meant the preserved tablet {cite:tafsir-baghawi-quran-com on 43:4}.

## Books of bayan
Khafaji says Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani took up in [[work/asrar-al-balagha|Asrar al-Balagha]] the studies of bayan: simile, trope and metaphor {cite:idah-khafaji-dar-al-jil 1 p.7}. Ibn Khaldun says al-Sakkaki refined the questions of the art in the Miftah and that later scholars epitomised it {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.761}.

## What this page does not reach
Asrar al-Balagha was not read, nor the chapters on trope, metaphor and metonymy in the Miftah, apart from the opening of the discussion of trope and the place of metonymy; the chapters on bayan in al-Idah were not read because the copy read does not carry them. The exegetes here explain the sense, and only al-Qurtubi's word 'metaphor' at two verses names the figure, so the classification in this science is that of al-Sakkaki and Ibn al-Mu'tazz, not of the exegetes. This record does not take up the dispute over trope in the Qur'an.
