## Summary
Chapter 15 treats the Sun's altitude at midday on any day: al-Battani finds the declination of the degree in which the Sun stands, subtracts it from the place's latitude if northern and adds it if southern, and subtracts the result from ninety. If the declination is larger than the latitude the Sun is north of the zenith. A second way: the latitude's complement is the altitude of the first of Aries, and northern declination is added to it, or southern declination subtracted. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.48}.

## The first way and the case of the larger declination
He says: if you want the Sun's altitude at midday on any day you like, find the declination of the degree the Sun is in; if northern, subtract it from the place's latitude, if southern, add it to it; subtract the latitude, after the addition or subtraction, from ninety, and what remains is the Sun's altitude at midday. If the declination is more than the place's latitude you know that the Sun lies to the north of the zenith point; add ninety to the latitude and subtract the degree's declination, and what remains is the altitude, reckoned from the northern horizon. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.48}.

## The second way from the altitude of the first of Aries
Then he says: if you want to know the Sun's midday altitude another way, subtract the place's latitude from ninety, and what remains is the altitude of the first of Aries. If the declination is northern add it to the altitude of the first of Aries, if southern subtract it from it; the altitude of the first of Aries after the addition or subtraction is the Sun's midday altitude. If it exceeds ninety, subtract it from one hundred and eighty, and what remains is the altitude from the northern horizon. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.48}.
