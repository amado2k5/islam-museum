## The proverb and its meaning
The proverb 'His own feet brought a doomed man to you' is one of the Arabic proverbs recorded in the old proverb books, as a research summary supplied to the museum sets out in a choice of ten. Its meaning, in the summary, is walking into one's own doom, that is, meeting ruin by one's own act. It belongs to the matter of the great Arabic proverb collections treated in the page [[concept/kutub-al-amthal-al-arabiyya|The Arabic proverb books]] {cite:islamport-5454-58}.

## The story as the books tell it
The pre-Islamic poet [[person/ubayd-ibn-al-abras|Abid ibn al-Abras]] said it when he arrived, without knowing, at the court of King [[person/al-mundhir-ibn-ma-al-sama|al-Mundhir ibn Ma' al-Sama]], to whose name the summary adds al-Nu'man, on the king's 'day of ill-fortune' (yawm bu's), when whoever appeared first was put to death. {cite:islamport-5454-58}

## The old sources
The summary lists the proverb in Majma al-Amthal 1/21, Jamharat al-Amthal 1/119 and al-Mustaqsa 1/37, with an Islamport page. Volume, page and serial numbers stand as in the summary, and the museum has not checked them {cite:islamport-5454-58}. Majma al-Amthal is the work of Abu al-Fadl al-Maydani, who died in 518 AH, 1124 CE, and holds according to the summary over 6,000 proverbs; the summary tabulates it with Abu Hilal al-Askari's Jamharat al-Amthal and al-Zamakhshari's al-Mustaqsa {cite:arabictypinglab-arabic-proverbs}.

## When the proverb is used
The proverb is used for what fits its meaning, that is walking into one's own doom, that is, meeting ruin by one's own act, and the hearer calls its tale to mind even when it is not told. The proverb books agree in keeping a proverb together with its story, because the story explains why it was said, and the sense it is applied to may widen with use; but the summary gives no other instances of its use, so the page gives none.

## A note on the nature of the story
The story here is as the books tell it. It is one of the widely repeated traditional reports, and the museum cannot establish that it happened; it is a narrative told to explain the proverb, not confirmed history. The museum has not opened any of the books and pages named.
