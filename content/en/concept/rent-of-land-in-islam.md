## Summary
Linguistically, rent (ray') means growth and increase, and technically it refers to the return on land {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 237}. Jurists differed on land rent—between a fixed rent and a share of the output, and between those who prohibited it and those who permitted it {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 238} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 239}.

## Meaning of Rent
In Arabic, ray' means growth, and also an elevated place; in modern economics it refers to land rent (rente foncière), the financial return on bonds, and sometimes to a public loan itself {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 237}. In theoretical economics, the author mentions the differential rent proposed by Ricardo between fertile land and the poorest land in a country {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 238}, quasi-rent (surplus profit arising from external circumstances), and unearned income as used by Ibn Khaldun {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 238}.

## Juristic Disagreement on Land Rent
Some jurists permitted a fixed rent for land, some permitted a partnership for a share of the output, and some considered sharecropping (muzara'ah) more lawful than leasing {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 238}. The author presents the prohibitive argument based on the hadith "Whoever has land, let him cultivate it or grant it to his brother," and regards it as a recommendation in light of another hadith: "For one of you to grant it to his brother is better for him than taking a fixed sum" {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 239}.

## The Worker's Share in Sharecropping
Al-Bukhari narrated that Umar contracted with people on the basis that whoever provided the seed would receive half, and if they provided the seed they would receive such-and-such; some jurists inferred from this that the worker's share cannot be less than half of the output, which the author does not accept unless the relationship is treated as fixed and unaffected by the relative importance of land, seed, and labor—a position he finds hard to accept {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 239}.

## Leasing Trees and Daman
Leasing trees to benefit from their fruit is not permissible, because fruit is sold rather than leased, and because it entails selling fruit before it appears or before its ripeness is evident {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 241}. By contrast, land attached to trees may generally be leased, which is called daman or qabalah {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 241}. Jurists hold three opinions on such a lease: prohibition, permissibility if the trees do not exceed one-third, and unconditional permissibility, which is the view of Ibn Aqil {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 242}. Ibn Taymiyyah relates that Umar leased out the land of Usayd ibn Hudayr to his creditors for several years to pay off his debt {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 242}. Al-Izz ibn Abd al-Salam notes that including the water of rivers and springs in such a lease is among the public-interest exceptions where the general rule was set aside {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 243}, and remarks that he will devote an independent study to this issue {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 243}.

## Leasing Assets and Subleasing
A lessee who subleases what they have rented at the same rent or at a higher rent is permitted to do so according to many early jurists, though al-Qadi of the Hanbalis prohibited any increase {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 260}. The author holds that subleasing at a higher rent is permissible so long as taking possession of the asset stands in place of taking possession of its usufructs, and among the Hanafis an increase is permissible if the lessee has made an addition to the asset {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 260} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 261}.

## Substitution to Maximize Rent
The author notes that some jurists permitted substituting a waqf property to maximize its rent, including Abu Yusuf, Abu Thawr, Ibn Taymiyyah, and Ibn Qadi al-Jabal {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 244} (see [[concept/profit-maximisation-in-islam|Profit Maximization]]).
