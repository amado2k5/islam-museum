## Summary
This page sets out what a research summary supplied to the museum says about Arabic in Hatay and southeastern Turkey: its legal status, the varieties spoken and the official figures, each as the summary gives it. The summary names three kinds of Arabic in Turkey, none with official status, and says no Turkish census has published language data since 1965. The museum did not open the pages the summary names, and the founder approved using it as supplied on 21 November 2026. Where the summary says that no official source or figure was found, this page says exactly that and supplies no number of its own.

## What this page is
This page is an article on the subject "Arabic in Hatay and southeastern Turkey", in the world chapter of the Arabic browser, one of the pages that treat Arabic outside the states counted as Arab. It answers three questions: what is the legal status of Arabic there, which varieties of it are spoken, and what official figures have been published on speakers, if any. The museum keeps here to what it keeps to on all its pages: a describing voice, not a commanding one, every statement ascribed to its page, and no verdict on a disputed question. All the material comes from one research summary, so the page describes what the summary said and does not claim to have read the sources.

## Background and dates
The documents the summary relies on here are the constitution of Turkey, the Treaty of Lausanne of 1923, and the 1965 census, the last to publish language data according to the summary. It ties the minority regime to Lausanne and limits it to non-Muslims. The history of Arabic in Hatay and the southeast the summary does not tell. For the arrival of Arabic in the Levant and northern Mesopotamia see [[concept/intishar-al-arabiyya-baada-al-fath|The spread of Arabic after the conquests]].

## Legal status
According to the summary, Article 3 of the Constitution of Turkey makes Turkish the sole official language and Article 42 bars mother-tongue schooling in other languages {cite:constituteproject-turkey-2017} {cite:eliteasia-languages-spoken-in-turkey}. Minority-language protections under the 1923 Treaty of Lausanne are restricted to non-Muslim minorities, leaving Arabic-speaking communities without formal minority-language status, though state broadcasting (TRT Arabi) and elective Standard Arabic school courses exist {cite:rightsagenda-minorities-in-turkey} {cite:uchicago-press-doi-10-1086-725440} {cite:dergipark-article-file-1014722}. This is the summary's statement about its pages, which the museum did not read.

## Varieties spoken
The summary names three kinds: North Levantine (Syrian sedentary) Arabic in Hatay (and Adana and Mersin); Anatolian Arabic, a qəltu-type North Mesopotamian Arabic, in Mardin, Siirt, Batman, Diyarbakır, Muş and Bitlis; and North Arabian Bedouin (Šāwi) Arabic on the Şanlıurfa and Harran plains {cite:langsci-press-catalog-235-chapter-1834} {cite:univie-phaidra-o-2143304}. For the first see [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-syria|Arabic in Syria]] and [[concept/lahjat-al-sham|The dialect of the Levant]]; for the places [[place/mardin|Mardin]] and [[place/anatolia|Anatolia]].

## Official figures
The summary found no official government census, from TurkStat, and no UN or UNESCO count of speakers, and adds that Turkey stopped publishing census language data after 1965. This is the summary's statement; the museum gives no number for speakers of any of the three kinds and estimates none.

## How the sources differ
The pages named for this record are of different kinds. Official texts and statistics: Constitution of Turkey (2017 text) {cite:constituteproject-turkey-2017}; Reference, academic and press pages: Which languages are spoken in Turkey? {cite:eliteasia-languages-spoken-in-turkey}, Minorities in Turkey {cite:rightsagenda-minorities-in-turkey}, University of Chicago Press article, DOI 10.1086/725440 (named for Arabic in Turkey) {cite:uchicago-press-doi-10-1086-725440}, DergiPark article file 1014722 (named for Arabic in Turkey) {cite:dergipark-article-file-1014722}, Language Science Press, catalogue 235, chapter file 1834-1 (named for the Arabic of Turkey) {cite:langsci-press-catalog-235-chapter-1834} and University of Vienna Phaidra object o:2143304 (named for the Arabic of Turkey) {cite:univie-phaidra-o-2143304}. The official texts and statistics speak for the state or the statistical office; the reference, academic and press pages describe and interpret; Wikipedia supports only names, places and dates. Where the kinds differ, the museum reports each in its own voice without choosing between them, and it opened none of them.
