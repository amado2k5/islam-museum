## Nature and selection
There are mountain, wild and garden kinds, and one that grows in the water itself; the kind near water is larger than the garden kind and has the same strength. One kind, called smyrnion, is larger than the garden celery, with a hollow whitish stem; kinds differ by country, some Roman and others not. Not every mountain kind is petroselinon, which is a rock plant. The author quotes Dioscorides at length on the kinds, among them mountain, rock, great celery and wild smyrnion {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.528} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.529}. The strongest is the Roman mountain kind {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.529}.

## Temperament
It is in the first degree of heat and the second of dryness. Rufus said the garden kind is moist, but its root is dry by agreement {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.529}.

## Actions and properties
It dispels wind, opens obstructions, brings sweat and soothes pains; the wild kind blisters and hurts, and the cultivated suits the hot-tempered {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.529}.

## Cosmetic uses
The wild kind is for alopecia, split nails, warts and chilblains; the garden kind sweetens the breath greatly {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.529}.

## Swellings and pustules
It dissolves phlegmatic swellings at the start, and hard and hot ones, especially the kind known as smyrnion {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.529}.

## Joints
The smyrnion in all its parts suits sciatica {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.529}.

## Head, ears and teeth
It is bad for epilepsy and provokes epilepsy in the epileptic. It is said that hanging its root from the neck helps toothache but crumbles the teeth {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.529}.

## Eyes
Garden celery goes into poultices for eye pains {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.529}.

## Chest and breathing
It helps cough, especially the smyrnion, and helps asthma and tightness and difficulty of breath; celery is among the poultices for hot swellings of the breast {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.529}.

## Digestion and the organs of nourishment
It helps the liver and spleen and stirs belching by dissolving; it is not quick to digest or descend, and its seed causes nausea and vomiting unless fried. Some say all kinds help the stomach; Rufus says it rather brings bad hot humours to it, and the raw stays long in the stomach and sickens, though the Roman kind is better for the stomach. Galen said it is fit to be eaten with lettuce, since it offsets the lettuce's cold, and to be taken after a suitable meal. Its seed helps dropsy and cleans and warms the liver {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.529} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.530}.

## Excretory organs
In all its kinds and parts it cleans the kidney, bladder and womb; its seed and leaf do not loosen the bowels, but its root does. The mountain kind breaks up stone, and celery helps difficult urination. The Roman kind is good for the colon, bladder and kidney, soothes wind in the rectum, and is drunk in particular for dropsy {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.530}.

## Excretory organs (labelled book listing, held for the founder's ruling)
The book says it brings on urine and the menses, is bad for the pregnant, and, if a woman takes it as a pessary, makes her miscarry; it expels the afterbirth, especially the wild smyrnion; and it fills the womb with a sharp moisture when eaten continually. Some say celery stirs desire, so that a nursing woman should be kept from it lest her milk spoil {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.530}.

## Fevers
It helps in the cycles of fever {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.530}.

## Poisons and bites
The root of wild smyrnion, drunk, suits the bites of vermin; the garden kind drunk with its decoction and roots helps against deadly drugs, vermin bites and litharge, and goes into theriac mixtures. Celery decoction with lentils is used to vomit after poison, and when a scorpion stings one who has eaten it, the matter grows worse {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.530}.
