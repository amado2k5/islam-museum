## Summary
The Arabic naming system, as a research file supplied to the museum presents it, is one of the most elaborate historical naming traditions: a name in it is not a first name and a hereditary family name but may record descent, family relationships, tribal affiliation, place of origin, occupation, religion, personal characteristics and standing. This page gives the system in outline, and its parts have their own pages: ism and kunya at [[concept/al-ism-wal-kunya|ism and kunya]], nasab and nisba at [[concept/al-nasab-wal-nisba|nasab and nisba]], laqab and khitab at [[concept/al-laqab-wal-khitab|laqab and khitab]]. The museum did not open the pages cited.
## What onomastics is
The study of names is called ilm al-asma, onomastics, and the summary holds it particularly important in Islamic historiography, genealogy, hadith transmission, manuscript studies and biographical literature {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names}. It says these conventions existed among Arabic-speaking Muslims, Christians and Jews, and were adopted by many non-Arab peoples within Islamic civilization.
## The five parts
| Part | Arabic | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ism | اسم | Personal or given name | Muhammad |
| Kunya | كنية | Parental designation or respectful nickname | Abu al-Qasim |
| Nasab | نسب | Genealogy or ancestral descent | ibn Abd Allah |
| Laqab | لقب | Epithet, nickname or honorific title | Salah al-Din |
| Nisba | نسبة | Geographical, tribal, occupational or other affiliation | al-Bukhari |

Not every person possessed all five, their order could vary, and a person might acquire further names through life {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names}.
## An example: Ibn Khallikan
The summary takes the historian and biographer [[person/ibn-khallikan|Ibn Khallikan]] (1211 to 1282): Shams al-Din Abu al-Abbas Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn Ibrahim ibn Abi Bakr ibn Khallikan al-Barmaki al-Irbili. Its analysis: Shams al-Din is the laqab, sun of the religion; Abu al-Abbas the kunya, father of al-Abbas; Ahmad the ism; ibn Muhammad ... ibn Khallikan the nasab; al-Barmaki a nisba claiming a Barmakid family affiliation; and al-Irbili a nisba for Erbil. For all the length of this name he is remembered as Ibn Khallikan, a designation associated with an ancestor, and classical sources give other explanations of its origin {cite:tdv-islam-ansiklopedisi-home} {cite:wafayat-ayan}. The summary's references are Abdulkerim Ozaydin's entry Ibn Hallikan in the TDV Islam Ansiklopedisi (1999) and Wafayat al-A'yan itself.
## Shuhra
The summary calls the name by which someone is predominantly known the shuhra, or ism al-shuhra, which is not an additional element but identifies which element or combination became the public name. So Abu Bakr is known by his kunya; Ibn Sina, Ibn Rushd and Ibn Khaldun by a designation from an ancestor; al-Bukhari and al-Tabari by a place nisba; al-Jahiz by a descriptive laqab; and Saladin by an honorific laqab {cite:bnf-kitcat-arabic-names}. This matters for historians, library cataloguers and digital databases.

## Open proposal
The file proposes that classical names be represented on person records as structured fields, not forced into a Western first-name and surname model: display name, Arabic display name, ism, kunya, nasab, laqab, nisbas, ancestral designation, shuhra, alternative spellings, full-name variants, references and certainty. Each element would carry multiple values with its own sources and context, and a nisba would link to a place, tribe, occupation, dynasty or school without asserting birth or biological descent {cite:onomasticon-arabicum-cnrs}. The museum has changed neither its schema nor any person record on this basis; the proposal awaits the founder's decision.
