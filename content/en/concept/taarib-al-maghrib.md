## Summary
What a research summary supplied to the museum says about the Arabisation of the Maghrib in two waves: a first, urban and coastal, that began with the founding of Kairouan in 670 CE and produced urban pre-Hilalian dialects, and a second, Bedouin, carried by the migrations of the Banu Hilal and Banu Sulaym in the 11th century and then the Ma'qil and the Banu Hassan between the 13th and 15th centuries, which Arabised the countryside and the steppes. The museum did not open the pages named, and the founder approved using the summary on 21 November 2026.

## What this page is
This page reads the Maghrib as a field of broad linguistic change that unfolded over centuries: from local languages, Amazigh mostly in the countryside and African Latin in the towns, to Arabic in two distinct kinds. It relays what the summary says, ascribed to its pages, and distinguishes the names and dates supported by Wikipedia from what the summary ascribes to a Cambridge book on the languages of the Maghreb, to a chapter by Paul Love of 2018 on Ibadi communities, to the UNESCO page on Kairouan and the Metropolitan Museum page, and from what the Encyclopaedia Britannica said on its Maghrib page, which the museum read itself. The page does not decide the historians' disagreement about the size of the Hilalian migrations.

## The first wave: pre-Hilalian urban Arabic, 7th to 10th centuries
The summary says Arabic first entered the Maghrib through urban and coastal settlements, beginning with the founding of Kairouan in 670 CE and then Tunis and other garrisons {cite:cambridge-the-languages-of-the-maghreb} {cite:wikipedia-arabization}. This phase ended African Romance in the cities of North Africa and produced settled urban pre-Hilalian dialects concentrated in urban centres and nearby coastal pockets, while most of the rural interior remained Amazigh-speaking {cite:wikipedia-arabization} {cite:wikipedia-pre-hilalian-arabic-dialects}. For comparison with what the summary treats as an old kind tied to Sicily see [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-al-andalus-wa-siqilliyya|Arabic in al-Andalus and Sicily]].

## Kairouan in 670
For the founding of Kairouan in 670 the summary names two non-Wikipedia sources: the UNESCO World Heritage List page, reference 499, an inscription of 1988, and the page of the Metropolitan Museum's Timeline of Art History on Western North Africa, the Maghrib, 500 to 1000 CE, which says Uqba ibn Nafi founded Kairouan in 670 {cite:unesco-whc-kairouan-499} {cite:met-heilbrunn-western-north-africa-maghrib-500-1000}. The city has a [[place/qayrawan|record]], and Uqba a [[person/uqba-ibn-nafi|record]]. The page notes that the details ascribed to the two pages are what the summary relayed, and the museum did not open them.

## The second wave: the Banu Hilal, the Banu Sulaym and after
The summary says that in the mid-11th century the Fatimid Caliphate in Cairo dispatched large Bedouin Arab confederations, chiefly the Banu Hilal and Banu Sulaym, into Ifriqiya against the Zirid dynasty, followed later by the Ma'qil and the Banu Hassan between the 13th and 15th centuries {cite:wikipedia-arabization} {cite:wikipedia-arab-migrations-to-the-maghreb}. Unlike the earlier urban wave, the Hilalian migrations spread Bedouin Arabic, the Hilalian dialects, across the rural plains, steppes and Saharan fringes, linguistically Arabising much of the countryside and shaping modern Maghrebi Arabic {cite:wikipedia-hilalian-dialects} {cite:wikipedia-maghrebi-arabic}. The museum has [[event/hilali-into-ifriqiya-440|The Hilalis enter Ifriqiya in 440]], the polities [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid Caliphate]] and [[polity/zirid-dynasty|Zirid dynasty]], and the places [[place/ifriqiya|Ifriqiya]] and [[place/tunisia|Tunisia]]. For these migrations the summary names a non-Wikipedia source: the chapter by Paul M. Love Jr. in Ibadi Muslims of North Africa, Cambridge University Press, 2018, titled Ibadi Communities in the Maghrib {cite:love-ibadi-communities-in-the-maghrib-2018}.

## Maghrebi Arabic today
The summary ties modern Maghrebi Arabic to both waves, but says the Hilalian one shaped it more {cite:wikipedia-maghrebi-arabic} {cite:wikipedia-hilalian-dialects}. For the dialects in detail see [[concept/lahjat-al-maghrib|The dialect of the Maghrib]], and for the countries [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-tunisia|Arabic in Tunisia]], [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-morocco|Arabic in Morocco]], [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-algeria|Arabic in Algeria]], [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-libya|Arabic in Libya]] and [[concept/al-arabiyya-fi-mauritania|Arabic in Mauritania]]. The figures for speakers that another summary supplied are on those pages, and this page adds nothing to them.

## How the sources differ
The page has sources of several kinds: Wikipedia pages on Arabization, the pre-Hilalian dialects, the migrations, the Hilalian dialects and Maghrebi Arabic, which support names, dates and places; the Cambridge book on diglossia and language contact, chapter The languages of the Maghreb; the Love chapter; the UNESCO and Metropolitan Museum pages on Kairouan; and the Encyclopaedia Britannica, which the museum read directly and which says that after the conquest of the 7th and 8th centuries the Arabs imposed Islam and Arabic on the native peoples, in its word {cite:britannica-maghrib}. The museum does not treat that wording as contradicting the summary's two waves, but it notes the difference in tone and names each holder.

## What this page did not reach
The museum opened none of the pages the summary names except the Britannica page Maghrib. It gives no numbers for the Hilalian migrants or for the share of Arabs and Amazigh in any century, since the summary gives none. Historians disagree on the size of these migrations and on how to explain the linguistic shift; the summary did not reach this and the museum does not settle it. The dates, 670, the mid-11th century and the 13th to 15th centuries, are as the summary gave them. The founder approved this use on 21 November 2026.
