## Summary
The phrase "praise belongs to God" opens the Fatiha. Sha'rawi holds that praise covers God's essence, attributes, favours, method and decree, and that God taught His servants praise in two words so that the learned and the unlettered stand equal in it. {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.55} {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.56}

## Favours before the human
Sha'rawi lists the favours that precede human existence: the sun, rain, air, the earth, the alternation of night and day, and the subjection of the cosmos to humans. He argues that no one creates these or can, and so one praises God for them. {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.56} {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.57} {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.58}

## Praise in heart and limbs
Sha'rawi holds that praise first passes through reason to grasp the meaning of favours, then settles in the heart, then moves the limbs so the believer stands in thanks. He lists everyday acts that call for praise: sleep and waking, food, transport, work and family. {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.61} {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.62}

## Praise for the decree
Sha'rawi says praise is owed even for what one dislikes, because God knows what is better for the person, and what he dislikes may be the good itself. {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.63}

## Author's view
This is Sha'rawi's reading and is attributed to him. The scan gives no other view on the sense of praise or on how it differs from thanks, and none has been checked. The edition is unverified.

## In al-Tabari's commentary: praise and "the worlds"
Al-Tabari explains that "al-hamd lillah" means thanks offered to God alone, to the exclusion of everything worshipped besides Him, for blessings beyond counting: the faculties made sound for obedience, the bodily members enabled to perform what is owed, provision in this world, and the means to lasting bliss. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 135}
He gives a report from Ibn Abbas, through Abu Rawq and al-Dahhak, that Gabriel told the Prophet to say "al-hamd lillah", and that Ibn Abbas understood it as thanks to God, submission to Him, and acknowledgment of His blessing, guidance and origination. The editor grades the chain as weak. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 135}
On "the worlds" he says the word is a plural with no singular from its own form: each kind of creation is a world, and each generation within each kind is a world of its time, so humankind is a world and so are the jinn. This is the view of Ibn Abbas and Sa'id ibn Jubayr and the common view of the exegetes. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 143}

## In al-Tabari's commentary: praise and thanks
Al-Tabari gives the view that "al-hamd lillah" is praise of God for His names and attributes, and "ash-shukr lillah" is praise for His blessings, and reports a saying of Ka'b al-Ahbar that "al-hamd lillah" is praise of God. The editor holds the chain sound but of no weight, since it ends at Ka'b. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 137}
He holds that "hamd" and "shukr" may each stand in the other's place, so "al-hamd lillah shukran" is correct Arabic, and he cites a hadith of al-Aswad ibn Sari' that nothing is more beloved to God than praise, which the editor grades as sound. The article "al" on "hamd" makes it all praise belongs to God; without it the phrase would mean only the speaker's own praise. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 138}
The readers and scholars agree on the nominative "al-hamdu", and not the accusative, which would imply the meaning "I praise God with praise". Al-Tabari holds that God praised Himself and then taught His servants to say it, and that "iyyaka na'budu" is part of what God taught them to say, the speech being understood from its context as Arabs often leave out a verb of saying. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 139} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 140}
