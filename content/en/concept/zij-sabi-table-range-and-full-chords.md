## Summary
Al-Battani closes the chapter on chords by saying no more is needed than he has set down: for the half-chords it is enough to know those of the degrees from one to ninety, since what passes ninety up to 180 has a chord like that of ninety read in reverse, and the full chords come from half the arc, taking half those degrees, reading its half-chord from the table and doubling it. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.21}.

## A quarter is enough
He says there is no need in knowing arcs and chords for more than he has made clear: for the half-chords it is enough to know the chords of the degrees from one to ninety, since beyond ninety up to the full 180 the chord is that of the ninety read in reverse; likewise for the full chords nothing beyond the chords of the semicircle is needed, as those of the other half are those of 180 reversed. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.21}.

## The full chord from half the arc
To know the full chords from arcs and the arcs from chords: for the full chord of any degrees you like, take half those degrees, read its half-chord in the table, and add the result to itself; that is the full chord of those degrees. To find the arc of a full chord, take half that chord, find its arc in the table and double the arc, which is the arc of that full chord. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.21}.

## The half-chord and the complement
He adds that any of these half-chords, multiplied by itself and subtracted from the radius multiplied by itself, has as the root of the remainder the half-chord of what remains of the arc to a quarter of the circle. This is the relation of cosine to sine as the text puts it, and al-Battani then gives a further rule for half of what remains to complete the arc to the quarter, whose wording is uncertain in the printed text. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.21}.
