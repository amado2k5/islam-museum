## Summary
The Gulf here is the region of Arabia on the [[place/persian-gulf|Gulf]], with [[place/kuwait|Kuwait]], [[place/qatar|Qatar]], the [[place/united-arab-emirates|United Arab Emirates]] and [[place/oman|Oman]]. This page gathers what was read of the speech of the people of the Gulf in two sources: the Encyclopaedia Britannica's brief modern description, and pointers in al-Suyuti's al-Muzhir to the speech of the people of Bahrain, Oman and al-Shihr in the old days. The page gives no description of the features of the contemporary Gulf dialect, since the references read do not state them.

## The modern description
In its article on Qatar the Encyclopaedia Britannica says Arabic is the official language, that most Qataris speak a dialect of Gulf Arabic similar to what the people of the surrounding states speak, that Modern Standard Arabic is taught in schools, that English is commonly used, and that Persian and Urdu are often heard among the expatriates {cite:britannica-qatar}. In its article on Bahrain it says Arabic is the official language, that English is widely used and is a compulsory second language in every school, that Persian is also common though spoken mostly at home, and that expatriates include speakers of Urdu, Hindi and Tagalog; it does not describe Bahraini speech itself {cite:britannica-bahrain}. In its article on the Arabic language it says the chief dialect groups in modern times are those of Arabia, Iraq, Syria, Egypt and North Africa {cite:britannica-arabic-language}, so the Gulf falls within Arabia. The statements agree that Gulf dialect is close to those around it, and do not state its features.

## The Arabs of Bahrain and Oman in the books of language
In al-Muzhir, in the chapter on knowing the eloquent, al-Suyuti relays from al-Farabi that the language was not taken from Abd al-Qays and the Azd of Oman because they were in Bahrain mixing with India and the Persians {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.168}. In the chapter on knowing the poor and blameworthy among dialects he relays from al-Tha'alibi in Fiqh al-Lugha that lakhlakhaniyya occurs in the speech of the Bedouin of al-Shihr and Oman, and gives as its example their saying 'masha Allah kan' for 'ma sha'a Allah kan' {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.177}. These are old reports from authors whose books were not themselves read, and the heading of the chapter carries its authors' judgement on these dialects; the museum sets them out ascribed to their authors without adopting the blame. Abd al-Qays is a tribe with its own page, [[group/abd-al-qays|Abd al-Qays]].

## What this page does not reach
No description of the features of the contemporary Gulf dialect was found in the references read, whether of its sounds, grammar or vocabulary, nor a count of its speakers, nor differences between the dialects of Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, the Emirates and Oman, nor the effect of Persian or Indian languages on it; and no modern dialect survey was read. Of the Encyclopaedia's articles only those on Qatar and Bahrain were read, so what they say is not extended to the other Gulf states beyond what the pages themselves say. What is in al-Muzhir is old talk of some coastal tribes, not a description of the people of the Gulf today. For the general picture of the standard language and the spoken varieties see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]]. Other references on the Gulf, dialect surveys and studies of the history of the coastal Arabs, should be added to this page once read and their content is clear.
