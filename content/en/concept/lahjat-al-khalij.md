## Summary
The Gulf here is the region of Arabia on the [[place/persian-gulf|Gulf]], with [[place/kuwait|Kuwait]], [[place/qatar|Qatar]], the [[place/united-arab-emirates|United Arab Emirates]] and [[place/oman|Oman]]. This page gathers what was read of the speech of the people of the Gulf in two sources: the Encyclopaedia Britannica's brief modern description, and pointers in al-Suyuti's al-Muzhir to the speech of the people of Bahrain, Oman and al-Shihr in the old days. The page gives no description of the features of the contemporary Gulf dialect, since the references read do not state them. Sections on classification, main features and speaker numbers were later added from a research summary supplied to the museum, and they supersede the remark that none were found.

## The modern description
In its article on Qatar the Encyclopaedia Britannica says Arabic is the official language, that most Qataris speak a dialect of Gulf Arabic similar to what the people of the surrounding states speak, that Modern Standard Arabic is taught in schools, that English is commonly used, and that Persian and Urdu are often heard among the expatriates {cite:britannica-qatar}. In its article on Bahrain it says Arabic is the official language, that English is widely used and is a compulsory second language in every school, that Persian is also common though spoken mostly at home, and that expatriates include speakers of Urdu, Hindi and Tagalog; it does not describe Bahraini speech itself {cite:britannica-bahrain}. In its article on the Arabic language it says the chief dialect groups in modern times are those of Arabia, Iraq, Syria, Egypt and North Africa {cite:britannica-arabic-language}, so the Gulf falls within Arabia. The statements agree that Gulf dialect is close to those around it, and do not state its features.

## The Arabs of Bahrain and Oman in the books of language
In al-Muzhir, in the chapter on knowing the eloquent, al-Suyuti relays from al-Farabi that the language was not taken from Abd al-Qays and the Azd of Oman because they were in Bahrain mixing with India and the Persians {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.168}. In the chapter on knowing the poor and blameworthy among dialects he relays from al-Tha'alibi in Fiqh al-Lugha that lakhlakhaniyya occurs in the speech of the Bedouin of al-Shihr and Oman, and gives as its example their saying 'masha Allah kan' for 'ma sha'a Allah kan' {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.177}. These are old reports from authors whose books were not themselves read, and the heading of the chapter carries its authors' judgement on these dialects; the museum sets them out ascribed to their authors without adopting the blame. Abd al-Qays is a tribe with its own page, [[group/abd-al-qays|Abd al-Qays]].

## What this page does not reach
No description of the features of the contemporary Gulf dialect was found in the references read, whether of its sounds, grammar or vocabulary, nor a count of its speakers, nor differences between the dialects of Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, the Emirates and Oman, nor the effect of Persian or Indian languages on it; and no modern dialect survey was read. Of the Encyclopaedia's articles only those on Qatar and Bahrain were read, so what they say is not extended to the other Gulf states beyond what the pages themselves say. What is in al-Muzhir is old talk of some coastal tribes, not a description of the people of the Gulf today. For the general picture of the standard language and the spoken varieties see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]]. Other references on the Gulf, dialect surveys and studies of the history of the coastal Arabs, should be added to this page once read and their content is clear.

## Classification
Glottolog 5.3 places Gulf Arabic (identifier gulf1241, ISO 639-3 code afb) within Afro-Asiatic, Semitic, West Semitic, Central Semitic, Arabian, Arabic, Arabian Peninsula Arabic and North Arabian Beduin Arabic, and lists as its subvarieties Al-Hasaa, Bahraini Gulf Arabic, Khamseh, Kuwaiti Hadari Arabic, North Qatari Arabic, South Qatari Arabic and Zubair-Faau Arabic {cite:glottolog-gulf-arabic}. The free page of Ethnologue (29th edition, 2026) places it in Afro-Asiatic within the Arabic macrolanguage, as a language of wider communication whose origin it gives as Kuwait, Iraq, Qatar and Saudi Arabia {cite:ethnologue-afb}.

The Encyclopaedia Britannica places Arabic in Central Semitic and groups the modern dialects into five chief clusters, one of them Arabia {cite:britannica-arabic-language}; its article on Kuwait says Kuwaiti nationals speak Gulf Arabic alongside Modern Standard Arabic {cite:britannica-kuwait}. Brill's Encyclopedia of Arabic Language and Linguistics, in its entries on Bahraini Arabic and on Saudi Arabia, describes the dominant Gulf varieties (for example the Bahraini ʿArab, or "A", dialects) as Najdi in origin and Bedouin in type, spoken in Kuwait, eastern Saudi Arabia (al-Hasa and Qatif), Bahrain, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, and distinct from the older sedentary Baḥārna, or "B", dialects {cite:brill-eall-bahraini-arabic} {cite:brill-eall-saudi-arabia}. The Gumar Corpus page of New York University Abu Dhabi says Gulf Arabic in the strict sense covers the western Gulf coast and leaves out Omani, Hijazi, Najdi and Baḥārna speech {cite:nyuad-gumar-corpus}, while a university speech-language laboratory guide groups it as a Southern Arabic dialect, al-khaliji {cite:adelphi-celf-arab-american-children}. These framings draw the edge of "Gulf Arabic" in different places; the museum sets them side by side and does not choose between them.

## Main features
**Phonology.** Brill reports that Najdi-type Gulf dialects keep the interdental consonants (th, dh and emphatic dh) and merge the Classical dad into emphatic dh, whereas the coastal Baḥārna and Qatif dialects shift th to f, dh to d, and both dad and emphatic dh to dad {cite:brill-eall-bahraini-arabic} {cite:brill-eall-saudi-arabia}. Classical q becomes g (fronting to j, [dʒ], next to front vowels), k fronts to č, [tʃ], next to front vowels, and j shifts to y in the Gulf "A" dialects {cite:brill-eall-bahraini-arabic} {cite:adelphi-celf-arab-american-children}. The glottal stop is lost, the gahawa syndrome appears (ghawa for "coffee"), the diphthongs ay and aw become the long vowels ē and ō, and syllables of the shape (C)CV(:)(C)(C) are allowed {cite:brill-eall-bahraini-arabic} {cite:adelphi-celf-arab-american-children}.

**Morphology.** Nouns have no case endings and verbs have no dual agreement; possession is expressed analytically with māl(at) and ḥagg, alongside a vestigial -in, the old tanwīn {cite:brill-eall-bahraini-arabic} {cite:adelphi-celf-arab-american-children}. The feminine singular "you" suffix is -ič in the "A" dialects and -iš in Baḥārna {cite:brill-eall-bahraini-arabic} {cite:brill-eall-saudi-arabia}. The verbal prefix b- marks future or imminent intent, gāʿid marks continuous aspect, Form VII (in-) is the main passive, and quadriliteral verbs are productive {cite:brill-eall-bahraini-arabic} {cite:adelphi-celf-arab-american-children}.

**Syntax.** The unmarked word order is subject, verb, direct object, indirect object, adverb; negation uses the prefix ma- on verbs and mub, mu or mi before non-verbal predicates; definite relative clauses take illi with a resumptive pronoun {cite:adelphi-celf-arab-american-children} {cite:brill-eall-bahraini-arabic}. Questions use š- in the "A" dialects and wēš in the "B" dialects {cite:brill-eall-bahraini-arabic}.

**Lexicon.** Brill and Glottolog point to many loanwords from Persian, Turkish, Hindi-Urdu, Portuguese and English, a possible non-Arabic Semitic substrate in coastal and Bahraini speech, and kinship vocatives with reversed roles such as yābū-k {cite:brill-eall-bahraini-arabic} {cite:glottolog-gulf-arabic}.

## Speaker numbers
The research summary found no speaker count for Gulf Arabic. The free page of Ethnologue (29th edition, 2026) keeps exact first- and second-language counts behind a paywall, and Glottolog, Brill, Britannica, UNESCO and the university sites it consulted state none {cite:ethnologue-afb}. This page therefore gives no figure and does not estimate one.

## Common phrases
A research summary supplied to the museum added a comparison of sixty expressions and words across six Arabic dialects, including greetings, asking how someone is, asking what someone wants, and the numbers one to five. The reference variety here is Kuwaiti and Gulf Arabic. Questions are generally addressed to one male speaker. Transliteration is normalized for comparison, and Arabic spelling may be normalized where a source uses phonetic transcription rather than Arabic script. The MSA equivalents are supplied for comparison and are not necessarily quotations from the dialect sources.
