## Summary
The ten readings are the readings of the seven well-known imams of recitation, [[person/nafi-al-madani|Nafi']], Ibn Kathir, Abu Amr, Ibn Amir, Asim, Hamza and al-Kisa'i, together with three more: Abu Jafar, Yaqub and Khalaf. [[person/ibn-al-jazari|Ibn al-Jazari]] wrote [[work/al-nashr-fi-al-qiraat-al-ashr|al-Nashr fi al-Qira'at al-Ashr]] on them. {cite:nashr-dahman-damascus-1345-v1 p. 54} {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 491}

## The imams by city
Ibn al-Jazari says the imams devoted themselves to recitation and mastered it until people travelled to them and the people of their city agreed to accept their reading, and it was attributed to them for that devotion. In Medina were Abu Jafar Yazid ibn al-Qa'qa', then Shayba, then Nafi'; in Mecca Abdallah ibn Kathir; in Kufa Yahya ibn Wuthab, Asim and al-A'mash, then Hamza, then al-Kisa'i; in Basra Abdallah ibn Abi Ishaq, Isa ibn Umar and Abu Amr ibn al-Ala', then Asim al-Jahdari and Yaqub al-Hadrami; in Syria Abdallah ibn Amir. {cite:nashr-dahman-damascus-1345-v1 p. 18} {cite:nashr-dahman-damascus-1345-v1 p. 19} A list he quotes names Abu Jafar and Nafi' of Medina, Ibn Kathir of Mecca, Ibn Amir of Syria, Abu Amr and Yaqub of Basra, and Asim, Hamza and al-Kisa'i of Kufa; Khalaf completes the ten in Ibn al-Jazari's own count. {cite:nashr-dahman-damascus-1345-v1 p. 48}

## Recognition of the ten
Ibn al-Jazari reports that Abu al-Ala' al-Hamadhani named the ten readers whom people follow among the people of Hijaz, Syria and Iraq in his Ghaya, and that Ibn al-Salah, in a reply to a query that Abu Shama quotes, made mass transmission a requirement for a reading used in recitation, counted the seven as meeting it, and held what lies beyond the seven or the ten to be forbidden with a prohibition of criminalisation, not mere dislike. {cite:nashr-dahman-damascus-1345-v1 p. 48} He also relates that al-Wasiti came to Damascus about 730 AH and taught the ten; a man who knew only al-Shatibiyya and al-Taysir envied him and tried to have him stopped; the scholars of the time wrote on it, did not differ over its permissibility, agreed the ten readings are one, and differed only over calling anything beyond them irregular. {cite:nashr-dahman-damascus-1345-v1 p. 48}

## The three that complete the ten
Ibn al-Jazari, quoting his father, holds that the three readings of Yaqub, Khalaf and Abu Jafar ibn al-Qa'qa' do not depart from the script and that calling them irregular is entirely without footing. {cite:nashr-dahman-damascus-1345-v1 p. 54} He quotes Ibn Taymiyya that readings established from imams such as al-A'mash, Yaqub, Khalaf, Abu Jafar and Shayba stand on a par with those established from the seven for those who have them established. {cite:nashr-dahman-damascus-1345-v1 p. 50} Al-Suyuti, reporting al-Bulqini's division, calls the readings of the three that complete the ten solitary reports, a division he questions. {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 491}

## What the number rests on
Ten is not the limit of what was gathered: Ibn al-Jazari names earlier compilers who collected far more, such as al-Hudhali, Yusuf ibn Ali ibn Jubara, whose al-Kamil gathers fifty readings from the imams, and Abu Ma'shar al-Tabari, whose Suq al-Arus holds 1,550 reports and routes. {cite:nashr-dahman-damascus-1345-v1 p. 44} {cite:nashr-dahman-damascus-1345-v1 p. 45} For him the number counts famous imams, not ahruf that the revelation came in; see [[concept/tasbi-al-qiraat-ibn-mujahid|Ibn Mujahid's limiting to seven]] and [[question/are-the-seven-readings-the-seven-ahruf|Are the seven readings the seven ahruf?]]

## The ten readers, their narrators and the features of their readings
A research summary supplied to the museum lists the ten mass-transmitted qira'at, and ascribes the fixing of the seven to [[person/ibn-mujahid|Ibn Mujahid]], d. 324 AH, and the establishing of the remaining three as equally authentic, in al-Nashr fi al-qira'at al-ashr, to [[person/ibn-al-jazari|Ibn al-Jazari]], d. 833 AH. Each reading is named after its primary reader and transmitted by two main narrators; the differences concern pronunciation, lengthening, assimilation, softening of the hamza, vowel inclination and a limited number of word variants, all compatible with the Uthmanic rasm and with Arabic {cite:research-summary-qiraat-dialects-quran-2026 on ten qirat}. In the summary's detail:

- [[person/nafi-al-madani|Nafi' al-Madani]], Medina, d. 169 AH. Narrators Qalun and Warsh. Frequent softening of the hamza, especially in Warsh; moderate assimilation; limited imala; Warsh is noted for lengthened madd and softening of the ra'. It reflects Medinan and Hijazi facilitation.
- [[person/abdallah-ibn-kathir|Ibn Kathir al-Makki]], Mecca, d. 120 AH. Narrators al-Bazzi and Qunbul. A preference for clear articulation over heavy assimilation; softening of the second of two consecutive hamzas; minimal imala; full realisation of the hamza in many cases.
- [[person/abu-amr-ibn-al-ala|Abu Amr ibn al-Ala' al-Basri]], Basra, d. 154 AH. Narrators al-Duri and al-Susi. Extensive major assimilation across words; softening or deletion of the hamza in certain positions; some imala.
- Ibn Amir al-Shami, Damascus, d. 118 AH. Narrators Hisham and Ibn Dhakwan. Retention of both hamzas in many dual-hamza cases; moderate assimilation; some imala and sakt, especially in Hisham.
- [[person/asim-ibn-abi-al-najud|Asim ibn Abi al-Najud al-Kufi]], Kufa, d. 127 AH. Narrators Shu'ba (Abu Bakr) and Hafs. Predominantly full realisation of the hamza; very limited imala, with Hafs having essentially one famous instance, majraha. Hafs from Asim is the most widespread reading today, and the modern printed mushafs follow it.
- [[person/hamza-al-zayyat|Hamza al-Zayyat al-Kufi]], Kufa, d. 156 AH. Narrators Khalaf and Khallad. A distinctive treatment of the hamza, including sakt before certain hamzas; extensive imala; specific rules of assimilation and stopping.
- [[person/al-kisai|al-Kisa'i al-Kufi]], Kufa, d. 189 AH. Narrators Abu al-Harith (al-Layth) and al-Duri. Extensive imala, the inclination of the fatha toward the kasra; careful handling of the hamza; assimilation in line with Kufan practice.
- Abu Ja'far al-Madani, Medina, d. about 130 AH. Narrators Ibn Wardan and Ibn Jammaz. Close to Nafi' in the general rules, including a tendency to softening, with some distinctive assimilation and hamza treatments; he completes the Medinan pair, as Ibn al-Jazari confirms in al-Nashr.
- Ya'qub al-Hadrami al-Basri, Basra, d. 205 AH. Narrators Ruways and Rawh. Follows Abu Amr closely in many general rules, including assimilation, with specific hamza and vowel realisations; he completes the Basran pair.
- Khalaf al-Ashir, Baghdad, d. 229 AH. Narrators Ishaq and Idris. Aligns with Kufan practice, nearing Hamza and al-Kisa'i in imala and hamza; he stands as the tenth by independent transmission.

The usul and farsh, the general rules and the word-specific variants, are treated systematically in Ibn al-Jazari's al-Nashr and his poem Tayyibat al-Nashr, and in earlier works such as al-Dani's al-Taysir and al-Shatibi's Hirz al-Amani. How these features relate to tribal dialects, for example imala, softening and assimilation, is discussed in works that analyse phonetic phenomena against the tribal tongues, and is taken up in [[concept/al-ahruf-al-sabaa|The seven ahruf]].

## The main works
The decisive reference on the ten is Ibn al-Jazari's al-Nashr fi al-qira'at al-ashr, with chains, usul, farsh and the relation to the ahruf. After it comes al-Suyuti's al-Itqan fi ulum al-Qur'an, which arranges the topics of the qira'at into types. Ibn Mujahid's Kitab al-Sab'a is the foundation for the selection of the seven. Supporting works are al-Dani's al-Taysir, al-Shatibi's Hirz al-Amani, the works of Makki ibn Abi Talib and their later commentaries. For modern overviews there are entries in the Encyclopaedia of the Qur'an (Brill) under readings and related linguistic topics. The summary gives no page numbers because they vary by edition {cite:research-summary-qiraat-dialects-quran-2026 on main works}.
