## Summary
The black seed is the subject of a report in both Sahih collections in which the Prophet says 'in the black seed is a cure for every disease except al-sam', and al-sam is death {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 5688} {cite:sahih-muslim 5766}. Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya says it is the shuniz and counts the views that make it mustard or the fruit of the terebinth a mistake {cite:zad-al-maad p.701}; he reads 'every disease' as a general expression meant in a particular sense, every disease that admits it, and holds it beneficial for the cold diseases {cite:zad-al-maad p.701}.

## What the reports say
In the entry on the letter ha among the simple drugs, Ibn al-Qayyim cites Abu Hurayra's report in the two Sahih collections: 'use this black seed, for in it is a cure for every disease except al-sam' {cite:zad-al-maad p.701}. In al-Bukhari it appears as 'in the black seed is a cure for every disease except al-sam', with Ibn Shihab's gloss that al-sam is death and the black seed is the shuniz {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 5688} [[passage/bukhari-api-5688|al-Bukhari 5688]]; in Muslim with the same sense {cite:sahih-muslim 5766} [[passage/muslim-api-5766|Muslim 5766]]; and in al-Tirmidhi {cite:jami-al-tirmidhi 2041} [[passage/tirmidhi-api-2041|al-Tirmidhi 2041]]. Another report in al-Bukhari gives a prescription for a sick man: five or seven seeds, crushed and dripped into his nose with drops of oil, with the narrator citing 'A'isha for the saying {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 5687} [[passage/bukhari-api-5687|al-Bukhari 5687]].

## Which seed it is
Ibn al-Qayyim says the black seed is the shuniz in Persian, the black cumin, also called Indian cumin. He relays that al-Harbi reported from al-Hasan that it is mustard and that al-Harawi reported it to be the green seed, the fruit of the terebinth, and he calls both views a mistake, the right one being that it is the shuniz {cite:zad-al-maad p.701}. This agrees with Ibn Shihab's gloss in the al-Bukhari report {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 5688}.

## The meaning of 'every disease'
Ibn al-Qayyim holds that 'a cure for every disease' is like the wording used of the wind in the Qur'an [[passage/quran-46-25|al-Ahqaf 25]], 'everything' that is able to be destroyed; it is a general expression meant in a particular sense, every disease that responds to it {cite:zad-al-maad p.701}. He says it helps in all the cold diseases and enters the hot, dry ones incidentally, carrying the force of cooling, moist drugs to them quickly because it penetrates fast when a little is taken {cite:zad-al-maad p.701}. He cites the author of the Canon and others for adding saffron to camphor tablets for its quick penetration, and says that the benefit of a hot substance in certain hot diseases by a special property is familiar to those of the craft {cite:zad-al-maad p.701}. For the wider question of 'every disease has a remedy' see [[concept/al-tibb-al-nabawi|prophetic medicine]].

## Its uses as Ibn al-Qayyim reports them
Ibn al-Qayyim says it is hot and dry in the third degree, dispels bloating, drives out the worms called gourd-seeds (hubb al-qar'), helps against vitiligo, quartan fever and phlegmatic diseases, opens obstructions and dissolves winds {cite:zad-al-maad p.701}. Pounded and kneaded with honey and taken with hot water it helps against stones of the kidney and bladder and promotes urine, menstruation and milk if taken for some days [[concept/asal|honey]] {cite:zad-al-maad p.701}. He lists benefits of its oil, including as a nasal drop for palsy and tetanic spasm, and says its smoke drives off vermin {cite:zad-al-maad p.702}. He gives the dose as two dirhams and notes that some claimed that taking much of it is deadly {cite:zad-al-maad p.702}. These are Ibn al-Qayyim's statements of the medicine of his age; the museum reports them and recommends none of them.
