## Overview
The angels in Islam are creatures of God, and belief in them is a pillar of faith for the Sunnis and the Community {cite:aqida-tahawiyya §72}. They are honoured servants who do not disobey God in what He commands them and do what they are commanded, and they have duties assigned to them in the heavens and on earth: among them are the messengers bearing revelation such as Jibril and Mika'il, and among them the recorders of deeds, the bearers, and those charged with the destinies of creation.

## The lexical and technical origin
"Malak" in the language is used for a messenger and for the strong; from it come "muluk" (kings), and "mala'ika" (angels) is the plural of "malak"; it is said among the Arabs that "malak" is among the most hidden of names on the tongue for the height of its meaning {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.14974}. In the technical sense: a creature of God made of light, dwelling in heaven, who does not eat or drink, does not marry, and does not sleep, worships God and obeys Him {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.14975}.

## In the Qur'an
The Qur'an mentions of their attributes and works:
- Their creation before Adam and their prostration to him by God's command, and Iblis's refusal {cite:tanzil-quran} — [[passage/quran-2-34|Al-Baqara 34]], [[passage/quran-7-11|Al-A'raf 11]], [[passage/quran-15-29|Al-Hijr 29]], [[passage/quran-17-61|Al-Isra' 61]], [[passage/quran-38-71|Sad 71]], [[passage/quran-38-72|Sad 72]], [[passage/quran-38-73|Sad 73]], [[passage/quran-35-1|Fatir 1]].
- Their mission: they descend with revelation and the Books upon the prophets {cite:tanzil-quran} — [[passage/quran-2-97|Al-Baqara 97]], [[passage/quran-26-193|Al-Shu'ara' 193]], [[passage/quran-69-4|Al-Qari'a 4]], [[passage/quran-81-19|At-Takwir 19]], [[passage/quran-16-2|Al-Nahl 2]].
- Their worship: they glorify night and day without flagging, they do not disobey God in what He commands, and they seek forgiveness for the believers {cite:tanzil-quran} — [[passage/quran-21-20|Al-Anbiya' 20]], [[passage/quran-66-6|Al-Tahrim 6]], [[passage/quran-40-7|Ghafir 7]], [[passage/quran-7-206|Al-A'raf 206]].
- Their works: recording deeds, being charged with destinies, and guarding {cite:tanzil-quran} — [[passage/quran-50-17|Qaf 17]], [[passage/quran-50-18|Qaf 18]], [[passage/quran-82-10|Al-Infitar 10]], [[passage/quran-82-11|Al-Infitar 11]], [[passage/quran-82-12|Al-Infitar 12]], [[passage/quran-86-4|At-Tariq 4]], [[passage/quran-32-11|As-Sajda 11]], [[passage/quran-41-30|Fussilat 30]].
- Their presence in the great scenes: the Isra' and Mi'raj, reinforcing the Muslims, the Night of Decree {cite:tanzil-quran} — [[passage/quran-97-4|Al-Qadr 4]], [[passage/quran-8-9|Al-Anfal 9]], [[passage/quran-8-12|Al-Anfal 12]], [[passage/quran-3-124|Al 'Imran 124]].
- Their station: each of them has a known station, noble recorders, and Throne-bearers {cite:tanzil-quran} — [[passage/quran-37-164|As-Saffat 164]], [[passage/quran-69-17|Al-Haqqa 17]].

## In hadith and Sunna
- In creation: the reports say that God created the angels of light before He created Adam of clay, and there are hadiths about Jibril having existed two thousand years before Adam; the sound position is that the details of creation are among what God kept to Himself, and what is preserved is that their creation preceded Adam's {cite:sahih-muslim} {cite:mustadrak-al-hakim}.
- In worship: "the angels neither sleep nor flag" in glorification; they bless whoever blesses the Prophet, seek forgiveness for the believers night and day, and pray mercy for them {cite:sahih-al-bukhari} {cite:sahih-muslim}.
- In number: it is reported that seventy thousand of the people of the Frequented House circumambulate it and never return, and that around the Throne are places of angels too many to count; the counting is of the unseen {cite:sahih-al-bukhari}.
- In obedience: "God sends forth... and the angels... who do not disobey God in what He commands and do what they are commanded" is an established Qur'anic description; and in hadith that among them are the noble recorders, among them the keeper of the covenant, and among them those who roam the earth seeking gatherings of remembrance {cite:sahih-muslim} {cite:sunan-abi-dawud}.

## In the creed
The Tahawiyya creed states it in settled wording: "we believe in the angels and the prophets and the Books sent down upon the messengers, and we bear witness that they were upon manifest truth" {cite:aqida-tahawiyya §72}; Ibn Abi al-Izz's commentary elaborates the meaning of belief in them and their attributes {cite:sharh-tahawiyya-ibn-abi-al-izz}.
- Belief in them includes: that they are honoured servants who do not disobey God, and that denying their existence or reviling them is unbelief in the majority view {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.14978}.
- Those who deny them: denying the existence of the angels or calling them liars in what the Prophet brought is unbelief, and whoever reviles them is killed in the majority view {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.14978}.

## Their attributes and standing
- They do not eat or drink, do not marry, do not sleep, do not give birth and are not born {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.14975}.
- They have the ability to take form — they assume human shapes by God's permission, as in the story of Jibril with Maryam and the story of Jibril with the Prophet in the form of a Bedouin {cite:sahih-muslim} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.14976}.
- They have wings, measures and command as in the Qur'anic description, and they have works in which they differ: from revelation to the clouds to recording deeds to taking souls {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.14976}.
- That it never occurs to them to precede God in word or deed, as in the sacred hadith: "they speak nothing but truth" and in the Qur'anic etiquette "nor do they precede Him in speech; they act by His command" [[passage/quran-21-27|Al-Anbiya' 27]].

## Their functions in the Qur'an and hadith
The exegetes have gathered the functions of the angels in the Qur'an; among what has been reported: bearing the Throne, prostration to Adam, the mission of revelation, recording, guarding, descending with mercy and rain, blowing the trumpet, the Angel of Death, the keepers of Paradise and the Fire, the angels of reinforcement, the angels who bless the Prophet, and roaming the earth in gatherings of remembrance {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:27:30}.
- Every believer has with him two recording angels who write his deeds, and every son of Adam has angels who guard him {cite:sunan-abi-dawud} {cite:jami-al-tirmidhi}.

## Notes for researchers
The functions reported in the Qur'an and hadith are those mentioned above; any further detail about the names and numbers of those charged is related in books of tafsir and reports and needs a chain given with each claim.

## In the Prophet's biography
- Jibril is the angel who was most in the Prophet's company: he brought him the first revelation in the cave of Hira', ascended with him on the Mi'raj, reviewed the Qur'an with him in Ramadan, stood beside him on the day of Uhud, and came to him in the form of a Bedouin asking about Islam, faith and excellence {cite:sahih-al-bukhari} {cite:sahih-muslim}.
- The angels of Badr and Hunayn descended for reinforcement, and the angels of revelation went back and forth between heaven and earth on the nights when revelation came; the Isra' and Mi'raj are at [[event/isra-and-miraj|the Isra' page]], and the reinforcement at Badr at [[event/badr-battle-day|the Badr page]].

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 4)
The Sheikh proposes an idea he says occurred to him on the spot: to give the angel of evil deeds a day off, by avoiding on that day backbiting, tale-bearing, listening to evil and unlawful food {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:56:00}. He compares this to an employee who takes a day or two of leave {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:56:00}. He says that whoever keeps this up for two or three months would see that the angel is a pure being who has been wearied by him all his life, writing against him since the age of fifteen {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:56:30}. He urges lengthening these days to two and then three, and putting to work the angel of good deeds, whom he says has been on leave for thirty years, by having him write down prayer, remembrance of God, good works, Qur'an reading and night prayer (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep04 00:57:00}.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 9)
The Sheikh holds that the believer swims against the current in this life, and that the angels take his soul so that it swims in the dominion of the heavens and the gates of heaven are opened for it, as he explained in last Monday's lesson {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:27:00}. He asks what it is that the angels manage on God's behalf [[passage/quran-79-5|An-Nazi'at 5]] {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:27:00}. He says that when the functions of the angels in the Qur'an are gathered together they come to eighteen (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:27:30}. He names the first as the two angels who sit on the right and on the left, the angel of good deeds and the angel of evil deeds, who are with a person twenty-four hours a day [[passage/quran-50-18|Qaf 18]] {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:27:30}. He says that on the Day of Judgment the angels tell their Lord they did not worship Him as He deserves {cite:sahih-muslim}. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:37:30}. He relates that the Prophet's mule once bolted with him, the Companions said its temper had turned bad, and the Prophet denied it {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:37:30}. He reports from the Prophet that the mule bolted because it hears in the graves what takes place of punishment {cite:sahih-al-bukhari} {cite:sahih-muslim}. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:38:00}. He then cites the hadith "the heaven groans" and attributes it to Muslim {cite:sahih-muslim}. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:38:00}. He explains the groaning as the creak of timber in a ceiling under a heavy load {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:38:30}. He cites from the hadith that the heaven groans, that there is no space of four fingers in it without the forehead of an angel in prostration glorifying God, and that when they are gathered before their Lord on the Day of Judgment they say they did not worship Him as He deserves {cite:sahih-muslim}. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:38:30}. He says the Companions responded with hearing and obedience, unlike the Children of Israel {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:41:30}. Among the functions of the angels he lists the roaming angels on earth who look for gatherings of remembrance, and says that were the veil lifted we would see angels covering the heavens {cite:jami-al-tirmidhi} {cite:sunan-ibn-majah}. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:41:30}.

## With the exegetes
Ibn Kathir details their attributes, mentioning that they are angels charged with matters, that they possess for themselves neither harm nor benefit, and that God called them "messengers" in places because they carry His command to whom He wills {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir}. Al-Qurtubi holds in his Mufhim that belief in the angels entails affirming what the Prophet reported of their states and attributes {cite:mufhim-qurtubi-v7}.

## The word and its Qur'anic setting in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
This and the following sections summarise the Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, article Mala'ika, whose first part, on the Qur'an and Sunni Islam, is by D. B. Macdonald and whose second part, on Shi'ism, is by W. Madelung. The article is an academic account; where it reports what Sunni scholars hold it says so, and its own comparisons are kept apart from that.

The article explains mala'ika as the broken plural of a word that goes back to early north-west Semitic, with Ugaritic, Aramaic and Hebrew cognates meaning messenger, and no Akkadian parallel {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 236}. Arab lexicographers and commentators refer it to a root they take to be native to Arabic, while the scholar A. Jeffery, as the article reports, judged that the nearest source of the Arabic word was Ethiopic {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 236}. The singular is normally malak, without the hamza, and so always in the Qur'an; the article notes that both singular and plural are used only in the sense of angel {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 236}. It counts the plural as very frequent in the Qur'an and the singular as occurring twelve times, and it lists the contexts: people who ask that a revelation come by an angel rather than a human being, the women who took Yusuf for an angel because of his beauty, the statement that an angel's intercession does not avail, and two collective uses beside the Throne and in rows {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}. A dual form occurs twice, for [[concept/harut-and-marut|Harut and Marut]] and in the account of [[person/adam|Adam]] and his wife being tempted in the Garden to become angels {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 236} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}.

## Named angels and classes of angels
The article observes that the angel of death is mentioned in the Qur'an but not by name {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}. [[person/jibril|Jibril]], the angel of revelation, is named three times; elsewhere he is unnamed and described as the Faithful Spirit who brings the revelation down to the heart of Muhammad, as the one who appeared to him plainly, as the spirit sent to Maryam, and as the Holy Spirit by whom God supported [[person/isa-ibn-maryam|Isa]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}. [[person/mikail|Mika'il]] is named once as an angel of the same rank; in the traditions the two appear to Muhammad and instruct him, Mika'il is said never to laugh, and Muhammad called the two his viziers among the angels {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}. The article says that [[concept/israfil|Israfil]], the angel of the trumpet of resurrection, has no mention in the Qur'an or in the canonical traditions but a large place in eschatological legend {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}.

Among the classes it lists are the keeper of Hell, called Malik by the damned, and the guards of Hell, whose number is given as nineteen; the text states that they are angels, which the article reads as a guard against thinking they are devils, and calls them rough and violent {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}. Another class is the angels brought near to God, who glorify Him night and day without pause; al-Baydawi calls them the high ones and the cherubim, those around the Throne {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}. The Sura of the Angels describes them as messengers with wings in twos, threes and fours, a description the article says shaped later accounts and pictures {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}. They are also guardians over people who know and write down what people do, and are called the heavenly host, who guard the walls of heaven against the listening of the jinn and the devils {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}.

The article draws attention to the puzzling phrase pairing the angels with the Spirit, which al-Baydawi, it says, found hard to separate: he gives the Spirit as an angel set over spirits, or the whole kind of spirits, or Jibril, or a creation greater than the angels {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}.

## Munkar and Nakir and the questioning in the grave
According to the article, the Qur'an does not mention [[concept/munkar-and-nakir|Munkar and Nakir]], the two angels who visit the dead on the night after burial and question him about his faith {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}. The doctrine holds that for an unbeliever the grave then becomes a first stage of Hell, and for a believer a first stage of purgatory from which he may pass to Paradise at the Last Day, or even a first stage of Paradise if he is a saint {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}. The article says this teaching is called the questioning and also the [[concept/adhab-al-qabr|punishment of the grave]], is among the matters accepted on the authority of transmitted report, and rests on the implicit sense of Qur'anic passages and on explicit traditions, and it cites al-Taftazani's commentary on the creed of al-Nasafi and the Mawaqif of [[person/al-iji-adud-al-din|al-Iji]] with al-Jurjani's commentary {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}. It compares the teaching, in its own words, with the lesser judgement of Christian theology, and names [[person/ibn-qayyim-al-jawziyya|Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya]]'s [[work/kitab-al-ruh|Kitab al-Ruh]] for a fuller account {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}.

## Obedience, the light tradition and the question of Iblis
The article stresses that the Qur'an insists on the angels' complete submission: they are not too proud to serve, they do not tire, and they carry out what they are commanded, and at the creation of Adam they are set apart in this respect from him and his descendants {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}. It then asks whether this obedience amounts to impeccability, and finds the Qur'anic data in tension {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}. All the angels bowed to Adam except [[person/iblis|Iblis]]; if the exception was within the group, as al-Baydawi argues, Iblis was an angel and the angels are not impeccable, yet another passage says he was of the [[concept/jinn|jinn]] and that he departed from his Lord's command {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}. Iblis defends himself by saying he was made of fire and Adam of clay, and the jinn are accepted as made of fire, but the article says the Qur'an gives no statement on what the angels were made of {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237}. The accepted view that they were made of light rests, it says, on a tradition from [[person/aisha|Aisha]] in which the angels were created of light, the jinn of fire, and Adam of what was described to the Muslims {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 237} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 238}.

The article lists the ways scholars have reconciled the data. Iblis may have been a jinni in his actions but of the angels in species; Ibn Abbas is reported to have said that there was a kind of angels who reproduced and were called jinn, and that Iblis was one of them; or he was a jinni raised among the angels; or the jinn were among those commanded to bow; or some angels were not impeccable though impeccability is their general character, as most men are not guarded from sin though the prophets are {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 238}. A further proposal is that some angels differ from devils only in accidents and qualities, as virtuous and wicked men differ, and that Iblis belonged to this kind; on light and fire, al-Baydawi is reported as saying the terms are not to be taken too strictly and each can pass into the other {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 238}. The article also gives the scholastic discussion in the Mawaqif, where the objector to angelic impeccability cites the angels' remark about the creation of Adam and the case of Iblis, and the reply is that the texts on obedience cannot prove that every angel is kept from all sin at all times, so the point cannot be settled absolutely {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 238}. Al-Baydawi's own long discussion of the angelic nature ends, the article says, by referring knowledge of the matter to God alone {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 238}.

On Harut and Marut, the article reports the belief that the two yielded to sexual temptation and are confined in a pit near [[place/babil|Babil]], teaching magic to people, and the replies given in defence of angelic impeccability: that the Qur'an says nothing of any fall, that teaching magic is not practising it, and that they first warn those who come to them that they are only a temptation {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 238}. It adds, from the commentary of al-Maturidi on the Fiqh al-akbar attributed to Abu Hanifa, a tradition of a Muslim devil, a great-grandson of Iblis, who appeared to Muhammad and was taught some suras {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 238}.

## Sex, and the standing of angels and humans
The article notes that the story of Harut and Marut suggests angels possess sex even if they do not reproduce, but quotes the creed of al-Nasafi that they are not to be described as male or female {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 238}. Al-Taftazani and the other commentators, it says, hold that there is neither transmitted authority nor rational proof on the point, so it should be left aside; the angels may have sex and not use it, and the human being, who must rule his lust and anger, has the higher potential for excellence {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 238}.

The second question that scholastic theology considered was the relative excellence of angels and humans. The article gives al-Nasafi's threefold statement: the messengers of mankind are better than the messengers of the angels, the messengers of the angels are better than ordinary people, and ordinary people are better than ordinary angels {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 238}. Al-Taftazani, it says, treats the middle claim as agreed and defends the other two by the angels' prostration to Adam, Adam being taught the names, God's choosing of prophets over all the worlds, and human achievement despite the hindrance of lust and anger {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 238}. The Mu'tazila, the philosophers and some Ash'aris held the angels to be superior, arguing that they are spirits free of matter and of the flaws of lust and anger, that prophets learn from them, that they are sometimes named before the prophets, and that a verse about the Messiah and the angels not disdaining servanthood implies their superiority; the article records the answers, among them that the philosophical description rests on philosophical rather than Muslim principles, that the angels are intermediaries while the prophets learn from God, and that the verse aims at the Christian claim that Isa is a son and not a servant {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 238}.

## Cosmology, the mystics and other views
The article describes [[work/ajaib-al-makhluqat|al-Qazwini's Aja'ib al-makhluqat]] as giving an objective account of the angels in all their classes, fitting the statements of the Qur'an and the Sunna into the Aristotelian and Neoplatonic universe of spheres, and says angels are counted as living inhabitants of the heavens but not among the animals, noting that al-Damiri includes people and jinn in his book on animals but not the angels {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 238}. It names [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]] as treating the mystery of angelic nature in smaller treatises, as part of the wider question of the nature of the spirit, and in the [[work/mishkat-al-anwar|Mishkat al-anwar]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 238} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 239}. Muslim literature, the article adds, also records the views of non-Muslims, among them philosophers, Christians, dualists and idolaters, briefly in al-Baydawi and at more length in al-Tahanawi's dictionary of technical terms {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 239}.

## The angels in Shi'i teaching
The second part of the article, by Madelung, describes the Imami view, which is a different tradition from the Sunni account above and is given here as that school's own teaching {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 239}. In Imami [[group/shia|Shi'ism]], angels are closely associated with the Imams. Imami doctrine consistently held that the Imams, like the prophets, are more excellent before God than the angels, with whom they share divine protection from sin and error, and leading theologians such as [[person/al-shaykh-al-mufid|al-Shaykh al-Mufid]] wrote treatises supporting this {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 239}. The Imams are nevertheless guided and aided by angels {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 239}.

A well-known Imami tradition says the Imams could hear the angels but not see them, unlike the messenger prophets, who saw angels awake and spoke with them, and ordinary prophets, who heard and saw them in sleep; other traditions say the Imams also see angels, and the restriction was taken to apply only at the moment of receiving divine instruction through an angel {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 239}. A tradition ascribed to the Imam Ja'far says the angels regularly visit the Imams, tread on their beds, attend their tables, spread their wings over the Imams' children, keep beasts from them and join them in every prayer {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 239}. At the appearance of the Twelfth Imam, according to Imami belief, angels will appear in the sky and call out his name, and Jibril and Mika'il will rally the faithful to pledge allegiance to him {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 239}. Imami doctrine also gives Munkar and Nakir a positive counterpart, a pair sent to comfort the saintly dead; some say it is the same pair changing function, others a different pair {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 239}. The OCR of the name of this counterpart is damaged, so it is not given here.

## The angels in Isma'ili teaching
In Isma'ilism, the article says, the ranks of the spiritual world are sometimes described as angels {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 239}. The triad of Jadd, Fath and Khayal, which mediates between the Universal Intellect and Soul and the prophets and Imams in the physical world, is commonly identified with the archangels Jibril, Mika'il and Israfil {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 239}. An early Isma'ili cosmogony names seven cherubim created from the light between the first two principles, followed by a group of twelve spiritual beings; in later Tayyibi Isma'ilism the third to ninth Intellects are called the seven cherubim {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 239}. Isma'ili doctrine also knows angels of a more conventional kind, all of one substance and differing only in name according to function, some in the spiritual world, some in the heavenly spheres and some in the physical world to preserve its regions, and seen only by prophets and those who rise spiritually to become like them {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 239}.

## In the context of the unseen
Created from light, never disobeying God; among them the Throne-bearers, the noble recorders, and Jibril the trustee of revelation.

## Notes
- Their number is uncountable; counting them is of the unseen, and the Qur'an and hadith have established their existence and works without counting them out.
- Each of them has a name and a charge, as in books of tafsir and hadith, and some names are reported in the athar rather than the Qur'an, such as Ridwan and Malik, keeper of the Fire.
