## Summary
This page sets out the place of [[language/arabic|Arabic]] in Djibouti as it appears in the Encyclopaedia Britannica article on Djibouti and in the Djibouti page of The World Factbook. It ascribes each statement to its page and estimates no number of speakers. The museum has a place record for the state: [[place/djibouti|Djibouti]]. The constitutional text is added in its original wording, as supplied in a research summary that the museum did not check against the page.

## Official status
The infobox on the encyclopedia's Djibouti page lists the official languages as Arabic and French {cite:britannica-djibouti}. The Factbook says: French (official), Arabic (official), Somali, Afar {cite:cia-factbook-djibouti}. In its article on the Semitic languages the encyclopedia says Arabic is official in Djibouti alongside French {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}. The three sources agree that Arabic and French are official, and the Factbook adds Somali and Afar to its list.

## Constitutional text
The Constitution of Djibouti of 1992, as revised in November 2025, Article 1 and Article 93. The research summary supplied to the museum gives the following Arabic wording. Article 1: «لغتاها الرسميتان هما العربية والفرنسية.», which the museum translates: "Its two official languages are Arabic and French" Article 93: «يُسجَّل هذا الدستور ويُنشر باللغتين الفرنسية والعربية في الجريدة الرسمية لجمهورية جيبوتي، ويكون النص الفرنسي هو المعتمد.», which the museum translates: "This Constitution is registered and published in French and Arabic in the Official Gazette of the Republic of Djibouti, and the French text is the authoritative one". The summary names the Journal Officiel de Djibouti and the Presidency of Djibouti as the official source; the museum did not open that page, so the wording is taken from the summary as supplied, and the founder approved using it so on 21 November 2026 {cite:official-constitution-djibouti} {cite:official-constitution-djibouti-presidency} {cite:alaraby-djibouti-constitution-1992}. The first quotation, from Article 1, is the Arabic rendering that the summary cites from an archived copy at Al-Araby Al-Jadeed; it also names the Presidency's page for the French text. The second, from Article 93, is the Arabic of the Official Gazette as published after the November 2025 revision. Article 93 makes the French text the authoritative one.

## Language shares
Spoken languages, multiple responses allowed, resident population aged 5 and over (N = 882,594): Somali 78.4%, French 41.6%, Arabic 26.6%, Afar 25.8%. Issued by INSTAD in the third general census, RGPH-3, 2024, published in November 2025, Tome 4, pages 33 to 35. The summary notes that the Arabic figure combines speakers of French and Arabic (21.6%) and of Arabic only (5.1%), and that the French figure combines French and Arabic (21.6%) and French only (20.2%). Mother tongue only: no official figure. The address the summary gives for the report is a google.com search link that wraps the INSTAD address; the underlying address is recorded as it appears in that link, and the museum has not verified that it works. This section comes from a research summary supplied to the museum; the museum did not open the pages it names, and the founder approved using the summary as supplied on 21 November 2026 {cite:instad-rgph3-tome4-socioculturelles}.

## The standard and the spoken
The two pages say nothing on the spoken Arabic of Djibouti or the difference between the standard and the colloquial, and name no dialect, so the museum sets no classification; for the duality in general see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Fusha and the dialects]]. What the encyclopedia says of Arabs in the country is that Djibouti city is home to a long-established community of Yemeni Arabs {cite:britannica-djibouti}. The Factbook gives the ethnic category called other, 5 percent, as mostly Yemeni Arab, also French, Ethiopian and Italian {cite:cia-factbook-djibouti}; that is the Factbook's classification.

## Other languages in the country
The encyclopedia says the two largest groups are the Somali and the Afar, whose languages are related Eastern Cushitic languages that are not mutually intelligible, that the Afar language forms a dialect continuum with Saho, and that the Somali are concentrated in the capital and the southeastern quarter {cite:britannica-djibouti}. The Factbook says the Somali are 60 percent and the Afar 35 percent, with 5 percent other {cite:cia-factbook-djibouti}. These are the Factbook's figures for ethnic groups, not shares of speakers. Compare [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|the languages of the tribes]], and for the Somali language in its wider setting see [[language/somali|Somali]].

## From the history of the language
The encyclopedia says the country was known as French Somaliland from 1896 to 1967 and as the French Territory of the Afars and Issas from 1967 to 1977, and took the name Djibouti when it gained independence from France on 27 June 1977 {cite:britannica-djibouti}. These are statements of history, not about Arabic. For the country among the Arab states, and its joining the League in 1977 as the encyclopedia states, see [[concept/al-alam-al-arabi|the Arab states and the League]].
