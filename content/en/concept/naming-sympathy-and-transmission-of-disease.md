## Summary
In the eighth chapter Ibn Sina closes his treatment of diseases with the ways diseases are named, the sympathy of organs, the six ranks of bodies, the passing of diseases one into another, and contagious, inherited and regional diseases {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.110-111}.

## How diseases are named
Diseases are named in several ways: from the organs that bear them, as pleurisy and pneumonia; from their symptoms, as epilepsy; from their causes, as a black-bile disease; from likeness, as the lion's disease and elephantiasis; from the first person said to have suffered it, as the Tylanian ulcer; from a town where it is frequent, as the Balkh sores; from someone famous for success in treating it, as the Cyrotian ulcer; or from their substance and essence, as fever and tumour {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.110}.

## Apparent, hidden and shared
He reports Galen's view that diseases are either apparent and known by sense, or hidden and easy to find out, as pains of the stomach and lung, or hard, as afflictions of the liver and the lung's passages, or not perceivable except by conjecture, as afflictions of the urinary passages. An organ shares another's disease because the two are joined by nature through instruments, as brain and stomach by the nerve and womb and breast by veins; or one is a way to the other, as the groin for a tumour of the leg; or they are neighbours, as lung and brain, especially when one is hot and weak and receives the surplus of its fellow, as the armpit does from the heart; or one is the noble source for the other's act, as the diaphragm for the lung in breathing; or one serves the other, as the nerve serves the brain; or both share a third organ, as brain and kidney through the liver. Sympathy may come back: if the stomach does not share with the brain and its digestion weakens, it sends up bad vapours and undigested food and increases the brain's own pain {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.110}.

## Ranks of bodies
The ranks of bodies in health and disease are six: a body in extreme health; one in health short of the extreme; one neither healthy nor ill; the upright body that quickly takes health; the body mildly ill; and the body extremely ill. Every disease is either yielding, where nothing stops its being treated as it should be, or not yielding, where an obstacle accompanies it that does not allow right management, as a headache joined with catarrh {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.110}.

## Passing from one to another
A disease that suits the temperament, age and season is less dangerous than one that does not, for an unsuited one arises only from a great cause, and the diseases of each season are hoped to dissolve at its start from the surpluses. Some diseases pass into others and depart, with good in it, so that one disease cures others, as quartan fever often cures epilepsy, gout, varices, joint pains, scab, itching, pustules and spasm; likewise a flux of the bowels cures ophthalmia, slipperiness of the intestines and pleurisy, and opening of the anal veins helps every black-bile disease and pain of the hip, kidneys and womb. Some passings make matters worse, as pleurisy passing to pneumonia and phrenitis to lethargus {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.110}.

## Contagious and inherited
Some diseases are contagious, such as leprosy, scab, smallpox, pestilential fever and putrid ulcers, especially in crowded dwellings or when the neighbour is downwind; ophthalmia, especially for one who looks into the eye; toothache, so that imagining something sour brings it on; pannus; and vitiligo. Some are inherited in the line, such as natural baldness, vitiligo, gout, pannus and leprosy, and some are regional and belong to a tribe or the people of a district or are frequent among them. Weakness of organs follows bad temperament or the dissolving of the structure {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.111}.
