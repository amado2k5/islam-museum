## Summary
In the fourteenth chapter, on the causes that lie in the motions of the soul, Ibn Sina shows that all the soul's accidents are followed or accompanied by a movement of the spirit, outward or inward, suddenly or little by little {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.131}.

## Outward and inward
Movement of the spirit outward is followed by coldness of the interior; if it overshoots, the spirit is dissolved at once and interior and exterior grow cold, followed by fainting or death. Movement inward is followed by coldness of the exterior and heat of the interior; it may be choked by the severity of its confinement, so that exterior and interior grow cold, followed by a great faint or death {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.131}.

## Examples
Outward at once, as in anger; outward gradually, as in pleasure and moderate joy. Inward at once, as in fright; inward gradually, as in grief. The choking and dissolving he mentioned follow only what comes at once, whereas the wasting and withering of the innate heat follow what comes little by little {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.131}.

## Two movements together
The spirit may move in two directions at once when the accident brings two others with it, as with anxiety, which may bring anger and grief so that the two movements differ. So with shame, which first draws inward, then reason and judgement return and spread out what was drawn in, so it rises outward and the colour reddens {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.131}.

## Imaginings of the soul
The body may also be acted on by states of the soul such as imaginings, which stir natural things: a child may resemble the one whose image the parent pictures at intercourse; the blood may follow its motion in one prepared for it who gazes much at red things; a person's teeth are set on edge when another eats something sour; he feels pain in a limb when another's like limb pains him and startles him; and the temperament changes through picturing what is feared or what is gladdening. He says that some may recoil from accepting such states because they have not come upon the obscure states of existence, while those who dive deep in knowledge do not deny them as impossible {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.131}.
