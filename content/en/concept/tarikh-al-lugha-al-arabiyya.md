## Summary
This page gathers what the sources read say about the history of [[language/arabic|Arabic]]: the disagreement over the oldest inscriptions ascribed to it, the Encyclopaedia Britannica's description of the Qur'anic language, Classical Arabic, Modern Standard Arabic and the spoken varieties, what [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]] and [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] report about the language after the conquests, and what [[person/al-suyuti|al-Suyuti]] relays from [[person/al-farabi|al-Farabi]] on the tribes from whom the language was taken. Each statement is ascribed to its speaker.

## The oldest inscriptions
In its article on the Arabic alphabet the Encyclopaedia Britannica says the alphabet probably developed in the 4th century CE as a direct descendant of the Nabataean alphabet, and that its origins and early history are vague. It reports that some scholars believe the earliest surviving example of Arabic script is a royal funerary inscription of the Nabataeans dating from 328 CE, while others hold that this inscription shows characteristics of Arabic but is essentially Aramaic, and that the earliest surviving Arabic is a trilingual inscription in Greek, Syriac and Arabic dating from 512 {cite:britannica-arabic-alphabet}. The article does not name the two inscriptions. The notes of this study, taken from Wikipedia pages that are not among the sources registered here, identify the first as the Namara inscription and the second as the Zabad inscription, and date the bilingual Arabic-Greek Harran inscription to 567–568; these identifications are given without citation, as notes not documented in the registered sources. For the script itself see [[concept/khatt-al-arabi|the Arabic script]] and [[group/al-nabt|the Nabataeans]].

## The Qur'anic language, Standard Arabic and the spoken varieties
The Britannica describes the language of the Qur'an as the ideal archetype, the fusha or most eloquent language, says Classical Arabic closely approaches it, and says Modern Standard Arabic differs only slightly from Classical in style and vocabulary and is used in most publications and formal broadcasts. It adds that the spoken varieties differ significantly, that some are mutually unintelligible, and that such variation may precede the written language by centuries {cite:britannica-arabic-language}. For more see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]] and [[concept/al-arabiyya-lughat-al-quran|Arabic as the language of the Qur'an]].

## The language after the conquests
Ibn Khaldun says in the Muqaddima that the Arabs' faculty had been the best of faculties; when Islam came and they left the Hijaz to seek the dominion held by other nations and mixed with the non-Arabs, the faculty changed through the deviations that reached the ear, because hearing is the father of linguistic faculties; scholars feared the language would be corrupted and the Qur'an and hadith closed to understanding, so they derived the rules of grammar {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.754}. He holds that the Arabic of the Bedouin of his own time is an independent language, different from that of Mudar and Himyar, lacking only the indication of agent and object by case endings and replacing it with word order and contextual cues, and that clarity and eloquence are greater and more deeply rooted in the Mudar tongue {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.766}. He also holds that the speech of the settled people is a language standing by itself, unlike Mudar's, and that its distance from the first language comes from mixing with the non-Arabs {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.770}.

Ibn Taymiyya relates in Iqtida' al-Sirat al-Mustaqim that when the early Muslims settled in Syria, Egypt, Iraq, Khurasan and the Maghrib, where the people of Egypt spoke Greek, those of Iraq and Khurasan Persian and those of the Maghrib Berber, they made the people of these lands accustomed to Arabic until it prevailed among Muslim and non-Muslim; later people grew lax and became accustomed to Persian in Khurasan until Arabic was abandoned among many of them {cite:iqtida-aql-1999 1 p.526}. This is his account and opinion, and the museum does not confirm it from another source.

## What al-Suyuti relays from al-Farabi
In al-Muzhir al-Suyuti relays from al-Farabi that the tribes from whom Arabic was taken and followed were Qays, Tamim and Asad, then Hudhayl and some of Kinana and of Tayyi', and that nothing was taken from others {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.167}. He lists tribes not drawn on because of their contact with other nations, and says that only the people of Basra and Kufa among the Arab cities transmitted the language and wrote it down in a book and made it a science and a craft {cite:muzhir-mansur-1998 1 p.168}. This is as al-Suyuti gives it; al-Farabi's own book was not read. See [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|the dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]].

## What this page does not reach
The inscriptions themselves and the specialist studies on them were not read; only the Britannica article was, which sets out the disagreement over the oldest without naming them. No dates separating Classical Arabic from Modern Standard Arabic were found in the sources read. Ibn Khaldun's chapters on rhetoric and literature were read only at their openings, and al-Farabi's book was not read. The page has no history of the spoken varieties before what Ibn Khaldun and the Britannica say.
