## Summary
In his commentary on verses 94 to 100 of al-A'raf, [[person/rashid-rida|Rida]] speaks of God's ways (sunan) among nations: how nations perish, when they endure, and what preserves them. {cite:tafsir-manar-v09 p.20}

## The righteous remnant
Rida holds that every nation has a remnant of the righteous, who serve as God's proof against its peoples, and that when this remnant is small in a nation, corruption prevails over it and God's retribution draws near. He gives as an example what happened to some Muslims of al-Andalus, whose descendants apostatised. {cite:tafsir-manar-v09 p.20} {cite:tafsir-manar-v09 p.5}

## The root of religion and the happiness of this world
Rida states that the root of religion requires happiness in this world before the next, from the very beginning of human creation, and cites what was sent down to Noah and Hud. He holds that these verses are proofs against those who claim that religion is a cause of weakness and poverty. {cite:tafsir-manar-v09 p.23}

## Blessings
Rida gives two meanings of the blessings in [[passage/quran-7-96|Al-A'raf 96]]: that the meaning is the blessings of revelation and faith, or rain and plants. He holds that the benefit of faith is to perfect human nature in spirit and body, and its purpose is the happiness of both abodes. {cite:tafsir-manar-v09 p.22}

## What caused the punishment
Rida holds that the punishment in these verses was caused by polytheism, superstitious practices and sins that corrupt the order of society, not because religion itself is a cause of destruction. {cite:tafsir-manar-v09 p.23}

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "History Taught Me"
[[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] opened his programme [[work/allamani-al-tarikh-program|"History Taught Me"]] with a whole episode on the sunan, which he made the frame for the stories that follow: the fall of al-Andalus and of Baghdad, the rising of al-Husayn and the conquest of Constantinople. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:02:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:03:00} He says that God told people the stories of those before them so that they would reflect on them and draw the lesson, and that a community that does not read its history cannot know its present or plan its future. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:02:30} In his view all the great events are governed by laws which the Qur'an calls divine sunan and cosmic sunan, and the programme is not for entertainment but for knowing "the laws that made history". {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:02:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:03:00}

### Two kinds of law
Al-Suwaidan holds that everything in the world, from rainfall, wind and volcanoes, to the stages of a person's creation in the womb, to the rise and fall of nations, their strength and weakness, their survival and extinction, happens neither by chance nor at random but according to general, precise and strict laws. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:04:30} He divides them into two: a material side, such as gravity, physics, mathematics, chemistry and biology, and a civilisational, human side. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:04:30} The material side is not disputed among people of learning; the Qur'an points to it and commands people to look at the heavens and the earth, and knowing it is open to believer and unbeliever alike, each according to his effort and research. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:05:30} He regrets that the community was once the master of the world in this field and then fell behind, so that West and East now lead it while the community sits "in the middle, watching and importing", and he hopes it will rise in it again. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:06:00}

The second side concerns human beings as individuals, nations and groups: the states they are in and what follows from them, whether ease or hardship of living, progress or backwardness, strength or weakness, even survival or extinction, and recompense in the hereafter as well. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:07:00} He calls these "civilisational laws", as against "cosmic laws". {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:07:30} He points out that the Qur'an does not call them laws but sunna, plural sunan. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:07:30}

### Their marks: constancy, regularity, generality
Al-Suwaidan describes God's sunna as constant, regular and general, citing verses saying that no change or turning aside will be found in God's sunna. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:08:00} He takes as an example the command to "take heed" that comes after the account of the expulsion of the Banu al-Nadir: what befell them befell them because they went against God's sunan, and the point is not to do as they did lest the same befall the doer, or else telling their story would have no meaning. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:08:30} He cites a verse commanding people to travel through the earth and see the end of those who denied, and explains it as saying that what happened to them will happen to whoever follows their way. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:09:00} For him the generality of the sunan means they apply to everyone "without favouritism or distinction", and he cites the Qur'an's address to the unbelievers of Quraysh asking whether they are better than the nations destroyed before them or hold a written exemption; there is, he says, no "intercession" in these laws. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:09:30} Were it not for their constancy, regularity and generality, in his view, the stories of the prophets and the reports of past nations would have no meaning. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:09:30}

### How they are known
Al-Suwaidan says the main source for knowing the civilisational laws is history; some come "ready-made" in the Qur'an and the Sunna, but not all, since God commanded people to look, reflect and search among past nations. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:11:30} He recommends three readings of the Qur'an: one to complete daily portions and full recitations, one with a commentary at least once to understand the meaning, and a slow reading after understanding the commentary in order to search for the sunan, a search he counts among religious duties because it gives insight into right conduct in life. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:12:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:12:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:13:00} He says he himself keeps a special reading whose only task is tracing the sunan, and that they appear sometimes as a comment on a story and sometimes as a general law followed by stories. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:13:30}

### An example: the struggle between truth and falsehood
Al-Suwaidan takes the sunna of mutual repelling (tadafu') as his example: falsehood does not win without a struggle, nor does truth win while falsehood looks on, and he cites the verse on God repelling some people by means of others. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:14:00} He explains that this is in reality a struggle between the people of truth and the people of falsehood, by force, arms and other means, and that the people of falsehood are not content to keep to their falsehood but seek to remove the truth and turn people from it with money and fighting. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:14:30} He holds that God's sunna in this struggle is that victory in the end belongs to the truth and its people, then asks how, in that case, the people of falsehood are seen winning these days, and answers with verses stating that God's sunna in giving victory to the believers does not fail, and that the victory is in this life, not in the hereafter alone. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:16:00} The surviving episode ends as he begins to answer the question of how a messenger who was killed can be victorious. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep01 00:16:30}
