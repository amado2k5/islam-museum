## Summary
Is Arabic one language or several? This page does not answer; it sets out how three well-known systems classify it: the Library of Congress in ISO 639-2, the ISO 639-3 code set kept by SIL Global, and Ethnologue in its 29th edition of 2026. It attributes to each system what that system says and prefers none. Its source is a research summary supplied to the museum by the founder, who approved its use on 21 November 2026 without the original pages being opened again, so the museum has not read those pages itself.

## The Library of Congress: one language
According to the summary supplied, the Library of Congress, in ISO 639-2, treats Arabic as a single language with the one code `ara` (short code `ar`), and the register has no separate codes for regional varieties {cite:loc-iso-639-2-ara}. The system is bibliographic in purpose: it sorts documents by their general language.

## ISO 639-3: a macrolanguage
ISO 639-3, kept by SIL Global, describes `ara` as a macrolanguage, an umbrella over individual languages, and counts 28 active individual languages beneath it. It also records two deprecated codes: `ajp`, South Levantine Arabic, merged into `apc` in January 2023, and `bbz`, Babalia Creole Arabic {cite:iso-639-3-ara}.

## Ethnologue: each variety its own language
Ethnologue, in its 29th edition of 2026, treats each Arabic variety as an individual language with its own code and profile. Its Arabic genetic subgroup lists 33 languages: 27 members of `ara`, plus Hassaniyya `mey`, Maltese `mlt` and four Judeo-Arabic varieties {cite:ethnologue-arabic-subgroup}. Its own `ara` page lists 28 individual languages, adding Juba Arabic `pga`, which Ethnologue places in the Creole family {cite:ethnologue-ara}. Speaker numbers and the year of each estimate are not available in the free edition; they sit behind a paid plan, so this page gives no figure.

## Where the systems differ
- What the Library of Congress sees as one language, Ethnologue sees as dozens.
- ISO 639-3 and Ethnologue share a frame, since both use `ara` as a macrolanguage, but the member count differs by the page read: 28 at ISO, 28 on Ethnologue's `ara` page and 33 in Ethnologue's Arabic genetic subgroup. The page does not claim the lists are identical; the museum did not compare the names one by one.
- The four Judeo-Arabic varieties `aju`, `jye`, `yhd` and `yud` sit in Ethnologue under `jrb`, not under `ara`.
- Hassaniyya `mey` and Maltese `mlt` are outside `ara` in Ethnologue and have no macrolanguage.
- Juba Arabic `pga` is a creole; it is counted within `ara` on its page while placed in the Creole family.

- Bahrain: Baharna `abv`
- Chad: Chadian `shu`
- Cyprus: Cypriot `acy`
- Egypt: Egyptian `arz`, Eastern Egyptian Bedawi `avl`, Sa'idi `aec`
- Iraq: Mesopotamian `acm`, North Mesopotamian `ayp`
- Kuwait: Gulf `afb`
- Libya: Libyan `ayl`
- Morocco: Moroccan `ary`
- Oman: Dhofari `adf`, Omani `acx`
- Saudi Arabia: Standard `arb`, Hijazi `acw`, Najdi `ars`
- South Sudan: Juba `pga`
- Sudan: Sudanese `apd`
- Syria: Levantine `apc`
- Tajikistan: Tajiki `abh`
- Tunisia: Tunisian `aeb`
- United Arab Emirates: Shihhi `ssh`
- Uzbekistan: Uzbeki `auz`
- Yemen: Hadrami `ayh`, Sanaani `ayn`, Ta'izzi-Adeni `acq`
- Juba Arabic `pga` is listed under `ara` but placed in the Creole family.
- Under `jrb` (Judeo-Arabic), outside `ara`: Judeo-Iraqi `yhd`, Judeo-Moroccan `aju`, Judeo-Tripolitanian `yud`, Judeo-Yemeni `jye`.
- Outside `ara`, with no macrolanguage: Hassaniyya `mey` (Mauritania) and Maltese `mlt` (Malta).

## Comment and cross-references
A code is neither a final linguistic verdict nor a religious one; it is a classification tool, and each body has its own purpose and method. The museum does not choose here between one language and many, as [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]] also does not, relaying the descriptions of the Encyclopaedia Britannica and Ibn Khaldun. For the old dialects see [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|the dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]]; for the modern ones see the pages on [[concept/lahjat-misr|the dialect of Egypt]], [[concept/lahjat-al-sham|the dialect of the Levant]], [[concept/lahjat-al-khalij|the dialect of the Gulf]], [[concept/lahjat-al-hijaz|the dialect of the Hijaz]], [[concept/lahjat-al-yaman|the dialect of Yemen]] and [[concept/lahjat-al-maghrib|the dialect of the Maghrib]]. For the language itself see [[language/arabic|Arabic]].

## What this page does not reach
The museum did not open the pages of the Library of Congress, ISO or Ethnologue; it relied on a research summary prepared with the help of an AI tool, which may be mistaken. No speaker numbers are given because they are not free. The criteria each body uses to tell a language from a dialect were not studied, nor was Glottolog's classification, for which the museum holds other records.
