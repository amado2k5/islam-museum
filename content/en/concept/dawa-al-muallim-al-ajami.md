## Summary
The verse [[passage/quran-16-103|al-Nahl 103]] relates the polytheists' saying that a human being teaches the Prophet, and answers it with an argument from language: the tongue of the man they incline to is foreign, while the Qur'an is in clear Arabic. The commentators agree on the sense of the answer and differ on the man's name.

## Who the man is
Al-Tabari lists the sayings with their chains {cite:tafsir-tabari-quran-com on 16:103}:

- **Bal'am**, a Christian smith in Mecca: from Ibn Abbas. The polytheists saw the Prophet go in to him and come out.
- **Ya'ish**, a slave of the Banu al-Mughira or of the Banu al-Hadrami: from Ikrima and Qatada. They said that he read books.
- **Jabr**, a Christian slave of the Banu al-Hadrami: from Ibn Ishaq and Abd Allah ibn Kathir. The Prophet used to sit with him often at al-Marwa.
- **Yasar and Jabr**, two slaves: from Abd Allah ibn Muslim al-Hadrami. They read a book in their own tongue, and the Prophet would pass by them and stand to listen.
- **Salman al-Farisi**: from al-Dahhak.
- **A scribe who used to write for the Prophet and then apostatised**: from Ibn Shihab, from Sa'id ibn al-Musayyab.

Al-Qurtubi adds other names: Addas, the slave of Utba ibn Rabia; Abis, the slave of Huwaytib ibn Abd al-Uzza; and Abu Maysara the Roman. He transmits from al-Naqqash that Jabr's master used to beat him and say: you are the one who teaches Muhammad, and he would answer: no, by God, he is the one who teaches me and guides me {cite:tafsir-qurtubi-quran-com on 16:103}. Al-Baghawi mentions that the man pointed to became a Muslim afterwards and his Islam was good {cite:tafsir-baghawi-quran-com on 16:103}.

## How the sayings were weighed
Al-Qurtubi holds that all the sayings are possible, because the Prophet sat with these men at different times to teach them from what God had taught him; and he transmits from al-Nahhas that the reports do not conflict, since the polytheists may have pointed to all of them {cite:tafsir-qurtubi-quran-com on 16:103}.

The saying about Salman was weakened by Ibn Kathir and al-Qurtubi with the same argument: the verse is Meccan, and Salman became a Muslim only in Medina {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-quran-com on 16:103} {cite:tafsir-qurtubi-quran-com on 16:103}.

## The point of the answer
Ibn Kathir says that the man they pointed to was foreign in tongue and knew of Arabic only the little needed to return an answer when spoken to, so how could the one who brought this Qur'an, in its eloquence, its rhetoric and its complete and comprehensive meanings, have learned it from a foreigner? He says that no one with the least grain of reason would say this {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-quran-com on 16:103}. Al-Qurtubi says: how could a foreigner teach him this speech, of which neither humans nor jinn can match a single sura {cite:tafsir-qurtubi-quran-com on 16:103}.

## The verse's words according to the philologists
Al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi transmit a distinction between the terms {cite:tafsir-qurtubi-quran-com on 16:103} {cite:tafsir-baghawi-quran-com on 16:103}:

- **A'jami**: one who is not fluent, even if he is an Arab or has lived in the desert.
- **Ajami**: one who belongs to the non-Arabs, even if he is fluent.
- **A'rabi**: a Bedouin. And **arabi**: one who belongs to the Arabs, even if he is not fluent.
- **Ujma**: concealment, the opposite of clarity. Hence the saying a'jamtu the book, meaning I removed its obscurity.
- **Lisan** in the verse means the Qur'an; the Arabs call a poem or a word a tongue.

## What this page does not reach
The books of the Sira were not read here, nor the books on the occasions of revelation, for this story, and the reference to the commentaries is by verse, not by a printed page.
