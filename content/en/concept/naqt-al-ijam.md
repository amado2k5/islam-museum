## Summary
"I'jam al-kitab" is dotting it; the author of al-Qamus says: so-and-so 'a'jama' the speech means he took it to foreignness, and the book, he dotted it, like 'ajama and 'ajjama, with the ayn lightened or doubled. Khalid al-Sabt says the Uthmanic codex was not dotted, and then presents two views of the origin of dotting. {cite:manahil-irfan-sabt p. 328}

## Two views of its origin
**First:** that dotting was known before Islam but deliberately left out of the codices so that a word would stay open to every possible reading, which al-Dani, Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn al-Jazari hold, and al-Sabt notes it is not free of difficulty. **Second:** that dotting was not known until later, through Abu al-Aswad al-Du'ali or some of his students, though it need not be far-fetched that it was known before in a limited way and used in writing only a little or rarely. {cite:manahil-irfan-sabt p. 328} Ibn al-Jazari relays that the codices were stripped of dots and vowel marks so that the one script's indicating both transmitted, heard wordings would be like one wording's indicating both of two meanings, because reliance was on memory. {cite:nashr-dahman-damascus-1345-v1 p. 43}

## Abd al-Malik and al-Hajjaj
Al-Sabt says that on the best-known view the codices were not dotted until [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan]]'s time, when he saw that Islam's domain had widened, Arabs and non-Arabs had mixed and foreignness threatened the soundness of the language, and confusion in reading the codices was pressing on people so that the common reader found it hard to tell the letters and words of an undotted codex apart. He charged [[person/al-hajjaj-ibn-yusuf|al-Hajjaj]] with the matter, who commissioned two men, [[person/nasr-ibn-asim|Nasr ibn Asim al-Laythi]] and [[person/yahya-ibn-yamur|Yahya ibn Ya'mar al-Adwani]], both capable, combining knowledge with practice, goodness and piety, and expertise in the foundations of the language and the modes of recitation; both were students of Abu al-Aswad al-Du'ali. They dotted the codex for the first time, dotting all its similar letters and holding to not more than three dots on any letter. {cite:manahil-irfan-sabt p. 328} {cite:manahil-irfan-sabt p. 329} See [[event/al-hajjaj-dotting-project|al-Hajjaj's project of dotting the codex]].

## Other reports and a way to reconcile them
Al-Sabt records that it is also said the first to dot the codex was Abu al-Aswad, and that Ibn Sirin had a dotted codex that Yahya ibn Ya'mar dotted; the two can be reconciled by supposing Abu al-Aswad was first to dot as an individual, Ibn Sirin followed, and Abd al-Malik first to do it officially and generally, so it spread among people to remove confusion. {cite:manahil-irfan-sabt p. 329} The report on Ibn Sirin is recorded by Abu Ubayd, Sa'id ibn Mansur, Ibn al-Duryis, Ibn Abi Dawud and al-Dani. {cite:manahil-irfan-sabt p. 329} Qatada says, as al-Suyuti relays: 'they began by dotting, then fives, then tens'; and another says the first dotting introduced was at the end of verses, then at the openings and closings. {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2245}

## What came before
Al-Suyuti relays from Yahya ibn Abi Kathir that they knew nothing of what was introduced into codices other than the three dots at the heads of verses. {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2245} {cite:itqan-suyuti p. 2246} For the early scholars' dispute over permitting dots see [[concept/tajrid-al-mushaf|keeping the codex bare]]; and see [[concept/naqt-al-irab|dotting for vowels]] and [[question/who-first-pointed-the-mushaf|Who first pointed the codex?]]
