## Summary
This page deals with two of the five parts in [[concept/ilm-al-asma-wal-alqab|onomastics]], the ism and the kunya, as a research file supplied to the museum sets them out, with the two hadith it cites for the kunya. The museum did not open the pages named, so everything here is ascribed to the summary.
## The ism
The ism is an individual's personal name, normally given at birth or shortly after. The summary says alam is the broader grammatical term for a proper name, and that an ism may be a single word or a compound {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names}. Its examples are Muhammad, praised; Ahmad, more or most praiseworthy; Ali, elevated; Hasan, good or handsome; Said, happy; Jamila, beautiful; Nur, light; Abd Allah, servant of God; and Abd al-Rahman, servant of the Most Merciful.
## The origins of names
Arabic personal names have several origins by the summary: pre-Islamic Arabian traditions, qualities and virtues, animals, natural phenomena, religious concepts and the names of prophets. Ibrahim, Musa, Yusuf and Maryam belong to the shared Abrahamic religious tradition. Its general reference is Annemarie Schimmel's Islamic Names: An Introduction (1989) {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names}.
## The kunya
A kunya is conventionally built with Abu, father of, or Umm, mother of. It often refers to a person's child and works as a respectful or familiar form of address, but does not necessarily establish real parenthood {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names}. Grammatically Abu changes with case: Abu in the nominative, Aba in the accusative, Abi in the genitive, so one name appears in different forms, as in Abu Talib and Ali ibn Abi Talib, which do not denote two people.

## Aisha and Umm Abd Allah
The Islamic tradition records, by the summary, that Muhammad gave Aisha the kunya Umm Abd Allah though she had no biological children, referring to her nephew Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr {cite:sunnah-com-abu-dawud-4970}; the reference is Sunan Abi Dawud, hadith 4970. The nephew has a record at [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr]]. It is an example of a kunya that does not point to a child of one's own.
## Abu Hurayra
According to the summary Abu Hurayra acquired the kunya because he cared for a small kitten, from his own account in Jami al-Tirmidhi, hadith 3839 {cite:sunnah-com-tirmidhi-3839}. His underlying personal name is disputed in the sources, commonly given as Abd al-Rahman, and the summary refers to Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani's al-Isaba fi Tamyiz al-Sahaba. It is a kunya that prevailed so far that his real name is in doubt.
## Kunyas that outran names
The summary lists Abu Bakr, whose ism is Abd Allah, Abu Hurayra and Abu Nuwas, whose ism is al-Hasan, among those known by their kunyas and not their names {cite:bnf-kitcat-arabic-names}. Set against these is the detailed technical definition at [[concept/kunya-fiqh|the kunya in jurisprudence]], and the difference between the summary's anthropological view and the juristic one stays.
