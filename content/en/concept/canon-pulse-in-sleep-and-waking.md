## Summary
In the twelfth chapter Ibn Sina shows that the pulse in sleep differs with the point reached in sleep and with the state of digestion, then describes the pulse of gradual and of sudden waking {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.179-180}.

## The start of sleep
The pulse at the start of sleep is small and weak, because the innate heat turns inward as a whole, moved by the soul, to digest the food and ripen the superfluities, and is left as if overpowered and confined; the pulse is then slower and more widely spaced, for although the heat grows by being shut in and gathered, it lacks the growth it has in waking from heating movement. Ibn Sina adds that the breath of a tired man is far greater than that of one whose heat is merely shut in, and that nothing heats more than motion {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.179}.

## The middle and depth of sleep
If food goes on in sleep the pulse returns and strengthens, for power grows with nourishment and what had turned inward to manage the food turns outward and back to its source; the pulse also grows great, the temperament warms with the food, and the instrument softens with what reaches it, yet no great speed or frequency is added because this does not raise need. As sleep goes on the pulse turns weak again, because the innate heat is shut in and power is pressed beneath the superfluities that waking, exercise and the unfelt evacuations would clear. If sleep meets an empty stomach with no food to digest, it tips the temperament toward cold, and smallness, slowness and wide spacing persist and keep increasing {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.179-180}.

## Waking
When a sleeper wakes by nature, the pulse inclines gradually to greatness and speed and returns to its natural state. One woken suddenly by an alarm first has a slackened pulse, since power gives way before the sudden thing, then a great, quick, frequent pulse that is uneven and inclined to trembling, because the movement resembles a forced one and inflames the heat, and power moves abruptly to drive off what has befallen; but it does not last long and soon returns to balance {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.180}.
