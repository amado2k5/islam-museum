## Summary
Al-Battani devotes chapters 8 and 9 to converting between the excess of the longest day and the pole's altitude, which is the place's latitude. Chapter 8 gives the altitude from the excess by a chain of chords built on the half day-arc; chapter 9 gives half the excess from the latitude and doubles it, with every fifteen degrees of it one equal hour, added to twelve hours for the longest day and subtracted from twelve for the shortest. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion pp.34-35}.

## Chapter 8: altitude from the excess
He says: to find the altitude of the north pole of the equator over the horizon, which is the place's latitude, from the excess of the longest day over the equinoctial day, take half the excess, which is the day of the first of Cancer, add it to ninety for the half-arc of the longest day, or subtract it for the half-arc of the shortest. Then subtract the whole obliquity from ninety, find the chord of what is left, multiply it by the chord of the half day-arc and divide by the radius; the arc of the result is the distance of the first of Cancer's rising from the north point. A proportion is then worked between the chord of half the day's excess, the rising's distance from the north point and its distance from the rising of the first of Aries, ending in a division by the chord of the half day-arc, and the arc of the result is the pole's altitude. The detail of the multiplications is disordered in the scan; what is given here is the structure of the working, not a formula to the letter. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.34}.

## Chapter 9: the excess from the altitude
In chapter 9 he says: to find the excess of the longest day and the shortfall of the shortest from the equinoctial day, given the pole's altitude, take the latitude and find its chord and the chord of its complement to ninety, and the chord of the whole obliquity and that of its complement; multiply the chord of the latitude by the chord of the whole obliquity, divide by the chord of the obliquity's complement, multiply the result by the radius and divide by the chord of the latitude's complement; the arc of the result is half the excess of the longest day, and equally half the shortfall of the shortest. Double it; every fifteen degrees of it is one equal hour, added to twelve hours for the longest day and subtracted from twelve for the shortest. For any other degree he works with its declination instead of the whole obliquity: if it is north the result is the lengthening of the day, if south its shortening. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.35}.
