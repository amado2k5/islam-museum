## Summary
Ibn Sina describes the wrist as many bones, seven principal and one extra; the metacarpus as four bones; and the fingers as three bones each except the thumb {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.55-57} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.60}.

## The wrist
The wrist is made of many bones so that no harm affects it all: seven principal and one extra. The seven lie in two rows. The row next to the forearm has three, since it adjoins the forearm and should be finer; the second has four, since it adjoins the metacarpus and fingers and should be broader. The eighth bone does not form either row but was made to shield a nerve beside the palm. The end of the three-bone row enters the hollow of the ends of the two forearm bones and forms the joint of straightening and bending, and the process of the lower forearm bone enters a hollow in the carpal bones and forms the joint of twisting and turning {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.55-56}. The ankle's tarsus differs from the wrist in being one row with far fewer bones, because the hand needs movement and grasp more {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.60}.

## The metacarpus
The metacarpus is made of several bones so that no harm affects it all, and so that the palm can be hollowed to grip rounded things. It has four bones, since four fingers join them, close together at the wrist and slightly spread toward the fingers. Their joints are firm and bound to one another so they do not scatter and weaken the palm, with a little yielding that allows the palm to hollow. The wrist joins the metacarpus by hollows in the carpal ends into which heads of the metacarpal bones, clothed in cartilage, enter {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.56}.

## The fingers
The fingers are tools that help in grasping. They were not made of flesh without bone, lest their actions be feeble, nor of one bone, lest their actions be hard; three bones were enough, since more would weaken the grip where firmness is needed, and fewer would give firmness but too little movement. Their bases are broader and heads finer, round, hard, and without cavity or marrow so that they hold firm; they are hollowed within and curved outside, with flesh on the inner side. The middle finger has the longest joints, then the ring, the index and the little finger, so that their tips level when gripping. The thumb balances all four fingers, and set elsewhere its use would be lost {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.56-57}. The finger bones are joined by ridges and hollows with a sticky moisture between, and small sesamoid bones fill the gaps of their joints {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.57}. Every finger but the thumb has three phalanges, the thumb two {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.60}.
