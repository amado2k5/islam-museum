## Overview
The most beautiful names of God (asma' Allah al-husna) are the names of perfection and majesty by which God named Himself; the famous hadith reports: "God has ninety-nine names — a hundred less one — whoever enumerates them enters Paradise" {cite:sahih-al-bukhari} {cite:sahih-muslim}.

## In the scholars' books
Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya devoted a section of Jala' al-Afham to the ninety-nine names, transmitting and commenting on them {cite:jala-afham-ibn-qayyim}; later writers expanded the subject into chapters on creed, remembrance and supplication, making it among the most-repeated topics in Islamic writing.

## In the Encyclopaedia of Islam: the theological question
The second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam treats the names in its article on al-Asma' al-Husna, "the most beautiful names". It begins by noting that pious Muslims have revered the mystery of the Name, which at once designates and veils the Named. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763} A chapter of the science of [[concept/tawhid|divine oneness]] is given over to the names, and the article frames it through questions: whether God can be named at all, what the names attributed to Him mean, and whether a name is the same thing as the one it names. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763}

On who may give God a name, the article sets out several positions and labels each. The narrators of Tradition and the strict adherents of the [[concept/ashariyya|Ash'ari school]] hold that a divine name may be used only if God Himself fixed it in the Qur'an or, secondarily, in the Sunna, and the hadith used for this must be authentic or good; some also admit a fixing by consensus. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763} The [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazila]] and the [[group/karramiyya|Karramiyya]] hold that when reason shows an attribute to be fitting for God, the corresponding name may be used whether or not the texts mention it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763} [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|Al-Ghazali]] accepts this for names that point to something added to the essence, but not for the name of the essence itself. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763} The Ash'ari [[person/al-baqillani|al-Baqillani]], followed by many later Ash'aris, takes a middle way: where a text gives God an attribute or speaks of an act of His, He may be called by the matching name according to the rules of the language, even if no text fixes it, but names that would suggest something unfitting to His perfection are excluded. The article gives the examples that God is not called knowing in the sense of one who has overcome inattention, and not called a jurist or an intelligent man. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763} The article says that this thesis, that the names must be scriptural or at least derived from scripture, has become the current one. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763}

Two related disputes are recorded. The Ash'aris hold the names to be eternal, against the Mu'tazili view that they are contingent. The Hanafi and Maturidi line holds the names to be equal in importance and excellence, while the Ash'ari line sees a hierarchy with the name Allah first, or, as the Sufis tend to say, with another name known to the initiated, or an ineffable name reached only through spiritual experience. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763}

## The ninety-nine names and their recitation
The article quotes the hadith reported by [[person/abu-hurayra|Abu Hurayra]]: God has ninety-nine names, a hundred less one; He is odd, that is unique, and likes to be called by these names; whoever knows them will enter Paradise. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763} It says that the meditated recitation of these names became one of the most diligent devotions in Islam. The pious Muslim repeats and ponders them, usually with the ninety-nine beads of the rosary, though the Wahhabis object to this practice as a reprehensible innovation. The article also mentions a Syriac Christian use of the rosary to count a much shorter list of divine names. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763}

The article stresses that the traditional list is not a closed record of the names. The ninety-nine do not exhaust the names found in the Qur'an, and some of them do not appear there word for word, so the list has not always been fixed and has variants. The article nevertheless judges that the place of the recitation in Muslim piety gives it outstanding importance, since it expresses the believer's faith in God and what the supreme name Allah, which gathers up all the others, means to him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763}

## The usual list and its order
The article reproduces the most commonly accepted list, as given by most commentaries on the Qur'an, with a short comment on each name. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763} Allah is often set apart as the hundredth name, as in the commentary of the Jalalayn, but it is sometimes counted first, in which case the name al-Wahid is dropped and joined to al-Ahad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763} The main references are al-Ghazali's al-Maqsad al-Asna and the [[work/al-mawaqif-fi-ilm-al-kalam|Mawaqif]] of [[person/al-iji-adud-al-din|al-Iji]] with the commentary of [[person/al-sharif-al-jurjani-ali-ibn-muhammad|al-Jurjani]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763}

The first thirteen names, or the second to the fourteenth when Allah heads the list, follow the Qur'anic enumeration in [[passage/quran-59-22|chapter 59, verses 22 to 24]]. After them the order seems mainly a memory aid, governed by assonance, by related verbal forms and by pairs of correlative or paradoxical sense. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763} The article links names to kinds of attribute, such as negative attributes, attributes of action, of speech, of knowledge, of will, of power and of relation, following al-Ghazali or al-Jurjani. It also notes that the Arabic root of several names carries different and sometimes opposite meanings at once, which a recitant holds together, so that a single European word often cannot render a name. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763}

## Meanings of the names, as the article gives them
In the article's account, Allah designates God Himself and may be applied to nothing else. Al-Rahman and al-Rahim both express mercy and depend on the attribute of will, though al-Ghazali holds that al-Rahman is applied to God alone. Al-Malik points to God's independence of all things, the dependence of everything on Him and the perfection of His power. Al-Quddus is read as separation from all blemish and as the mystery that neither imagination nor sight can penetrate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 763} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 764}

For al-Mu'min the article says that the doctors of theology speak of God's uncreated faith in Himself, and al-Iji takes the name to mean that God authenticates Himself and His Messenger, while it can also mean a source of security. Al-Aziz is read by al-Ghazali as rare and unique, so necessary that nothing exists without Him, and by al-Iji as without father or mother and unlike anything. Al-Jabbar is the one whom no will can resist, and also, from another sense of the root, the one who sets right. The names of creation lead to al-Musawwir, the Organiser, and al-Ghazali sees in the creating names a passage from non-being to being, first by decree, then by bringing into existence, then by arranging forms. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 764}

Al-Ghaffar and al-Ghafur are explained differently by the two authorities: al-Iji and al-Jurjani take them as identical, as with al-Rahman and al-Rahim, while al-Ghazali says that al-Ghaffar stresses the pardon of repeated sins and al-Ghafur conveys infinite pardon without limit. Al-Razzaq concerns the bodily needs of everyone according to al-Jurjani, and also the spiritual needs of rational beings according to al-Ghazali. Al-Fattah is given three shades of meaning: the one who brings victory, the judge, and the one who reveals what was hidden. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 764}

Six names, three pairs of opposites, are called traditional because they come from Qur'anic roots without appearing in the Qur'an word for word: al-Qabid and al-Basit, who restrain and expand; al-Rafi', who raises in dignity, and its opposite, who humbles; al-Mu'izz and al-Mudhill, who give honour and who abase. The article says they express the sheer gratuitousness of God's gift. Al-Sami' and al-Basir are two attributes of the essence which the Qur'an affirms and which, the article says, reason cannot prove. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 764} Other names are glossed in the same way: al-Halim as slow to punish, al-Latif as the one who creates grace to help His servants, al-Adl as supreme justice from which nothing bad comes, and al-Shakur, in a figurative sense, as the one who gives much for little and praises the obedient. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 764}

The article gives al-Muqit four shades of meaning: the nourisher, the determiner who fixes destiny, the witness, and the present. It reads al-Karim as the generous, with four shades, and al-Wadud as the very loving, and notes that al-Ba'ith, the one who revives all creatures on the Day of Resurrection, has only a traditional origin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 764} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 765} Al-Hayy is the living, whose life is the highest and most perfect because of the perfection of His activity and knowledge, and al-Qayyum is the one who subsists in and by Himself and by whom creatures subsist. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 765} The article distinguishes al-Ahad, the One by essence, expressing the absolute simplicity of the essence, from al-Wahid, the One God beside whom there is no other. It gives al-Samad five senses, among them the master, the one to whom one prays, and the one in whom there is no hollow, which denies all mixture and division into parts. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 765}

Several names come in pairs: al-Awwal and al-Akhir, the First and the Last, before and after everything; al-Zahir and al-Batin, the Patent and the Latent; al-Muqaddim and al-Mu'akhkhir, who bring near and send away. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 765} Toward the end of the list come al-Nur, the Light, which the article reads as perfectly evident in itself and making all things manifest; al-Hadi, the Guide; al-Baqi, the Eternal; al-Warith, the Inheritor, to whom returns what creatures possess; al-Rashid, the Leader; and al-Sabur, the Very Patient. Al-Darr and al-Nafi' are two traditional names which teach that affliction and benefit derive only from God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 766} After the list the article names further names that appear elsewhere, among them al-Rabb, al-Mun'im, al-Muti, al-Sadiq and al-Sattar. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 766}

## Grouping and meditation in Sufi writing
The article ends by observing that many studies group the names by attributes, as al-Ghazali does, often giving the presentation the air of spiritual meditation, with many examples in [[concept/tasawwuf|Sufism]]. In such works the aim is less to comment on the ninety-nine names than to apply the rules of fixing and of language to magnify the divine mystery. For the use of the names in Sufi prayer it refers readers to its article on [[concept/dhikr|dhikr]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 766}

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 3)

The Sheikh says that God is the Guide before there exists anyone who needs guidance, that is, from eternity, and that He is the First, the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep03 00:04:00}. He adds that God is the Provider before there exists anyone who needs provision {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep03 00:04:00}.

The Sheikh says these are among the most beautiful names of God, and when they settle in the servant's heart he gains full certainty in the sincerity of his reliance on God {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep03 00:04:30}. He explains that God's guidance exists before the existence of those who need it, and that His provision exists before the servants are created {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep03 00:04:30}.

The Sheikh stresses again that God is the Provider before He creates those who need provision {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep03 00:05:00}. He then turns to the softening of hearts, likening it to a gradual process of making something supple, prays that God soften the hearts of those present, and links this to opening the Qur'an and reading it {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep03 00:05:00}.

## In Ahmad Farid's lessons 'The Boat'
In [[work/the-boat-ahmad-farid|'The Boat']] [[person/ahmad-farid|Ahmad Farid]] names knowing God as the first thing by which the tree of faith grows: the more a servant knows God, the more he loves Him, fears Him and relies on Him, as the Prophet said he was the most knowing of God and the most fearful of Him. {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep09 00:00:30} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep09 00:01:00} We should come to know God by His Most Beautiful Names, he says, and 'husna' is the plural of 'ahsan', the best, not of 'hasan', good, because God chose for Himself the best names, which contain the best attributes; souls are made to love perfection, so the more one knows the Exalted Lord the more one loves Him. {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep09 00:01:30} He gives [[person/abdullah-ibn-abbas|Ibn Abbas]]'s reading of the verse that jinn and humans were created to worship as 'to know', that is, to know their Lord by His names, attributes, lordship and divinity. {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep09 00:02:00}

In the seventeenth episode of his programme [[work/ossali-alayh|'Osalli Alayh']], [[person/ahmed-abdussalam|Ahmed Abdussalam]] dwells on the names of God in the praise with which the Prophet opened his night prayer. {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep17 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep17 00:03:00} He reads 'the Light' as an invitation to ask God to bring one out of the darkness of poverty into the light of sufficiency, and out of the darkness of defeat and disgrace into the light of honour and covering. {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep17 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep17 00:02:30} 'The Sustainer' he reads as God's lasting care for His servants' affairs, with the example of a student who sleeps trusting his mother to wake him for an examination, and sleep overtakes them both. {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep17 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep17 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep17 00:04:30} He distinguishes 'the Owner' from 'the King': an owner may own while another commands, as a father owns a building that his son runs, whereas God is both Owner and King. {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep17 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep17 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep17 00:07:00} He adds that God is forbearing: He gives respite but does not neglect. {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep17 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-ahmed-abdussalam ossali-alayh/ep17 00:06:00}

## In al-Tabari's commentary: forbidden and permitted names
Al-Tabari explains the order of "Allah", then "al-Rahman", then "al-Rahim" in the basmala: names God forbade creatures to bear come first, because they are His alone, such as Allah, al-Rahman and al-Khaliq. Names He permitted creatures to bear among themselves follow, such as al-Rahim, al-Sami', al-Basir and al-Karim. Since no one can be a god in any sense besides Him, the name Allah is put first. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 133} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 134}
He reports al-Hasan al-Basri's view that al-Rahman is among the names God forbade His servants to bear, and supports it with [[passage/quran-17-110|al-Isra' 110]], where God is called upon as Allah or as al-Rahman. The editor calls this argument one of the best that has been said. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 133} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 134}
