## Summary
For Maryam Ekhtiar, nasta'liq is an elegant, lyrical script that originated in Iran and Central Asia and spread east to Mughal India and west to Ottoman Turkey. As the favoured script of Persian poetry — called the "bride of scripts" — it was especially popular in Iran from the fifteenth century. {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}

## Origins: the legend and the scholars' view
The author reports a legend that [[person/mir-ali-tabrizi|Mir Ali Tabrizi]] (active late fourteenth century) invented it after [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]] — traditionally regarded as the first calligrapher in Islam — appeared to him in a dream and told him to make letters like the wings of flying geese. Scholars, however, hold that nasta'liq developed in the second half of the fourteenth century in [[place/shiraz|Shiraz]] and [[place/tabriz|Tabriz]] as a gradual merging of [[concept/naskh-script|naskh]] and [[concept/taliq-script|ta'liq]], joining the evenness and legibility of naskh to the sweeping strokes of ta'liq. {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}

## The line of masters
Mir Ali's fame was matched in the next generation by his pupil [[person/jafar-tabrizi|Ja'far al-Tabrizi]], head of Prince Baysunghur's scriptorium at [[place/herat|Herat]] from 1419 to 1433. One of Ja'far's pupils trained [[person/sultan-ali-mashhadi|Sultan Ali Mashhadi]] (about 1440–1520), author of an influential treatise on penmanship and its spiritual side; Sultan Ali's pupils included [[person/sultan-muhammad-nur-calligrapher|Sultan Muhammad Nur]], known for a minute hand (khafi), and [[person/mir-ali-heravi|Mir Ali Haravi]] (1476–1544), much prized at the Mughal court. {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27} Later [[person/mir-imad-hasani|Mir Imad al-Hasani]] (1552–1615) was chief calligrapher at the court of [[person/abbas-i-safavi|Shah Abbas I]] in Isfahan; he was killed in 1615, possibly, the author says, at the shah's command, and his style remained a model in Iran into the nineteenth century, through Mirza Ghulam Riza Isfahani, and in Mughal India and Ottoman Turkey. {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 28}

## In Mughal albums
The Met holds two folios from the Shah Jahan Album, begun by [[person/jahangir-mughal|Jahangir]] and continued by [[person/shah-jahan-mughal|Shah Jahan]]: one by Sultan Ali Mashhadi with love verses rich in Sufi metaphor in diagonal lines, the other a quatrain by Ibn Yamin signed by Mir Ali Haravi, both reset in seventeenth-century illuminated margins, the second with birds and animals painted by Nanha. {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 53} {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 54} {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 56}
