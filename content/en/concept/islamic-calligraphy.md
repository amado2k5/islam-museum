## Overview
The art of writing and shaping the Arabic letter beautifully. It is the most honoured of the Islamic arts because of its link to the writing of the Qur'an, and its scripts varied among kufic, naskh, thuluth and diwani. {cite:wikidata}

The second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam treats khatt, writing, at length. It says that the Arabic script, used according to tradition as early as the lifetime of [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] for setting down the text of the Qur'an, spread together with the faith and with the civilisation in which it came to full fruition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1157} It describes an alphabet of consonants, with vocalisation signs added as accents, and says that writing Arabic became an art as well as a science reserved for initiates. The best masters aimed at the double distinction of artist and respected sage. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1157} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1158}

## The script beyond the Arab lands
The article notes that it is hard to separate the Arabic script from the other scripts of the Islamic world that borrowed its alphabet, and just as hard to draw the map of its use, because language, religion and script did not always spread together. Arabic writing disappeared from Spain and Sicily after the reconquests, apart from ornamental inscriptions. In some places the script outlived the spoken language as an administrative or literary medium. In South-East Asia and Black Africa it travelled further than the language. In modern times Latin and sometimes Cyrillic letters replaced it for Turkish, Malay, Malagasy and several African languages. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1158}

Two later developments changed the conditions of writing. The article points to the wide manufacture of paper in Muslim lands from the third century AH, the ninth century CE, which gave scribes a smooth, light, durable and affordable medium. It also points to printing, which reached the Near East in the second half of the nineteenth century. In the article's judgement this hardened some usages and halted the evolution of letterforms in favour of the common eastern cursive. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1158}

## Many hands for many purposes
Government scribes, calligraphers working for luxury patrons, pious copyists of the Qur'an, scholars taking dictation, merchants writing letters and makers of amulets did not all write alike. How legible a text had to be shaped its letters. The article cites decorative scripts on objects, such as figural letters on inlaid metalwork, and rigid angular figures on astrolabes. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1159}

On regional schools the article urges caution. It allows that Iranian and Turkish craftsmen, working in Khurasan, Sistan and Buyid Baghdad, were probably behind some of the finest compositions in angular letters, but it says their exact share cannot be measured. It adds that the pen and the way it was cut, the inks, and the techniques of carving in stone and stucco, painting on plaster or pottery, metalwork and mosaic of glass, brick and ceramic all helped to produce different styles. Leading calligraphers often drew models on paper for inscriptions made in other materials, so the article rejects a sharp divide between inscriptional and manuscript scripts. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1159}

## The earliest script
The archaic script of the first century AH, the seventh century CE, was used in Arabia in Muhammad's lifetime and under his first successors. Evidence outside literary sources is thin: undated Qur'an fragments and letters on parchment whose authenticity is not firmly shown, coins of the Arab-Sasanian and Arab-Byzantine types, an epitaph of 31 AH/652 CE in the Cairo museum, and a dam inscription of 58 AH/677-8 CE in the name of [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya]] near al-Ta'if. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1163}

The alphabet had seventeen basic characters, eighteen counting lam-alif, for twenty-eight sounds. Dots above or below the line later separated letters that shared one shape, and a sign for doubling a consonant was added. The article says this may imitate Syriac practice, and that the Mu'awiya inscription suggests small oblique strokes were used early. The script was cursive, with many ligatures. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1163} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1164}

Its origin is unsettled. Ancient accounts credit legendary persons with the invention. The opinion that long prevailed derives the script from the Nabataeans of Petra, but specialists in Nabataean deny any resemblance in cursive forms and point to a gap of centuries. They look instead to scripts of the Christian Arab kingdoms of Lower Mesopotamia around [[place/al-hira|al-Hira]], none of which survive, and observe that early Arabic follows the same principles as Syriac. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1164}

## The Umayyad period
The article calls the next stage an imperial script, marked by balance and regularity. It says the caliph [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik]] required Arabic of the scribes in central and provincial administration and ordered Arabic legends on coins, and it credits his son [[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|al-Walid]] with leaving an epigraphic band of unequalled importance on the walls of the [[building/dome-of-the-rock|Dome of the Rock]]. Letters were sorted into three groups by simple geometric schemes, namely tall straight strokes, loops, and curves below the line. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1164}

The same shapes were set rigidly on stone and parchment, or rounded on papyrus for letters. The article rejects the older view of two originally separate scripts, angular kufic and flexible naskhi, and stresses the basic unity of early Arabic writing. It nonetheless places in the Umayyad period the start of the two branches that later diverged. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1165}

## Kufic
The angular branch was slow to write and naturally ornamental. In the early Abbasid age it was used for Qur'ans until the middle of the fourth century AH, the tenth century CE, for other books on parchment, and for monumental inscriptions. The article describes plain kufic first, then ornamental kufic, which diversified until the turn of the sixth century AH into regional styles that can be named after dynasties, such as Aghlabid, Tulunid, [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]] and [[polity/ghaznavid-empire|Ghaznavid]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1165}

Craftsmen balanced an inscription band that was empty above and crowded below. They turned the tops of letters into palmettes, let the lower strokes curve upward, stretched or bent the letters, or added motifs behind them. Modern classifications speak of kufic with leaves or flowers, with a scrolling stem, with floral, geometric or human-figure borders. Some experiments placed human heads or animals within the letters, and some distorted the shapes so far that the letters were hard to recognise. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1165} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1166}

The article says the western provinces, Andalusia and the Maghrib, kept a sober style that hid a scholarly script. After the sixth century AH, the twelfth century CE, kufic became a purely ornamental script, used for short religious invocations and architectural surfaces. Varieties such as square kufic, which the article says was influenced by certain Chinese characters, produced rigid lacework patterns from Central Asia to Turkey. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1166}

## The rounded scripts and the great masters
After the Umayyads a rounded script spread beyond the chancery. Copyists used it from the end of the third century AH for ordinary books, and in the fourth century AH it came to dominate copies of the Qur'an. A hybrid, sometimes called broken kufic or naskhi kufic, seems to have spread first in the far Iranian provinces and lasted longest in the Muslim West, where it shaped later Maghribi writing. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1166}

The article names three masters of the eastern tradition. The vizier [[person/ibn-muqla|Ibn Muqla]] set out, in the first half of the fourth century AH, the proportions between the letters and treated the best-known six styles systematically. [[person/ibn-al-bawwab|Ibn al-Bawwab]], at the start of the fifth century AH, was the artist whose reputation then eclipsed Ibn Muqla's as theorist, and in his time cursive writing was judged fit for Qur'ans. [[person/yaqut-al-mustasimi|Yaqut al-Musta'simi]], towards the end of the seventh century AH, marks the peak of the rounded script, often called Ayyubid naskhi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1166}

Mamluk scribes then turned to redundant forms and dense interwoven vertical strokes. In Iran and Turkey, under Ilkhanid and Timurid princes, writers broke the line into sections hung obliquely one above another, an art that triumphed with [[person/mir-ali-heravi|Mir Ali of Herat]]. Another trend monumentalised cursive for large surfaces, as in the school of [[person/shaykh-hamdullah|Shaykh Hamd Allah]] in the tenth century AH, the sixteenth century CE. From the sixth century AH rounded forms also replaced the angular ones in stone inscriptions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1166}

## How the history is known
Medieval writing manuals, called kitab al-khatt or kitab al-kitaba, dealt with script types, spelling and chancery practice. The oldest go back to the third century AH. They include a work by al-Baghdadi, which has links with passages by [[person/ibn-qutayba|Ibn Qutayba]], and the Fihrist of [[person/ibn-al-nadim|Ibn al-Nadim]]. The chapter of [[person/al-qalqashandi|al-Qalqashandi]] lists six principal types of Arabic script, among them thuluth, tawqi and riqa. The article says that attempts to read these texts against surviving documents have often reached an impasse. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1163}

For modern study the article stresses how little is settled. Arabic palaeography is described as a young discipline with no complete manual, since Adolf Grohmann's work stopped at two volumes. Papyri, coins, archives and above all Qur'an manuscripts supply the firmest evidence, yet the article says the dating of the ornamental kufic Qur'ans remains fragile and calls for judgement to be suspended until the manuscripts are grouped by families. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1160} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1161} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1162}

## Persia
The article says Baghdad's tradition culminated in Yaqut al-Musta'simi, after which the six styles, called aqlam-i sitta, passed to Herat, Mashhad, Tabriz and eventually Tehran. Persian calligraphers carried them further and added two styles of their own, ta'liq and nasta'liq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1166} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1167}

It then lists the styles. Kufic remained in use for about five centuries, mainly for Qur'ans, and later for titles and decoration. Muhaqqaq served for long-format Qur'ans, with the copy made for the Timurid prince Baysunghur especially famous, and rayhan was a smaller version. Thuluth, the longest-lived of the six, served for frames and book titles. Naskh in Persia followed Yaqut, and [[person/ahmad-nayrizi-calligrapher|Ahmad Nayrizi]] gave it new life in the eighteenth century. Tawqi was used for colophon pages and riqa for letters. The large jali form of thuluth reached its height under the Timurids and Safavids. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1167} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1168}

Ta'liq took final form by the seventh century AH and was used for books, letters and chancery correspondence. Its broken, rapid form, shikasta ta'liq, appeared in the eighth century AH. Nasta'liq, said to join naskh and ta'liq, is traditionally credited to [[person/mir-ali-tabrizi|Mir Ali Tabrizi]], but the article says surviving manuscripts show it existed by the seventh century AH. Its Khurasani style flourished under masters such as [[person/sultan-ali-mashhadi|Sultan Ali Mashhadi]] and Mir Ali of Herat and reached its highest beauty with [[person/mir-imad-hasani|Mir Imad]], whom modern Persian calligraphers follow. Shikasta nasta'liq arose under the Safavids at the start of the eleventh century AH. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1168}

## Ottoman lands
The Turks, the article says, followed Yaqut in the six styles until the conquest of Istanbul in 857 AH/1452 CE. After refinements introduced by Shaykh Hamd Allah, a distinctively Turkish style emerged, and Istanbul became a centre of the art. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1169} Thuluth and naskh reached their peak with [[person/hafiz-osman|Hafiz Osman]], and modern Turkish calligraphers follow his school. In the Ottoman period a teacher signed a student's diploma, called ijaza, in riqa script, which is why riqa also had that name. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1169}

Diwani is traditionally said to have been created for the chancery founded by [[person/mehmed-ii|Mehmed II]], though the article argues it existed earlier, probably developed from ta'liq. Its chancery calligraphers did not sign their documents, and it reached perfection in the nineteenth century. A variant, jali diwani, was used for imperial decrees. The script passed to the Arab lands through Ottoman influence. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1169} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1170} Nasta'liq came to Turkey with Persian calligraphers in Mehmed II's reign, and Yesari-zade Mustafa Izzet freed the Turkish form from Persian influence. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1170}

The appendix lists artificial scripts such as picture writing, riddling arrangements of sayings, tree-like and hyacinth scripts, and flame writing, which the article says the first-rank Persian and Turkish calligraphers valued little. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1170}

## India and South-East Asia
Early Arabic writing in India includes kufic inscriptions of 290 AH/902 CE and 294 AH/906 CE near Thatta. The [[building/quwwat-ul-islam-mosque-delhi|Quwwat al-Islam mosque]] at [[place/delhi|Delhi]], built from 587 AH/1191 CE, carries inscriptions in several scripts, including kufic, naskhi and thuluth. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1171} Before the Mughals a special Qur'an style called Bahar arose, named for a paste applied to the paper. The introduction of nasta'liq is mostly attributed to the Mughals. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1171}

Under [[person/jalal-al-din-akbar|Akbar]] the court patronised calligraphers, nasta'liq was favoured, and the chancery used a style called khatt-i diwani for decrees. His successor Jahangir awarded honorific titles to leading calligraphers. Under Shah Jahan two masters came from Persia, and the article says [[person/kifayat-khan-calligrapher|Kifayat Khan]] invented a shikasta style. [[person/aurangzeb-mughal|Aurangzeb]] copied Qur'ans for the Holy Cities without signing them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1171} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1172}

In the Malay world, Arabic script was until about 1500 used almost only for Arabic, and around the start of the sixteenth century it became common for Malay and other Indonesian languages. The oldest dated Arabic inscription the article names there is the Leran tombstone of about 475 AH/1082 CE, probably imported. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1172}

## For Sprouts
The art of writing Arabic letters in beautiful forms that decorate Qur'ans and mosques, showing the skill and love of beauty of calligraphers.

## For Explorers
Islamic calligraphy is a revered art form that arose to record and preserve the Qur'an with utmost care. Over centuries, calligraphers developed elegant styles including geometric Kufic, clear Naskh, monumental Thuluth, Diwani, and Nastaliq, transforming written words into artistic masterworks that grace manuscripts, domes, and walls across the world.

## For Scholars
The preeminent visual art of Islamic civilization, distinguished by its intimate connection with the transcription of the Qur'an, state chancery, and monumental architectural epigraphy. Developing from early Hijazi and Kufic scripts, it achieved geometric codification through the proportional system established by Ibn Muqla, refined by Ibn al-Bawwab and Yaqut al-Musta'simi. Encompassing canonical scripts like Naskh, Thuluth, Nastaliq, Diwani, and Maghribi, Islamic calligraphy harmonizes semantic clarity with rhythmic abstraction, embodying a primary aesthetic emblem of Islamic culture.
