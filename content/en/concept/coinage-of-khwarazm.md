## Summary
This card summarises what Shohruxmirzo Ismoilov sets out in his book on the coinage of Khwarazm between the eighth and early thirteenth centuries, in which he counts 237 types. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 222}

## The old Khwarazmian drachms
Early Afrighid coins show a crowned, beardless king on the obverse and a horseman on the reverse, with the dynasty's tamgha (mark) and the king's name in Old Khwarezmian and sometimes Sogdian script. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 9} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 33} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 35} Using Vainberg's typology the author follows kings such as Azkajuvar (Chaghan), Kanik, Shavat and Savshafan. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 32} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 33} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 34} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 35}

## Arabic script arrives
The Arabic names of caliphal governors appear on the drachms of Azkajuvar-Abd Allah in the late eighth and early ninth centuries: al-Fadl, Ja'far, "Dhu al-Ri'asatayn", Khalid, Talha and others; these are the first coins in Khwarazm with Arabic script. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 38} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 39} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 40} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 51} The Oghuz yabghus of the lower Syr Darya struck imitations of these drachms naming Abd Allah ibn Tahir, and one of them names its mint, something unknown on the old Khwarazmian coins. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 41} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 42} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 43}

## Islamic coinage
- Abd Allah ibn Tahir struck coins at Khwarazm in 224/839 on the Abbasid model that goes back to the reform of Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan; for U. Linder-Welin they are the first Abbasid-type coins struck in Khwarazm. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 43} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 44}
- His governor Amir Mikal struck fulus in 226 and 228 AH, some showing a horse with a swastika sign above it. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 45} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 46}
- The fulus of the Samanid Nuh ibn Asad of 203/818 are the first coins in Khwarazm to carry a Qur'anic quotation, from the wording of [[passage/quran-61-9|al-Saff 61:9]] and [[passage/quran-9-33|al-Tawba 9:33]]. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 47} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 175}
- The author notes that the horse first appears on Islamic coins on Umayyad fulus struck at Homs, but it had been on Khwarazmian coins since the fourth century and stayed for about two centuries after Islam arrived. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 175}

## Periods without minting
The author holds that minting in the name of Khwarazm's rulers stopped four times: 818–893 under caliphal prohibition, 893–959 under the Samanids, 1014–1042 under the Ghaznavids, and 1041–1129 under the Seljuks. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 225}

## Ma'munids, Shah Malik and the Anushtegins
Ma'munid coins are wholly Islamic, carrying verses from al-Tawba, al-Saff, al-Fath and al-Rum and the names of Samanids and caliphs. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 82} The coins of Shah Malik the Oghuz carry a sword, a six-pointed star, an eagle and a lion, in Oghuz tradition. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 11} Under the Anushtegins the mints spread to some thirty cities, verses were dropped from most coins, and images returned on some. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 95} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 164}

## Trade and terms
Coins of the rulers of Khwarazm have been found from eastern Europe to Semirechye and China and from Scandinavia to Damascus; among them two drachms of Azkajuvar-Abd Allah in the Swedish History Museum, found in Östergötland and Gotland, and the Kreshcheny Baran hoard near Kazan (1905), which held 46 coins of Ali ibn Ma'mun. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 39} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 71} {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 224} Citing al-Zamakhshari, the author says pre-Islamic silver coins were called *tazija*, and that *daniq* and *mithqal* were coin names; he also records an old Khwarazmian coin called *aster*. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 223} From al-Narshakhi he reports that Khwarazmian silver was current in Bukhara in 185/801 and that people accepted it reluctantly. {cite:ismoilov-xorazmshohlar-tangalari-2025 p. 37}
