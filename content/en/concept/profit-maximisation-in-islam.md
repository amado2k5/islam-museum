## Summary
The author holds that profit maximization—striving for the greatest possible profit within constraints—is permissible in Islam, as is loss minimization {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 208} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 295}.

## The Principle of Profit Maximization
If two investments are equal in every respect except profit, one must choose the higher-yielding one, because choosing the lower is foolishness and lack of sound judgment {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 208}. This is close to opportunity cost, but frames the issue from the profit side {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 208}.

## The Principle of Loss Minimization
If two situations are equal in every respect except that one causes greater losses, and one of them must be chosen and neither can be avoided, one must choose the lesser loss—which falls under choosing the lesser of two evils {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 208}. The author cites the story of al-Khidr scuttling the boat, noting the following verse that the king was seizing every seaworthy boat (see [[passage/quran-18-79|Al-Kahf 79]]) {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 208} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 296}.

## Profit Maximization as a Term
The author considers objections to this wording unfounded, as i'zam carries the sense of maximization (ta'zim) and appears in the Quran {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 290}. He mentions synonymous terms in classical texts: tawfir (increasing), tanahi (striving to the utmost), and seeking ghibtah and hazz (advantage and fortune) {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 290}.

## Constraints
Profit maximization has constraints—legal in a capitalist economy and jurisprudential in an Islamic one—which may overlap or differ: they overlap regarding narcotics and bribery, and differ regarding riba, gambling, gharar (excessive uncertainty), alcohol, and matters of honor {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 291} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 292}.

## Juristic Precedents
The author notes that al-Mawardi said regarding the guardian of an orphan's property: it is not permissible to sell for less when one is able to obtain more, because foregoing the increase is a departure from the orphan's best interest {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 292}. He adds that jurists required selling at the full market price unless there is a strong likelihood of a further rise {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 292}. The author treats this financial management principle as a single rule governing orphans' property, endowments (awqaf), the public treasury (bayt al-mal), and mudarabah {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 293}.

## Ibn Khaldun and Trade
Ibn Khaldun states that a merchant maximizes profit by storing goods until markets shift, transporting a commodity to a land where it is in greater demand, or taking on greater risk {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 291} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 292}.

## Unfair Pricing
A contract is not annulled for gross price disparity (ghabn fahish) unless accompanied by fraud, deception, or concealment of a defect, and the buyer is deemed negligent for failing to gather expertise before purchasing {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 293} (see [[concept/ghabn-in-sale|Ghabn in Sale]]).

## Maximizing Endowment Yield
The author notes that substituting an endowment property (istibdal) to maximize its yield was permitted by some jurists, including Abu Yusuf, Abu Thawr, and Ibn Taymiyyah {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 244} (see [[concept/rent-of-land-in-islam|Land Rent]]).

## Summary
Profit maximization is permissible in Islam when pursued through lawful means; other factors being equal, one chooses the largest profit, the quickest return, and the lowest cost {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 295}.
