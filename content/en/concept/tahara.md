## Overview
*Tahara*, ritual purity, is the lifting of ritual impurity, *hadath*, and the removal of filth, *najas*, by water or by dry ablution, *tayammum*, in the terminology of the jurists. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.489} The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, describes the word as a verbal noun that signifies cleanliness or freedom from disgusting matter, and it is the heading under which manuals of jurisprudence take up questions of ritual order. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 114} The article stresses that the subject is cleanliness in the formal and ritual sense, not in the sense of hygiene, whatever some apologists have claimed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 114}

## The word and its origin
The article notes that some dictionaries put cleanliness as the basic sense of the word. It adds that the presence of the word in Syriac and Hebrew with a ritual meaning suggests it was already a technical term from its first use in the Qur'an, perhaps first for the cleansing of menstrual blood flow, as a dictionary entry quotes Ibn Abbas. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 114} It also offers, as a possibility, that the root has to do with distinction, a setting aside through cleansing. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 114}

## The two aspects
According to the article, the word has a material aspect and a formal aspect. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 114}

### The material aspect
The material aspect covers foods and other substances to be avoided or removed, for example pork, faeces, blood and carcasses, together with the means of removing them: the number of washings, the qualities of the water used, and similar matters. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 114} In general, the article says, the substances to be avoided are those connected with death, most but not all substances that come from within the body, such as blood, urine and semen, and items associated with carrion or with animals not eaten, such as dogs or pigs. When they cannot be avoided they must be removed in the proper manner, a subject treated under [[concept/najasa|najasa]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 114} The test that purity has been restored is that the thing can no longer be perceived in taste, smell or colour. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 114}

### The formal aspect
The formal aspect concerns whether a person is fit to carry out ritual practices and duties. Menstruation, childbirth, sexual excitement and consummation, defecation and urination, and various kinds of loss of control, including sleep while reclining according to some, quarrelling and violent laughter, call for an appropriate ritual cleansing. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 114} The article names two kinds. Ablution, [[practice/wudu|wudu]], answers minor or transient events, the *hadath* in the narrow sense, such as urination, defecation and breaking wind. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 114} The more complete washing, *ghusl*, answers events that bar a person from the religious community, a state called *janaba*, such as sexual activity or menstruation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 114} These matters are treated in the records on [[concept/hayd-menstruation|menstruation]], [[concept/nifas|postnatal bleeding]], [[concept/janaba|janaba]] and [[practice/ghusl-al-janaba|the washing for janaba]].

## Minor and major impurity
A separate EI2 article on [[concept/hadath-impurity|hadath]] describes it as the minor ritual impurity, distinguished in jurisprudence from the major impurity of janaba. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 28} It lists two ways of incurring it. The first is contact with an unclean substance that soils the person or clothing, such as sperm, pus, urine and fermented liquor, with some disagreement about corpses and the bodies of animals. The article observes that only the Maliki school holds that the pig and the dog do not soil while alive, and that, except among the Shi'a, contact with a human being never soils in Muslim law. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 28} The second is certain events: the emission of any solid, liquid or gaseous substance from the anus, urethra or vagina, loss of consciousness through sleep, fainting or madness, apostasy, and some other circumstances. The article adds that a Kharijite minority alone worked out a moral theory of hadath. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 28}

A person in a state of hadath regains purity through the simple ablution, which may be replaced by [[practice/tayammum|tayammum]] when water cannot be used. A part of the body soiled by an unclean substance is washed in ritually pure water, and the same holds for the clothing of a person at prayer and for the place where the prayer is to be made. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 28} In that state a person cannot perform the [[practice/salah|prayer]] or make the circumambulation of the [[building/kaaba|Kaba]], known as [[practice/tawaf|tawaf]], and cannot touch a copy of the Qur'an, though such a person may carry fragments of it, for instance on a medal or a piece of paper, and may recite from it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 28} The article adds that the requirement of *ghusl* after janaba partly explains why public baths exist in towns. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 28}

## Differences between the schools
The EI2 article says that the differences between the legal schools are too many to detail. In general, it says, the rules of the [[group/imamiyya|Imami]] and [[group/zaydiyya|Zaydi]] Shi'a are stricter, and these schools are more likely to see an impure thing or a person in janaba as spreading impurity by contact. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 114} The article adds that the Shi'a likewise do not accept People of the Book as butchers and providers of food. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 114} Elsewhere the encyclopaedia records the Maliki view that the living pig and dog do not soil, and the Shi'i view that contact with a human being can. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 28}

## In the hadith of Abu Malik al-Ash'ari
The hadith presents purity in a great faith-related form: "Purity is half of faith," reported by Muslim, number 223. In the version of al-Tirmidhi it is "ablution is half of faith," and in the version of Mu'awiya ibn Sallam in Ibn Majah, "perfecting ablution is half of faith." {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.489}

## With other deeds
In the same hadith purity comes together with the balance of praise, the light of prayer, the proof of charity, the radiance of patience and the argument of the Qur'an. In his commentary Ibn Rajab explains that it is a balance with which the servant weighs his deeds on the Day of Resurrection. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.490}

## In Zaghloul El-Naggar's series al-Hadara al-Nabawiyya
In [[work/el-hadara-el-nabaouia|al-Hadara al-Nabawiyya]], [[person/zaghloul-el-naggar|Zaghloul El-Naggar]] treats the command of the tooth-stick as an extension of bodily and inner purity. He says physical purity of body, clothes, shoes and place, in the mosque, the home and the office, is the way into inner purity and a help to humility in prayer, and that prayer, recitation of the Qur'an and circumambulation all call for purity in both senses. {cite:assabile-zaghloul-el-naggar el-hadara-el-nabaouia/ep25 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-zaghloul-el-naggar el-hadara-el-nabaouia/ep25 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-zaghloul-el-naggar el-hadara-el-nabaouia/ep25 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-zaghloul-el-naggar el-hadara-el-nabaouia/ep25 00:09:00} He lists the five practices of the natural way, circumcision, shaving the pubic hair, plucking the armpits, clipping the nails and trimming the moustache, as aids to physical purity. {cite:assabile-zaghloul-el-naggar el-hadara-el-nabaouia/ep25 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-zaghloul-el-naggar el-hadara-el-nabaouia/ep25 00:10:00}
