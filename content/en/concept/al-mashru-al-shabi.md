## Overview
Zaydan Qa'dat presents "people's institutions" as the form suited to enterprises in Islamic society, because in his view society's strength rests on the activities of human power, and Islam came to liberate the human being from restrictions and exploitation {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 93}.

## Who Works in the Enterprise
The author holds that everyone capable of contributing has a place in the people's enterprise: whoever lacks money, skill, or knowledge must contribute physical effort, and honorable work in his view is better than unemployment {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 93}. He counts three categories: those who contribute physical effort, those who contribute mental effort—experts, thinkers, and planners—and skilled technicians, alongside owners of capital who support people's enterprises {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 94}. He contrasts this with capitalist and communist enterprises, which view the human being only in terms of the profit or productivity he generates {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 94}.

## Advantages of This Form
The author lists four advantages: consultation in financial, organizational, and administrative decisions; rejecting the domination of capital; returning the benefits resulting from work to everyone; and self-monitoring to advance the enterprise's interest {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 95}. For this he stipulates educational programs focused on technical institutes to guide each person according to his inclinations {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 95}.

## Image of the Productive City
The author proposes establishing a people's city or village specialized in a particular trade or trades, supported by theoretical institutes for the same specialization, and establishing productive projects in universities to serve each department, such as an agricultural project for the agriculture department and a project for materials used by engineering students {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 95}, {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 96}. This is his own vision, and the book presents no reference experiment for it.
