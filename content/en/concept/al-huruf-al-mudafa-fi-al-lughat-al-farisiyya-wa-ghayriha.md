## Summary
This page concentrates on the letters languages added to the Arabic script, not on the languages themselves, which have their page at [[concept/al-lughat-al-maktuba-bil-harf-al-arabi|languages written in the Arabic script]]. It is as a research file supplied to the museum presents them: four Persian letters shared by many languages, then letters each language holds alone for its own sounds. The museum did not open the pages cited, so the letters and sounds are as the summary gave them.
## The four shared letters
The summary says Persian (32 letters) added four letters to Arabic, پ (p), چ (ch), ژ (zh) and گ (g), and has a yeh and a kaf distinct from the Arabic ones {cite:wikipedia-persian-alphabet-en} {cite:openarabic-perso-arabic-alphabet}. The same letters then appear in Urdu, Pashto, Sorani Kurdish and Uyghur, and with close sounds in Ottoman and Jawi and in some African Ajami, which makes them the most widely spread of the additions {cite:wikipedia-ottoman-turkish-alphabet-en} {cite:wikipedia-arabic-script-en}.

| Sorani Kurdish | ڕ ڵ ڤ ۆ ێ ە | Rolled r, dark l, v and vowel letters |

## Urdu and Pashto
To the four letters Urdu adds three retroflexes, ٹ ڈ ڑ, the nasal letter ں, the long yeh ے and two hehs, ہ and ھ, the second marking aspiration {cite:wikipedia-urdu-alphabet-en}. For Pashto the summary names twelve letters after the four and does not detail their sounds {cite:openarabic-perso-arabic-alphabet}. Urdu has its record at [[language/urdu|Urdu]] and Pashto at [[language/pashto|Pashto]].
## Kurdish and Uyghur
Sorani Kurdish has thirty-four letters, the four shared, a rolled r (ڕ), a dark l (ڵ), ڤ and three vowel letters ۆ ێ ە {cite:wikipedia-kurdish-alphabets-en}. Uyghur has thirty-two, with ڭ, ۋ and six vowel letters, which are mandatory in it {cite:wikipedia-uyghur-arabic-alphabet-en}. The Arabic script, which leaves short vowels unwritten, thus came in these two languages to write all the vowels.
## Jawi and Ottoman
Jawi has six extra consonants, چ ڠ ڤ ݢ ۏ ڽ, for Malay sounds {cite:academickids-jawi}. Ottoman has the four with ڭ, a Persian kaf giving the nasal n {cite:wikipedia-ottoman-turkish-alphabet-en}. The contrast is plain: Jawi added much for its sounds, while Ottoman was content with what Persian added and put one more.
## African Ajami
For Swahili there are two standardisations, by Mwalimu Sikujua and Yahya Ali Omar, which added پ چ ڤ ڠ ٹ ڈ ھ and vowel marks for e and o {cite:wikipedia-swahili-ajami-en}. Hausa has dotted letters in a Maghrebi style such as ٻ, forms for the implosive d and the ejective k, and mandatory vowels {cite:wikipedia-limba-hausa-ro}. Wolof has ݒ ݖ ݧ ݝ ݤ with six new vowel marks, bringing the vowels to nine {cite:wikipedia-wolofal-alphabet-en} {cite:wikipedia-wolofal-fr}. Dots and marks are Ajami's way to sounds Arabic lacks.
## Sindhi
The summary counts for Sindhi eighteen or more dotted letters added in the standardisation of 1853 to give implosives, retroflexes and aspirates, reaching fifty-two letters {cite:britannica-sindhi-language}. It does not detail the letters one by one.
## What remains unsettled
The summary wavers over Urdu between 39 and 40 letters, does not detail Pashto or Sindhi, and is brief on Hausa and Wolof, so no letters are ascribed here that it did not give. The letters are written as they came, and the museum did not open the references.
