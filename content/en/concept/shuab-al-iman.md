## Summary
The branches of faith are the traits the Prophet's hadith counts as parts of faith: belief in the heart, speech of the tongue and action of the body. The root of the subject is Abu Hurayra's hadith: "Faith is seventy-odd or sixty-odd branches; the best of them is saying there is no god but God, the least of them is removing harm from the road, and modesty is a branch of faith" {cite:sahih-muslim 0035}. On this hadith al-Halimi, al-Bayhaqi and Ibn Hibban built their books identifying the branches, and from it al-Bayhaqi's book takes its name, [[work/shuab-al-iman|Shu'ab al-Iman]].

## The text of the hadith and its versions
In Sahih al-Bukhari, through Abu 'Amir al-'Aqadi from Sulayman ibn Bilal from 'Abd Allah ibn Dinar from Abu Salih from Abu Hurayra: "Faith is sixty-odd branches, and modesty is a branch of faith" {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 9}. In Sahih Muslim it is "seventy-odd or sixty-odd", with the doubt, and with the best and the least named {cite:sahih-muslim 0035}. Al-Nawawi gathers the versions: al-Bukhari reports "sixty-odd" without doubt; Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi and others report it through Suhayl as "seventy-odd" without doubt; and al-Tirmidhi reports it by another route with the words "sixty-four gates" {cite:sharh-sahih-muslim-al-nawawi p.117}. He reports from Abu 'Amr ibn al-Salah that the doubt is Suhayl's, as al-Bayhaqi said; that preferring the smaller number is closer to precision and caution; and that some scholars preferred the larger, which al-Halimi chose, because the ruling goes to the one who retained the addition with certainty {cite:sharh-sahih-muslim-al-nawawi p.117}.

## What "odd" and "branch" mean
Al-Nawawi reports from al-Qadi 'Iyad that bid' in counting is between three and ten, or, it is said, from three to nine, and that a shu'ba is a piece of a thing, so the hadith means: seventy-odd traits {cite:sharh-sahih-muslim-al-nawawi p.116}. He reports from him that faith in the language is assent, and in the law assent of the heart and the tongue; that the outward sense of the texts applies it to deeds, as in this hadith; and that faith is perfected by deeds and completed by acts of obedience {cite:sharh-sahih-muslim-al-nawawi p.116}. This is why the subject touches the dispute over [[concept/iman|what faith is]] and whether deeds are part of it.

## The highest and the lowest
Al-Nawawi says the Prophet pointed out that the best branch is the declaration of God's oneness, required of everyone, and that the least is removing from the Muslims' road whatever harm is feared for them, and that numbers remain between these two ends {cite:sharh-sahih-muslim-al-nawawi p.116} {cite:sharh-sahih-muslim-al-nawawi p.117}. Removing means setting aside and taking away, and harm is anything that hurts: a stone, a clod, a thorn or the like {cite:sharh-sahih-muslim-al-nawawi p.117}. Al-'Ayni reports from al-Nawawi that no branch is valid until the declaration of oneness is sound, and that one must believe in the full number even when not every branch can be named {cite:umdat-qari-ilmiyya-2001-v1 p.212}.

## Why modesty is counted as faith
The hadith singles out [[concept/haya|modesty]] among the branches. Al-Nawawi reports from al-Qadi 'Iyad and other commentators that modesty is counted as faith, though it is an instinct, because it can also be a character acquired like other good deeds, because using it by the measure of the law needs acquisition, intention and knowledge, and because it prompts good deeds and bars sins {cite:sharh-sahih-muslim-al-nawawi p.117}. To those puzzled by the hadith's words that modesty is wholly good, he answers, from Ibn al-Salah, that what stops a person from facing another with the truth is not modesty in reality but weakness and faintness, and calling it modesty is a figure of speech {cite:sharh-sahih-muslim-al-nawawi p.117}.

## Who counted the branches
Al-Nawawi reports that Abu Hatim ibn Hibban studied the hadith's meaning for some time: he counted the acts of obedience and they far exceeded the number; he counted what the Prophet called faith in the Sunan and it fell short of seventy-odd; he counted what God called faith in His Book and it fell short too; he then joined the Book to the Sunan, dropped the repetitions, and the total was seventy-nine branches, as the scan reads; he set this out in his book Wasf al-Iman wa-Shu'abih {cite:sharh-sahih-muslim-al-nawawi p.117}. He reports from Ibn al-Salah that many books identify the branches, that among the richest is the Minhaj of Abu 'Abd Allah al-Halimi, and that al-Bayhaqi followed his path in Shu'ab al-Iman {cite:sharh-sahih-muslim-al-nawawi p.117}. Al-'Ayni adds Ishaq ibn al-Qurtubi and his book al-Nasa'ih, then says he saw none of them settle the matter, and gives his own count {cite:umdat-qari-ilmiyya-2001-v1 p.212}.

## Al-'Ayni's count: seventy-seven branches in three groups
Al-'Ayni says the root of faith is assent of the heart and affirmation by the tongue, and that complete faith is assent, affirmation and action: three groups {cite:umdat-qari-ilmiyya-2001-v1 p.212}.

**Group one: belief, thirty branches** {cite:umdat-qari-ilmiyya-2001-v1 p.212}:
1. Belief in God, including belief in His essence, His attributes and His oneness.
2. Believing that everything other than God came into being.
3. Belief in His angels.
4. Belief in His books.
5. Belief in His messengers.
6. Belief in the decree, its good and its ill.
7. Belief in the Last Day, including the questioning in the grave, the resurrection, the reckoning, the scale and the bridge.
8. Trust in the promise of Paradise and of abiding in it.
9. Certainty of the threat of the Fire and its punishment.
10. Love of God.
11. Loving for God's sake and hating for God's sake, including love of the Companions and of the Prophet's family.
12. Love of the Prophet, including invoking blessings on him and following his sunna.
13. Sincerity, including leaving ostentation and hypocrisy.
14. Repentance and remorse.
15. Fear.
16. Hope.
17. Leaving despair.
18. Gratitude.
19. Keeping faith.
20. Patience.
21. Humility, including respect for elders.
22. Mercy and compassion, including compassion for the young.
23. Contentment with what is decreed.
24. Reliance on God.
25. Leaving conceit and vanity, including leaving self-praise.
26. Leaving envy.
27. Leaving rancour.
28. Leaving anger.
29. Leaving deceit, including ill suspicion and scheming.
30. Leaving love of this world, including love of wealth and rank.

**Group two: acts of the tongue, seven branches** {cite:umdat-qari-ilmiyya-2001-v1 p.212} {cite:umdat-qari-ilmiyya-2001-v1 p.213}:
1. Uttering the declaration of oneness.
2. Reciting the Qur'an.
3. Learning knowledge.
4. Teaching knowledge.
5. Supplication.
6. Remembrance, including asking forgiveness.
7. Avoiding idle talk.

**Group three: acts of the body, forty branches of three kinds** {cite:umdat-qari-ilmiyya-2001-v1 p.213}.

What concerns the individual, sixteen branches:
1. Purification, including purity of body, clothing and place, ablution and the bath.
2. Establishing the prayer, obligatory, voluntary and made up.
3. Paying the zakat, including charity, the alms of the fast-breaking, generosity, feeding others and honouring the guest.
4. Fasting, obligatory and voluntary.
5. The pilgrimage, including the 'umra.
6. Retreat in the mosque, including seeking the Night of Decree.
7. Fleeing with one's religion, including emigration.
8. Fulfilling vows.
9. Care in oaths (the word on the scan can be read two ways).
10. Paying expiation.
11. Covering the body in prayer and outside it.
12. Offering the sacrifices.
13. Attending to funerals.
14. Paying debts.
15. Truthfulness in dealings.
16. Giving true testimony and not concealing it.

What concerns dependants, six branches:
1. Chastity through marriage.
2. Meeting the rights of the household, including kindness to servants.
3. Honouring parents and avoiding undutifulness.
4. Raising children.
5. Keeping ties of kinship.
6. Obedience to masters.

What concerns the public, eighteen branches:
1. Holding command with justice.
2. Keeping with the community.
3. Obeying those in authority.
4. Making peace between people.
5. Helping one another in good.
6. Enjoining right and forbidding wrong.
7. Upholding the fixed penalties.
8. Jihad, including guarding the frontier.
9. Rendering trusts, including paying the fifth.
10. Lending, and repaying the loan.
11. Honouring the neighbour.
12. Fair dealing, including earning wealth lawfully.
13. Spending wealth rightly, including leaving waste and excess.
14. Returning the greeting.
15. Responding to the one who sneezes.
16. Keeping harm from people.
17. Avoiding frivolity.
18. Removing harm from the road.

Al-'Ayni then says: "these are seventy-seven branches" {cite:umdat-qari-ilmiyya-2001-v1 p.213}. This is al-'Ayni's count and his own reasoning, not a text of the hadith; Ibn Hibban counted seventy-nine, and al-Halimi and al-Bayhaqi arranged them otherwise.

## The pillars within the branches
Al-'Ayni's count shows that the [[concept/arkan-al-iman|six pillars of faith]] are the first branches of the group of belief, and that the [[concept/five-pillars|five pillars of Islam]] are spread over the other two groups: uttering the declaration of oneness heads the acts of the tongue, and the prayer, the zakat, the fast and the pilgrimage are among the first acts of the body {cite:umdat-qari-ilmiyya-2001-v1 p.212} {cite:umdat-qari-ilmiyya-2001-v1 p.213}. The four themes of this browser overlap in his scheme: the pillars are the roots of the branches, and the [[concept/khisal-al-mumin|traits of the believer]] are their offshoots.

## Al-Bayhaqi's book
In [[work/shuab-al-iman|Shu'ab al-Iman]] al-Bayhaqi takes the branches one by one, reports under each its hadiths and reports with his chains, and opens the chapter with al-Halimi's words. In volume 2 of the Dar al-Kutub al-'Ilmiyya edition: "the twelfth of the branches of faith: a chapter on hope in God", opening with al-Halimi's statement that hope is of several kinds {cite:shuab-iman-dki-zaghlul-v2 p.3}. Volume 10 of the Maktabat al-Rushd edition holds branches fifty to fifty-seven {cite:shuab-iman-dar-al-rushd-v10}. The browser shows excerpts from both volumes.
