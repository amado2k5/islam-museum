## Overview
Iddah is the waiting period a woman observes after divorce or widowhood; the Kuwaiti Fiqh Encyclopedia treats it across the marriage and divorce chapters {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3}.

## The institution in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, defines the iddah as the period of abstention from sexual relations imposed on a widow, a divorced woman or a woman whose marriage has been annulled before she may marry again {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1042}. The article says that in pre-Islamic Arabia the institution is thought to have been unknown for a divorced woman, and that the Qur'anic provisions behind it were not always respected in the early years of Islam. It adds that the jurists very early gave them legal force by treating any marriage contracted during an iddah as completely void {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1042} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1043}.

The article presents two methods of calculation. The first counts months and days and applies mainly to widows. The second counts three menstrual periods and applies to a divorced woman or one whose marriage has been annulled. A third event ends the iddah in every case, namely childbirth. The article says the Qur'anic rules were too clear for the great jurists to alter them {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1042}.

## Pregnant women
For a pregnant woman the iddah lasts until she gives birth and ends with the birth, whatever ended the marriage: the husband's death, divorce or annulment {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1042} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1043}. The article notes that almost only the Twelver Shi'is and the [[group/zaydiyya|Zaydis]] differ, and only for widows: they extend the iddah beyond the birth until four months and ten days have passed. They hold that the period exists not only to avoid confusion over paternity but also out of respect for the dead husband, and they think it unfitting that a widow should remarry soon after his death merely because she has given birth {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1043}.

## Widows and divorced women
A widow who is not pregnant observes four months and ten days. According to the article this applies whether or not the marriage was consummated and whether or not she has reached puberty, provided the marriage was validly concluded. The article says the reasons for this figure are not very clear, and that here the idea of avoiding doubt about paternity gives way to social and family propriety {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1043}.

A divorced woman who is too young or too old to menstruate observes three lunar months. A divorced woman who menstruates waits for three of the periods the Qur'an calls qur'. The article says the meaning of this word has been disputed among commentators since the beginning of Islam {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1043}.

- The [[group/shafii-madhhab|Shafi'i]] and [[group/maliki-madhhab|Maliki]] schools, and the Ja'fari Shi'is, take it to mean the intervals of purity between menstruations.
- The [[group/hanafi-madhhab|Hanafi]] and [[group/hanbali-madhhab|Hanbali]] schools, and the Zaydis, take it as a synonym of [[concept/hayd-menstruation|menstruation]].

The article says this produces slight variations in the length of the iddah {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1043}.

## Consummation and seclusion
The article observes that a widow must abstain even if the marriage was never consummated, whereas for divorced women and women whose marriage was annulled, Muslim law requires an iddah only when the marriage was consummated. In Shafi'i law it must be true consummation, because that school rejects the theory of [[concept/khalwa-fiqh|khalwa]], by which consummation is presumed when the spouses were alone together in a place where intercourse was possible. The other schools accept the presumption. The article reports that most Hanafi, Maliki and Hanbali writers hold that khalwa does not oblige an iddah when there was an insurmountable obstacle to consummation, such as the husband's emasculation {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1043}.

## Changes of status during the iddah
The article says the classical writers anticipated cases in which one method of counting gives way to the other. A girl who reaches puberty during an iddah counted in months is one. Another is a woman in a revocable divorce whose husband dies during the iddah, because the marriage is not yet finally dissolved; from his death she is bound by the widow's term {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1044}.

A woman finally divorced, called ba'in, always observes the term counted by menstruations, even if her husband dies during it, since her widowhood comes after the marriage ended. Only when the divorce was pronounced during the husband's last illness, when the wife keeps her right to inherit, must she observe two terms and the longer of them applies {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1044}.

## When the iddah begins
For a valid marriage the iddah begins when the marriage ends, that is at the husband's death or at the pronouncing of the divorce, even if the wife does not know of it. The article says that before modern laws required divorces to be made public, a wife could complete her whole iddah without knowing. By contrast, an annulment, or a husband's decision to separate for a particular reason, must be reported to the wife, and the iddah then runs from the moment she hears of it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1044}.

## Practical difficulties and false claims
Counting by menstruations depends in the end on the woman's own statement. The article says she might claim no third period to lengthen the iddah, or claim very short periods to shorten it. The first behaviour was, in its words, particularly frequent among Hanafi populations, because Hanafi law allows a finally divorced woman her whole maintenance, nafaqa, throughout the iddah, so a woman with no prospect of remarrying had reason to delay announcing the third period. The Shafi'is and Malikis allow her only residence in the husband's house, which the article calls a fairly miserly concession, and it says deception is much less frequent there {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1044}.

Modern legislation has addressed this by limits. The article says Egypt and Sudan keep the iddah open but stop maintenance after twelve months, Syria stops it after nine months, and Ottoman and Jordanian law set a maximum of nine months for the iddah itself. Some countries have also fixed a minimum of three months for an iddah counted by menstruations {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1044} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1045}.

The opposite deception was foreseen too: a divorced woman who already has a marriage offer and fears the suitor will tire of waiting might claim shorter periods. In Hanafi law, following Abu Hanifa, the iddah may never be shorter than 60 days, while the other schools set minima of 30 to 39 days. The article says the woman's word alone is not enough even at the school's minimum of 30 days, and she must be examined further {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1044}.

## Female slaves
The article gives brief rules for female slaves. A slave who is only a concubine observes a retreat of one menstruation, or one month if she is not of menstruating age, from the time she passes to a new owner who intends to have relations with her, or when her legal status changes; this is called istibra {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1044}. A married slave observes an iddah by the rule of the half-rate, so a widow's term is two months and five days and that of a divorced slave not of menstruating age is one and a half months. Because three menstruations cannot be halved, the divorced slave of menstruating age observes two intervals of purity in Shafi'i and Maliki law, or two menstruations in Hanafi and Hanbali law. An umm walad is the exception and is treated as a free woman {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1044}.

## Rights and duties during the iddah
The article says a widow never has the right to full maintenance, even if pregnant, and that all schools agree. She is expected to complete the iddah in her husband's house, and as a moral obligation to go into mourning and to wear no jewellery or cosmetics. She may leave the house more freely than a divorced woman, since she may have to earn a living. She shares in the estate {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1044}.

For the finally divorced woman the schools differ {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1044}.

- The Hanafis make the former husband responsible for food, clothing and housing throughout the iddah, even if she is not pregnant, except where she herself caused the end of the marriage, for example by adultery or apostasy.
- The Hanbalis refuse her any right.
- The Malikis and Shafi'is give her only the right to housing.
- All of these, if she is pregnant, require full maintenance for her.

She does not inherit unless he divorced her in his last illness, and in Shafi'i law not even then. Most schools do not require her to go into mourning, and the article says only Hanafi law does so, probably in compensation for the maintenance that school grants. The position of a woman in a revocable divorce is the same as that of a married woman, and every woman observing an iddah has both the right and the duty to do so in her former husband's house {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1044}.

## Marriage during the iddah
A marriage entered into during an iddah is void, or fasid in Hanafi law. The couple must separate, or a judge pronounces the nullity. The wife receives the full customary dower if the marriage was consummated, and children born are legitimate. After the second marriage ends, the Shafi'is and Hanbalis require two terms: what remained of the first iddah and a new one of three periods. The Hanafis require one term of three periods, against which the part of the first already completed may be set. The article calls the doctrine on combining the two terms obscure and diverse. The wife may then marry the second husband, with a new contract and a new [[concept/mahr|dower]]. Only the Malikis and the Shi'is, citing a decision attributed to [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] that he is said to have reversed, say she is forbidden to him for ever {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1044}.

## Modern codes
The article says modern codes, from the Ottoman Family Law of 1917 to the Iraqi Code of Personal Status of 1959, kept the two Qur'anic periods without significant departure from classical law. The Tunisian code replaced the three menstruations with a fixed period of three months. The few other changes, mostly in Hanafi countries, concern a maximum for the counting by menstruations {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1044} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1045}.

## In the sources
The encyclopedia examines the term of the iddah, the supervening iddah, residence in the iddah home, and the effect of miscarriage on the iddah {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3}.

## For Scholars
Idda is the term a woman observes after divorce or widowhood; the Kuwaiti fiqh encyclopaedia treats it in the marriage and divorce chapters, covering the term's length, the interim idda, dwelling in the idda house, and the effect of miscarriage on it.

## In al-Sha'rawi's programme "Liqa' al-Iman"
In episode 15 of [[work/likaa-al-iman|Liqa' al-Iman]] the presenter notes that the waiting period of a widow is four months and ten days, and asks its wisdom now that science can tell what is in the womb. {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep15 00:00:00} {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep15 00:00:30} [[person/muhammad-mutawalli-al-shaarawi|Al-Sha'rawi]] says the waiting period was set to establish that the womb is free, so that no man waters another's crop, but not for that alone: the woman is not a beast, and if it were only for the womb it would not have been set at three menstrual cycles, which lengthen the term. {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep15 00:00:30} {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep15 00:01:00} He holds that divorce comes from aversion while death does not, so affection and longing remain, and the widow's waiting period needed a longer span for the soul to settle. {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep15 00:04:30}
