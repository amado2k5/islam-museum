## Summary
Ibn Sina explains that bath water may warm or cool, that cooling comes from long soaking, from the cold nature of water and from excessive moistening, and that the bath may also warm by dissolving, where it finds undigested food or unripened humour {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.140-141}.

## Warming
Bath water warms through its heat if it is hot, while water whose heat is below lukewarm cools and moistens. When cold water meets the body it confines the heat gained from the bath's air and gathers it in the viscera. The bath also warms by dissolving, when it finds undigested food and an unripened cold humour, which it then digests {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.140-141}.

## Cooling
The bath cools when soaking is prolonged, in two ways. Water is cold by nature, so in the end it cools, and any accidental warming does not last but passes, leaving the natural action of the water the body has absorbed, which is cooling. Second, water, hot or cold, is the more moistening, and if it moistens to excess it confines the innate heat by the amount of moisture and quenches it, so the body cools {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.141}.
