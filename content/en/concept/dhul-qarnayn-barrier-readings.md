## Summary
[[passage/quran-18-83|Al-Kahf 83]] and the verses after it tell of Dhu al-Qarnayn, a king given power on earth who followed a way until he came between two barriers, found a people who complained of the corruption of Gog and Magog, and raised for them a rampart of iron blocks over which molten metal was poured. The verses do not give the builder's name, his age or the place of the rampart, and so the exegetes and the geographers offered several readings of the place and of the man. {cite:tanzil-quran} {cite:jami-ahkam-quran-alam-kutub-bukhari vol. 11 pp. 46-62}

## What the verses say
From [[passage/quran-18-83|Al-Kahf 83]] to [[passage/quran-18-98|Al-Kahf 98]] the builder makes three journeys: to where the sun sets, at a muddy spring, among a people over whom he was given a choice; then to where the sun rises; then to a place between two barriers, beyond which lived a people who hardly understood speech. They offered him payment for a barrier; he refused the payment and asked for their labour, and they brought blocks of iron until the gap was level with the two sides. He had the iron blown upon and then poured molten metal over it, and no one could climb it or pierce it. He said this was a mercy from his Lord and that when his Lord's promise came He would level it. See [[passage/quran-18-94|Al-Kahf 94]], [[passage/quran-18-96|Al-Kahf 96]] and [[passage/quran-18-98|Al-Kahf 98]]. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Where the exegetes placed the barrier
Al-Qurtubi relates from Ata al-Khurasani, from Ibn Abbas, that the two barriers were two mountains on the side of Armenia and Azerbaijan. {cite:jami-ahkam-quran-alam-kutub-bukhari vol. 11 p. 55} Ibn Kathir says they were two facing mountains with a gap from which Gog and Magog came out upon the land of the Turks. {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-harvest-0012994 on 18:92, p. 712} Al-Qurtubi also gives figures for the rampart's length and breadth from Wahb ibn Munabbih; they are Wahb's report, not a chain going back to the Prophet. {cite:jami-ahkam-quran-alam-kutub-bukhari vol. 11 p. 62} Ibn Kathir notes that al-Tabari transmitted from Wahb a long report on Dhu al-Qarnayn's travels and the building of the barrier, which Ibn Kathir calls long, strange and objectionable. {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-harvest-0012994 on 18:94, p. 713}

In geography, Yaqut's entry on the barrier of Gog and Magog collects al-Sha'bi's report on Dhu al-Qarnayn's travels and then Sallam the Interpreter's account. Al-Wathiq dreamt that the barrier had been opened and sent Sallam with fifty men; he travelled with letters to the rulers along the road until he reached forts near a smooth mountain cut by a valley, where stood an iron gate with an enormous lock and key, and the chief of the forts struck the lock on set days so that those behind the gate would hear it. Yaqut closes by saying he has written what he found in the books, does not vouch for it because the reports differ, and that the barrier itself is beyond doubt since the Qur'an mentions it. {cite:mujam-al-buldan-yaqut-sader-shamela23735 vol. 3 pp. 197-200} The account is originally in Ibn Khurdadhbih, who says Sallam dictated it to him, and al-Idrisi's Nuzhat al-Mushtaq carries it from Ibn Khurdadhbih and from Abu Nasr al-Jayhani. {cite:masalik-mamalik-ibn-khurdadhbih pp. 162-168} {cite:nuzhat-al-mushtaq-2002 pp. 934-936} The museum keeps a separate record of this mission: [[event/sallam-sadd-expedition]] and [[person/sallam-al-tarjuman]]. Ibn Kathir himself mentions al-Wathiq's mission, saying the party saw a structure of iron and copper, a great door and great locks, and were away more than two years. {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-harvest-0012994 on 18:95, p. 714}

## Who Dhu al-Qarnayn was
Al-Qurtubi gathers many views on his name and time: Alexander the Greek of Macedon; Hermes or Hardis; al-Sa'b ibn Dhi Yazan the Himyarite, which he attributes to Ibn Hisham; Wahb ibn Munabbih's view that he was a Roman; al-Suhayli's judgement that there appear to have been two, one in Abraham's day and one near Jesus's; and some who said he was Afridun. He also reports from Ali that he was a righteous servant who counselled God, in one report neither prophet nor angel, and that Umar and Ali objected when someone called another person "O Dhu al-Qarnayn", saying people had not been content with the names of prophets until they took the names of angels. {cite:jami-ahkam-quran-alam-kutub-bukhari vol. 11 pp. 46-48} Al-Tabari records in his history that some Persian genealogists claimed Afridhun was Dhu al-Qarnayn, Abraham's companion who judged for him at Beersheba. {cite:tabari-khayyat-part1 scan p. 231}

:::perspectives
**Sunni: Ibn Kathir.** He distinguishes two men: Alexander son of Philip of Macedon, whose minister was Aristotle and who lived about three hundred years before the Messiah, and the Dhu al-Qarnayn of the Qur'an, whom he places in Abraham's time, citing al-Azraqi and others for his circling the House with him. He weakens the report from Uqba ibn Amir that he was a youth of the Romans who built Alexandria, saying its raising to the Prophet is not sound. {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-harvest-0012994 on 18:83, pp. 706-707}

**Sunni: al-Qurtubi relays the disagreement without choosing.** In his pages the candidates are Alexander of Macedon, al-Sa'b the Himyarite, Afridun and others, with al-Suhayli's remark that there were two. {cite:jami-ahkam-quran-alam-kutub-bukhari vol. 11 pp. 46-48}

**Academic: Muhammad al-Tahir ibn Ashur.** In al-Tahrir wa-l-Tanwir he lists the three clearest views as Alexander of Macedon, the Himyarite king Tubba Abu Karib, and the Persian Afridun. He then draws from the verses a set of traits for the builder, including that he was a just, righteous and inspired king, rules out Alexander because he was not righteous but a pagan and is not known to have raised a barrier between two lands, and prefers a Chinese king who built the wall dividing China from the Mongol lands, whom he names in transliteration as Tsin Shi Hwang-ti. This is a reasoned opinion that departs from the majority. {cite:tahrir-wa-l-tanwir-ibn-ashur-dar-al-tunisia on 18:83, pp. 18-23}

**A modern view that he was Cyrus of Persia.** Modern writing reports that Abul Kalam Azad held him to be Cyrus the Achaemenid. His own text was not read in this pass, so the claim is recorded here as reported, not confirmed. [[person/abul-kalam-azad]]
:::

## What the sources do not specify
The Qur'an does not give the builder's name, language or people, and does not say whether he was a prophet or a king. It does not name the two mountains, their country or the date of the building. Every length, breadth and colour given for the barrier is a report from Wahb ibn Munabbih or a traveller's account; it is not a Qur'anic text, and not a sound raised hadith in the text read here. Ibn Kathir held that what some of the People of the Book relate in this matter cannot be relied on. {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-harvest-0012994 on 18:94, p. 713}
