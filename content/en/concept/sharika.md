## Summary
The author explains that Islam encouraged partnerships because of the cooperation they embody; he groups their forms into agricultural partnerships (muzara'ah, musaqah, and mugharasa) and commercial partnerships (mudarabah, partnerships of capital, partnerships of labor, and partnerships of reputation), noting that partnerships allow enterprises to grow larger and be managed more efficiently {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 114} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 143}.

## Overview
The author considers partnership a means of large-scale production, some forms based on land and others based on profit alone. Islam encouraged it because it fosters cooperation through which the scale of an enterprise grows and is managed more efficiently {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 143}.

## Hadith on Partnership
The author cites the hadith "God's hand is with the two partners as long as they do not betray one another," and the sacred hadith "I am the third of two partners as long as neither betrays the other" {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 114}. He points out that developing classical partnerships and devising modern forms for them is sufficient to accommodate large enterprises, without restricting them to the public sector {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 114}, a view he quotes from Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr.

## Agricultural Partnerships
Muzara'ah is a partnership between a landowner and a worker for a share of the crop; musaqah is a partnership between the owner of land and trees and the worker who irrigates them for a share of the fruit; and mugharasa is a partnership between a landowner and a worker who plants trees for a share of the planted trees {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 143} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 144}.

## Commercial Partnerships
A profit partnership rests on agreement over profit rather than crops, fruit, or plantings. It is divided into a partnership of capital (a profit partnership among owners of monetary capital), a partnership of labor (a partnership in earnings between two workers without joint capital), and a partnership of reputation (a partnership in earnings or profit between two reputable individuals, or between a reputable person and an obscure one, in which goods are bought on credit) {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 144} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 145}. He notes that the forms agreed upon as permissible are mudarabah and the partnership of capital, and that the Hanafi and Hanbali schools are the broadest in permitting these partnerships {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 145}.

## Legal Personality and Limited Liability
The author describes the modern distinction between a natural person and a legal person, whereby a company has an independent name and financial liability and a shareholder's liability is limited to their share; he argues that classical jurisprudence recognized limited liability in qirad, where the capital provider is liable only to the extent of what they contributed {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 146} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 148}.



## Partnership in Output, Profit, and Risk
The author distinguishes between a partnership in output—such as muzara'ah, musaqah, and mugharasa—and a partnership in profit, such as mudarabah: output consists of crops, fruits, and plantings, whereas profit is what remains after costs {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 298}.

The author holds that a partnership of labor (taqabbul) and a partnership of reputation earn entitlement to profit through work or guaranteed capital, and that liability alone does not entitle one to profit except derivatively {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 283} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 284} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 286}.
