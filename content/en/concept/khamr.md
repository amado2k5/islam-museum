## Overview
The sources differ on wine (*khamr*) in language and in jurisprudence. Here are two accounts: a literary description in al-Masudi, and a jurisprudential disagreement in *Duha al-Islam*. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 707-709} {cite:duha-islam p. 119-120}

## Al-Masudi's description
In a gathering held for al-Mustakfi, al-Masudi reports that wine took the excellence of the four mothers: the colour of fire, the lightness of air, the sweetness of water, and the coolness of earth. He mentions the similes of the poets, among them Abu Nuwas. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 707-709} This is a report of what was said at a literary gathering, not a ruling on it.

## The jurisprudential disagreement
*Duha al-Islam* notes that the three imams, Malik, al-Shafi'i, and Ahmad, defined wine to include every intoxicating drink, and held that all of them are wine and forbidden. Abu Hanifa defined it as the juice of grapes on the basis of the language, and permitted some drinks if they were boiled and a quantity was drunk that does not intoxicate. He notes that he followed in this Ibn Mas'ud and the majority of the Successors from the people of Kufa, and that Abd Allah ibn Idris was the only one among the jurists of Kufa who forbade it. {cite:duha-islam p. 119-120}

This presentation is only Ahmad Amin's view. It needs a further source from the books of jurisprudence themselves.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lecture series Al Dinu Al Qayem

The Sheikh recounts the revelation on wine, saying that it has some benefit for people but that its sin outweighs its benefit, and that Umar asked the Prophet for a clear ruling {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep02 00:28:30}. He reports that Abu Bakr said he had never drunk wine, neither in the Jahiliyya nor after Islam {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep02 00:29:00}. He says that the Qur'anic term for avoidance in prohibitions appears only in connection with idolatry and wine (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep02 00:30:00}.

He recounts Anas going through Medina announcing the ban on wine, and the Muslims pouring out the wine jars into the streets of the city; he says the smell lingered there for many days (needs_source: the duration) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep02 00:31:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep02 00:32:00}.

The Sheikh recounts Umar ibn al-Khattab rebuking men who drank behind a closed door, asking them whether they thought God did not see them {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep01 00:42:00}. He tells, in his own account, of the son of Umar who was accused of drinking in private and brought to Amr ibn al-As, who applied the prescribed penalty {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep05 00:26:30}.

The Sheikh holds, as his own opinion, that the Western permissiveness towards wine does harm, and cites the case of a pilot who was drunk {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep01 01:03:30}.

## For Scholars
Khamr in the language and in fiqh is a subject on which the sources differ; two transmissions here — a literary description in al-Mas'udi and a juristic dispute in the Dawn of Islam. Al-Mas'udi records in a night-talk of al-Mustakfi that wine drew the virtue of the four mothers: the color of fire, the smoothness of air, the sweetness of water and the coolness of earth, and he records the poets' comparisons, Abu Nuwas among them — an account of what was said in a literary circle, not a ruling. The Dawn of Islam records that the three imams Malik, al-Shafi'i and Ahmad glossed khamr as covering every intoxicating brew — all of it wine and forbidden — while Abu Hanifa glossed it as grape wine on the language's strength, permitting some brews if cooked and drunk below the point of drunkenness; it records that he followed Ibn Mas'ud and the generality of the Kufan Followers in that, and that Abdullah ibn Idris was the only Kufan jurist to forbid it.

## From Omar Abdelkafy's series on the Hikam of Ibn Ata Allah
Omar Abdelkafy describes the gradual prohibition of wine: the Qur'an first said that in it are sin and benefits and that its sin is greater than its benefit, and then came the prohibition. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep07 00:26:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep07 00:27:00} He relates that a Western country passed a law banning alcohol, known as the 'dry law', after statistics on its harms, and that the people of Medina poured wine into the streets when the prohibition came down. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep07 00:28:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep07 00:29:00} (needs_source: the statistics he refers to are not named.)
