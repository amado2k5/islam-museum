## Overview
The author places this discussion in the introductory section, treating private interest as legitimate so long as it does not cause harm, and leaving it to the Muslim to discipline themselves as necessary so as to yield to the public interest when a conflict arises {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 18} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 19} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 80}.

## From the book Usul al-Iqtisad al-Islami: Part Two (PDF pp. 101–200)
When interests conflict, they are ranked and the one with the greatest benefit is secured; when harms conflict, the greater harm is avoided, following these maxims: general interest takes precedence over particular interest, private harm is borne to ward off public harm, and warding off harms takes precedence over securing benefits {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 131} {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 132}.

The author holds that worldly affairs are not separate from religion, and are governed by unattested public interest (masalih mursalah), juristic preference (istihsan), custom, Sharia-compliant governance, consultation (shura), and the opinions of experts {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 142}.

## Private and Public Interest in the Financial Crisis (pp. 357–358)
The author states that the capitalist system focuses on the individual's pursuit of self-interest, causing private interests to override public and charitable interests, whereas Islam does not prohibit private interest—viewing it as a sign of sound judgment—while requiring that when it conflicts with the public interest, the public interest must prevail {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 357}.
