## Summary
Ibn Sina shows that the four natural faculties are served by the four first qualities, heat, cold, moisture and dryness: heat serves them all, cold only by accident and not by itself, and dryness and moisture according to the work of each faculty {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.96-98}.

## Heat and cold
The service of heat is in truth common to all four. Cold may serve some of them, but by accident and not by itself, for what cold is by itself is to oppose all the faculties, since all their acts are by movements, and cold is deadening, lowering and a barrier to these acts; yet it helps retention by accident, by holding the fibre in the right enclosing posture, so that it prepares the tool and does not enter the act of the faculty itself. The expulsive profits from cold in that it hinders the dissolving of the wind that helps the push, helps in thickening, and gathers and shelters the broad squeezing fibre, which is again preparation of the tool; were it to enter the act itself it would harm it and still the movement {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.96-97}.

## Dryness
Three faculties need dryness in their acts: the two carrying ones, the attractive and the expulsive, and the retentive. The carrying two need the firmer hold that dryness gives, which the movement of the spirit carrying these powers toward their act, with strong thrust, cannot do without, and which moist slackness in the spirit's substance or the tool would prevent. The retentive's need for dryness is greater than its need for heat, because its time of stillness is longer than the time it spends moving the transverse fibre to grip, and since the temperament of children leans much toward moisture, this faculty is weak in them {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.97}. The attractive needs heat more than dryness, since most of its time of action is movement and it needs strong movement. Drawing is completed either by the drawing power itself, as the magnet draws iron, or by the compulsion of the void, as water is drawn into syringes, or by heat, as a lamp's flame draws oil, though the third, for the verifiers, returns to the compulsion of the void; so whenever heat helps the attractive faculty, drawing is stronger. The expulsive needs less dryness than the attractive and retentive, as it has no need to be still at all, only to move and to condense a little to help squeeze and push {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.97}.

## The digestive and children
Most in need of heat is the digestive, which has no need of dryness but of moisture, to ease the food and prepare it to enter the channels and take shapes {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.98}. Ibn Sina answers the objection that if moisture helped digestion, children would not fail with hard foods: the cause is likeness and distance from likeness, for what is hard does not resemble the children's temperament, so their digestive faculties do not master it, the retentive does not accept it and the expulsive pushes it out quickly, while for youths it suits their temperament and nourishes them {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.98}. In sum, the retentive needs gripping and holding the grip long with little help in movement; the attractive a very short grip and much help in movement; the expulsive a grip without notable holding; and the digestive melting and mixing; so the faculties differ in their use of the four qualities {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.98}.
