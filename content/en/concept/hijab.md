## Overview
The hijab in juristic usage is that which veils the woman; the Kuwaiti Fiqh Encyclopedia gives it a dedicated entry distinguishing its material senses — veiling the 'awra, screening during prayer — from its figurative ones {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3}.

## In the sources
The jurists detail the rulings of covering {cite:sabiq-fiqh-al-sunnah}; the encyclopedia's hijab entry names khimar and niqab among related terms {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3}.

## Senses of the hijab in jurisprudence
The word has two senses: a material one, a body that stands between two things, and a figurative one, anything that blocks the reaching of an aim. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.6819} For the majority, the 'awra of a woman that must be veiled from a stranger is her whole body except the face and hands. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.6819} Veiling the 'awra in privacy is recommended out of shame before God, and uncovering it is allowed for need, such as treatment, circumcision or testimony. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.6820} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.6821} Figuratively, the word appears in the saying that there is no hijab between the prayer of the wronged and God. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.6823} A judge may not withdraw from people without excuse; the Shafi'is and Hanbalis dislike a judge having a chamberlain, while the Malikis and Hanafis allow it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.6822} On testimony heard from behind a veil, the Hanafis, Malikis and Hanbalis accept hearing alone when the speaker is known, while the Shafi'is require sight as well. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.6822} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.6823} Veiling as a term of inheritance is a separate headword, hajb. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.6832}

## The article on hidjab in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, defines hidjab as any veil set in front of a person or an object to hide it from view or to set it apart, and notes that in medicine the word names membranes such as the diaphragm and the pleura. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379} It says little is known of the word's pre-Islamic use, and that the Qur'an, where it occurs only seven times, gives the main evidence for its basic and figurative meanings. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379} The article then follows the word in four directions: the veiling of women, the curtain of rulers, the mystics' veil, and the amulet. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379}

## The word in the Qur'an and the hadith
According to the article, hidjab in the Qur'an means a separation. It is the screen behind which Mary withdrew from her family, and the separate quarters that were first required only of the wives of the Prophet, an arrangement the article says came apparently on the advice of [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379} It is also the barrier that separates the saved from the damned on the Day of Judgement, which the commentators gloss as a wall, and the veil from behind which God addresses a human being. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379} It can also be a veil that envelops, as night does the sun, or a figurative veil over people and things. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379}

In this last sense the article reports that the commentators disagree about a verse describing a hidden curtain placed between the Prophet and those who disbelieved in the afterlife: al-Jalalayn took it as an invisible screen sent to hide him from those who sought to kill him, while [[person/al-baydawi-abd-allah-ibn-umar|al-Baydawi]] took it as a veil dulling the understanding of the unbelievers so that they could not grasp the recitation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379} The article adds that in a hadith reported by Abu Dharr the word stands for the veil of death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379}

## The veiling of women
The article says the separation first required of the Prophet's wives was later extended to all free Muslim women, and that wearing the veil marks the passage from childhood to puberty and from the unmarried to the married state. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379} It notes that the cloth itself is usually called by other words such as litham, kina and burku, whereas hidjab, besides meaning the veil, refers in particular to the separation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379}

On the history, the article states that pre-Islamic poetry shows the custom was already known, the veil being the privilege of women of a certain rank. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379} It places the Qur'anic verses that tell the wives and daughters of the Prophet and the believing women to cover themselves and their bosoms with wraps, to a date the article gives as probable, though the year is garbled in the scan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379} It says [[person/aisha|A'isha]] is reported to have worn the veil from the time of her marriage, that the custom was very little observed in Medina, and that the Qur'an gives as the reason that the women would be distinguished and so spared harm. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379} With the expansion of Islam the custom spread quickly in Arabia and beyond and was adopted by almost all town women, especially those of leisure, while Bedouin, peasant and working women never adopted it fully. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379}

The article judges that veiling, being general in the towns where intellectual life developed, contributed largely to keeping Muslim women in a kind of seclusion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 379} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 380} That is the author's assessment.

## Women's dress in early Islam
The article on clothing, Libas, gives a fuller picture of what women wore. It says that already in the time of the Prophet covering the head out of modesty and respect was the norm for both men and women, an old Near Eastern practice, and that a woman could draw her long mantle over her head even when a hat or veil was worn beneath it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 182} It adds that in early Islamic times women normally covered the head and face with various veils in public and were often wrapped from head to foot in a large outer wrap leaving one eye free. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 183} Among the face veils it names the litham, covering the nose and lower face, and the burku, a mask-like piece attached to the headband, and it notes that the burku is still worn by married women among the Sinai Bedouin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 183} Men too might draw their mantles over head and face, and the article gives the case of young men veiling against the evil eye. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 183}

For the medieval period, the article draws on the Cairo Geniza documents and finds that Jewish and Muslim women dressed alike under the [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimids]] and Ayyubids, and that the Jewish women, like the Muslim, went veiled. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 186}

## Unveiling in the modern period
Both articles tell the story of the change in the Arab East. The article on hidjab says that late in the nineteenth century, influenced by the reforming ideas of the Egyptian ruler Isma'il, who founded the first school for girls in Cairo around 1290/1873, some Egyptian women abandoned the veil. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 380} It names [[person/qasim-amin|Qasim Amin]] as the real champion of feminism: in a work shaped by the liberal interpretation of [[person/muhammad-abduh|Muhammad Abduh]], he denounced the subjection of women, asserted their right to education and stressed the evils of the veil, without asking for its complete abolition, only that it conform to the strict demands of religion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 380} As a jurist he stated, the article says, that Islamic law contains no text justifying the veil as then practised. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 380} His books shocked Egypt. Tal'at Harb led the opposition and defended the old system in the name of religion, and the women were on Amin's side, among them Mayy Ziyada, who wrote a series of articles on the problem. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 380}

The article places the birth of the Egyptian feminist movement in 1925 and says its president, [[person/huda-shaarawi|Huda Sharawi]], formally abandoned the veil in 1926, a gesture many Muslim women then imitated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 380} The Libas article gives the year as 1923, saying she is supposed to have cast her veil into the Mediterranean on returning from a women's congress in Rome, and says upper and upper-middle class women soon followed her while the lower-middle class stayed the last stronghold of tradition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 188} The two articles thus differ on the date.

The Libas article adds that Christian and Jewish women left the veil a generation or more before Muslim women, and gives as a factor that the segregation of women in those communities had never been as strict. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 188} It says that by the end of the nineteenth century the question whether to veil had become a burning issue for modernist reformers such as Qasim Amin and for the small Egyptian feminist movement. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 188}

## The curtain of rulers
A second meaning of hidjab in the article is the curtain behind which caliphs and rulers hid from their household. The article says the custom seems unknown to the early people of the Hijaz and was apparently introduced by the [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyads]], probably under Sasanian influence. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 380} The partition was also called sitara and sitt, and the article distinguishes it from the office of the chamberlain, called hidjaba. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 380} It cites a work attributed to al-Jahiz saying that [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya]] and most of his successors were screened by a curtain so that none of their household could see the caliph when drink had taken away his self-control, and that the Abbasid al-Amin preferred the company of his companions to isolation behind a hanging. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 380}

The institution spread to al-Andalus, North Africa and Egypt and grew more elaborate, above all under the Fatimids, whose ceremonial the article compares to the Byzantine. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 380} It says that under the Fatimids it answered other needs, since the caliph, held to be the embodiment of the Active Intelligence of the world, was almost an object of worship and was expected to hide from the eyes of his followers, who were thus protected from the radiance of his face. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 380} In Cairo an official called master of the curtain, usually the chief eunuch, told the vizier when the caliph was seated and gave the order for the curtain to be raised before the ruler and lowered at the end of the audience. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 380} The curtain was used at receptions and in the Ramadan Friday visits to the mosque and on the festival of the end of Ramadan, when at a signal everyone veiled and the veiled caliph spoke, and at the Shi'i mourning ceremony for al-Husayn the veiled caliph received veiled dignitaries. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 380}

[[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]], the article says, distinguished several kinds of hidjab that a state adopts as it develops: one when the sovereign leaves nomadic ways and lets only intimates cross his threshold, a second when court etiquette limits who may approach him, and a last, in decline, when powerful dignitaries cut the sovereign off from his family and councillors and make him believe his dignity requires it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 380} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 381}

## The veil in the mystics' language
For the mystics, the article says, hidjab is everything that veils the true goal, all that makes a person insensitive to the divine reality, produced by images of the tangible world impressed on the heart. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 381} The person who is veiled is the one whose heart is closed to the divine light because passion dominates his awareness, and it quotes [[person/al-hallaj|al-Hallaj]] saying that a person's veil is his infatuation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 381} The opposite of the veil is unveiling, kashf, a state of the heart's expansion that no human effort can produce or remove, since both come from God, though when the lower soul is so weakened that passion is annihilated, the veil is rent and the seeker attains the fullness of his desire. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 381}

## The veil as protective amulet
Finally, the article says hidjab is a mystical separation, a supra-terrestrial protection, in effect an amulet that makes the wearer invulnerable and ensures success. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 381} A shaykh or fakir writes signs and verses of the Qur'an on paper and gives it to petitioners for a small sum; the writings are held to attract a husband's love, cure the sick, make a barren woman fertile, and even protect from bullets, and they are worn round the neck and not removed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 381} The article notes the same use of the word in Syria and among the Negev nomads. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 381}

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "Al-Wasatiyya": the hijab, worship or custom (episode 29)
The [[work/al-wasatiyya-program|"Al-Wasatiyya" programme]] gave an episode to the question whether the hijab is worship or custom. Its presenter, [[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]], said people are divided into three views: those who see it as a major matter of religion, those who see it as a secondary one, and those who hold it is not part of religion at all but a social custom. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:00:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:01:00} He noted that the dispute had reached thinkers and scholars and that the question now has laws in France and elsewhere. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:01:00} He hosted the writer and journalist Iqbal Baraka, author of a book on the hijab she called "a modern view", as the advocate of the view that it is a social custom. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:27:00}

### The polls
- He asked the audience whether the Muslim woman's hijab is a religious obligation or a social custom: 96% chose obligation and 4% custom. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:02:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:04:00}
- He asked whether keeping the hijab helps society towards chastity or arouses desire: 99% saw it as linked to chastity. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:41:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:43:00}
- He asked whether the hijab hinders women's progress and their part in building civilisation: 86% said it does not. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:57:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:59:00}

### Terms and ruling according to the sharia guests
Dr Abd al-Majid Abu Diyya, a specialist in language and sharia, defined four terms: hijab originally means screening, appears in the Qur'an in connection with the Mothers of the Believers, and today people use it for the legal dress; the jilbab is the wrap covering the head and body apart from the face and hands; the khimar is the head covering, and he said women in the Jahiliyya covered their heads but left chest and throat bare, so the command came to cover them; and the niqab covers all or part of the face and was an Arab custom. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:07:30} The participants agreed that in the episode "hijab" means the legal dress covering the whole body except the face and hands. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:08:30} Abu Diyya said the hijab is obligatory, rewarded when kept and sinful when left, and that in his view the niqab is recommended, not obligatory. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:09:00} Dr Ruhayyil Gharaiba, a specialist in women's rights in the sharia, agreed that the hijab is obligatory but held the niqab to be merely permitted, not recommended. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-wassitiya/ep29 00:10:00}

### The hadiths and consensus
