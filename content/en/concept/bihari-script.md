## Summary
Sheila Blair writes that the script used in India to transcribe the revelation became extremely stylised and visually distinct, as the maghribi style did at the other end of the Islamic lands: a stately, slow-moving hand with wedge-shaped letters, thick round bowls for endings and wide spaces between words, a tendency to wedge-shaped endings that she already sees in a Tughluq decree. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 426}

## The name
It came to be called bihari, but the origin of the name is obscure and even its correct vocalisation is disputed. It is sometimes, without justification, thought to come from the Persian word for spring (bahar); more probably it refers to the state of Bihar in north-eastern India, annexed by the Delhi sultanate in the fourteenth century, held by the sultans of Jawnpur in the fifteenth, then by the kings of Bengal, then the Mughals. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 426}

## Manuscripts
The earliest surviving manuscripts date to the late fourteenth century. Blair mentions a Qur'an in the Kabul Museum copied by Qadi Abu Bakr Ya'qub ibn Nasir al-Din at Lahri in Sind on Friday 17 Rabi' I 776 (26 August 1374), unpublished and perhaps lost. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 426} Better known is a small Qur'an once in the collection of Sadruddin Aga Khan, completed on 17 Dhu al-Qa'da 801 (21 July 1399) by the scribe Mahmud Sha'ban in the fort of Galyur (Gwalior), with thirteen lines to the page, an interlinear Persian translation and thirty-four illuminated double pages; she calls its script an early version of bihari, with an alif pitched slightly to the left and many final letters with long horizontal flourishes. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 426} The museum's record of a Gwalior Qur'an of 1399 may refer to this copy, but the sources read do not confirm it; see [[object/mushaf-ghwaliyur|the Gwalior Qur'an]].

## In the fifteenth century
Bihari came to the fore in the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries. It is seen in several Qur'ans, including one dated 888 (1483) in the Archaeological Museum at Bijapur and many undated ones attributed to the late fifteenth century; they vary in size, are copied on crude paper eaten away by the acidic green pigments, and have eleven to fifteen lines to the page, sometimes with an interlinear Persian translation or a marginal commentary. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 427} She suggests the style may have developed at Jawnpur. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 427} See [[object/bahmanid-quran-bijapur|the Bahmanid Qur'an at Bijapur]].
