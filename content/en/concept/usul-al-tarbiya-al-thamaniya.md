## Summary
Ahmad Muhammad al-Barri derives eight principles of upbringing and education from the Quran and the Prophetic Sunnah, holding that they build a person who knows their Lord, acts upon their knowledge, and benefits their community, and that Islamic education is devotional before it is vocational. {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 8} {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 9}

## First Principle: Direct Connection Between the Servant and Their Lord
The author holds that Islam removed intermediaries between the human being and their Lord, affirmed God's closeness to His servants, and that this freed the mind from superstitions and illusions. {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 28}

## Second Principle: Establishing General Equality
The author states that society before Islam was divided into clergy, government officials, and commoners, and that Islam established that an Arab has no superiority over a non-Arab except through piety or righteous deeds. {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 28} {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 29}. He cites reports about Umar and Jabalah ibn al-Ayham, as well as Abu Bakr's speech, attributing to them the principle of equality before the law; the author presents these reports as he cites them without verifying their chains of transmission. {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 32}

## Third Principle: Islam's Call to Knowledge
The author holds that religion does not contradict science, but rather encourages it, and that the Quran sets forth eight foundations for scientific knowledge in its verses, including that a Muslim should recognize their own limits and ignorance, not rely on conjecture, and be accountable for what they hear, see, and comprehend. {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 33} {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 37} {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 38}

## Sixth Principle: Freedom of Inquiry and Reflection
The author states that Islam permitted inquiry and independent legal reasoning in religious matters just as it permitted reflection in matters of creed, and that the disagreement that arose among jurists was a peaceful disagreement aimed at discovering the truth. {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 175} {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 176}. He attributes to al-Shafi'i the statement to his student: "Do not imitate me blindly in everything I say"; this attribution is presented as cited by the author and has not yet been verified. {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 177}

## Seventh Principle: Consultation and Sincere Counsel
The author holds that Islam granted every individual the right to oversee the government and express an opinion on public affairs, that religion is sincere counsel to God, His Messenger, and the believers, and that enjoining good and forbidding wrong is a duty upon every Muslim. {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 180} {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 181}

## Eighth Principle: Action as the Basis of Felicity
The author states that felicity in this world and the hereafter rests upon personal deeds, and that kinship ties and intercession are of no avail for anyone unless God permits them, citing the Prophet's words to Fatimah: "Work, O Fatimah." {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 215} {cite:usul-al-tarbiya-azhar p. 216}
