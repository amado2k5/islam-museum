## Summary
What was found on Arabic in Iraq: Article 4 of the 2005 constitution on the official languages as the Constitute Project shows it, the Library of Congress study's account of Iraqi Arabic and of Kurdish, and the classification of the Mesopotamian dialects, with the differences between sources and what was not found. For the place see [[place/iraq|Iraq]]. For the Arab states in general see [[concept/al-alam-al-arabi|The Arab states and the League of Arab States]].

## Official status
Article 4 of Iraq's 2005 constitution, in the translation on the Constitute Project, makes the Arabic language and the Kurdish language the two official languages of Iraq, guarantees Iraqis the right to educate their children in their mother tongue, such as Turkmen, Assyrian or Armenian, in government educational institutions under educational guidelines, and in any language in private ones. It makes Turkmen and Syriac two further official languages in the administrative units where their speakers are dense, lets each region or governorate adopt another local language as an additional official language if most of its people approve in a general referendum, requires the institutions of the Kurdistan region to use both official languages, and leaves a law to define what "official" means and how it is applied {cite:constituteproject-iraq}. Armenian does not appear in the article as an official language but in the guarantee of mother-tongue education. The article on the Semitic languages in the Encyclopaedia Britannica lists Iraq among the states where Arabic is official, with Kurdish {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}.

## The spoken varieties
The Library of Congress study says "almost all Iraqis speak at least some Arabic" and that Arabic has three forms: Classical, chiefly the Qur'an's; Modern Standard, the literary language; and the spoken form, which in Iraq is Iraqi Arabic, and that Iraqi Arabic is close to the dialects of Syria, Lebanon and parts of Jordan {cite:loc-iraq-people}. It says about 60 percent of Iraqis are uneducated and understand Modern Standard Arabic but cannot speak it; that is the page's own figure, from an old series that gives a figure for 1988 for Arabic, and it cannot stand for today. The University of Manchester, by contrast, makes "Iraq and western Iran" a separate Mesopotamian group, distinct from Levantine, which it gives as Palestine, Syria, Lebanon and Jordan, and says some Iraqi and peripheral eastern dialects show the influence of neighbouring languages in word order {cite:manchester-arabic-structural-variation}. Glottolog's tree lists "Gilit Mesopotamian Arabic" and "North Mesopotamian Arabic", the latter under the label Qeltu {cite:glottolog-arabic}. So the sources do not agree on where Iraqi stands: close to the Levant in the Library of Congress study, a group of its own at Manchester and Glottolog, and the museum does not choose.

## Other languages
The Library of Congress page on the Kurds says they are about 19 percent of the population, that there are "several Kurdish dialects, of which Kirmanji tends to be the standard written form", that Kurdish is a separate language, not a dialect of Persian, that it belongs to the Indo-European family, and that language is their most distinguishing trait {cite:loc-iraq-kurds}. The other page mentions the Turkomans and Assyrians as relatively small groups and says nothing of their languages {cite:loc-iraq-people}. The share and the variety names come from that old study, and no recent shares were read.

## The standard and the spoken varieties together
The University of Manchester's page on the dialects of Arabic describes the general situation across the Arabic-speaking world as three-layered: Modern Standard Arabic, the language of the state, education, public services and the media; Classical Arabic, found in the Qur'an, religious texts and worship; and the colloquial dialects of everyday exchange, which the page says people usually regard as of little value even though they are common in song, theatre and film {cite:manchester-arabic-sociolinguistic}. This is a description of the Arab world in general, not of this state alone, and no study measuring it here was read. For the relation of the standard to the spoken varieties see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]], and for the differences among the tribes' speech before the standard [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|The dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]].
