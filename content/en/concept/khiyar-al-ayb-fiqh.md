## Summary
Khiyar al-ayb (option of defect) is a compound of 'option' and 'defect'. The majority use this name; the Malikis usually call it the option of deficiency (khiyar al-naqisa). Ibn Nujaym and Ibn al-Humam define a defect as what the sound natural state lacks, so as to count as deficient; Ibn Rushd as what falls short of the natural creation or the lawful form, with a real effect on the price; al-Ghazali as any disliked attribute whose absence customarily is assumed for the thing. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7959} Lawfulness of return for defect: the jurists agree in general that a defective thing may be returned. They cite the Qur'an, that trade must be by mutual consent (Surah al-Nisa, 4:29), since knowledge of a defect means the consent is not full; and the Sunnah: the report of Aisha about a man who bought a slave, used him, found a defect and returned him, and the Prophet's ruling that the yield goes with the liability. Al-Kasani also cites the hadith of the sheep with its udder held full, and argues by analogy that the buyer pays a price for a sound thing and does not get it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7959}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7960} Duty of the seller to disclose: the seller must tell the buyer of a defect that gives the option. Not mentioning a defect that gives no option is not forbidden deceit, in the view of Imam al-Haramayn. The majority hold that disclosure is obligatory, and whoever hides a defect is sinful, a matter on which there is no dispute according to Ibn Qudama and al-Subki. Ibn Rushd counts it as eating wealth falsely. The sources cited include the hadith of Uqba ibn Amir that a Muslim sells to his brother only with the defect shown, the hadith of Wathila ibn al-Asqa' against selling without explaining what is in it, and the saying «whoever cheats us is not of us», from the heap of grain that the Prophet found wet. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7960}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7961} Concealment and the validity of the sale: a sale that conceals a defect giving the option is valid though sinful, according to most jurists, since the prohibition is on concealment, not on the contract. If the buyer learns of the defect after buying and accepts it, the seller is still at fault and the sale holds, as al-Shawkani holds. The Companions' practice included Ibn Umar buying camels with mange, and accepting them when told of it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7961} Duty on others than the contracting party: the duty to disclose is not limited to the seller but extends to anyone who knows of the defect, on the report of Wathila and the many texts on giving counsel. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7961}
