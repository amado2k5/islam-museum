## Summary
Khiyar al-ayb (option of defect) is a compound of 'option' and 'defect'. The majority use this name; the Malikis usually call it the option of deficiency (khiyar al-naqisa). Ibn Nujaym and Ibn al-Humam define a defect as what the sound natural state lacks, so as to count as deficient; Ibn Rushd as what falls short of the natural creation or the lawful form, with a real effect on the price; al-Ghazali as any disliked attribute whose absence customarily is assumed for the thing. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7959} Lawfulness of return for defect: the jurists agree in general that a defective thing may be returned. They cite the Qur'an, that trade must be by mutual consent (Surah al-Nisa, 4:29), since knowledge of a defect means the consent is not full; and the Sunnah: the report of Aisha about a man who bought a slave, used him, found a defect and returned him, and the Prophet's ruling that the yield goes with the liability. Al-Kasani also cites the hadith of the sheep with its udder held full, and argues by analogy that the buyer pays a price for a sound thing and does not get it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7959}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7960} Duty of the seller to disclose: the seller must tell the buyer of a defect that gives the option. Not mentioning a defect that gives no option is not forbidden deceit, in the view of Imam al-Haramayn. The majority hold that disclosure is obligatory, and whoever hides a defect is sinful, a matter on which there is no dispute according to Ibn Qudama and al-Subki. Ibn Rushd counts it as eating wealth falsely. The sources cited include the hadith of Uqba ibn Amir that a Muslim sells to his brother only with the defect shown, the hadith of Wathila ibn al-Asqa' against selling without explaining what is in it, and the saying «whoever cheats us is not of us», from the heap of grain that the Prophet found wet. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7960}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7961} Concealment and the validity of the sale: a sale that conceals a defect giving the option is valid though sinful, according to most jurists, since the prohibition is on concealment, not on the contract. If the buyer learns of the defect after buying and accepts it, the seller is still at fault and the sale holds, as al-Shawkani holds. The Companions' practice included Ibn Umar buying camels with mange, and accepting them when told of it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7961} Duty on others than the contracting party: the duty to disclose is not limited to the seller but extends to anyone who knows of the defect, on the report of Wathila and the many texts on giving counsel. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7961} Timing of the disclosure: a seller or a third party who knows of a defect should disclose it before the sale, so that the buyer may refrain; if the third party is not present, or cannot, then after the sale, so that the buyer may return the thing. The duty is strongest when only a third party knows. The purpose of the option is to remove harm from the buyer: a sale implies a sound object, and when soundness fails through a defect the buyer has the choice, because consent is part of the sale's nature. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7962}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7963} Three conditions for the option of defect: a real defect must appear; the buyer must not have known of it at the contract; and the seller must not have stipulated release from defects. A defect counts when it lowers value or defeats a valid purpose, and when soundness in that respect is the normal state of that kind of thing. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7963}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7964} Definitions: the Hanafis take any thing that lowers value in the eyes of experts, whether or not the substance is reduced; a grave defect in a dowry moves it from good to middling to poor quality. The Shafiis take a defect that lowers value or substance so that a valid purpose is lost, when the usual state of such things is free of it; the Hanbalis count loss of substance even where value rises, and loss of value even where substance is unchanged. The Malikis count a house where someone was killed as defective, since people shun it and its price falls. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7963}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7964}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7965} Normal state: the freedom from the defect must be the normal state of the kind of thing. What is common in such things is not a defect; for instance a usual sediment in oil. Experts decide: merchants for goods, craftsmen for crafted things. The Hanafis require the agreement of experts (if they disagree, there is no right of return); the majority of the Shafiis accept one expert, and the author of al-Tatimma requires two. If the two disagree and no other authority is available, the seller's word stands with his oath. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7965} Conditions of effect: the defect must be in the object itself, not in the other party, a pledge, or a guarantor. It must be old, that is present at the contract or arising before receipt; a defect that arises after receipt gives no option, though the Malikis apply the rule of warranty (three days for slaves; a year for madness, leprosy and vitiligo). The Hanafis make an exception for leasing, which is annulled by a new defect, since leasing is a contract of benefits that accrue gradually. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7966} The buyer's own act: a defect caused by the buyer before receipt gives no option, in the Shafii view. The defect must remain after delivery and until return: if it disappears before the buyer returns the thing, the right to return lapses. The Hanafis and Shafiis require that the buyer prove the defect is present with him, since a defect present with the seller may be removed. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7966}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7967} Removable defects: no option where the defect can be removed without hardship, such as a maker's stamp on cloth that washing does not harm, or stain on a garment that washing removes. If the seller minimised the defect and the buyer accepted it, the Malikis grant the buyer the right to return unless a new defect has arisen; then he may choose between return and compensation (arsh), as in a legal opinion by al-Wansharisi about a beast wounded by a spear. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7967}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7968} Proof of defects by how visible they are: a visible defect needs no proof beyond the buyer's sight, and he may sue the seller. A hidden defect known only to experts (such as pain in the liver or spleen) is proven by two Muslim men or one just man of expertise. A defect only women see is proven by the word of one just woman, two being safer; in the Hanafi view it proves the claim but not return. A defect known only by trial, such as a slave who absconds, needs two men or a man and two women. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7968} The seller's oath: if the buyer cannot prove the defect was with the seller, the two companions of Abu Hanifa have the seller sworn; Abu Hanifa does not. If the defect may occur in the buyer's hand, the oath is absolute (a definite oath); if the seller only knows of another's act, he swears on knowledge. If the seller refuses to swear, the defect is established against him. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7968}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7969} Knowledge of the defect: the buyer must be unaware of the defect. If he knew it at the contract or at receipt he has no option, since by buying or taking the thing with knowledge he consents in effect. A defect that is obvious and that anyone would see counts as known, and so does a defect the seller pointed out. Where the seller said that a thing is faulty as a condition of sale, as with a bull sold on condition that it sleeps in the plough, the seller is free of blame. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7969} A buyer who knows of a thing that is a defect without knowing it is one: if he takes it and only later learns that it is a defect, the question is whether the defect is plain. A plain defect that anyone sees gives no right to return; a defect that only experts know gives the right. Likewise a buyer who knows a coin is false, but not how many are false, has not consented to the defect. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7970} The release from defects: the option is lost if the seller stipulates release. A release is valid in the Hanafi view whether the defect was known or unknown to the seller and whatever the thing; Malik and al-Shafii allow it only for animals. Such a sale with release is a special type called the sale of release. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7970}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7971} Origin and the views: the report of Ibn Umar, who sold a slave with release and was challenged over a hidden illness; Uthman ordered him to swear he did not know of it, and he refused, took the slave back, and later sold him for more. The jurists differ on the release: release from all defects whether known or not (Abu Hanifa, Abu Thawr, from Ibn Umar and Zayd); no release unless the defect is named (Ibn Abi Layla, al-Thawri); no release unless the buyer has seen it (some Shafiis, Shurayh, Ata, a report of Ahmad and Ishaq); release only from hidden defects in animals (Malik, and the apparent view of al-Shafii); a release only in a sale of spoils, of a bankrupt, or in debt payment; or a voiding of the sale altogether, a Shafii view. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7971}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7972} Kinds of release: particular (of a named defect) or general (of all defects). If it refers to a defect existing at the contract, it covers only that. If it refers to a future defect arising before receipt, the condition is void and the sale invalid. If unqualified, Abu Hanifa and Abu Yusuf take it to cover defects at the contract and until receipt; Malik, Muhammad, Zufar and al-Hasan ibn Ziyad limit it to defects at the contract. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7972} Contracts that take the option of defect (Hanafi list): sale and purchase (the valid sale, not the void one, and including exchange of money); leasing, which takes it whether the defect is old or new, and where the lessee may return before and after receipt; division; settlement on property; the dowry; the compensation of khul'; and the settlement for willful blood. For these, return is only for a grave defect, not a slight one. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7972}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7973} Ibn Rushd: contracts divide into three: those meant for exchange, which take the option without dispute; those not meant for exchange, such as a gift without return, which do not; and mixed contracts, such as a gift with intent of return, which he thinks do not. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7973} Timing of the return: three views. (1) Immediately, meaning as soon as custom allows; whoever knows and does not return loses the right and bears the loss without arsh, the Shafii view and one of Ahmad. The Hanafi al-Hawi also holds this; Ibn Nujaym calls it strange, and the Hanafi view taken is that the option lasts. (2) Delayed, as long as no sign of consent appears, the view of the Hanafis (as relied on) and a sound Hanbali report. (3) A day or two, the Maliki view, where continued possession after that counts as consent. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7974}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7975} Effect on the contract's rule: the defect does not stop the transfer of ownership: the buyer owns the thing at once and the seller owns the price, since the sale is absolute in its pillars. The implied condition of soundness only stops the contract from being binding, not its basic effect. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7975}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7976} What the buyer may choose: three views. (1) Hanafis and Shafiis: return against full price, or keep the thing for the full price with no arsh; arsh only if return is impossible. (2) Ahmad and Ishaq: return, or keep with arsh, even when return is possible; but if keeping with arsh leads to ribā, keeping is free. (3) Malikis: a grave defect allows return or keeping free; a slight defect in land means arsh only, and in goods return, by the apparent view of the Mudawwana. The source of the dispute: whether the defect reduces the substance or the attribute. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7976}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7977} Conditions of return: the option must still exist; the thing returned must be as received, without a further defect such as a defect of partnership arising from split of the deal; and the deal must not be split before the whole thing is received, unless the seller accepts, since then it is damage the seller accepts. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7977}
