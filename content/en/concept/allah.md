## Overview
Allah is God the Unique, the Creator and the Lord of judgment, and the Encyclopaedia of Islam describes him as the centre of Islamic thought and its one reason for existence. The pre-Islamic Arabs knew Allah as one of the Meccan deities, possibly the supreme deity and certainly a creator-god; the name is most likely a contraction of al-ilah, "the god". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 428}

## In the Qur'an
A Muslim tradition holds that the sura 96 was the first revealed, so the mission of the Prophet began with the preaching of the Word of Allah. In it Allah is the Lord who created man and who teaches man what he did not know. The basmala formula opens each sura, and the root of the word for mercy came to mean, in later centuries, benefaction and clemency. The Qur'an is not a systematic theology of God; it is God's word, in which he says what he wishes about himself. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 428-429}

## The great themes
Three themes predominate in the Qur'an. First, God as creator, judge and recompenser: he creates by his command, is the supreme judge, and is the master of life and death; the early suras proclaim his sovereignty over his creation and announce the judgment and the imminent Hour. Second, God is one and unique, the core of the message: "your God is one" is repeated throughout, and the short sura of Unity states his oneness both in relation to man and in his nature. This is the content of the testimony of faith. Third, God is omnipotent and merciful, Lord of the worlds, whose power is matched by forgiveness; the believer is called to commit himself to him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 429}

## The signs and Names of Allah
The signs of the universe are signs of God, and human reason is religiously obliged to reflect on them and to see in what is perishable the evidence of a necessary and transcendent Creator. God alone abides, and he will annihilate all things at the Hour and raise them again for the Gathering. His perfections, which are the Names, are those the Muslim tradition set at ninety-nine "most beautiful Names" for memorizing and meditation. The main themes that emerge are that he is one, the living, the real, the sublime, the high, the light, the wise and the omnipotent. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 429}

## More Names and God's knowledge
The Names include the Omnipotent, the Creator (badi', khaliq, khallaq), the Hearing and the Knowing, the Witness, the Bountiful, the Benefactor, the Surety, the Protector, the Generous, the Merciful, the Forgiver, the Compassionate, the Benevolent and the Best of Judges. Some recur throughout the Qur'an, others are stressed in one period more than another. The divine omniscience extends to the smallest act of the smallest creature, so that God is fully present in every human deed and in every movement of the heart. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 429-430}

## Two groups of verses
The first concerns retribution and the divine decree. Divine omnipotence is affirmed both as the power of the decree and as the justice of a judge who weighs every act, and the Qur'an presents these as complementary, not contradictory, affirmations aimed at a required attitude of heart. The Qur'an does not pose the theological problem of predestination or the philosophical problem of human freedom; it evokes the mystery of the relation between creature and Creator. The verses about the decree speak of the elect whom God favours and of those whom he leads astray, and the responsibility of man and the decree of God meet in the final affirmation of judgement. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 429-430}

The second group are the ambiguous verses (mutashabih), whose picturesque wording, taken literally, seems to ascribe human features to God: God sits on his throne, has a hand, a face and eyes. These verses were later the object of exegetical and theological dispute. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 429-430}

## Conclusion: the unexpressed mystery
The Qur'anic teaching about God rests on his oneness, his transcendence and his absolute perfections, and it maintains the inaccessibility of the divine nature. God, who is near and knowing, can be known only through his Word, his Names and the acts of his sovereignty, which he himself reveals. The name Allah is the proper name of God, expressing the sole and incommunicable godhead; in himself God remains the unexpressed mystery, the unseen. Western scholars have called Allah a personal God, but Islamic theology did not use the term shakhs for him; the article explains the vocabulary, since shakhs in Arabic always connotes the individual figure. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 429-430}

## The personal God and the believer's attitudes
The term "personal God" is accepted by the article in its Indo-European sense, as implying an absolute perfection: a God who subsists in himself, is incommunicable in his godhead, and is the source of all perfection, infinitely distinct from every creature. The Qur'an teaches this while keeping God's inmost life in its own mystery and insisting on the Word communicated through the prophets and the inner attitude demanded of the believer. Facing the incommunicable mystery, the believer is required to show reverent fear (taqwa), piety (birr), gratitude (shukr) and trust (tawakkul). For the damned, fear becomes dread of punishment, while those who believe in the unseen and seek the face of God find in him their protector and guide and their final refuge. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 430-431}

## Kalam and the development of the doctrine
Tradition and kalam built on the Qur'an through two sciences: the science of hadith, which preserved sayings on God's mercy and forgiveness, his absolute kingship, human responsibility and the preordaining decree; and tafsir, the exegesis that treated the verses about God, especially the anthropomorphic passages. The problems of kalam, which in essence come from the Mu'tazilites, rest on two principles: the unity of God (tawhid) and the justice of God in requiting human acts ('adl). The Mu'tazilites called themselves "the people of unity and justice"; later schools kept the problems and changed their titles, dealing with the existence and attributes of God and with the actions of the Most High. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 430-431}

The schools agree that the Qur'an bids the reason reflect on the signs of the universe and rise to the Creator, but they differ on the status of that duty. For the Mu'tazilites it is inherent in reason before the Law; for the Maturidites reason could in principle have reached God unaided but was in fact brought to him by the revealed law; for the Ash'arites it is a purely legal obligation. The argument itself is common to them: the world is contingent and perishable, has a temporal beginning, and so requires a necessary Creator who alone exists from eternity. The Ash'arites therefore treat the existence of God as a rational proof prescribed by revealed authority. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 430-431}

On the attributes, the traditionists held to the letter of the texts, and their opponents called them corporealists (mujassima) or anthropomorphists (hashwiyya). The Mu'tazilites insisted on the via negativa (tanzih), denying God every created thing, and identified his attributes with his essence; the Jahmiyya almost denied the attributes altogether. The orthodox schools also practised tanzih, saying that God is neither body nor substance nor accident. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 430-431}

## The Ash'arite position on the attributes
Against the literalists and the Mu'tazilites alike, the Ash'arites took a middle course, summed up in the principle "without how and without likeness" (bila kayf wa la tashbih). The Mu'tazilite negation of the attributes, they said, amounted to suspending them altogether and making God an empty concept. The Ash'arites accepted the full reality of the attributes, since the Qur'an speaks of them, but held that this reality cannot compromise the perfect unity of God. Later Ash'arites, following al-Ghazali, said that the attributes are neither God nor other than him. The Hanafi-Maturidite line kept an old statement in the Fiqh akbar II that God is a "thing" (shay'), in the sense of positive existence, and some Karramites and Hanbalites used the word "body" (jism) in the same sense. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 431-432}

## The lists of attributes
The guiding rule was to affirm no attribute not expressly stated in the Qur'an, but the doctors of kalam allowed a move from a participle to a noun, and a list of attributes gradually took shape. Essential attributes include existence, eternity, permanence, dissimilarity to created things and self-subsistence. Attributes that add a concept to the essence include power, will, knowledge, life, speech, hearing and sight. Attributes of action include the creation of the world, command, decree and predestination, and God's consent. The Ash'arites and Maturidites agree that the attributes of essence are eternal, although their objects may be contingent, and most attributes can be proved by reason, while visibility, speech, hearing and sight depend on revelation alone. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 431-432}

## Vision and speech
The vision of God was accepted by the pious traditionists in the literal sense, and denied by the Mu'tazilites, who explained the relevant verses figuratively. The Ash'arites and Hanafi-Maturidites affirmed it "without how": the believers will see God in the hereafter, but not as one sees a limited object in space. The attribute of speech was the most contested: the Mu'tazilites made the Qur'an created speech, because God speaks in time; the Ash'arites, after the affirmation for which Ibn Hanbal was imprisoned, held that God's speech is an uncreated essential speech, distinct from its created expression in the Book and in human recitation. Ibn Taymiyya later revived the faith of the early pious and declared that this speech, in its mystery, is the Torah, the Gospel and the Qur'an. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 431-432}

## The ambiguous verses
The ambiguous (mutashabih) anthropomorphic verses, which seem to liken God to created things, posed the question whether they should be taken at face value or interpreted (ta'wil). The early traditionists took them literally and committed their meaning to God (tafwid): God sits on his throne and has a hand, but no one knows the sense God gives these terms; this attitude was attributed to Malik b. Anas. The Mu'tazilites, who wished to defend the Muslim God against the God of the philosophers, used ta'wil widely: al-Zamakhshari used philology, and the vision of God was explained as the hope of the faithful for their Lord's bounty. The first Ash'arites rejected this use of reason in tafsir. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 431-432}
