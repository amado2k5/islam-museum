## Overview
Fiqh is the science of deriving practical rulings from their detailed evidence. Throughout history Muslims divided into the major legal schools, the madhhabs, in it. {cite:wikidata} The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose account this page follows for the rest, explains that the word originally meant understanding, knowledge or intelligence and could be applied to any branch of learning, but became the technical term for jurisprudence, the science of religious law. In its widest sense it covers religious, political and civil life alike: rules of ritual and worship, family law, inheritance, property, contracts and obligations, criminal law and procedure, constitutional law, and the laws governing the administration of the state and the conduct of war. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 924}

## An older and narrower sense
The article says that in older theological language the word did not have this comprehensive meaning. It stood in contrast to knowledge, ilm, which meant the Qur'an and its interpretation together with the legal decisions handed down from the Prophet and his Companions. Fiqh meant instead the independent use of one's own judgment to settle a legal point where no handed-down ruling was known. The product of that reasoning was called ray, opinion. Scholars were distinguished accordingly: the alim was a master of transmitted knowledge and the faqih a master of reasoned decision, and one person could combine both. The article gives early examples, among them Ibn Umar, remembered as strong in hadith but not in fiqh, and Ibn Abbas, who knew the transmitted decisions and also gave sound opinions on new cases. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 924}

## Opinion in the earliest period
In the earliest period, the article says, judges and the authorities who oversaw religious life often had to rely on their own ray, because the Qur'an offered little legislative material and there were few ancient precedents. A verdict resting on knowledge was preferred, but opinion was not discredited: it counted as a legitimate factor, and its results were later regarded as the decisions of old authorities. It reports that Ata ibn Abi Rabah, asked whether a judgment of his was knowledge or opinion, called it knowledge if it rested on a precedent. Stories it cites, which it says reflect Umayyad conditions, show Mu'awiya consulting Zayd ibn Thabit and the caliph Umar II leaving a verdict to an Egyptian judge. In fuller versions of such reports the article finds analogy named as a regulator of opinion, together with an inquiry into the reason behind a rule, and it adds that the general usage of the community, established by wide agreement, early became a further source. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 924}

## Foreign elements, as the article presents them
The article notes that this usage contained an appreciable amount of foreign matter, since administrative and commercial practices of the conquered provinces survived where they suited the new religion. It quotes a report that jurists disagreed on whether a caliph may alter an ancient non-Arab custom that Islam had neither changed nor abolished: Abu Yusuf said he may not, while Malik and al-Shafii said he may. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 925}

On this basis the article states that elements of Roman and provincial law, Talmudic law, the canon law of the Eastern churches and Persian Sasanian law entered Islamic law during its formative period. Talmudic influence is seen above all in ritual and worship, while Persian and church influences are established only in a few cases and their extent is still to be investigated. For Roman and Talmudic law the influence is said to reach legal concepts and maxims, methods of reasoning such as analogy, and even basic ideas of legal science; the scholarly concept of agreement seems to the authors to have been modelled on the Roman opinion of the jurists. Goldziher even suggested that the terms fiqh and fuqaha, like the Jewish equivalents, may have been influenced by the Latin terms for jurisprudence. The article explains the route as indirect, through educated non-Arab converts trained in Hellenistic rhetoric, and says it is out of the question that early Muslim jurists consciously adopted any principle of foreign law. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 925}

## The four roots and the widening of the word
As the Qur'an, the sunna, agreement and analogy came gradually to be accepted as the four roots of legal knowledge, fiqh lost its limitation to deductions not based on tradition. It came to mean the science that coordinates all branches of knowledge derived from the four roots, and the fuqaha were its masters, the jurists. The word could also denote the result: the sum of the deductions drawn from the sources. In a still wider use it meant religious learning in general, and fuqaha could mean students of religion or theologians. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 925}

## Two accounts of the beginnings
The article sets two accounts side by side. The traditional account of Muslim scholars places the origins of jurisprudence in the generation of the Companions: the caliphs of Medina and a few specialists drew conclusions from the Qur'an and the Prophet's words and acts by independent reasoning, the other Companions approved them, explicitly or silently, and they became binding on the community. The Successors continued this work, and the generation after them saw the founding of the schools. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 925}

Recent historical research, the article says, has shown that Islamic jurisprudence arose towards the end of the first century of the hijra, the early eighth century. For most of that century, it argues, law in the technical sense did not yet exist as a religious subject. Arab customary law as modified by the Qur'an survived, and the Muslims adopted the legal, commercial and administrative institutions of the conquered lands where these suited their new religious ideas. The caliphs of Medina, and after them the Umayyads and their governors, acted largely as lawgivers, and the first judges, appointed by Umayyad governors, decided by their own discretion on custom and administrative rules. The article judges that later developments hid their part from Islamic jurisprudence itself. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 925} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 926}

Only towards the end of that century, it continues, do the first specialists in religious law appear as historical figures, for example [[person/ibrahim-al-nakhai|Ibrahim al-Nakhai]] in [[place/kufa|Kufa]] and Said ibn al-Musayyib in Medina. They were pious men who surveyed all fields of contemporary activity, law included, from an Islamic angle and worked out a way of life by individual reasoning. On the article's reading, this is the beginning of Islamic jurisprudence, which did not grow out of an existing Islamic law but created it by endorsing, changing or rejecting popular and administrative practice. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 926}

## The ancient schools
As these groups grew, they developed in the early eighth century into the ancient schools of law, of which those of Kufa, Medina and Syria are known in some detail. The article says their differences came mostly from geographical factors, local social conditions and custom, not from disagreement over principles. [[place/iraq|Iraq]] was the great centre, and influence ran almost always from Iraq to the Hijaz. The schools shared a body of doctrine and a legal theory built on the idea of a living tradition, the ideal practice of a place as represented by the agreement of its scholars. At first this tradition was anonymous, and from the early second century it began to be ascribed to great figures of the past. By a literary convention favoured in Iraq, scholars put their doctrines under their masters' names, so that works attributed to Abu Yusuf and al-Shaybani present themselves as coming from Abu Hanifa, from Hammad, from Ibrahim. The Medinese traced their teaching to seven early authorities, the seven lawyers of Medina; the Kufan doctrine was attributed to Ibn Masud, the Meccan to Ibn Abbas. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 926}

## The traditionists and the new method
Opposition arose to the majority opinions of these schools. The most important single event of second-century jurisprudence, in the article's view, was the movement of the traditionists, the ahl al-hadith. They set against the living tradition, largely resting on opinion, the authority of individual reports from the Prophet, which they circulated in growing numbers, and held that fiqh must rest on these alone. The ancient schools had to cast their own doctrines as such reports and take account of their opponents', until the outlines and many details of the law took the form of traditions from the Prophet. Later Muslim scholars, who could not acknowledge so basic a change, described the struggle as one between the ahl al-hadith and an imaginary group called the ashab al-ray, and third-century traditionists attacked the Iraqi school of Abu Hanifa for its casuistry. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 927}

Written works on jurisprudence begin soon after the middle of the eighth century; the article states that a Majmu al-fiqh attributed to Zayd ibn Ali is early but not authentic. It names successive Iraqi stages in Hammad, Ibn Abi Layla, [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]], Abu Yusuf and al-Shaybani, describes the Syrian [[person/al-awzai-abd-al-rahman|al-Awzai]] as an archaic type of doctrine, and says [[person/malik-ibn-anas|Malik]] in his Muwatta aimed to set out the average doctrine of Medina in his day. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 927}

According to the article, [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafii]], who began in the school of Medina, accepted the traditionists' thesis that reports from the Prophet override other authority, defended it in debate against the ancient schools, and wrote in his Risala the first treatise on the method of legal reasoning, which makes him the founder of usul al-fiqh, the science of the roots. The detailed rules derived from the roots are called furu, branches. He was not a mere traditionist: he criticised their faulty reasoning, excluded opinion and istihsan, and insisted on strict analogy. Some of his disciples, in particular [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]], emphasised the traditionist side of his teaching, and Dawud ibn Khalaf, called al-Zahiri, went further and relied only on the literal meaning of the Qur'an and hadith, rejecting opinion, istihsan and analogy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 927}

## From ancient schools to personal schools
About the middle of the second century circles formed around individual masters inside the ancient schools, and by the middle of the third they had turned into personal schools carrying on the doctrine of a master and his disciples. The Kufan school became that of the [[group/hanafi-madhhab|Hanafis]], the Medinese that of the [[group/maliki-madhhab|Malikis]], and the Syrian that of al-Awzai, with a separate line from Sufyan al-Thawri. Although al-Shafii disclaimed any intention of founding a school, his followers became the [[group/shafii-madhhab|Shafii school]]. Ahmad ibn Hanbal's followers formed the [[group/hanbali-madhhab|Hanbali]] school, which, the article notes, never absorbed its parent traditionist movement as completely as the Hanafi and Maliki schools absorbed theirs. The [[group/zahiri-madhhab|Zahiris]] were the only school named after a principle of legal theory. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 927} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 928}

The article states that since about 700/1300 only four schools survive in orthodox Islam, the Hanafi, Maliki, Shafii and Hanbali, and that they regard one another as alternative and equally valid interpretations of the religious law. Despite divergent roots they share a common legal theory that took hold in the third century, accepting the traditionists' principle that reports from the Prophet are the only evidence of sunna but subordinating its practical use to the agreement of the scholars. The article therefore judges the theory of the roots to be of little direct importance for the positive doctrines of the schools. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 928}

## Independent reasoning, taqlid and the fatwa
From the middle of the third century, according to the article, the view spread that only the great scholars of the past had the right to independent reasoning, and in the fourth century an agreement gradually established itself that later activity should be confined to explaining, applying and at most interpreting the doctrine laid down once and for all, called taqlid. This implied joining one of the existing schools. Original thought did not vanish, the article observes, but could express itself only in abstract systematic constructions that left established doctrine untouched. New facts were met by specialists with the traditional tools; the decision is a fatwa, given by a mufti, and once accepted by common scholarly opinion it became part of his school's doctrine. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 928}

## Khariji and Shii law
The article says that the legal doctrines of the Kharijis and the Shia, who split from the Sunni majority on political grounds about the middle of the first century of Islam, differ from the Sunni schools on the question of leadership of the community and on some other questions differ more widely from the orthodox schools than these do from one another. It cautions that the features common to Khariji, Shii and Sunni law should not be taken as older than the schisms: in the second and third centuries these sects stayed close enough to the Sunnis to adopt the doctrines being developed in the schools, with only superficial changes required by their own tenets. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 928}

## The Abbasids, courts and practice
When the Abbasids overthrew the Umayyads in 132/750, the article says, jurisprudence, though still forming, had its essential features. The [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasids]] presented themselves as champions of Islam, recognised the law taught by the pious specialists as the only legitimate norm, and drew such specialists to court; at the request of [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]], Abu Yusuf wrote his Kitab al-Kharaj on public finance, taxation and criminal justice. Judges were now appointed by the caliph and were to apply nothing but the sacred law. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 928}

The article judges this effort short-lived. The judges depended on political authorities to carry out their rulings and, bound by the formal law of evidence, could not deal with criminal cases, much of which passed to the police. A double system of justice thus arose in most Islamic countries, the judges' competence being restricted to family law, inheritance and endowments. The article presents this as one aspect of a long tension between theory and practice, between jurisprudence and customary law, and names the Ottoman Empire as the most notable attempt to bridge it. Jurisprudence, it adds, took notice of the practice it could not overcome and tried to control it in works on practice, legal devices and documents. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 928} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 929}

## Character of the law, and the modern period
The article describes jurisprudence as adaptable and growing until the early Abbasid period and increasingly rigid afterwards. It judges that this rigidity gave the law stability over centuries in which political institutions declined, and that if the law grew out of touch with later developments of state and society, it gained more in power over minds than it lost in control over bodies. It quotes Snouck Hurgronje's description of fiqh as a doctrine of duties, interpreted by scholars, not legislators, so that the schools' handbooks are not codes, and it calls Islamic law a jurists' law. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 929}

For the modern period the article reports that in British India and French Algeria Islamic law, fused with Western legal thought, gave rise to separate systems, Anglo-Muhammadan law and Algerian Muslim law. Modernism, it says, while accepting that religion should regulate law too, has denied the validity of traditional jurisprudence; institutions of Islamic law have been reshaped, sometimes beyond recognition, by secular legislation in a number of Muslim countries, and a programme has been put forward of deriving a modern law from the general principles worked out by early jurists. Both tendencies, it says, seek to put a new Islamic jurisprudence in place of the old. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 929}
