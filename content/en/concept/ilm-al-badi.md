## Summary
The science of badi' is the third of the sciences of [[concept/ilm-al-balagha|rhetoric]]; for al-Qazwini it is the science by which are known the ways of embellishing speech after its matching of the situation and its fasaha have been kept. Before it became a school science, badi' was the name of certain colours in poetry and prose that Abd Allah ibn al-Mu'tazz gathered in a book he called al-Badi'. This page sets out its definition in [[person/jalal-al-din-al-qazwini|al-Qazwini]], Khafaji and Ibn Khaldun; Ibn al-Mu'tazz's preface and what the editor of his book says of the rise of the name; and the ways of embellishing that [[person/al-sakkaki|al-Sakkaki]] lists, with their Qur'anic examples and what the exegetes say at some of them. Each statement is ascribed to its author, and verses are pointed to by their pages without writing out their text.

## Its definition
Al-Qazwini says that what teaches the ways of embellishing speech, after its matching the situation and its fasaha have been kept, is the science of badi' {cite:idah-khafaji-dar-al-jil 1 p.50}. Khafaji notes that these ways are incidental and outside the bounds of balagha, that they count as embellishments only once matching and fasaha are kept, and that if embellishments are brought as embellishments they are studied in badi', but if they are considered as matching they are studied in ma'ani {cite:idah-khafaji-dar-al-jil 1 p.48}. Al-Qazwini built his book on the view that the third art guards against nothing and only embellishes the wording {cite:idah-khafaji-dar-al-jil 1 p.17}. For Ibn Khaldun it is the looking into the adorning of speech with a kind of polish, whether by saj' that divides it, jinas that makes its words alike, tarsi', or tawriya in which a hidden meaning is hinted at by suggesting another {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-shahada-1981 1 p.761}.

## Ibn al-Mu'tazz's preface
[[person/ibn-al-mutazz|Ibn al-Mu'tazz]] says in his preface that he set out in his chapters some of what he found in the Qur'an, in language, in hadith, in the speech of the Companions and the Bedouin and in the poems of the ancients of what the moderns called badi', to show that Bashshar, Muslim, Abu Nuwas and those who followed their path did not precede others in this art, but that it grew frequent in their poems and became known in their time until it was given this name; then Abu Tammam was smitten with it until it overcame him, and he did well in some of it and badly in some {cite:badi-ibn-al-mutazz-dar-al-jil-1990 p.73} {cite:badi-ibn-al-mutazz-dar-al-jil-1990 p.74}. He adds that he left out the chains of transmission of the hadiths and named only the well-known {cite:badi-ibn-al-mutazz-dar-al-jil-1990 p.75}. The editor says that the colours of badi' for him are five, metaphor, jinas, mutabaqa, returning the end of a line to the beginning and the kalami method, that he counts the rest as beauties of speech, and sees no harm in adding these or others to badi' {cite:badi-ibn-al-mutazz-dar-al-jil-1990 p.22}.

## The rise of the name, and the dispute over it
The editor of Ibn al-Mu'tazz's book says the name was not coined by Ibn al-Mu'tazz: Muslim ibn al-Walid gave it, and it was earlier known as 'the delicate'. He relates that writers split in two: one party held that if badi' were good, the Qur'an, hadith and the poetry of the ancients would have the first claim to it over the moderns' writing; another found it fine; then a middle way arose that accepts this ornament so long as it is close to nature and far from strain {cite:badi-ibn-al-mutazz-dar-al-jil-1990 p.59}. Elsewhere he sets out two views on its origin, one that it reached the Arabs from the Greeks and one that it is an original element in Arabic, and then prefers that it is Arab colouring that grew and developed under Persian and Greek influence {cite:badi-ibn-al-mutazz-dar-al-jil-1990 p.20}. That is the editor's view, and this page does not decide it.

## Ways of embellishing in al-Sakkaki
At the end of the discussion of ma'ani and bayan al-Sakkaki says speech has particular ways that are often resorted to in order to beautify it, and they are of two kinds: one going back to the meaning and one to the wording. Of the first is mutabaqa, joining two opposites, for which he cites verses including [[passage/quran-3-26|Al Imran 26]], [[passage/quran-9-82|al-Tawba 82]] and [[passage/quran-18-18|al-Kahf 18]] {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.423}. Then muqabala, of which an example is [[passage/quran-92-5|al-Layl 5]] to [[passage/quran-92-10|10]], where ease is made shared among giving, godfearing and belief, and its opposite, hardship, shared among their opposites {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.424}. Then mushakala, naming a thing by the word of another because it stands beside it, for which he cites verses including [[passage/quran-2-138|al-Baqara 138]], [[passage/quran-2-194|al-Baqara 194]], [[passage/quran-3-54|Al Imran 54]], [[passage/quran-5-64|al-Ma'ida 64]], [[passage/quran-5-116|al-Ma'ida 116]] and [[passage/quran-42-40|al-Shura 40]] {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.424}. After these he lists mura'at al-nazir, muzawaja, laff and nashr, jam', tafriq and taqsim {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.425}, and i'tirad {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.428}. Of the second kind are the sorts of jinas and returning the end to the beginning {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.429} {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.430}.

## What the exegetes say at al-Sakkaki's examples
At [[passage/quran-18-18|al-Kahf 18]] al-Baghawi explains that whoever saw them would think them awake while they slept, since their eyes were open and they breathed without speaking {cite:tafsir-baghawi-quran-com on 18:18}. At [[passage/quran-92-5|al-Layl 5]] al-Qurtubi says Ibn Mas'ud and most exegetes hold it to be about Abu Bakr, and that 'gave' means he spent, 'feared God' that he kept from what God forbade, and 'believed in the best' in God's recompense for his giving {cite:tafsir-qurtubi-quran-com on 92:5}; Ibn Kathir says the sense is that he gave what he was commanded to give out and feared God in his affairs {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-quran-com on 92:5}. At [[passage/quran-3-54|Al Imran 54]] al-Qurtubi relays from al-Zajjaj that God's plan is the requital of their plan, so the requital is called by the name of the beginning, as in other verses he cites {cite:tafsir-qurtubi-quran-com on 3:54}, and al-Baghawi gives the same view with the reason that the requital stands opposite the beginning {cite:tafsir-baghawi-quran-com on 3:54}; this verse is among al-Sakkaki's verses of mushakala. At [[passage/quran-28-73|al-Qasas 73]] Ibn Kathir says that the mention of rest in the night and seeking bounty in the day is a case of laff and nashr {cite:tafsir-ibn-kathir-quran-com on 28:73}, which is also among al-Sakkaki's examples {cite:miftah-al-ulum-zarzur-1987 p.425}. This page sets out these statements and does not settle the naming of the kinds.

## What this page does not reach
The later books of badi' were not read, nor Ibn Rashiq's al-Umda that Ibn Khaldun names, nor al-Sina'atayn. Of Ibn al-Mu'tazz's al-Badi' only the two introductions and the opening of the chapters were read, not the fifth chapter or the beauties he adds. The chapters on badi' in al-Idah were not read because the copy read does not carry them. The exegetes' statements quoted here concern the sense of the verses, and of them only Ibn Kathir names a badi' figure, laff and nashr; the naming in the other examples is al-Sakkaki's.
