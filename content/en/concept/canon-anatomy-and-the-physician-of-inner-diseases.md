## Summary
Ibn Sina holds that whoever reads inner diseases must first have anatomy, and lists what it gives him: the substance, make and place of each organ, its sympathies, what can be held in it and its function {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.156}.

## What anatomy teaches
The physician must know the substance of each organ, whether fleshy or not, and its make, so as to tell whether a swelling of this shape is in it or in another, and whether something may be held in it or slip from it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.156}.

## Place and sympathy
He must know its place, to judge whether a felt pain or swelling is on the organ or at a distance, and its sympathies, to judge whether the pain is its own or by sympathy, whether the matter rose from it or came from its partner, and whether what comes away is of its substance or only passes through from elsewhere {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.156}.

## Function and conclusion
He must know what the organ holds and what it does, to read its illness from a defect in that work. Ibn Sina concludes that a physician who undertakes the diseases of the inner organs cannot do without anatomy {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.156}.
