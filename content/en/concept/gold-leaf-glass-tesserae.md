## Summary
Gold-leaf tesserae are small glass cubes carrying a leaf of gold (or silver), used to decorate the [[building/umayyad-mosque|Great Umayyad Mosque]] of Damascus and other Umayyad buildings. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 58} The mosque was commissioned by [[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|al-Walid I]] in 705/706 and completed by 715, its walls covered inside and out with glass mosaic; the author estimates its mosaics needed about 170 tons of glass. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 57} {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 58} Tenth-century Arab sources, she notes, say the tesserae and workmen came from the Byzantine empire. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 58}

## The gold of the tesserae
Nadine Schibille analysed the gold leaf of tesserae from Damascus (21) and Khirbat al-Minya (9) alongside earlier ones from the Roman villa of Noheda in Spain, St Mary's at Ephesus and Hagia Sophia. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 98} The Damascus and Khirbat al-Minya tesserae contain 77% to 97% gold, considerably less than Byzantine and early Umayyad gold coins of Syria, with more silver. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 98}

## Source of the gold
Platinum and palladium ratios to gold show that the gold of the Damascus, Khirbat al-Minya and Hagia Sophia tesserae came from a common stock different from that of Byzantine and Umayyad coinage, ruling out a direct material link between tesserae gold and the coinage of the sixth to eighth centuries. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 99} The author suggests craftsmen used unrefined native gold, since tesserae did not need the purity of coin gold and native gold was presumably cheaper. {cite:schibille-islamic-glass-2022 p. 99}
