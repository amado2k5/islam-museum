## Nature and selection
Some copper is red tending to yellow, the Cypriot, which is the best; some bright red and some red tending to black. One kind is called talqun; the burnt is pungent with some astringency, and washed it is a fine drug for sealing in soft bodies, unwashed in hard ones. The flower of copper is finer than it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.581}.

## Temperament
Hot and dry in the third degree {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.581}.

## Actions and properties
Burnt copper has astringency, sharpness and healing. Among the rumours is that plucking hair with tweezers of talqun copper prevents its growth, as is said {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.581}.

## Cosmetic uses
It blackens the hair {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.581}.

## Wounds and ulcers
It heals the malignant creeping ulcer and stops it creeping, and eats excess flesh; the washed kind heals wounds, and it is said that smeared with honey it suits hardened ulcers gathered in hard bodies {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.581}.

## Eyes
It sharpens sight and helps hardness of the eyelids {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.581}.

## Digestion and the organs of nourishment
Drunk with adromali it loosens the yellow water, and if the palate is rubbed with it it provokes vomiting. The stated measure is not carried over here {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.581}.

## Poisons and bites
One must take care not to leave in copper vessels, or drink from them, anything salty, bitter or fatty, such as oils and meats, or sour or sweet, for they will certainly release verdigris, and verdigris is a deadly poison. This is the book's own warning, and no use of it is given here {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.581}.
