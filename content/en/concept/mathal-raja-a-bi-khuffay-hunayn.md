## The proverb and its meaning
The proverb 'He returned with Hunayn's two boots' is one of the Arabic proverbs recorded in the old proverb books, as a research summary supplied to the museum sets out in a choice of ten. Its meaning, in the summary, is returning with nothing, that is, coming back empty-handed. It belongs to the matter of the great Arabic proverb collections treated in the page [[concept/kutub-al-amthal-al-arabiyya|The Arabic proverb books]] {cite:arrafid-article-amthal-stories}.

## The story as the books tell it
Hunayn was a cobbler in al-Hira, and he fell out with a Bedouin over the price of a pair of boots. Wanting revenge, Hunayn dropped one boot on a road in the desert and the other further on. The Bedouin left his camel to fetch the first boot, and Hunayn stole the camel and the load on it; the Bedouin went back to his tribe with nothing but Hunayn's two boots. {cite:arrafid-article-amthal-stories}

## The old sources
The summary places the proverb in Majma al-Amthal and ascribes the account to an article in the journal al-Rafid. Volume, page and serial numbers stand as in the summary, and the museum has not checked them {cite:arrafid-article-amthal-stories}. Majma al-Amthal is the work of Abu al-Fadl al-Maydani, who died in 518 AH, 1124 CE, and holds according to the summary over 6,000 proverbs; the summary tabulates it with Abu Hilal al-Askari's Jamharat al-Amthal and al-Zamakhshari's al-Mustaqsa {cite:arabictypinglab-arabic-proverbs}.

## When the proverb is used
The proverb is used for what fits its meaning, that is returning with nothing, that is, coming back empty-handed, and the hearer calls its tale to mind even when it is not told. The proverb books agree in keeping a proverb together with its story, because the story explains why it was said, and the sense it is applied to may widen with use; but the summary gives no other instances of its use, so the page gives none.

## A note on the nature of the story
The story here is as the books tell it. It is one of the widely repeated traditional reports, and the museum cannot establish that it happened; it is a narrative told to explain the proverb, not confirmed history. The museum has not opened any of the books and pages named.
