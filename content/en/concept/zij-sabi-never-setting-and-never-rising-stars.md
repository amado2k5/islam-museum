## Summary
Al-Battani shows that on every northern parallel there is a circle centred on the northern pole of the equator of the day, with radius equal to the pole's altitude: stars inside it never set; a matching circle about the southern pole with radius equal to the pole's depression holds stars that never appear; all other stars rise and set, and one near the boundary may be seen twice in a night. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.26}.

## The circle of no setting
He says the stars in the circle centred on the northern pole of the equator, drawn at the distance of the pole's altitude from the horizon on that line, never set at that place. Stars just outside this circle may be seen twice in the one night, once near its start and once near its end, and be hidden between. The stars at the zenith are those which cut off, on the circle through the poles of the equator, an arc between the star and the equator equal to the line's distance from the equator. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.26}.

## The circle of no rising
He says the stars always hidden are those inside the circle centred on the southern pole of the equator, drawn at the distance of the pole's depression below the horizon, and that all the other stars outside these two descriptions rise and set. So the text defines constant visibility and constant hiding purely by where a star lies relative to these two circles. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.26}.
