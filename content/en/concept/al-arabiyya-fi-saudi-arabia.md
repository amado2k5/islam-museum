## Summary
What was found on the place of Arabic in Saudi Arabia: the text of the Basic Law as the Constitute Project shows it, what the encyclopedia and the Library of Congress study say of the Hejaz, the common language and the diversity of the regions, and the dialect classifications of the University of Manchester and Glottolog. The page says what was not found rather than filling it with an estimate. For the place see [[place/saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]]. For the Arab states in general see [[concept/al-alam-al-arabi|The Arab states and the League of Arab States]].

## Official status
The Constitute Project shows the Basic Law of Saudi Arabia, titled on the page "1992 (rev. 2013)". Its Article 1, in the translation shown, says the Kingdom is a sovereign Arab Islamic state. Under Article 1 the site places a field "Language: Arabic" beneath the heading of official or national languages, but a search for the words "Arabic" and "language" in the text shown found no explicit language clause: the field is the site's description, not a sentence of the Law {cite:constituteproject-saudi-arabia}. The article on the Semitic languages in the Encyclopaedia Britannica counts Saudi Arabia among the states where Arabic is official {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}. The two sources agree in the result and differ in the basis, and no other text of the Law naming the language was read.

## The spoken varieties
The encyclopedia places the Hejaz in the west of Arabia along the Red Sea, with Mecca and Medina {cite:britannica-saudi-arabia}. Glottolog's tree of Arabic lists, under "Arabian Peninsula Arabic", Hijazi Arabic with its northern, southern, coastal-Tihama and valley-Tihama branches, Najdi Arabic with its central, northern and southern branches, and a Gulf Arabic group, giving some of them their own ISO code {cite:glottolog-arabic}. The University of Manchester divides the Arabic dialects into large geographical units, one of them the dialects of the Arabian Peninsula, and says most Peninsula dialects keep archaic features {cite:manchester-arabic-structural-variation}. These are two different classifications and the museum does not choose between them: one geographical group at Manchester, separate named varieties at Glottolog. The Manchester page does not name the states inside the Peninsula unit, so placing Saudi Arabia in it is an inference from its location. No phonetic or grammatical description of any particular Saudi dialect was found. For more on the dialects see [[concept/lahjat-al-hijaz|The dialect of the Hejaz]], [[concept/lahjat-al-khalij|The Gulf dialects]].

## Other languages
The Library of Congress study says a common Arabic language is one element of the Kingdom's cultural homogeneity, together with adherence to Sunni Wahhabi Islam as the study puts it {cite:loc-saudi-cultural-homogeneity}. In its page on diversity it says Jeddah and the Hejaz held people from other parts of the Arabic-speaking world and of Persian, Hadrami and African descent, that Mecca had large Indian and Indonesian communities, that Asir was tied to Yemen by population and geography more than to the rest of the Kingdom, and that the Shia of the Eastern Province had cultural links to Iran and Bahrain {cite:loc-saudi-diversity}. That page describes populations, not languages, so it does not say which language these groups spoke. No list of the languages of Saudi Arabia other than Arabic, and no shares for them, was found in what was read.

## The standard and the spoken varieties together
The University of Manchester's page on the dialects of Arabic describes the general situation across the Arabic-speaking world as three-layered: Modern Standard Arabic, the language of the state, education, public services and the media; Classical Arabic, found in the Qur'an, religious texts and worship; and the colloquial dialects of everyday exchange, which the page says people usually regard as of little value even though they are common in song, theatre and film {cite:manchester-arabic-sociolinguistic}. This is a description of the Arab world in general, not of this state alone, and no study measuring it here was read. For the relation of the standard to the spoken varieties see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]], and for the differences among the tribes' speech before the standard [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|The dialects of the tribes and the tongue of Quraysh]].
