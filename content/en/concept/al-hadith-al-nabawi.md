## Overview
The prophetic hadith is what is reported from the [[person/muhammad|Prophet]] in word, deed, tacit approval, or description, and reaches us through a chain of transmission (*sanad*) that ends with him. {cite:sahih-al-bukhari} {cite:sahih-muslim}

## Its standing
It is the second source of Islam after the Qur'an. It explains what the Qur'an states in general terms, and it sets out the details of worship, dealings, the conduct of life, and ethics.

## How it has come down to us
The hadith comes through [[concept/isnad|the chain of transmission]]. It was gathered in books arranged by chapters or by the musnads of transmitters, and the best known of these are the six canonical collections.

The hadith is linked to the discipline of the [[concept/al-sira-al-nabawiyya|Prophetic biography]]. Further detail needs a further source.

## Words for tradition
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose article on Hadith this part of the page follows, begins with vocabulary. Hadith, meaning narrative or talk, is used with the definite article for Tradition, an account of what the Prophet said or did, or of something said or done in his presence that he tacitly approved. Khabar, news, is sometimes used of traditions from the Prophet and sometimes of those from Companions or Successors. Athar, a trace, usually names traditions from Companions or Successors. Sunna, custom, refers to a normative custom of the Prophet or of the early community; see [[concept/sunna|the sunna]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 32}

## How hadith grew in authority
The article says that Tradition's rank as second to the Qur'an came through a long process. The Prophet had made a deep impression, and Islam survived his death and spread far beyond Arabia, so those who had known him naturally had much to tell and new converts were eager to hear. Many Companions settled in conquered lands, where they were presumably questioned, but at first the retelling was informal and there was little effort to record it. The article adds that nobody then thought of Tradition as second to the Qur'an, because there was no collected body of it; only as new problems arose did people feel the need for a subsidiary authority. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 32}

Interest in Tradition developed in individuals and groups in various regions, and many traditionists travelled to learn from authorities in other countries; the annual Pilgrimage also let people from different regions meet and exchange traditions. Gradually the need to name authorities arose. The article thinks the practice was to some extent in force before the end of the first century, but that a complete chain back to the source seems to have become essential only late in the second. It points to [[person/ibn-ishaq|Ibn Ishaq]], who died in 150/767 or 151/768 and quoted authorities in his biography of the Prophet, though not always with a full chain, and to [[person/malik-ibn-anas|Malik ibn Anas]], who died in 179/795, whose law book the [[work/muwatta-malik|Muwatta]] gives some traditions with complete chains, some with partial chains and some with none. Once books of tradition were compiled, each tradition had two parts: the chain of authorities, isnad or sanad, running back to the source, and the text, matn. The chain is treated on the page on [[concept/isnad|the isnad]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 32} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33}

## Hadith beside the Qur'an
While traditionists collected and tried to verify traditions, others were unwilling to lay great weight on Tradition, and parties disputed. The article credits largely the genius of [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]], who died in 204/820, with the victory of the party of Tradition, so that Hadith came to be recognised as a foundation of Islam second only to the Qur'an. He stressed an argument that seems to have circulated even earlier: when the Qur'an speaks of the Book and the Wisdom, it means the Qur'an and Hadith. The article remarks that this gave Hadith a kind of secondary inspiration: not the eternal word of God like the Qur'an, but, like it, divine guidance. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33}

## Writing down and the first collections
The article notes a theory that traditions should be conveyed orally and not written, and that the books themselves contain traditions supporting it. Abu Dawud gives two in succession, the first saying the Prophet commanded that traditions be written and the second that he forbade it. Whatever justification the prohibition had, quite early some men made notes for their own guidance, and these became a basis for larger works. The article names [[person/urwa-ibn-al-zubayr|Urwa ibn al-Zubayr]] in Medina, who died in 94/712 or 99/717 and is quoted as passing on many traditions from his aunt [[person/aisha|Aisha]], and Ibn Shihab al-Zuhri, who died in 124/741, settled in Syria and was one of the most widely quoted authorities. It also mentions sahifas, scripts, in which some Companions collected traditions; see [[concept/kitabat-al-hadith|the writing down of hadith]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33}

The first formal books were of the musnad type, gathering each Companion's traditions together. The article calls this arrangement interesting but inconvenient, since a reader seeking one subject had to read much unrelated material. The works of [[person/abu-dawud-al-tayalisi|al-Tayalisi]], who died in 203/818, and of [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]], who died in 241/855, follow this plan. Malik had arranged the Muwatta by subject, and the third/ninth century was the time of the important musannaf works, arranged by sub-sections, which proved much more convenient. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33}

## The six books and other collections
Six of the musannaf works eventually took precedence. The most authoritative were held to be the Sahihs of [[person/al-bukhari|al-Bukhari]] and [[person/muslim-ibn-al-hajjaj|Muslim]], who died in 261/875, followed by the Sunan works of [[person/abu-dawud-al-sijistani|Abu Dawud]], [[person/al-tirmidhi-muhammad-ibn-isa|al-Tirmidhi]], who died in 279/892, [[person/al-nasai|al-Nasa'i]], who died in 303/915, and [[person/ibn-majah|Ibn Maja]], who died in 273/886. The Sahihs also contain biographical material and Qur'an commentary besides the details of observance, law, commerce and conduct that are the main interest of the Sunan works. The article says the corpus provides details to regulate every aspect of life in this world and to prepare people for the next, and that criticism has been made even of some traditions of al-Bukhari and Muslim. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33}

No official body commissioned these books, so each made its own appeal to the community. By the fourth/tenth century the collections of al-Bukhari and Muslim were fairly generally recognised and the others gained recognition after longer periods. [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]], who died in 808/1406, did not recognise Ibn Maja's book and spoke of five. Other works, such as those of [[person/al-darimi|al-Darimi]], [[person/al-daraqutni|al-Daraqutni]] and [[person/al-bayhaqi-abu-bakr-ahmad-ibn-al-husayn|al-Bayhaqi]], are recognised as important and quoted. There are also commentaries and many selections, a favourite type being the Arba'in, forty traditions on one subject, and larger ones such as al-Baghawi's [[work/masabih-al-sunna|Masabih al-Sunna]], enlarged into the still more popular [[work/mishkat-al-masabih|Mishkat al-Masabih]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33}

## Shi'i collections
Those are the works Sunnis recognise. The Shi'is have their own books, accepting only traditions through Ali's family, an important purpose being to support the Shi'i claims. The article names al-Kafi of [[person/al-kulayni|al-Kulayni]], who died in 328/939, a work by al-Babuya al-Qummi, who died in 381/991, and the Tahdhib al-ahkam of al-Tusi, who died in 459/1067 or 460/1068, with its shorter version al-Istibsar. Like the Sunni books they are arranged by subject. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33}

## Fabrication and the criticism of transmitters
Before the recognised books were compiled, the article says, Tradition had grown enormously and serious students recognised that much of it was fabricated. Storytellers invented extraordinary traditions with seemingly impeccable chains to astonish the public and be paid, and their spurious nature was easily recognised. Others fabricated traditions to spread false doctrine, sometimes so cleverly that it might pass. The article gives a report in which the Prophet says there will be no prophet after him unless God wills: the phrase is so common it could go unnoticed, yet men of insight saw its heretical tendency despite an excellent chain. Some who had invented traditions for heretical teaching later confessed, but as their traditions were already in books the damage could not be undone. At the other extreme were pious men, disturbed by the laxity of their times, who invented traditions urging righteousness. Yahya ibn Said, who died in 143/760, is reported to have found more falsehood in those reputed good than in anyone, and Abu Asim al-Nabil, who died in 212/827, to have found the pious lying most about Hadith. That different kinds of people invented traditions, the article judges, shows how important Hadith had become. The museum treats this on [[concept/wad-al-hadith|the invention of hadith]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 33} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 34}

Transmitters were criticised for other reasons: carelessness, inaccuracy in old age, pretending to transmit from books they had lost, relying on faulty memory. [[person/al-hakim-al-nisaburi|Al-Hakim]], who died in 405/1014, accuses some of tracing back to the Prophet traditions that went back only to Companions or Successors. The effort to test genuineness produced biographical works on the people in chains. Their birth and death years mattered because they show whether a transmitter could have met the person he quotes. Judgements on trustworthiness were also recorded, though they were often contradictory. The books were mainly compiled from the third century, but as the Arabs were genealogists the article thinks material for earlier periods was probably available. See [[concept/jarh-wa-tadil|criticism and praise of transmitters]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 34}

## Grades and technical terms
The criticism of traditions soon developed technical terms, many found in the six books. Al-Tirmidhi made a notable contribution, supplying notes to most of his traditions and ending with a chapter on some points. Traditions were divided into sahih, sound, hasan, good, and daif, weak; see [[concept/al-hadith-al-sahih|the sound hadith]] and [[concept/al-hadith-al-hasan|the good hadith]]. The article lists seven grades of sahih: those given by both al-Bukhari and Muslim; by al-Bukhari alone; by Muslim alone; those given by neither but meeting the conditions of both; of al-Bukhari only; of Muslim only; and traditions sound in the opinion of other authorities. Hasan traditions are held slightly less strong but are necessary for establishing points, and most legal traditions are of this type. Weak traditions have degrees too: allowance is made for using them on exhortation, stories and good conduct, but not on law or on what is permitted and forbidden. Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani, who died in 852/1449, distinguishes salih fit as proof, equal to hasan, from salih fit for consideration, weak in itself but possibly corroborated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 34}

Terms on the number of transmitters include mutawatir, so many reliable transmitters that collusion is impossible, see [[concept/al-hadith-al-mutawatir|the mutawatir hadith]]; mashhur, more than two transmitters, see [[concept/al-hadith-al-mashhur|the mashhur hadith]]; mustafid, which some equate with mashhur, some with mutawatir and most treat as intermediate; aziz, see [[concept/al-hadith-al-aziz|the aziz hadith]]; gharib, from only one Companion or a single man at a later stage, see [[concept/al-hadith-al-gharib|the gharib hadith]]; and shadhdh, a single authority's report differing from what others transmit, rejected if it conflicts with more authoritative reports or its transmitter is not reliable enough alone, see [[concept/al-hadith-al-shadhdh|the shadhdh hadith]]. Ahad names traditions from a relatively small number of transmitters, distinguished from the report of a single man, see [[concept/khabar-al-ahad|the single report]]. The article notes that Ibn al-Salah, who died in 643/1245, and al-Nawawi, who died in 676/1278, differ over fard and gharib. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 34}

On the nature of the chain: muttasil is unbroken, marfu if it reaches the Prophet and mawquf if it stops at a Companion; musnad generally means a fully connected chain to the Prophet; maqtu stops at a Successor; munqati includes unnamed people. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 34} For accepted traditions, makbul is sahih or hasan and mahfuz outweighs a shadhdh one. For rejected ones, munkar has a sole transmitter who differs from a reliable one or lacks standing to be accepted alone, see [[concept/al-hadith-al-munkar|the munkar hadith]]; mardud is the opposite of makbul; matruk comes from a single transmitter suspected of falsehood in Tradition; and mawdu, fictitious, is the worst of all. One group of terms, on corroborating solitary reports, is too damaged in the scan to report. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 35}

## Western scholarship
The article states that the Muslim criticism was very detailed and shows a genuine effort to clear away what was false, but that Western scholars have tended to argue it did not go far enough. It names [[person/ignaz-goldziher|Goldziher]], who showed that Hadith stands on less firm ground than the conventional doctrine supposes, and who has been followed by many. The article lists what Western readers notice: phrases from the Old and New Testaments put in the Prophet's mouth; references to distant towns to be conquered, even towns not yet founded in his time; named parties of the early period such as the Kharijis, Murji'a, Qadariyya and Jahmiyya; references to the rightly guided caliphs and unmistakeable ones to the Umayyads and Abbasids; many miracles, though the Qur'an does not present him as a miracle-worker; and elaborate detail on the tribulations before the end of the world, the Last Judgment, heaven and hell. The Western mind, it says, finds it hard to accept such material as genuinely from the Prophet. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 35}

The article then reports J. Schacht's argument that chains grew as time passed, so legal traditions of a later period were eventually traced back to the Prophet. Its author does not feel justified in explaining away the whole of Tradition on these lines, but finds it clear that much later material has been attributed to the Prophet, which makes a satisfactory criterion of genuineness very hard to find. He suggests that material accumulated in a circle may later have seemed to come from a Companion who settled there, and been attributed to him on the assumption that he had the Prophet as authority. One result of Western criticism, he says, is that one must be chary of accusing men like [[person/abu-hurayra|Abu Hurayra]] of inventing many traditions, since they probably transmitted very little of what they are reputed to have told. These are the views of Western scholars and of the article's author; the classical Muslim position is the one set out in the sections above. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 35} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 36}

## The sciences of hadith
The study is called the sciences of hadith, ulum al-hadith. The first comprehensive work was by [[person/al-ramahurmuzi|al-Ramahurmuzi]], who died in 360/971, in seven parts. Al-Hakim wrote a more systematic work in 52 categories, a method later writers followed, and Ibn al-Salah's classical work has 65. The study covers classes of traditions and transmitters, with special stress on knowing the Companions and Successors, the methods of learning and transmitting, rules for writing traditions and correcting a manuscript, and even the ages at which to begin and stop transmitting. As an illustration, the article says that when the customary blessing follows the words Messenger of God, a scribe must not end a line on Messenger, lest a reader glancing at the next line think God was being asked to bless Himself. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 36}

Ibn al-Salah lists seven methods of receiving traditions, not all agreeing on their relative weight: hearing from the shaykh, held superior; reciting to him, called ard; the licence, ijaza; handing over, munawala, which he held valid only with a licence though some traditionists held it sufficient; correspondence, mukataba; bequest, wasiyya, which he held gives no right to transmit though some think it does; and a find, wijada, usable if one explains how the book was obtained. Some of these were used in the third/ninth century or earlier, the article says, noting that al-Bukhari has chapters on reading to a shaykh and on munawala and mukataba. Al-Hakim is precise about the transmission words: haddathani for hearing a shaykh alone and haddathana when others are present, akhbarani for reading to him alone and akhbarana for hearing another read, anba'ani for submitting traditions and receiving a spoken licence. The word an is generally allowable for a reliable transmitter, and al-Khatib al-Baghdadi, who died in 463/1071, justifies it for long chains. The strictest rules require a whole book to carry its own chain, though the article observes that printing has largely made this unnecessary. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 36}

## Contradictory traditions and abrogation
The existence of seemingly contradictory traditions has caused difficulty, and the article names [[person/ibn-qutayba|Ibn Qutayba]]'s work on the subject as standard; see [[concept/mukhtalif-al-hadith|the apparently contradictory hadith]]. With ingenuity they may be reconciled, or one preferred for its superior transmitters, or the later held to abrogate the earlier from knowing when each was promulgated; see [[concept/naskh|abrogation]]. A tradition cannot be abrogated by consensus, only by a tradition. The article also says that it is often claimed that validity depends on the chain, not the text; this is generally true but not the whole story, since al-Hakim mentions traditions with very reliable men in the chain that he holds faulty and weak, which he says needs considerable knowledge and discussion with the learned to detect. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 37}

## For Sprouts
Hadith is what was passed down from the Prophet, his words, deeds or approvals, and it reaches us through a chain of narrators. It is the second source of Islam after the Qur'an.

## For Explorers
Hadith is what was passed on from the Prophet: his words, his deeds, his approvals or his description. It was kept by a chain of narrators, each linked to the one before. It is the second source after the Qur'an, and it explains the details of worship and of good conduct.

## For Scholars
Hadith is what is transmitted of the Prophet's words, deeds, tacit approvals and descriptions, reaching us through a chain of narrators ending at him. It is the second source after the Qur'an, explaining its generals and fixing the details of worship, dealings, biography and conduct. It was gathered into works arranged by legal chapter or by narrator, the most famous being the six books. The science of the isnad shows who passed a report and how it came down, and on it judgements of soundness are based. The museum gives each hadith with its source and its grade, and does not grade a hadith itself.
