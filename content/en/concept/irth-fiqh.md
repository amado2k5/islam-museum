## Meaning and related terms
In the lexicon irth is what is original and old, which one inherits from the first, and the remainder of anything; it is used for a thing passing from one people to another, and for the thing inherited. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2098} Tarika (estate) is close to it in this use. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2098} The science of inheritance, also called the science of fixed shares (fara'id), is a science of the principles of law and arithmetic by which each heir's right in the estate is known. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2098}
Technically, the Shafi'is and Hanbalis define irth as a right capable of division that is established for its beneficiary after the death of the one who held it, by kinship or the like. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2098}
## The importance of inheritance
Knowing the fara'id is among the most important sciences after the pillars of religion, and the Prophet urged its learning and teaching; the report says that knowledge will be taken away until two men dispute a share and find no one to decide between them. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2098} The Companions praised one another when they met to discuss it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2098}
Many jurists wrote separate books on fara'id, beginning in the second century AH; the early writers include Ibn Shubruma, Ibn Abi Layla and Abu Thawr. The law books of the second and third centuries had no inheritance rules, and law books began to include them only in the fourth century. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2098} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2099}
## The evidence for its lawfulness
Inheritance is lawful by the Qur'an, the Sunna and consensus. The Qur'an has the verses on inheritance; the Sunna includes the saying "give the shares to their people, and what remains goes to the nearest male", and the inheritance of a grandmother on her mother's side as attested by al-Mughira and Muhammad ibn Maslama before Umar; consensus includes the inheritance of a paternal grandmother by Umar's ijtihad, and analogy has no place in it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2099}
## Gradual legislation of inheritance
In the pre-Islamic period people inherited by two means, kinship and cause. By kinship they did not inherit to minors or women, only to one who fought and took booty; this continued until the verses on women and children were revealed. The causes were alliance and contract, and adoption; these were left for a time and then abrogated. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2099} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2100} A verse on alliance was revealed (al-Nisa, verse 33), and then abrogated by the verse of al-Ahzab (verse 6). {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2100}
## The rights attached to the estate and their order
Juristic usage of tarika is what the deceased left in property and rights; for the Hanafis it is what he left as property free of another's right in a specific thing. Under Hanafi principle only rights that are attached to property, such as the right to build higher and rights of use, pass to heirs; the option, pre-emption and the usufruct of a bequeathed thing do not pass in their view. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2100} Blood-money due for accidental killing, or agreed in place of wilful killing, is part of the estate. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2100}
The Malikis, Shafi'is and the well-known Hanafi view begin with the debts attached to particular goods, such as a mortgaged thing, because the deceased could not dispose of what another's right attached to during life. If the whole estate is mortgaged, the deceased is not prepared for burial until the debt is met, or from the surplus; otherwise his burial falls on the one who was obliged to maintain him in life. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2100}
The Hanbalis and the less known Hanafi view put the shroud and burial first, as the maintenance of an insolvent is put before his creditors' debts; then the debts are met from the rest of his property. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2100} The order of debts after burial differs: for the Hanafis, if the debt is owed to people and several creditors claim, if all are debts of health, or all debts of sickness, the balance is divided by the proportion of each debt; if both kinds meet, the debt of health is preferred for its strength. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2100}
## A sick person's acknowledgement of debt
If a sick person acknowledges a debt for which there is no proof but his own word, it is a debt of sickness and has its rule, and in his acknowledgement the surplus over a third is weakened. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2100} If he acknowledges a debt whose existence is known by observation, as the price due for property he owned or destroyed, it is a debt of health, since its obligation was known without his acknowledgement. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2101}
## Debts owed to God
If the debt is one of God's rights, such as fasting, prayer, zakat, the pilgrimage, a vow or an expiation, it must be carried out from a third of his remaining property if the dead man willed it, after the debts owed to people; if he did not will it, it is not obliged. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2101}
The Malikis pay, after burial, the debts owed to people, then the pilgrimage offering of a deferred pilgrim who died after throwing the pebbles at the Aqaba, then the zakat al-fitr and the expiations he neglected if he had stated in health that they were owed, which come out of the capital whether he willed them or not. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2101} The Shafi'is, after burial, pay the debts owed by the dead person from the capital, whether to God or to people, and the debt owed to God, such as zakat, precedes the debt owed to people; if the debts are attached to specific goods, they come before burial. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2101} The Hanbalis, after burial, satisfy the pledgee, then the debts not attached to goods; the creditors' right attaches to the whole estate, and if the debts exceed it they share it in proportion to their debts, like the property of a bankrupt; then the bequest to a stranger is carried out from a third of what remains, and a bequest to an heir needs the consent of the other heirs. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2101}
## Debts take precedence over bequests
The jurists agree that debt takes precedence over bequest, on the report from Ali that the Prophet decided that debt comes before bequest, and because the need of the deceased for debt absorbs what he has, as his burial costs do; then his bequests are carried out. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2101} God mentions the bequest before the debt in the verse because the bequest resembles inheritance in being taken without a return, so it is hard for the heirs to pay and is apt to be neglected; the order there urges its fulfilment and shows that it is like debt in being obligatory. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2102}
## The bequest from a third
After burial and debts, bequests are carried out from a third of what remains in all four schools, not from the capital, since what came before has been spent on his needs, and a third could otherwise take the whole remainder and deprive the heirs. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2102} Khwahar-zadeh of the Hanafis said that a specified bequest comes before inheritance, while a general bequest, of a third or a quarter, is like an inheritance, so the legatee becomes a sharer with the heirs, gaining if the estate grows and losing if it shrinks. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2102} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2103}
## The heirs and the pillars of inheritance
After burial, debts and bequest, the remainder is divided among the heirs whose right is established by the Qur'an, that is, those named in the verses of inheritance; by the Sunna, as the grandmothers' sixth; or by consensus, as the grandfather and the son's son and daughter. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2103}
A pillar (rukn) in the lexicon is the stronger side of a thing; in law it is part of a thing's reality. Inheritance in the sense of entitlement has three pillars: the one who leaves (muwarrith), who is the dead or one attached to them; the heir (warith), who is alive after the death or one attached to the living; and the estate (mawruth), which is not limited to property. If one pillar is missing there is no inheritance. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2103}
## Conditions and causes of inheritance and its blockers
A man who dies leaving an heir but no property leaves nothing to inherit, since inheritance concerns property and what is more than property. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2103} In usul a condition is what its absence necessitates absence, but whose presence does not necessitate presence. Inheritance has three conditions: the death of the testator, or his being counted as dead by judgment, as with the missing person declared dead by the judge, or by estimate, as with a foetus separated by a blow to its mother that requires a ghurra; the living of the heir after the testator's death, or his being counted as living, as with a child born alive; and knowledge of the basis of inheritance (marriage, kinship or patronage) and of the degree in which the deceased and the heir meet. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2103}
There are four causes of inheritance: three agreed, namely marriage, patronage and kinship, which the Hanafis call ties of blood (rahm), and a fourth disputed, Islam, by which, for the Malikis and Shafi'is, the treasury (bayt al-mal) inherits. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2103} A blocker is what its presence necessitates absence. The blockers agreed among the four schools are three: slavery, homicide and difference of religion; the apostasy, difference of abodes and the constructive gap (al-dawr al-hukmi) are disputed; for the Malikis the uncertainty of the order of the deaths is a blocker, and for some Shafi'is mutual curse (li'an) between spouses. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2103}
## Slavery
The four schools agree that full slavery blocks inheritance, since what is in a slave's hand belongs to his master, so inheriting from his kin would pass the estate to the master without cause, which is void by consensus. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2104}
## Homicide
The four schools agree that homicide carrying retaliation blocks an adult sane killer from inheriting, when the killing is direct. They differ on what killing is meant, and on the cases of a child, a madman, a causer of death, and accidental killing. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2104} The three imams and Abu Yusuf and Muhammad hold that wilful unlawful killing that carries retaliation is a person intending to kill an inviolable human and killing him by what probably causes death. Abu Hanifa holds that it is a sword-blow or what is like it in dividing the parts. The Hanafis hold that quasi-wilful and accidental killing also block inheritance, and that the causer of death, such as the digger of a well, and a child or madman killer, are not blocked. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2104} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2105}
The Hanbalis and, in the sounder view, the Malikis hold that the wilful killer, direct or by cause, is blocked from property and blood-money even if he is a child or a madman. The Shafi'is hold that anyone with any part in a killing is blocked, even if it was lawful, such as the executioner, the imam, the judge, the hangman or a witness. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2104} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2105}
If the testator is killed by retaliation, as a prescribed penalty or in self-defence, the heir is not blocked according to the Hanafis, Malikis and Hanbalis. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2104} The Shafi'is' reason for blocking is fear that the heir would hasten to the inheritance, on the maxim that one who hastens a thing before its time is punished by being deprived of it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2105}
## Difference of religion
Most jurists, with Ali, Zayd ibn Thabit and most Companions, hold that an unbeliever does not inherit from a Muslim, even if he converts before the estate is divided, since the inheritance was due at the death of the testator. Ahmad holds that a convert before the division inherits, on the report that whoever embraces Islam keeps what he has. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2105} The four schools agree that a Muslim does not inherit from an unbeliever, on the report that people of different religions do not inherit from each other. Mu'adh, Mu'awiya, al-Hasan and others allowed a Muslim to inherit from an unbeliever, on the saying that Islam is exalted and is not exalted over. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2106}
## Apostasy
There is no disagreement that an apostate inherits from no one, since his ruling is that he repents or is killed, and an apostate woman is confined until she repents. As to whether he is inherited from, the Malikis, Shafi'is and the well-known Hanbali view hold that his whole property becomes a fay' (booty) for the treasury if he dies or is killed as an apostate; Abu Yusuf, Muhammad ibn al-Hasan and one narration of Ahmad hold that his Muslim heirs inherit, citing the practice of Abu Bakr and Ali. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2106} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2107}
## The inheritance of an apostate and the time of the heir
When an apostate dies, his Muslim heirs inherit what he acquired while he was Muslim, but not what he acquired during his apostasy, which becomes fay' for the Muslims. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2107} The reports from Abu Hanifa differ on who the heir is: al-Hasan's report makes it whoever was an heir at the time of apostasy and lived until the apostate's death, so one who became an heir after the apostasy does not inherit, since the cause is the apostasy and entitlement is completed by death. Abu Yusuf's report takes the heir present at the time of apostasy, whose right is not voided by his own death before the apostate's. Muhammad's report, which is the sounder, takes whoever is an heir at death or killing, since what arises after the cause has formed but before it is complete counts as present at its start. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2107}
Muhammad counted the apostate's flight to the enemy territory as his death, so his estate is divided from that flight. Abu Yusuf took the heir at the judge's ruling on the flight; the wife inherits if she is in her waiting period, on the view of the two companions, since the marriage was ended by apostasy but he fled from her inheritance, and a fleeing husband's wife inherits if she is still in her waiting period when he dies. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2107}
## Difference of religion among unbelievers
Among the Hanafis, and according to the sounder Shafi'i view and a Hanbali narration, unbelievers inherit from one another, being one community in face of the Muslims; a Jew inherits from a Christian and the reverse, and a Magian inherits from a Christian or a Jew and is inherited from by them. The opposing Shafi'i view says they are communities, so communities do not inherit from one another. The Malikis have a preferred view that Jews and Christians are each one community and all others are different communities. Ibn Abi Layla holds that Jews and Christians inherit from each other but Magians do not inherit from them and they do not inherit from Magians. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2107} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2108}
Those who forbid argue from the saying that people of two different communities do not inherit from each other, and from the verses that distinguish the communities. Ibn Abi Layla argues that Jews and Christians agree on monotheism and on Moses and the Torah, unlike the Magians. The Hanafis argue that God made religion two, truth and falsehood, and made people two parties, and that the disputants are two, so that all unbelievers are one community when faced with the Muslims. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2108}
## Difference of abodes
The jurists mean by difference of abodes a difference of protection (mana'a): an army and a separate rule and authority, so that the bond between them is cut and one may lawfully kill the other. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2109} It is agreed that Muslims inherit from one another however their lands and states differ, since the lands of Islam are one abode, and each Muslim's guardianship belongs to Islam. A Muslim in enemy territory who entered under safe conduct and returns is in the ruling of the abode of Islam; a real difference counts only when no ruling difference stands against it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2109}
For the Malikis, some Hanbalis and one Shafi'i view, the difference of abodes does not block inheritance between unbelievers, since there is no proof of blocking once its cause and condition are established. For Abu Hanifa, the sounder Shafi'i view and some Hanbalis, it blocks, since there is no support and alliance between them when their states differ, and alliance is the basis of inheritance. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2109}
## The constructive gap
For al-Shafi'i the constructive gap (al-dawr al-hukmi) is a blocker: that inheritance would entail its own absence. Thus a holder of property who, in appearance, acknowledges a person who would block him, as a full brother acknowledging a son of the deceased of unknown descent, establishes the descent but the son does not inherit, because if he inherited he would block the brother, who would then not be an heir, so his acknowledgement would be void, and descent would not be established, and so inheritance would not be established. Establishing inheritance leads to its denial, and what leads by its establishment to its negation is negated from the start. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2109} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2110}
## Acknowledging another's descent alongside the blocking heirs
If the acknowledger holds the property and acknowledges someone who would block him by full exclusion, the descent and the inheritance are established, as when he acknowledges another son, brother or paternal uncle, because inheritance follows descent and descent is established. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2110} If one of two sons who hold the estate acknowledges a third son and the other denies it, the third son's descent is not established, by consensus, and he does not inherit outwardly for want of descent; whether he shares inwardly is disputed. The sounder view of al-Shafi'i is that he shares in a third of what is in the acknowledger's hand, the view of the Hanbalis and Malikis, while the second view gives him half, the view of Abu Hanifa and one narration from Ahmad. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2110}
## Those entitled to the estate
The estate is taken in order: the holders of fixed shares; then the agnatic residuaries by descent, and by the Hanafis the residuaries by cause; then those who take by return; then the blood-kin without fixed shares (dhawu al-arham); then the client by alliance; then the one acknowledged as kin by another; then the legatee of more than a third; and finally the treasury. Each of these classes has disagreements and detail. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2110} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2111}
## The fixed shares
The fixed shares named in God's book are six: a half, a quarter, an eighth, two-thirds, a third and a sixth. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2110} A half is named in three places: the share of one daughter, the share of the husband when there is no child, and the share of a full or paternal sister when the deceased has no child. A quarter is the husband's share with a child, and the wives' share with none. An eighth is the wives' share with a child. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2110} Two-thirds is the share of two or more daughters, of two or more sisters, and of two or more daughters of a son when there is no daughter. A third is the mother's share with no child and no siblings, and the share of two or more uterine siblings. A sixth is the share of the parents with a child, of the mother with siblings, and of one uterine sibling. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2111}
## The holders of fixed shares
Twelve persons take fixed shares: four men, the father, the true grandfather, the uterine brother and the husband; and eight women, the wife, the daughter, the son's daughter, the full sister, the paternal sister, the uterine sister, the mother and the true grandmother. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2111} The two spouses are called holders of causal shares, since their inheritance is by marriage; the rest are holders of kinship shares, since kinship is called descent. Fixed shares may combine with residuary shares. Holders of fixed shares inherit when no one blocks them by full exclusion. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2111}
## The father's three situations
The father has three situations. First, he inherits only a fixed share, when the deceased leaves a male descendant, such as a son or son's son; he takes a sixth. Second, he takes both a fixed share and a residuary share, when the deceased leaves a female descendant, such as a daughter or son's daughter; he takes a sixth as a fixed share and then the remainder as a residuary. Third, he takes only as a residuary, when the deceased leaves no descendant at all, and takes the whole estate or what remains after the holders of fixed shares, on the verse of al-Nisa (verse 11) about the parents' sixth when there is a child. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2111}
## The mother's inheritance
The mother has three situations. First, she takes a sixth as a fixed share when the deceased leaves a descendant who inherits by fixed share or residue, or a plurality of siblings, on the verse of al-Nisa (verse 11). The word child covers male and female, one and many, and a son's child counts as a child. Siblings means two or more, from any side, even if they are themselves blocked. Ibn Abbas differed, holding that only three or more siblings block the mother, while the majority hold that two count as a plurality, as two daughters or two sisters take as a plurality, and that the plural is used for the dual in the Qur'an. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2112} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2113} Mu'adh and al-Hasan al-Basri held that the mother is blocked to a sixth only by brothers or by brothers with sisters. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2113}
Second, she takes a third of the whole estate when the deceased leaves no descendant and no plurality of siblings, and no spouse, and only the father. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2113} Third, she takes a third of the remainder after a spouse's share, when the deceased leaves the mother, the father and one spouse, with no plurality of siblings. These two cases are called the two cases of Umar, since he decided them; they are also called the gharrawiyya and the gharība. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2113}
## The true grandfather's situations
The true grandfather is one whose relation to the deceased does not pass through a mother, such as the father's father however high. He is a holder of fixed shares and a residuary. He is blocked by the father; if the father is absent he takes his place and has the father's three situations when there are no siblings. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2113}
With siblings: the jurists agree that uterine siblings do not inherit with the grandfather. Full and paternal siblings inherit with him according to Malik, al-Shafi'i, Ahmad and the two companions of Abu Hanifa. Abu Hanifa makes the grandfather like the father, so he blocks the siblings, a view shared by Ibn Jarir al-Tabari, al-Muzani and Abu Thawr. Abu Hanifa argues that the grandfather is a father, called father in the Qur'an and Sunna, and is nearest to the deceased; only the father blocks him; and the grandfather and the brothers are of the same degree through the father. Their opponents argue that the siblings' inheritance is established by text and no text bars it, and that the grandfather does not replace the father in all rulings, as for a minor. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2114}
## The grandfather's share with the siblings
The Qur'an and the Sunna give no measure for the grandfather's share with siblings; the ruling rests on the ijtihad of the Companions. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2114} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2115} Ali's well-known view is that the grandfather takes what remains after the shares of the sisters when there is no brother with them, as long as it is not less than a sixth; otherwise he takes a sixth. He also held the grandfather as one of them at all times. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2115} Zayd ibn Thabit's view is that the grandfather residues the siblings in every case; he shares with them as a full or paternal brother, provided his share is not less than a third of the estate. This is the view of Malik, Ahmad, Abu Yusuf and Muhammad; the Shafi'is limit it to the absence of a fixed-share holder, and where one is present the grandfather may choose among three: sharing, a third of the remainder, or a third of the whole. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2115}
Ibn Mas'ud's view is that the grandfather takes the remainder after the shares of sisters who stand alone without a brother or an heir in the line, provided his share is not less than a third, since with the daughters of the deceased alone his share is not less than a third, and a brother should be treated no worse than that. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2115}
## The two cases excepted by Abu Hanifa
Abu Hanifa excepted two cases from making the grandfather stand in the father's place in inheritance and blocking: first, a husband, a mother and a grandfather, where the mother takes a third of the whole estate, though with a father she would take a third of the remainder; second, a wife, a mother and a grandfather, where the mother takes a third of the whole. The scholars of dictation report from Abu Yusuf that on the view of Abu Bakr al-Siddiq the mother takes a third of the remainder in both cases, which the people of Kufa report from Ibn Mas'ud, while the people of Basra report otherwise. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2115}
## The inheritance of grandmothers
Grandmothers are of two kinds: true grandmothers, whose relation to the deceased does not pass through a father, or who connect through an agnate or a fixed-share holder, such as a mother's mother; and false grandmothers, who connect through one who is neither an agnate nor a fixed-share holder, such as a mother's father's mother. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2116} Inheritance for the grandmother is not in the Qur'an; it is established by the well-known Sunna, as in the report of al-Mughira ibn Shu'ba that the Prophet gave the grandmother a sixth, and by the consensus of the Companions and later scholars. A true grandmother is a holder of a fixed share, a false one is among the blood-kin without fixed shares. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2116}
A true grandmother has two situations. First, she takes a fixed sixth, which one grandmother takes alone and several grandmothers share. If a grandmother with two lines of descent is with one with a single line, Abu Yusuf, the Malikis and, in the sounder view, the Shafi'is have them share the sixth equally; Muhammad, Zufar and al-Hasan ibn Ziyad divide it into thirds, two-thirds for the one with two lines and a third for the other. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2116} Second, all grandmothers are blocked by the mother, since the maternal grandmothers connect through the mother, and the paternal ones are weaker and are blocked by the mother too; paternal grandmothers fall away by the father, the view of Uthman, Ali, Zayd and others. The nearer grandmother blocks the more distant one from any side, according to Ali and the Hanafis; according to a second narration from Zayd, the nearer on the father's side and the more distant on the mother's side are equal, while the sounder Shafi'i view is that the nearer on the father's side does not block the more distant on the mother's side. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2117}
## The inheritance of spouses
The Qur'an states the inheritance of spouses. The husband takes half of his wife's estate by fixed share if she leaves no inheriting descendant, and a quarter if she has one, whether from him or from another. The wife inherits only by fixed share: a quarter if her husband leaves no inheriting descendant, and an eighth if he does. One wife takes the quarter or the eighth alone, and several wives share it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2117} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2118} Inheritance by marriage needs two conditions. First, the marriage is valid: if the contract is void there is no inheritance, according to Abu Hanifa, al-Shafi'i and Ahmad; Malik holds that where the defect is agreed, as marrying a fifth wife while having four, there is no inheritance, while where it is disputed, as marrying without a guardian, inheritance stands if one dies before the annulment. Second, the marriage exists at death, in fact or by ruling, as when a woman in a revocable divorce is in her waiting period; a wife divorced irrevocably inherits nothing, unless the husband who caused the separation is treated as fleeing inheritance by divorcing in his last illness. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2118}
## The conditions of daughters
The Qur'an gives the law of the daughters of the deceased (al-Nisa, verse 11). There are three situations for daughters of the body. First, when there is a son or sons with them, all are residuaries, the male taking the share of two females; they take the whole estate if there are no fixed-share holders, or the remainder after them. Second, when the deceased has two or more daughters and no son, they take two-thirds of the estate equally; the case of the two daughters of Sa'd ibn al-Rabi' after his death at Uhud is the report on which two daughters are given two-thirds. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2118}
## Two daughters
The four schools and all the Companions agree that two daughters take two-thirds, on the report of the two daughters of Sa'd ibn al-Rabi' after his death at Uhud. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2119} It is reported from Ibn Abbas that two daughters are ruled as one, taking a half, but he withdrew this, and al-Shanshuri reports consensus against the report. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2119} A single daughter takes a half by fixed share if no agnate male, such as the son of the deceased, takes with her. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2119}
## The situations of the son's daughters
A son's daughter is any daughter who connects to the deceased through a son, however far down. She has six situations: three when she stands in the place of a daughter of the body, namely one who takes a half if alone with no agnate, two-thirds if there are several with no agnate, or residue if there is an agnate with her; and three when she does not stand in that place because a nearer descendant exists, namely a sixth to complete two-thirds with a daughter of the body, unless an agnate is with her; nothing when two or more daughters of the body or nearer granddaughters are present; or nothing when a son or a nearer son's son is present. Ibn Mas'ud differs in the second case, holding that son's daughters do not inherit with a son's son. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2120} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2121}
## The situations of full sisters
Full sisters have five situations, established by the Qur'an, the Sunna and consensus. A half for one who stands alone with no blocker or full brother, and two-thirds for two or more when no full brother is with them; the verse of kalala (al-Nisa 176) gives these. When sisters exceed two they take two-thirds, by analogy with daughters. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2121} The third situation is a full brother with them, when they take the residue with him, the male taking the share of two females. The Malikis and Hanbalis hold that a full sister becomes residuary by the grandfather when there is no brother to make her one. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2121} The fourth is when the full sister is a residuary with another, when the deceased leaves a female descendant heir: the daughter takes a half and the sister takes the residue by agnation, a ruling given by Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud as a judgment of the Prophet. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2121} The fifth is exclusion, when the deceased leaves a male descendant or a father. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2121}
## The situations of paternal sisters
Paternal sisters have seven situations. A half for one when there is no full sister or paternal brother to make her a residuary; two-thirds for two or more when there is none; and a sixth for one or more with one full sister to complete two-thirds, since the full sister's share is a half, so the paternal sister takes a sixth to complete two-thirds, unless a paternal brother is with her, when he makes her a residuary. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2121}
## Completing the situations of paternal sisters
A paternal sister is blocked by two full sisters unless a paternal brother is with her, when the two take the residue, the male taking the share of two females. She is blocked by the father, the son, the son's son however low, the full brother, and the full sister when the full sister becomes a residuary with a daughter or son's daughter, since in this case the full sister is like a full brother as a nearer residuary. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2122}
## The uterine siblings
By uterine siblings is meant the deceased's brothers and sisters from the mother's side only. They always inherit by fixed share and never as residuaries, since their kinship is through the mother alone, so they do not become residuaries alone or with others. Males and females among them are equal, since preference of the male belongs to residuary status, which is absent in kinship through the mother. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2122} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2123} They have three situations: a sixth as a fixed share for one, male or female, when the deceased has no inheriting descendant and no inheriting male ascendant; a third as a fixed share for two or more, shared equally, when there is no inheriting descendant or male ascendant; and blocking by the son, the son's son, the daughter, the son's daughter, the father and the grandfather. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2122} The proof is the verse of kalala (al-Nisa 12), which means the uterine siblings by consensus, and the reading of Ubayy and Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas with the addition "from the mother". {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2122} The equality of male and female, and the rule that their share does not exceed a third, rest on the same verse, which says that if they are more than one they share in the third. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2122}
## Inheritance by residue
The lexical sense of 'asaba is a man's sons and his kin on his father's side, so called because they surround him. In law, an agnate by himself takes the whole estate if alone, or what remains after the fixed shares, and this is the sense meant when the word is used alone; al-Fanari in his commentary on al-Sirajiyya defines him as any male whose connection to the deceased does not pass through a female, so uterine siblings are not agnates. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2123} Agnatic kin are of three kinds: agnate in himself, agnate by another, agnate with another. There is also the causal agnate, the freed slave and his male agnates. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2123} The agnates in themselves are four classes: the deceased's own offspring, then his origin, then his father's offspring, then his grandfather's offspring; within them the nearer comes first. Thus the sons come first, then their sons however low, then the father, then his father however high, then the brothers, then their sons, then the paternal uncles, then their sons, then the uncles of the father, then the uncles of the grandfather. The father and the grandfather count in one class. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2123} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2124}
The Malikis and Shafi'is count seven classes: sonship, fatherhood, grandfatherhood with brothers, the sons of brothers, paternal uncles, the client, and the treasury. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2124} If the agnate is one, he takes the whole estate when there is no fixed-share holder, or the remainder. If the classes differ, the class of sonship comes first. If they are in one class, the nearer in degree comes first; then the stronger kinship, so a full brother before a paternal brother; if all are equal they share. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2124}
## Agnate by another
Four women become agnates by another: the daughter of the body, the son's daughter when there is no daughter, the full sister, and the paternal sister when there is no full sister, who become agnates through their brothers of their own strength. The son's daughters are also made agnates by their paternal cousins of their degree, and the sisters by their brothers' sons and their cousins' sons if they need them in inheritance. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2124} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2125} The Malikis and Hanbalis hold that a sister becomes an agnate by the grandfather when no brother makes her one. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2124} The basis is the two verses on the daughters and the siblings (al-Nisa 11 and 176), in which a male takes the share of two females. A female without a fixed share, such as a niece or an aunt, is not covered by the text, so she does not become an agnate by her brother or her uncle. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2124}
## Residuary with another
A residuary with another is any female who becomes a residuary with another female: the full or paternal sister with a daughter, whether a daughter of the body or a son's daughter, one or several, on the report "make the sisters residuaries with the daughters". {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2125} The difference from residuary through another is that the one who makes another residuary is himself a residuary by self, so the status of residuary passes through him to the female; in residuary with another there is no residuary by self at all. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2125}
## Inheritance by causal residuary status
The jurists agree that a freed slave, male or female, inherits all the property of the one who freed him, or what remains, if the two share the religion, and the freer leaves no heir, or leaves one who takes only part. If their religions differ, the majority hold that there is no inheritance between them; the sounder Hanbali view is that a Muslim inherits from his unbelieving freed slave by client-right, and the reverse. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2125}
## Client by alliance
The contract of alliance (muwala) is a cause of inheritance for the Hanafis, ranked after the client by manumission. Whoever embraced Islam at another's hand, allied himself and made the contract, and dies leaving no other heir, has his estate. This is reported from Umar, Ibn Mas'ud, al-Hasan and Ibrahim al-Nakha'i. They rely on the verse of the contract, which they hold is not abrogated, and on the report of Tamim al-Dari that the convert at another's hand is the nearest of people to his life and death. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2125} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2126} Malik, al-Shafi'i, Ahmad, Ibn Shubruma, al-Thawri and al-Awza'i hold that his estate goes to the Muslims. The Malikis rely on the saying that the client-right belongs to the one who frees. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2125} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2126} Al-Hasan held that the verse on the contract was abrogated by the verse of al-Ahzab (verse 6) on the kindred, and Mujahid held that the duty owed to such a person is the blood-money, support and assistance. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2126}
## The treasury
The treasury (bayt al-mal) is the body to which every property the Muslims are entitled to goes when its owner among them is not fixed, such as spoils; the Shafi'is also call it the Islamic side. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2126} For the Hanafis, the Hanbalis and an isolated Maliki view, the treasury is not an heir; the estate or what remains of it goes to it as property with no owner, like a lost find, and it is spent on public good; al-Muzani and Ibn Surayj among the Shafi'is agree. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2126} For the Malikis and Shafi'is the treasury is an agnate, ranked after the freeing patron, and it inherits the whole or the remainder as an agnate by blood does. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2126} The later jurists ruled that when the treasury's affairs are not in order, as when the imam is absent or unjust, the remainder goes to the holders of fixed shares, since the estate's destination is limited to them and the treasury; when the treasury is unavailable they are determined. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2126}
## Blocking
Blocking (hajb) in the lexicon means preventing; a veil is so called because it prevents seeing, and a doorkeeper is called hajib because he prevents entry. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2126}
## The definition of blocking
Blocking, in the definition of the author of al-Sirajiyya, is preventing a particular person from inheriting, wholly or in part, by the presence of another person; the definitions in the other schools do not depart from this. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2127}
Blocking is of two kinds. Blocking by an attribute is what the scholars of inheritance call a bar, such as the killer's exclusion from inheritance. Blocking by a person is the sense meant when the word is used alone, and it has two kinds. Blocking by exclusion (hirman) is when one person drops another altogether; it does not touch six heirs by consensus, namely the two parents, the two spouses and the children, the rule being that anyone who connects to the deceased directly is excluded only by a nearer heir, except the freer. Blocking by reduction (nuqsan) is passing from a larger share to a smaller one; it touches five heirs: the husband from a half to a quarter by a child or a son's child; the wife from a quarter to an eighth by a child; the mother from a third to a sixth by a child or son's child or two or more siblings; the daughter of a son with a daughter of the body from a half to a sixth to complete two-thirds; and the paternal sister with a full sister from a half to a sixth. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2127}
The barred by an attribute, that is, one who is excluded by a bar, does not block others, either by exclusion or by reduction, according to the majority of jurists including the four imams, because his existence is like his absence. Ibn Mas'ud differs in blocking the spouses and the mother by a child, and in the case of unbelieving siblings, slaves and killers, and Dawud al-Zahiri followed him in the three, while al-Hasan al-Basri, al-Husayn ibn Salih and Ibn Jarir al-Tabari hold it for the killer only. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2127}
## The rules of blocking
The jurists set rules for blocking. The first is that one who connects to the deceased through an heir is excluded when that heir is present, since the nearer has the better claim, the distant being connected only through the near, and whoever stands in place of the source does not inherit while the source exists. This rule holds for the agnates without exception: the father blocks the grandfather, and the full brother blocks the son of a brother. It holds for many holders of fixed shares: the father blocks the grandfather from his share, and the mother blocks the mother's mother. It does not hold in some cases of the holders of fixed shares, such as the uterine siblings with the mother, who inherit with her, and when they are several they reduce her share; and the father and the grandfather block them, though they are not connected through them, because the text limited their inheritance to the case in which there is no source and no descendant. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2126} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2127}
## The second and third rules of blocking
The second rule is that the nearer blocks the more distant when both are entitled by their kind and character. It is wider than the previous rule, since it covers the distant one who connects through a nearer one and the one who does not connect through him at all: a son blocks a son's son though he is not his father, two daughters block a son's daughter from the fixed share, a brother blocks an uncle though the uncle does not connect through him, and a nearer grandmother blocks a more distant one though she does not connect through her. This rule applies alike to agnates and holders of fixed shares. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2128}
The third rule is that the stronger kinship blocks the weaker: a full brother blocks a paternal brother, and a paternal sister does not take a half with a full sister. This holds in every case where the degree is the same and the strength of kinship differs; where the degree is the same, blocking follows the nearer. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2128}
## Awl (increase)
In the lexicon awl means increase; the calculation of the shares "increased" when the sum of the fixed shares exceeds the basic unit by its fractions. In law it is the excess of the shares of the fixed shares over the root of the problem, which reduces each heir's share in proportion; for example a woman who dies leaving a husband, a mother and a full sister, where the husband takes a half, the mother a third and the full sister a half, so the shares exceed what the estate can be divided into. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2127} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2128}
This was the first case of awl in Islam, in the early caliphate of Umar, and the Companions were consulted; he said he did not know which of them God had put forward and which He had put back, since if he began with the husband the sisters would lose their due, and if with the sisters the husband would. Al-'Abbas ibn Abd al-Muttalib advised him, by the well-known report, or Ali ibn Abi Talib or Zayd ibn Thabit in other reports, to adopt awl, and Umar ruled by it. It is related from Ibn Abbas that Umar was the first to apply awl when the shares clashed and pressed on one another; he divided the estate by proportion and entered into each share's due what the awl brought in, and none disagreed until the caliphate passed to Uthman, when Ibn Abbas expressed his disagreement. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2128}
## The reason for awl and the roots of the problems that awl
Those who hold awl argue that the heirs are equal in the cause of entitlement, so each takes his full due if the estate is ample, and if it is short they share it proportionally like creditors; no one's right may be dropped; this is the view of the four imams. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2129} Ibn Abbas argues that the rights in property are not equal, so when the estate falls short the stronger takes precedence: one who moves from a fixed share to another fixed share is a holder of a fixed share in every respect, so he is stronger than one who moves to a share not fixed, who is a fixed-share holder in one respect and a residuary in another; so reducing him is better, since holders of fixed shares come before residuaries. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2129}
By induction, the roots of problems that undergo awl are those whose root is six, twelve, or twenty-four. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2129} A root of six may increase to seven, eight, nine or ten: a husband and two full sisters make seven; a husband, two paternal sisters and a mother make eight; a husband, two full sisters and two uterine brothers make nine; and a husband, a full sister, a paternal sister, two uterine brothers and a mother make ten. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2129} A root of twelve may increase to thirteen, as a wife, a mother and a paternal sister; to fifteen, as a husband, two daughters, a mother and a father; and to seventeen, as a wife, a mother, two paternal sisters and two uterine brothers. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2129} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2130} A root of twenty-four increases only to twenty-seven, as a wife, two daughters, a mother and a father. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2130}
Other roots, namely two, three, four and eight, do not undergo awl: two cannot, since the problem from two is two halves or a half and the rest; three cannot, since the output is a third and the rest, or two-thirds and the rest, or a third and two-thirds; four cannot, since the output is a quarter and the rest, a quarter and a half, or a quarter and a third; and eight cannot, since the output is an eighth and the rest, or an eighth and a half. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2130}
## Return (radd)
Radd in the lexicon means return. In law it is handing back the excess of the fixed shares of the holders of kinship shares to them in proportion to their rights, when no other heir has a claim. Return happens only if two conditions hold: the fixed shares do not exhaust the estate, since if they do nothing remains to return; and there is no agnate, by kinship or cause, on the question of this; if there is an agnate, even a holder of a fixed share such as the father or grandfather, he takes the remainder as a residuary after the fixed share. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2130}
Return is disputed among the Companions, who divided into two camps. One camp holds that the excess returns to the holders of fixed shares, and it is followed by Abu Hanifa and, in the better-known of his two reports, Ahmad, though they differ on whom it returns. Ali held that it returns to the holders of fixed shares in proportion to their shares, except the two spouses, which is the Hanafi view and the sounder Hanbali view; Uthman held that it returns to the two spouses too. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2130}
## The views of Uthman, Ibn Mas'ud and Ibn Abbas
Uthman held that the excess returns to the two spouses as well, since the sheep is for the one who bears the loss: as their shares are reduced by awl, they should be increased by return. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2131} Ibn Mas'ud held that the excess returns to the holders of fixed shares except six: the two spouses, the son's daughter with the daughter of the body, the paternal sister with the full sister, the uterine siblings with the mother, and the grandmother with any fixed-share holder. Another report from Ahmad excepts only the spouses, the uterine siblings with the mother and the grandmother with a fixed-share holder. Ibn Abbas held that it returns to the holders of fixed shares except the two spouses and the grandmother. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2131} Later Shafi'i scholars, from the fifth century on, agreed that the excess returns to the holders of fixed shares and that the blood-kin inherit when the treasury is not in order, that is, when there is no imam at all or when he lacks some of the conditions of office. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2131}
## The arguments of those who hold return
Those who hold return for other than the spouses have several arguments. The first is the verse on the blood-kin in al-Anfal (verse 75): its sense is that some of them are nearer to the inheritance of others by ties of blood, so the blood-kin deserve the whole inheritance by kinship; the spouses are excluded because they have no blood tie. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2131} The second is the report of Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas, who said that only a daughter would inherit from him and asked whether he might will all his property; the Prophet forbade him more than a third, which shows that the daughter takes what exceeds her half by return. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2131} The third is that the Prophet gave a mother who is in a li'an the whole of her child's property, which can only be by return. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2131} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2132} The fourth is that the holders of fixed shares share with the Muslims in Islam and are preferred by kinship; though kinship does not make a residuary, it gives preference, as the mother's kinship gives preference to a full brother over a paternal one, so the remainder returns to them in proportion to their shares, and as the nearer and stronger are disregarded in the root of the problem they are disregarded in return too. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2132}
## The view against return
Another camp holds that nothing returns to any holder of a fixed share. If the fixed shares do not exhaust the estate and no residuary is found, the remainder goes to the treasury, since they do not hold that the blood-kin inherit or that return happens. Zayd ibn Thabit held this, and it was taken by Urwa, al-Zuhri and the imams Malik and al-Shafi'i. Some Malikis restrict the payment to the treasury to a case where the imam is just and spends the money on its lawful purposes; if he is not just, the excess returns to the holders of fixed shares, and if they are absent, to the treasury. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2132} Those who refuse return argue from the verse on inheritance, that the fixed amount stated by the text forbids adding to it, since adding goes beyond the lawful limit; and that the excess over the fixed shares is property with no heir, so it goes to the treasury, since return can be on no ground: not that of the fixed share, nor of residuary status, nor of kinship, since each of these gives precedence to the nearer. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2132}
## The kinds of return cases
Return cases fall into four kinds, according to whether one or several of those who take the return are present, and whether one who does not take the return is present. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2133} If there is one kind of return-taker and no one who does not take return, the root is the number of heads, as with two daughters, two sisters or two grandmothers, and each takes half the estate. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2133} If two or three kinds of return-takers are present, the root is the sum of their shares, as with a grandmother and a uterine sister, or two uterine siblings with a mother. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2133} If a non-return-taker such as a husband or wife is present with return-takers, the non-return-taker's fixed share is taken from the smallest root, and the rest is divided by the number of the return-takers' heads, with correction where the division does not come out even: a husband with three daughters, or a husband with five daughters. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2133} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2134} If several kinds of fixed-share return-takers are present with a non-return-taker, the root is the root of the spouse's share, the spouse takes his share from it, and the rest is divided among the return-takers in proportion to their fixed shares, with the correction as before: a wife, a mother and two uterine brothers, or a wife, a mother and two granddaughters. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2134}
## Dhawu al-arham, the blood-kin without fixed shares
Rahm in the lexicon is the womb and kinship, and in the language of the scholars of inheritance it means every relative who is neither a holder of a fixed share fixed in the Qur'an, the Sunna or consensus, nor an agnate who takes the whole estate when alone. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2134} The Companions and later jurists disagreed on whether the blood-kin inherit. Those who gave them inheritance among the Companions include Ali, Ibn Mas'ud and, in the best-known report, Ibn Abbas, with Mu'adh ibn Jabal, Abu al-Darda' and Abu Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah; among the Successors, Shurayh, al-Hasan, Ibn Sirin, Ata' and Mujahid. Those who denied it include Zayd ibn Thabit, a report from Ibn Abbas, Sa'id ibn al-Musayyib and Sa'id ibn Jubayr; the denial is attributed to Abu Bakr, Umar and Uthman, but that is not sound, and Abu Hazim the judge told al-Mu'tadid that the Companions other than Zayd agreed on their inheritance. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2134} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2135}
The least that can be said is that giving them inheritance is an addition to God's book, which is not established by a single report or by analogy; and there is the report that the Prophet was asked about the inheritance of the paternal and maternal aunts and said that Gabriel told him there is no inheritance for them. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2135}
The proponents rely on the verse of al-Anfal (verse 75) that some of the blood-kin are nearer to others: it establishes their entitlement by the general attribute of kinship when the particular attribute of fixed share or agnation is absent, and there is no conflict between the two. They also rely on the report that God and His Messenger are the patron of him who has no patron, and that the maternal uncle inherits from him who has no heir. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.2135}
