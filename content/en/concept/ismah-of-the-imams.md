## Summary
Twelver Imami Shi'ism requires infallibility ('ismah) in the Imam, meaning that the Imam commits neither minor nor major sins, does not become inattentive or forget, and does not err in interpretation. Al-Sallabi presents this doctrine from Shi'i books and then responds to it from a Sunni perspective; in his presentation, he describes what the Shi'a say without passing judgment on its followers. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 244}

## The Status of the Imamate in Their View
The author notes that for the Shi'a, the Imamate is not among the secondary principles in which ignorance is excused, as Sunnis maintain, but is the foundation of religion; he cites al-Kafi to the effect that Islam is built on five pillars, including allegiance to the Imams (walayah), and that people "took four and left this one." He notes that al-Majlisi said walayah is superior to all bodily acts of worship, and that al-Muzaffar said the Imamate is a foundational principle without belief in which faith is incomplete. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 236}

The author notes that Ibn Babawayh al-Qummi equated denying 'Ali's Imamate with denying the prophethood of all the prophets, that Yusuf al-Bahrani said disbelief in the Imams is no less than disbelief in God and His Messenger, and that al-Mufid reported consensus on declaring anyone who denies any of the Imams an unbeliever. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 237} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 238}

## Infallibility as Defined by Its Proponents
The author notes that al-Mufid reported consensus that the Imams, who stand in the place of the prophets in implementing rulings, are infallible like the prophets, incapable of major or minor sins or inattentiveness in anything concerning religion. He quotes al-Majlisi as saying that the Imamiyyah agreed on the Imams' infallibility from sins, "neither intentionally, nor through inattentiveness, nor through error in interpretation." {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 244} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 245}

He notes that Ibn al-Mutahhar al-Hilli said the Imamiyyah and the Isma'iliyyah affirmed the infallibility of the Imam while all other sects opposed them in this, and that al-Muzaffar, al-Zanjani, al-Bahrani, and al-'Askari affirmed the same infallibility. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 244} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 245}

## Contradiction of Narrations on Infallibility
The author presents what he sees as a contradiction within Shi'i books themselves: al-Majlisi states that the issue is "extremely problematic" because many reports and verses indicate that inattentiveness occurred from them, while the companions of the school unanimously rejected its possibility. The author sees in this an admission that the consensus of later scholars contradicts their own narrations. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 245} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 246}

The author quotes Shi'i sources to the effect that denying inattentiveness in the Imams was initially the belief of an obscure group in Kufa, then evolved to become, according to al-Mamaqani, one of the essentials of the school, and that Muhsin al-Amin declared anyone who denies an essential to be an unbeliever according to them—meaning, in the author's view, that later scholars declare earlier ones unbelievers. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 246}

The author holds that discrepancies in the actions of the Imams led some Shi'a to leave Shi'ism: after Karbala, some companions of al-Husayn said that al-Hasan's action in making peace with Mu'awiyah and al-Husayn's action in fighting could not both be right, so they doubted their Imamate and adopted the view of the general Muslim public. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 259}

The author notes that al-Tusi acknowledged that Shi'i reports are so contradictory that there is scarcely a report without an opposing one alongside it, and that his attempt to explain them as taqiyyah only compounded the difficulty. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 259}

## Qur'anic Arguments and the Response to Them
The author notes that the Shi'a cited God's words regarding the covenant of leadership, interpreting it to mean that a wrongdoer cannot attain the covenant and thus only the infallible is worthy of it. He presents his response: scholars differed on the meaning of the covenant—some saying prophethood, others leadership in the sense of emulating the righteous—and negating wrongdoing does not establish infallibility, nor is one who committed polytheism and then repented described as a wrongdoer. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 247} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 248}

The author presents the argument based on the Verse of Purification and the Hadith of the Cloak, arguing that the address in the preceding and following verses is directed to the Prophet's wives, that the masculine plural in "from you" and "purify you" is used by predominance for the Household, that the divine will in the verse is legislative rather than predestinative, and that God's purification of believers generally in other verses does not entail their infallibility. He also mentions the hadith of Umm Salamah regarding her inclusion under the cloak after the supplication. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 250} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 253} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 254}

## From 'Ali's Statements in Nahj al-Balaghah
The author notes that 'Ali said in Nahj al-Balaghah, "I do not consider myself above making a mistake, nor am I safe from that in my actions," and that he told people they needed a ruler, whether righteous or wicked, without stipulating infallibility in the ruler. He holds that the seeking of forgiveness by 'Ali and his sons in their supplications recorded in Nahj al-Balaghah contradicts infallibility, and that Shi'i scholars were perplexed in explaining them. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 257} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 258}

## The Author's Critique of Infallibility
The author argues that claiming infallibility for the Imams rivals prophethood, because an infallible person must be obeyed in everything he says, whereas God commanded believers to say, "We believe in what was revealed to us and what was given to the prophets," that the community is protected from error through the Book of its Lord and the Sunnah of its Prophet, and that disputes are referred back to God and the Messenger, not to an Imam. He contends that the community under the caliphate of the first three caliphs enjoyed greater benefit and divine grace than during 'Ali's caliphate in the time of civil strife, and that the Imams of the Prophet's Household after 'Ali held no political authority through which the divine grace claimed by the Shi'a could be realized. {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 256} {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 257}

## Impact of the Doctrine Today
The author states that infallibility has influenced Shi'i practice through what is attributed to the Imams, through excessive veneration of graves and shrines, through placing the Shi'i jurist (mujtahid) in a position where rejecting him is equated with rejecting God, and through basing belief on attributing the religion to the Imams. This paragraph summarizes the author's description of the doctrine's real-world impact, not a judgment by the museum {cite:sallabi-ali-vol2 p. 260}.
