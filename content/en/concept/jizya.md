## Overview
In al-Baladhuri, the *jizya* is what the People of the Book pay on their heads under the terms of a treaty, in return for being left to their religion and their land. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407} The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, defines the *jizya* as the poll-tax which, in traditional Muslim law, is levied on non-Muslims in Muslim states. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 586}

## The first to pay it
He relates from al-Zuhri that, as far as is known, the first of the People of the Book to pay the *jizya* were the people of [[place/najran|Najran]], who were Christians. Then the people of Aylah, Adhruh, and Adhri'at paid the *jizya* during the expedition of Tabuk. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p. 79-80}

## Forms of the settlement
One of its forms, in his account, was a dinar on every adult male each year. The people of Aylah made peace for three hundred dinars; the people of Jarash made peace for a dinar per adult male, together with the hospitality of the Muslims; and the people of Adhruh made peace for a hundred dinars each Rajab. The Jews of Muqanna made peace for a quarter of the hunt of the 'Uruk, a quarter of the women's spinning, and a quarter of the fruits, and after that they owed neither *jizya* nor forced labour. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p. 79-80}

## Najran in the time of Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz
When [[person/umar-ibn-abd-al-aziz|Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz]] came to power, he ordered that the people of Najran be counted, and found them at a tenth of their earlier number. He said that he regarded this settlement as a *jizya* on their heads, not a treaty over their land, and that the *jizya* on the dead and on Muslims was dropped. He therefore imposed on them two hundred garments. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p. 91-92}

## Syria
Al-Baladhuri states, citing al-Awza'i, that at the beginning in Syria the *jizya* was a *jarib* (measure of grain) and a dinar on each head. He then reports accounts of Umar ibn al-Khattab's assessment of it by the classes of people. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p. 170-171}

See [[concept/kharaj|the land tax]].

## The Encyclopaedia of Islam: origins
The second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam calls the history of the origins of the *jizya* extremely complex, for three reasons. First, the writers of the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid]] period who gathered the material met texts in which *jizya* and *kharaj* carry different meanings, sometimes wide and sometimes technical, and tended to read them in the sense current in their own day. Second, the system that grew out of the Arab conquests was a series of individual, non-identical agreements or decisions. Third, it followed earlier systems without overthrowing them, and those are imperfectly understood. The article offers its account only as a provisional guide. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 586}

It says the word, perhaps connected with an Aramaic original, occurs in a verse of the ninth sura, where it is already applied to dues asked of Christians and Jews, probably in the loose sense of "compensation" for not adopting Islam and as a collective tribute not set apart from other taxes. Precedents may exist in pre-Islamic Arabia, where oases submitted to stronger tribal groups in return for protection. For the conquerors, the article says, the tax was material proof of the subjection of the [[concept/ahl-al-dhimma|dhimmis]]; for the inhabitants it continued what they had paid to earlier regimes. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 586}

## One tax or two: the scholarly debate
The article states that Abbasid-period texts show a clear theoretical distinction between the *kharaj*, a tax on land that could not be abolished except in particular cases because the land had been conquered once for the permanent Muslim community, and the *jizya*, a tax on persons that ended if the taxpayer became Muslim. In the first century of Islam, it stresses, this distinction was far from always made. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 586}

It then reports a dispute. Wellhausen, followed by Becker, Caetani and others, held that the Arabs at first levied a collective tribute without distinguishing its sources, and that only the many conversions at the end of Umayyad rule, particularly in [[place/khurasan|Khurasan]], led to a distinction between a tax on the person and a tax on land. The article notes that this theory contradicts all the classical jurists and has been severely breached by Løkkegaard and still more by Dennett, whose conclusions it finds no longer refutable in general lines, though they leave problems unanswered. They showed that the texts often distinguish the two taxes even when the terms vary, and Dennett argued that no uniform system existed straight after the conquest. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 586} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 587}

## Systems before the conquests
The article describes a Sasanian system with a general land tax from which the aristocracy was exempt. The Byzantine system was more complex and in many points uncertain: a personal tax existed but was scarcely used except for colonists and non-Christians, and the general tax made no distinction between persons and land. On large autonomous estates it was apparently reckoned by the number of workers, which made it proportional to population, yet the article says it was not a specific poll-tax, since it was not added to a separate land tax. It asks that this point be kept in mind for what followed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 587}

## The conquests, region by region
Some conquests were by force, leaving the system to the conqueror's discretion, others by capitulation treaties that might stipulate a fiscal system or fix a sum. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 587}

In [[place/iraq|Iraq]], conquered in general by force, the Arabs with native subordinates kept the traditional pattern: a poll-tax distinct from the land tax, at a rate probably raised to 1, 2 and 4 dinars, which the article equates with 12, 24 and 48 dirhams, graded by wealth. Aristocrats declared allegiance to Islam to be exempt from it. In most towns of [[place/syria|Syria]] and Upper Mesopotamia the occupation was by treaty, with the tax usually calculated, as at [[place/al-hira|al-Hira]], on a fixed contribution of generally one dinar per head. In Egypt most Christian communities paid in kind, a land tax of one dinar per *faddan* and a poll-tax of two dinars per head; the article says that, contrary to earlier belief, this poll-tax must have burdened the mass of people almost as heavily as the land tax. In most of Iran and central Asia local rulers were kept in office and paid a fixed tribute, and in Khurasan taxpayers continued under the Sasanian dual system of land tax and poll-tax. The article sums up that the duality existed at the taxpayer's level for most peasants, while a unitary contribution prevailed in most Syrian towns and in Upper Mesopotamia, and that the conquerors held aloof from these distinctions so long as the tribute was paid. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 587}

## Evasion, conversion and the Umayyad response
In Egypt monks were exempt from the poll-tax, and the article says taxpayers could escape it by entering a monastery, so all monks were made liable in turn and movement required authorisation, with taxpayers marked by seals and passports. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 587} In Iraq evasion took the form of conversion to Islam. At first the convert left his land to a non-convert who guaranteed its tax, which the administration did not look on amiss; when [[person/al-hajjaj-ibn-yusuf|al-Hajjaj]] came, the article reports, he sent the peasants back to the land, taxed them again, poll-tax included, and in practice forbade conversion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 587} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 588} In Khurasan the native aristocracy, who had to make good any shortfall, persecuted converted peasants and sought to burden poorer Muslims more heavily than non-Muslims, which the article calls inequality in reverse. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 588}

The article judges that such repression could not last in a Muslim state. Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz is credited with encouraging conversion by remitting all taxes on non-Muslims, but it says the most authoritative texts, citing Gibb, do not confirm so utopian a view. It suggests instead that, under the jurists who elaborated the doctrine of [[concept/fay|fay']], the *kharaj* came to be regarded as a charge on land compatible with Muslim status, and the poll-tax as such was to disappear, the treasury not necessarily losing since the convert owed [[concept/zakat|zakat]]. It adds that [[person/nasr-ibn-sayyar|Nasr ibn Sayyar]] later tried this in Khurasan, and that in Syria, from the time of Yazid and [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik]], the poll-tax seems to have been given a truly personal character beside the land tax. Once the two were distinguished in assessment, the collective liability of places ceased to apply to the poll-tax, and the administrative word for "fugitives," *djawali*, became a synonym of *jizya* in that sense. The peasant emigration problem was not solved, and peasants were still sometimes forced back. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 588}

## Terms and rules from the Abbasid period
In the Abbasid period, according to the article, terminology and institutions became specialised, at least in technical writing and works of law: *kharaj* meant only the land tax and *jizya* only the poll-tax on dhimmis. It lost financial importance when non-Muslims ceased to be the majority, and was not uniform. Syria-Palestine and Egypt kept their own system until the eighteenth century despite the theorists, among whom it names [[person/al-baladhuri|al-Baladhuri]] but excludes [[person/malik-ibn-anas|Malik]] and [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafii]], while the graded system attributed to Umar continued in the East and later passed into the non-Arab provinces of the [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottoman]] empire. The many dhimmis of Saljuq Asia Minor, under the [[polity/rum-seljuk-sultanate|Seljuks of Rum]], gave the tax great importance again. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 588}

The article lists rules it calls generally valid from then on. The tax falls only on males who are adult, free, capable and able-bodied, so children, old men, women, invalids, slaves, beggars, the sick and the mentally deranged are excluded. Foreigners are exempt if they do not settle permanently, and frontier peoples enrolled in military expeditions were released for the year. It was a fixed personal sum collected by lunar years, sometimes in Ramadan under the [[polity/mamluk-sultanate|Mamluks]], so that it could be separated from agricultural taxes in tax-farming and *iqta* grants. Money was stipulated, but payment in kind was admitted under an official scale. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 588}

On the Qur'anic wording of payment, long read as "by hand" and in person, the article says this may be wrong, citing Rosenthal and Cahen; administratively it suggests a need to count the non-Muslim population, hence the ban on village notables paying a lump sum for their dependants. It reads the verse's phrase about being humbled in connection with notables or Arabs who, though Christians, refused to pay, rather than as implying the humiliating procedure that, in its words, later rigorists claimed to find in it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 588} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 589} As an example of use of figures, an estimate of 130,000 dinars for the Egyptian yield under [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Saladin]], at an average of two dinars, suggests about 65,000 Christian heads of families. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 589}

## Use, variation and decline
In principle the *jizya*, like zakat, was for pensions, salaries and charities, but it often went to the prince's treasury. The article says Malik and al-Shafii admit that the rate could be raised, and arbitrary demands appeared in difficult or religiously strict periods, yet the scarcity of gold and currency devaluation often brought it below the doctrinal level. Where the [[polity/mongol-empire|Mongols]] ruled directly, before their conversion, the poll-tax on non-Muslims was abolished; afterwards zealous agents sought forty years of arrears from Christians, in one case the article cites. In Sicily, after the Norman conquest, the poll-tax on Muslims and Jews was called *jizya*. The article concludes that it has disappeared from modern Muslim states through the growing equality of religions, military service and new fiscal systems. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 589}

## Ottoman practice
By the tenth/sixteenth century the Ottoman documents preferred *kharaj* to *jizya*, later *cizye*, and the collector was a *kharajji*, later a *cizyedar*. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 589} The Ottomans identified some earlier poll-taxes with it; in Hungary they accepted the old tax of one gold florin per family. Regarded as a religious tax, it was usually collected directly for the state treasury, rarely granted as a *timar*, and its revenue went mostly to military purposes; [[person/mahmud-ii-uthmani|Mahmud II]] assigned it to his reformed army. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 589} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 590} After a conquest the judge drew up a register of those liable; censuses were renewed only after long intervals. Under [[person/mehmed-ii|Mehmed II]] a village's fugitives' share was split between the *timar* holder and the remaining payers, and the reform of 1102/1691 made each payer answerable only for himself, certified by a receipt. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 590}

Clerics and monks seem to have been exempt at first, but in 1691 all clerics except the truly disabled became liable; a petition of 1103/1692 for retired monks was rejected on the strength of a different opinion of Imam Yusuf. The government, following the sharia, always exempted children, women, blind men and the unemployed poor. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 590}

The law books distinguished a *jizya* fixed by agreement, whose amount could not change, called *maktu*, from one levied on individuals. A Christian vassal prince paid an agreed yearly tribute, and some dhimmi communities under direct rule were allowed a fixed sum, mostly to escape collectors' abuses; the article gives the mountain tribes of Klementi in [[place/albania|Albania]], who paid a nominal thousand akce in 902/1497 for guarding a highway. The Jewish community of [[place/safad|Safad]] used it to spare its clerics. As central control weakened, Christian notables took over collection, which the article links to their rise as a local aristocracy in the [[place/balkans|Balkans]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 590} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 591}

Each year the sultan announced the rates on a fatwa of the Shaykh al-Islam by the sharia scale of three grades, paying 48, 24 and 12 dirhams of pure silver, or four, two and one gold dinars. The silver rates rose between 1102/1691 and 1249/1834; Mahmud II stressed that the increases adjusted the fixed amounts to the currency of the day and were not new taxes, yet they caused widespread discontent among dhimmis. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 591} In some areas one rate applied to all classes, 80 akce in lands taken from the Mamluks, which the article sees as a survival of late Mamluk practice. Exemption was in principle a waste of treasury revenue and was granted exceptionally, mostly for military service, for example to Christian soldiers called voynuks, martolos and eflaks. Border provinces paid the lowest rate, and in 1170/1757 the dhimmis of twenty-one villages in [[place/chios|Chios]] producing mastic all paid at the lowest rate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 591} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 592}

## For Scholars
Jizya in Islamic fiqh is what the People of the Book pay per head under treaty and dhimma, in exchange for remaining on their religion and land under protection — al-Baladhuri records its first collection from the Christians of Najran and the peoples of Ayla, Adhruh and Adhra'at at Tabuk. The schools differ on its amount, on whom it falls and how it is taken: adult free able-bodied men of the People of the Book — not women, children, monks or the poor on the common view; the Hanafis grade it across rich, middling and poor, the Shafi'is set a dinar per head; some allow it on the Zoroastrians and differ on the Arab polytheists. Jurists agree the contract rests on the dhimmi's security — protection of life, property and worship in churches and synagogues under rules — and historians place its emergence inside the fay' arrangements and earlier Byzantine and Sasanian treaties; it is debated today in the fiqh of minorities and citizenship.

## In Ragheb El-Sergani's programme What Did Muslims Bring to the World
At the end of the twelfth episode [[person/ragheb-al-sergani|Ragheb El-Sergani]] says that there is no state, old or new, whose inhabitants pay no tax, that the jizya paid by minorities was lower than the zakat paid by Muslims, and that the one who paid it took no part in fighting but was defended by the Muslims, and if he took part in defending the state it was lifted from him. {cite:assabile-ragheb-el-sergani what-did-muslims-bring-to-the-world/ep12 00:23:00} {cite:assabile-ragheb-el-sergani what-did-muslims-bring-to-the-world/ep12 00:23:30} He lists those on whom it does not fall: the woman, the boy even if rich, the poor man, the monk, the disabled and the very old, whereas a Muslim with wealth pays zakat whoever he is. {cite:assabile-ragheb-el-sergani what-did-muslims-bring-to-the-world/ep12 00:24:00} {cite:assabile-ragheb-el-sergani what-did-muslims-bring-to-the-world/ep12 00:24:30} He concludes that there is no injustice in the jizya. {cite:assabile-ragheb-el-sergani what-did-muslims-bring-to-the-world/ep12 00:25:30}

## The jizya in Abu Yusuf's Kitab al-Kharaj

[[person/abu-yusuf-al-qadi|Abu Yusuf]] states in [[work/kitab-al-kharaj-abu-yusuf|Kitab al-Kharaj]] that the jizya is taken from Jews, Christians, Magians, Sabians and Samaritans, and that the governor may not exempt or excuse any of them, since their lives and property are secured by paying it; for him it stands on the same footing as land-tax revenue. {cite:kitab-al-kharaj-salafiyya-1392 p.135} He sets it in tiers: forty-eight dirhams for the well-off, such as money-changers, cloth merchants, traders and physicians; twenty-four for the middle; and twelve for those who work with their hands, such as tailors, dyers and cobblers. {cite:kitab-al-kharaj-salafiyya-1392 p.135} {cite:kitab-al-kharaj-salafiyya-1392 p.136} For him there is no zakat on the livestock or property of the protected peoples except on goods they move in trade, which pay half a tenth. {cite:kitab-al-kharaj-salafiyya-1392 p.135}

## How it is collected, and gentleness

Abu Yusuf says that none of the protected peoples is to be beaten in collecting the jizya, nor made to stand in the sun or elsewhere, nor subjected to bodily hardship; they are to be treated gently and held until they pay. {cite:kitab-al-kharaj-salafiyya-1392 p.135} He advises the caliph to entrust its collection in the cities to a righteous and trustworthy man, and to instruct his officials to take nothing from those not liable, to intend no injustice or harshness, and not to accept a village headman's composition for less than is due. {cite:kitab-al-kharaj-salafiyya-1392 p.135} {cite:kitab-al-kharaj-salafiyya-1392 p.136} On gentleness he cites the hadith "whoever wrongs a covenanted person or burdens him beyond his capacity, I shall be his adversary", Umar's dying charge that the protected peoples be dealt with faithfully, defended and not burdened beyond their capacity, and the objections of Sa'id ibn Zayd, Hisham ibn Hakim and Umar to officials who made protected people stand in the sun over the jizya. {cite:kitab-al-kharaj-salafiyya-1392 p.137} {cite:kitab-al-kharaj-salafiyya-1392 p.138} He relates that Umar saw an old blind Jew begging, asked him why, and was told "the jizya, need and age"; Umar took him home, gave him something, and sent word to the treasurer: "look after this man and those like him; by God we have not dealt fairly with him if we consumed his youth and then abandon him in old age", and lifted the jizya from him and his like. {cite:kitab-al-kharaj-salafiyya-1392 p.138}
