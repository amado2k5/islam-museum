## Detail by kinds and the trick at despair
The venesection of the vein under the tongue is not delayed but hastened to, even that day, above all when the veins under the tongue are stretched; and the jugular may be needed, and scarifying the tongue itself, and cupping the shank — very beneficial; and whoever is accustomed by the quinsies must be bled before they arise, and in spring. Of the strongest benefit is hastening to the very strong clysters and the suppository-forms; the limbs are bound with what he described; their food is kept to the third day on the stated drinks, then graduated to barley-water, then egg-marrow, and once swallowing eases the broths are used. If swallowing grows hard, cupping-glasses are set on the neck by sucking or by fire so the passage widens little by little, and once done they are removed; and it is not wrong to introduce a wrapped cleaner with cotton on it, and a worked tube of gold or silver may be introduced into the throat helping the breath, and pressing the shoulders strongly may help (all carried as the book's descriptions, not procedures). As for the drugs at the start, the astringents — and of what experience brought forth, the mixed compound astringents benefit more than the simple single (a saying ascribed to experience on the book's wording). In the bilious, most of the venesection is spent toward cooling with astringency, and in it smears, gargles, blower-insufflations and dustings of what he described are used; at the true end one seeks full cleansing and bursting by what he described. For the phlegmatic: a greased bent rod wrapped with rags is introduced into the throat, the tumour painted with it and the moisture cleared by it, and he is purged and clystered with the strong clysters (the book's description, not a procedure). For the melancholic the most beneficial are the drugs having a specific property agreeable at every time — of what he described: the white dog's dung, man's dung especially the boy's, swallows' burnt droppings, the ox's bile and the turtle's bile, the salted little-fish heads, and the lard of pig, ape and hyena and the boy's ordure among what he described (animal and human matters carried as the book names them, set before the founder). The choked man's tongue may also swell — after venesection the matters are drawn downward by what he described. For the vertebral kind, what avails is a trick of pressing the place gently backward so the vertebra may return — the pressing by a tool like a bit introduced into the throat or by the finger; and pressing is very harmful in the tumours. And if the quinsies grow severe, the drugs fail and destruction is certain, what deliverance is hoped by is the slitting of the windpipe: the ligaments between two of the windpipe's rings are slit without touching the cartilage so he breathes through it, then it is sewn when the tumour's regimen is done (carried as the book's description, not a procedure). And the one delivered from a severe quinsy must be bled, clystered and made to sip sips of what he described for days {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.292}
