## Overview
Zaydan Qa'dat begins his book with a critique of materialist definitions of economics, arguing that they reduce it to a study of wealth alone without regard for its purpose or its moral means {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 9}.

## The Definitions He Quotes
The author presents definitions from a number of prominent economists: for Adam Smith, economics is the study of the means by which a nation becomes materially wealthy; for John Stuart Mill, it is the science of the laws of society arising from people's labor in producing wealth; for Alfred Marshall, it is the study of mankind in ordinary life; for Wicksteed, it is the study of the general principles of administering resources; and for Wicksell, it is every serious effort to satisfy a material need {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 9}.

## The Author's Critique
The author argues that these definitions agree on the materialist principle, make no mention of the kind of means that lead to the ends, and virtually ignore the moral dimension, implying permissiveness toward economic behavior of any kind {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 9}{cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 10}. He cites the definition of Reuter, who criticizes materialist definitions, and then turns to criticize Reuter's own definition because it pays no attention to the legitimacy or nature of needs {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 11}.

## The Place of the Definition in the Book
The author treats this critique as an introduction to the "moral economy" on which his book is based, for economics is inseparable from values, being rather a balance between individuals' behavior and material alternatives {cite:qadan-manhaj-iqtisad-quran p. 10}. The definitions quoted here represent the views of their authors as presented by the author, and have not been checked against their original sources.
