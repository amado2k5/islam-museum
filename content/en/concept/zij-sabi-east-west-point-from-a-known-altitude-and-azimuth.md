## Summary
When the Sun's place is known, no observation before and after noon is needed: al-Battani observes a chosen altitude at some time of day and marks the middle of the width of the rod's shadow on the circumference. He divides the quadrant containing that point into ninety parts and counts from the point, away from the direction of the altitude's azimuth, as many parts as the azimuth, and the count ends at the east or the west point. If the horizon is clear at sunrise or sunset the point is known from the azimuth of the rising of the Sun's degree. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.40}.

## Observing the altitude and counting the azimuth on the quadrant
He says: when the Sun's place is known to you, find the azimuth of the altitudes you want at some time of day and observe the chosen altitude; when the altitude is reached, mark on the circumference the middle of the width of the rod's shadow. Then divide the quadrant of the circle in which that point falls into ninety parts, and count from the point, away from the direction of that altitude's azimuth, as many as the azimuth; where the count falls is the point of east or west according to the time you worked in, before midday or after. The azimuth is the one computed in chapter 11. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.40}.

## A clear horizon at rising and setting
He then gives the way to the two lines: from that mark draw a line through the circle's centre and quarter the circle on it with another line through the centre at right angles; you then know the meridian line from that line, and the east-west line from the term before it. Likewise, if the circle has a clear horizon at sunrise or sunset, the east or west point is known from the azimuth of the rising or setting of the Sun's degree on the horizon circle. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.40}.
