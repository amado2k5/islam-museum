## Epilepsy of children
Children's epilepsy is treated by setting right the nurse's food, made to incline to a gentle heat with good chyme; the nurse avoids everything that generates watery, corrupt or thick milk (what the book says the nurse is to avoid belongs to the womb section, a labelled listing held for the founder's ruling). The child is to be guarded from everything with sudden fright or disturbance — great sounds and loud blasts like the drum, trumpet, thunder, bells and criers' shouting — and kept from sleeplessness, anger, fear, severe cold and heat and bad digestion; he is given gentle exercise before food and forbidden motion after it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.126}

## Food, drink and sleep
All epileptics use wholesome foods of good, not excessive, moistening; they guard against fullness and beware bad digestion by stopping short of complete satiety; one not used to a single meal divides his food — which is below his fill — into three parts, taking a third at midday and two thirds at night after gentle exercise. They do not take much wine, for it fills the brain strongly; if some drink is unavoidable, a little, aged and clarified; the most harmful things to them are drinking right after bathing and sudden cold; they guard the head from meeting excessive heat or cold and do not linger in the bath. The epileptic avoids thick heavy foods and the constipating, narcotic and vaporous ones (the kinds of meat, greens and fruits detailed in the book are not carried). Fullness of drink is very harmful, but a little rouses the soul, strengthens the pneuma, quickens it and spares drinking much water — drinking much of it being the most harmful thing. A long midday sleep, and much sleep generally, is harmful, especially on much fullness, while excessive sleeplessness weakens and dissolves the pneuma and yet fills the brain with vapours {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.126} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.127}

## Exercise and drawing the matter downward
The first rule of epilepsy's regimen is avoiding the provoking causes mentioned; stillness and calm suit it best. If exercise is needed after evacuation and purification of the body, it is taken not on a full stomach, an exercise not reaching exhaustion, with rest after it; he is to keep his head upright, not lower it as far as possible and not move it much lest it draw the materials to it; he moves the lower parts when moving the upper. Among what draws the matter down is rubbing the body gradually from top to bottom, beginning at the chest and what follows, rubbing it with rough cloths until it reddens, then descending by degrees to the leg, each round stronger than the one before, the head kept upright throughout; then he is set to walk and rested in a place until his breath returns and his disturbance calms; when all the materials have been drawn down, only then may his head be rubbed and combed to warm it and change its temperament {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.127} {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.128}

## At the time of the fit, and evacuation
They begin by evacuating the matter by containing it, then aim at purifying the head with what draws; if it seizes him in recurring fits or comes often with abundance of humours, evacuation is done in spring for reinforcement, letting out the humour that prevails over him. If nothing bars venesection he is bled in spring as the book states — the venesection, cupping and cautery sites mentioned are not carried. When the time of the fit comes, the book records putting in what keeps the mouth open and placing what comes between the teeth (the details of the means are not carried) {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v2 p.128}
