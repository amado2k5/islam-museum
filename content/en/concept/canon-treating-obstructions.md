## Summary
In chapter 24 Ibn Sina says obstructions come from thick, viscous or abundant humours, distinguishes dissolving, cutting and evacuating in treating each, and warns against the two errors of weak and of violent dissolving {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.303}.

## Three causes
Obstructions come from thick humours, from viscous humours or from abundant humours. When the humours are abundant and nothing else is joined to them, removing them by venesection and purging is enough; if they are thick, dissolving and cleansing drugs are needed; if viscous, cutting drugs {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.303}.

## Thick and viscous
The difference between the thick and the viscous is the difference between clay and dissolved glue. The thick needs a dissolver to thin it so that it is driven out easily; the viscous needs a cutter to come between it and what it has stuck to and free it, and to cut its parts into small pieces, since the viscous blocks by sticking and by the clinging of its parts {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.303}.

## Two errors in dissolving
In dissolving the thick, two opposite things must be guarded against: weak dissolving, which enlarges the matter without dissolving it so that the obstruction grows, and violent, strong dissolving, with which the fine part dissolves and the coarse part hardens. When strong dissolving is needed, it is followed by gentle softening with a substance holding no thickness and with a moderate heat {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.303}.

## The hardest obstructions
The hardest obstructions are those of the veins, harder still those of the arteries, and hardest those in the principal organs. When openers combine astringency and fineness they suit better {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.303}.
