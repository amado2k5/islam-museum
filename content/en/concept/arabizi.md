## Summary
Arabizi is an informal Latin-letter writing of colloquial Arabic, and its name blends Arabi, Arabic, with Inglizi, English, as a research file supplied to the museum presents it. This page shows what it is and how it writes the sounds the Latin alphabet lacks, when it arose, and what the academic references say of its spread. The museum did not open the pages cited, so what is here is as the summary gave it.
## What it is
The summary describes it as an informal Latin-letter system for colloquial Arabic on instant messaging, SMS and social media, using Latin letters together with numerals (arithmographemes) to represent Arabic sounds that the Latin alphabet lacks: 2 for the hamza, 3 for the ayn, 7 for the ha and 9 for the sad or qaf {cite:awej-tls-arabizi-12-1-pdf} {cite:ala-journals-uni-lj-3arabizi-69}. The numerals are chosen for shapes that resemble the Arabic letter: 3 looks like an ayn, and 7 like a ha.
## When it began
The summary says Arab internet and mobile users developed it in the late 1990s and early 2000s in the ASCII era, when computers, chat rooms and early mobile phones lacked native Arabic keyboards or reliable rendering of Arabic script {cite:jlls-arabizi-3962} {cite:researchgate-yaghan-2008-arabizi}. By the summary it was a solution to a technical problem before it became a social habit, and its history is tied to that of [[concept/lawhat-al-mafatih-al-arabiyya|the Arabic keyboard]].
## Academic references on its spread
The summary points to David Palfreyman and Muhamed al-Khalil (2003), "A Funky Language for Teenzz to Use": Representing Gulf Arabic in Instant Messaging, in the Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication 9(1), with Mark Warschauer, Ghada R. El Said and Ayman Zohry (2002) in the same journal 7(4), and Mohammad Ali Yaghan (2008) {cite:cerj-hajir-rasman-mcinerney-2022-pdf} {cite:ala-journals-uni-lj-3arabizi-69} {cite:researchgate-yaghan-2008-arabizi}. These show, in the summary's account, that Arabizi spread from an ASCII workaround into a widespread peer code among the young.
## Why it stayed and spread
The summary adds that English-medium schooling and bilingual code-switching reinforced it, and that its social function as an informal marker of group solidarity kept it {cite:awej-tls-arabizi-12-1-pdf}. That is, the first technical reason vanished with the availability of Arabic keyboards, and the social reason remained.
## The limits of what the summary says
The summary gives no full table of letters and numerals, no figure for how many use it, no separation of the dialects Palfreyman's study covered from others, and does not show whether 2, 3, 7 and 9 are the whole list. This page takes what it says and adds no numbers of its own.
## Its relation to the Arabic script
Arabizi is not a replacement for the Arabic script in formal writing, only a writing of colloquial speech in informal messages, which differs from languages that exchanged their script, as in [[concept/al-lughat-al-maktuba-bil-harf-al-arabi|languages written in the Arabic script]]. Arabic itself has its record at [[language/arabic|Arabic]].
## The numerals the summary names
| Numeral | Arabic sound |
|---|---|
| 2 | The hamza |
| 3 | The ayn |
| 7 | The ha |
| 9 | The sad or qaf |

These four are what the summary gave as examples of what represents sounds the Latin alphabet lacks, and it called them arithmographemes, a word that shows that the numeral stands in for a letter in writing, not for a number in counting.
## Which reference said what
The AWEJ for Translation and Literary Studies article is the one the summary uses for the description, the numerals, the role of English-medium schooling and code-switching, and the solidarity function; the Ljubljana ALA article for the description, the numerals and the Palfreyman study; the JLLS article for its birth in the ASCII era; Yaghan's study on ResearchGate for that birth also and the slang-like usage; and the Cambridge educational journal for the paper by Hajir, Rasman and McInerney, which carries a reference to Palfreyman's study.
