## Overview
Intertwined plant ornament that became a hallmark of Islamic art. It flows across plaster, stone, wood, manuscripts, mosques and palaces. {cite:wikidata} The Encyclopaedia of Islam, in its article on the arabesque, gives the word a narrow meaning: a stylised, non-naturalistic vegetal design built from leaves that grow out of stalks. The article notes that for a long time art literature used the term more loosely, for several kinds of typical Islamic ornament, vegetal, calligraphic and even figural, and that the first edition of the encyclopaedia, in an entry by Herzfeld, still took this wider view. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 583}

## Definition and principles
The article follows the definition set out by the art historian Alois Riegl in his *Stilfragen*: the arabesque is a particular and exclusively Islamic form of denaturalised vegetal ornament, made of shoots of split or bifurcated leaves on inorganic tendrils. The leaves may be flat or curved, pointed, round or rolled, smooth or rough, feathered or pierced, but they are never isolated and always remain joined to the stalk, as an addition or a terminal. The stalk itself may be undulating, spiral or interlaced, passing through the leaf or issuing again from it. The article quotes Herzfeld's wording that stalk and leaf are completely grown into each other. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 583}

Several principles govern the design: reciprocal repetition, the formation of palmette or calyx forms from pairs of split leaves, and the insertion of geometric interlacings, medallions or cartouche compartments. Two aesthetic rules are said to be scrupulously observed, a rhythmical alternation of movement that always has a harmonious effect, and the wish to fill the whole surface with ornament. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 583} By its balance and serenity the arabesque avoids the convolution, excitement, restless whirling and violent twisting of the northern ornament with which it otherwise has much in common. Contrast is obtained through differences in density, the stalk sometimes almost vanishing under abundant foliage and at other times dominating the pattern. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 585}

## The name
The article holds that the name is justified, because the invention of this ornament was certainly the outcome of a particular Arab attitude, with parallel developments in Arabic poetry and music. This is the article's own judgement. It adds that the Arabic term tawrik clearly implies a description restricted to foliage, and that the term survives in the Spanish word ataurique, which Spanish authors commonly use for the genuine arabesque in Riegl's sense. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 585}

## Combinations with other ornament
The arabesque can be combined with every kind of geometric decoration, such as the [[concept/islamic-geometric-patterns|geometric patterns]] of Islamic art. In epigraphy it may form a background to [[concept/islamic-calligraphy|calligraphy]], letters may end in arabesques, or letters and arabesque may be interwoven. Animals may be drawn in the form of arabesques, which may also be combined with human figures, rendered more or less recognisably. Sometimes a grotesque decoration appears in which masks and figures are fitted into an arabesque scheme. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 585} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 586}

The article states that the arabesque never carries symbolic meaning and is simply one ornament from a large stock that includes other vegetal forms, such as palmettes, rosettes and naturalistic flowers, and abstract forms such as cloud-bands, though at certain periods it played a predominant role. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 586} A later article in the same encyclopaedia, on ornament in general, takes a more open position on the meaning of Islamic ornament, and is summarised in the last section below.

## Origins and development
According to the article, the arabesque has its prototype in certain acanthus, vine-leaf and cornucopia forms of late antiquity, which progress in undulations or with bifurcations. It is not yet completely developed in the Umayyad period, acquires its typical shape in the ninth century under the Abbasids and in Islamic Spain, and appears fully developed in the eleventh century under the Seljuks, Fatimids and Moors. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 586} The dynasties named are those of the [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad]], [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid]] and [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]] caliphates and the [[polity/seljuk-empire|Seljuk empire]], while Islamic Spain is [[place/al-andalus|al-Andalus]].

From then on the arabesque occurs throughout the Islamic world in countless variations, so many that the article thinks it impossible to classify them by chronological order or by national or dynastic preference. Persian and Turkish artists understood its language as well as Arabic-speaking artists, and over the centuries they competed with one another in creating ever more varieties. The ornament is not tied to one material. It appears in architectural decoration, in painted decoration, in pottery, glass and metalwork, and above all in book illumination. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 586}

The illustrations of the article show how widely it travelled. They include a motif from the [[building/masjid-amr-fustat|mosque of Amr at Fustat]], around the year 800, a motif from a mosque of Kairouan, a Qur'an manuscript made in Granada in the fifteenth century, Egyptian wood carving of the eleventh and thirteenth centuries, a faience mosaic in a tomb at [[place/konya|Konya]] of the thirteenth century, and a Persian stucco tile of the twelfth. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 584} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 585}

## The birth of the arabesque in Yeomans
Yeomans sees the genesis of the Islamic arabesque in [[concept/samarran-stucco-styles|Samarra style B]], where leaves and palmettes become abstract, lose their stems and are organised into the surface geometry of polygons and polylobes. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.37} The style survives in situ in the arch soffits of the [[building/masjid-ibn-tulun|Mosque of Ibn Tulun]], where vine leaf, acanthus, palmettes and rosettes fill networks of interlacing circles, triangles and squares in a resolution of organic and geometric form. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.38} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.39}

In Yeomans's view geometric forms in Islamic art are dynamic and open-ended, rarely coming to rest, and the vegetal arabesque is stylised and two-dimensional, respecting the surface, unlike closed, naturalistic Graeco-Roman ornament; he cites Owen Jones's *Grammar of Ornament* of 1856: the leaves of Greek scrollwork grow out of the surface, while Islamic design works with it. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.39}

## The arabesque in Europe
In the Hispano-Moresque art of the twelfth century and later, the article says, the arabesque dominates almost to the exclusion of other ornamental forms. From Islamic Spain it reached the Christian countries of Europe in the late fifteenth century, where it was known as moresque and became fashionable in the first half of the sixteenth century. The article names Hans Holbein among the artists who brought it to Germany, and says that other artists imitated it, mainly in pattern-books for jewellers and armourers, such as the *Livre de moresques* printed in Paris in 1546. The names of the artists who introduced it to Italy and France cannot be read reliably in the copy used, so they are left out here. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 586}

## Ornament more widely: the article on zakhrafa
A later article in the encyclopaedia, by Eva Baer, treats Islamic ornament as a whole under the Arabic word zakhrafa, meaning ornament or ornamentation. It links the word to zukhruf, which means gold and then ornamental work and occurs in the Qur'an, and mentions a suggestion that the origin of zukhruf lies in a distortion, through Syriac, of a Greek verb meaning to paint. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 436}

Baer notes that Islamic ornament has some qualities, not exclusive to it but distinct enough to be recognisable. One is its independence from the underlying structure, a building or an object, so that it can pass easily from one material or technique to another. Its commonest elements are vegetal, geometric, epigraphic and figural, and it is organised by geometry and symmetry, with framing and linking as typical methods. Plants may grow completely into each other and form an infinite pattern, generally called arabesque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 436}

She divides the development of vegetal ornament into three broad periods: a formative one, roughly the seventh to the tenth centuries; one of ornamental integration, from about the tenth to the later thirteenth; and a final phase from about the fourteenth to the seventeenth. In the first centuries a great variety of motifs was adopted from earlier civilisations. Acanthus and vine leaves, palmettes, half-palmettes, lotus blossoms and grapes were flattened into two dimensions and composed without a background, giving abstract designs whose floral origin remains recognisable. The best examples come from late eighth-century Syria and from ninth-century [[place/samarra|Samarra]], and spread from there to Egypt and elsewhere. Spain was less affected, keeping the deeply carved, folded and so three-dimensional leaves of Umayyad tradition, as in the mosaics of the [[building/dome-of-the-rock|Dome of the Rock]] and the eastern facade of Mshatta. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 436}

On the arabesque itself, Baer writes that its preliminary stages may date from the eleventh century but that it reached its full scope in the two centuries that followed, culminating in Asia Minor, with works made at Konya as the witness. After the [[polity/mongol-empire|Mongol]] invasions, Central Asian motifs such as lotus blossoms, peonies and composite flowers enriched the vegetal repertoire from the second half of the thirteenth century, and Chinese-inspired floral motifs appeared first on ceramics before passing to metal, wood, glass, textiles and manuscript illumination. By the second half of the fourteenth century, she says, intricate floral patterns were being turned into flat coloured faience mosaics in Persian and Central Asian architecture. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 436}

## Possible meanings
Baer observes that ornament can be classified by its elements or by how it is organised, and also interpreted symbolically, since it can communicate ideas or carry metaphor. The opening of her discussion of meaning cannot be read in the copy used, but the legible part suggests that fruit-bearing trees were charged with old associations of fertility, conveyed blessings to the dead in the afterlife and were linked with Paradise, that flowers conveyed well-being, and that arches and arcades, resembling the [[concept/mihrab|mihrab]], were sometimes given religious symbolism. Domes could carry cosmological meaning, a cupola or ceramic bowl standing for the vault of heaven, which Persian poetry calls an upturned bowl. She adds that the search for the meaning of ornament is still at its beginning. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 438}

The two articles therefore differ in emphasis. The first says the arabesque has no symbolic significance; the second says ornament in general may be read symbolically and that this question awaits study. The museum records both positions without choosing between them.
