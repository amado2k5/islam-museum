## Overview
In [[work/nuzhat-al-nazar|*Nuzhat al-Nazar*]], [[person/ibn-hajar-al-asqalani|Ibn Hajar]] divides accepted reports into those acted upon and those not. If a report is free of conflict, with no report contradicting it, it is definitive (*muhkam*), and there are many examples. If it is contradicted by a rejected report, that has no effect, since a weak report's contradiction does not affect a strong one; if it is contradicted by an equally accepted report, there is an order of steps. {cite:nuzhat-al-nazar-itr-bushra-2011 p. 71}

## Reconciliation: mukhtalif al-hadith
If the meanings of the two hadiths can be reconciled without strain, this is the genre called *mukhtalif al-hadith*. [[person/ibn-al-salah|Ibn al-Salah]]'s example is "There is no contagion and no evil omen" together with "Flee from the leper as you flee from the lion", both in the Sahih. {cite:nuzhat-al-nazar-itr-bushra-2011 p. 71-72}

Ibn al-Salah, following others, reconciled them by saying that these diseases do not infect by their own nature, but God made contact between the sick and the healthy a cause of infection, which may sometimes fail to follow. Ibn Hajar prefers to say that the denial of contagion stays general, citing "nothing infects anything" and the Prophet's answer to the man who said a mangy camel among healthy ones makes them mangy: "Then who infected the first?"; the command to flee from the leper is a precaution, lest something befall the one in contact by God's decree from the start, and he think it came from the contact and so come to believe in contagion. {cite:nuzhat-al-nazar-itr-bushra-2011 p. 72}

[[person/al-shafii|Al-Shafi'i]] wrote *Ikhtilaf al-Hadith* on this genre without aiming to be exhaustive, and after him Ibn Qutayba, [[person/al-tahawi|al-Tahawi]] and others wrote on it. {cite:nuzhat-al-nazar-itr-bushra-2011 p. 73}

## Abrogation
If reconciliation is impossible and the dates are known, with the later report established, the later abrogates and the other is abrogated. Ibn Hajar defines abrogation as the lifting of the attachment of a legal ruling by a later legal proof, and calls the proof "abrogating" only figuratively, since in reality the one who abrogates is God; see [[concept/naskh|abrogation]]. {cite:nuzhat-al-nazar-itr-bushra-2011 p. 73}

Abrogation is known by several means. The clearest is an explicit text, like Burayda's hadith in the *Sahih* of Muslim, "I used to forbid you to visit graves; now visit them". Another is a Companion stating that one ruling came later, as Jabir said: the last of the two practices of the Messenger of God was not performing ablution after eating what fire had touched. Another is knowledge of the dates. It does not include a report by a Companion who became Muslim late that contradicts an earlier one, since he may have heard it from an older Companion and passed it on without naming him, unless he states he heard it himself after his conversion. Consensus does not abrogate, but indicates abrogation. {cite:nuzhat-al-nazar-itr-bushra-2011 p. 73-74}

## Preference, then suspension
If the dates are not known and one of the two can be preferred on some ground relating to the text or the chain, that must be followed; otherwise acting on either is suspended. Ibn Hajar prefers "suspension" to "mutual cancellation", since the failure to find a ground of preference is only relative to the present inquirer, and another may see what he missed. So the order is: reconciliation, then abrogation, then preference, then suspension. {cite:nuzhat-al-nazar-itr-bushra-2011 p. 74-75}

The editor adds that al-Hazimi listed fifty grounds of preference, al-'Iraqi more than a hundred, and [[person/al-suyuti|al-Suyuti]] reduced them to seven classes: the narrator's state, the mode of receiving, the manner of transmitting, the time of the report, its wording, the ruling, and particular factors such as agreement with the plain sense of the Qur'an. {cite:nuzhat-al-nazar-itr-bushra-2011 p. 75}
