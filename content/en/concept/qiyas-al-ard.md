## Summary
Al-Farghani's measurement of the earth is an estimate of its circumference: he measured one degree of the meridian circle in cubits and multiplied the degree by three hundred and sixty. He valued the degree at fifty-six and two-thirds miles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 100}.

## The mile and the cubit
The mile is four thousand cubits of the black cubit, "as tested in al-Ma'mun's days, when several scholars joined in its measurement" {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 100}. The degree thus comes to fifty-six and two-thirds miles.

## Circumference and diameter
Al-Farghani multiplies the degree by the three hundred and sixty degrees of the zodiac's course, giving the earth's circumference as twenty thousand four hundred miles, then divides the circumference by three and seven-sevenths to obtain a diameter of about six thousand five hundred miles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 99}.

## The books differ on the diameter
Elsewhere in the book al-Farghani gives other figures for the earth's diameter while treating its area; the OCR reading of them is unclear, so they are not relied on without checking the page image {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 49}.

## Chapter eight: the figures together
In chapter eight al-Farghani repeats the same measurement: the degree is fifty-six and two-thirds miles, the circumference twenty thousand four hundred miles, and the diameter about six thousand five hundred miles {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 162}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 163}. He gives the inhabited world's length from east to west as ten thousand two hundred miles, half the circle {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 163}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 164}. A large figure he gives for the area is OCR-doubtful and is not relied on {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 163}. The inhabited world's breadth from the equator to where the pole's elevation reaches sixty-six and a third parts he reckons at three thousand seven hundred and sixty-four miles in one doubtful OCR reading, while elsewhere he gives the sum of the climes' latitudes as about two thousand one hundred and forty miles; the difference calls for checking against the image {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 164}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 166}.

## Pages 201 to 388: the degree measured at Sinjar and the cubits
Golius transmits from Abu'l-Fida that a company of scholars in al-Ma'mun's reign gathered in the Sinjar desert and split into two parties, one marching north and the other south until the pole rose one degree for each; the distance came to fifty-six and two-thirds miles for some parties and fifty-six without fraction for others, and the larger was adopted {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 316}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 317}.

The text also describes the cubits used in the measure, among them the Hashimite cubit of eight palms or thirty-two fingers, and the black cubit adopted by al-Ma'mun, reckoned at six palms and three fingers, that is twenty-seven fingers — OCR figures to be checked against the image {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 317}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 318}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 319}.
