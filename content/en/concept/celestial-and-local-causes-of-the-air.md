## Summary
Chapter 8, on changes of air that are not altogether against the natural course, divides them into celestial and earthly, and then shows the effect of the stars, the latitude of a country and the height or depth of its ground {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.122-123}.

## Celestial and earthly
Ibn Sina traces what was said of the seasons to heavenly and earthly matters. The heavenly follow the stars: at times many bright planets gather with the sun in one region, so that the heating is excessive for the places beneath or near, and at times they stand far from the zenith and the heating lessens. The earthly are due partly to the latitude of countries, partly to the height or depth of the ground, and partly to mountains, seas, winds and soil {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.122}.

## Latitude and the lasting of the overhead sun
Any land near the tropic of Cancer or of Capricorn is hotter in summer than one farther from it, yet the sun's standing overhead has little effect by itself; what acts is its lasting. So the heat after mid-afternoon is greater than at noon, and the heat when the sun is at the end of Cancer and the start of Leo is greater than when it is at its greatest declination. Under the equator the sun is overhead only a few days and then moves off quickly, so that ground is near moderate. The hottest lands are those whose latitude is close to the full declination, and then those about fifteen degrees beyond it {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.122-123}.

## Highland and lowland
A place in a hollow is always hotter and a high place always colder, since the air nearest the earth is warmer from the strength of the sun's rays close to it, and what is farther off is colder. A hollow shaped like a pit confines the rays more and is hotter still {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.123}.
