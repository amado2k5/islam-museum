## Summary
In the second chapter Ibn Sina gives a way to tell a disease that begins in an organ from one that comes by sympathy: look at which came first and which stays after the other has gone, and he warns of an error when the original cause is not felt {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.157-158}.

## The distinguishing mark
Diseases arise first in an organ or by sympathy, as the head shares with the stomach in their ailments. The physician considers which came first and judges that one primary and the other sympathetic, and considers which remains after the other has gone and judges that one primary. By contrast the sympathetic one is judged to be the one that comes last and quiets when the first quiets {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.157-158}.

## Where the error lies
An error can arise: the original cause may be unfelt and painless at the start, its harm felt only after the sympathetic illness appears, so the sympathetic is taken for the primary, or only the incidental one is noticed and the original overlooked altogether. The safeguard is a physician who knows the sympathies of organs from anatomy and the ailments that strike each organ, who pronounces a disease primary only after considering what might follow from it, asks the patient about signs of disease in the partner organs, and above all weighs the harm to functions: if that came first, he judges the disease sympathetic {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.158}.

## Organs whose diseases follow others
Some organs more often fall ill after others: the head most often by sympathy with the stomach, the reverse being rarer. Ibn Sina promises the signs of each organ's diseases in its own chapter; of diseases of composition, the outward are known by sense, and the inward are deferred to the particular discourses {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.158}.
