## Summary
This page gathers what the sources read say of the influence of [[language/arabic|Arabic]] on other languages, and it separates what the registered sources support from what was read in other articles not registered here. It gives no example words, since the sources read gave none for Persian, Turkish, Urdu or Spanish, and it leaves out the English examples because they do not rest on a registered source.

## The United Nations list
The United Nations page on World Arabic Language Day says Arabic exerted a direct or indirect influence on many languages, including [[language/turkish|Turkish]], [[language/persian|Persian]], [[language/kurdish|Kurdish]], [[language/urdu|Urdu]], [[language/malay|Malay]], [[language/indonesian|Indonesian]] and Albanian; African languages such as [[language/hausa|Hausa]] and [[language/swahili|Swahili]]; and several European languages of the Mediterranean, particularly Spanish, Portuguese, Maltese and Sicilian. It says Arabic helped transmit Greek and Roman scientific and philosophical learning to Europe {cite:un-world-arabic-language-day}. The page gives no example words.

## Maltese
The article on Maltese says it developed from a dialect of Arabic and is closely related to the western Arabic dialects of Algeria and Tunisia, that Sicilian strongly influenced it, and that it is the only form of Arabic written in the Latin alphabet {cite:britannica-maltese-language}. The article on the Semitic languages says it is a national language in [[place/malta|Malta]] with some 370,000 speakers {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}. For more see [[concept/al-lughat-al-samiyya|Arabic among the Semitic languages]].

## Swahili
The article on Swahili says it has been greatly influenced by Arabic and has an enormous number of Arabic loanwords, among them the word swahili itself, from the Arabic sawahili, a plural adjectival form of an Arabic word meaning "of the coast", and that its origin lies in the contacts of traders from Arabia with the east coast {cite:britannica-swahili-language}.

## The alphabet
The article on the Arabic alphabet says it was adapted to Persian, Turkish, Spanish and Swahili, and that Nasta'liq is the primary hand for Persian, Dari, Pashto and Urdu {cite:britannica-arabic-alphabet}. For the script see [[concept/khatt-al-arabi|the Arabic script]].

## Persian, Turkish, Urdu and Spanish
Encyclopaedia Britannica's articles say that Modern [[language/persian|Persian]] is written in Arabic characters and has many Arabic loanwords {cite:britannica-persian-language}; that Old [[language/turkish|Turkish]] absorbed a great many Arabic and Persian words, even some grammatical forms, and was written in Arabic script, until the Latin alphabet replaced it after the republic of 1923, the article giving 1928 {cite:britannica-turkish-language}; that [[language/urdu|Urdu]] borrowed extensively from Arabic and Persian, and the article warns that some of the prefixes and suffixes it names are Persian, not Arabic {cite:britannica-urdu-language}; and that Mozarabic, the dialect of Arab-occupied Spain before the twelfth century, is a remarkably archaic form of Spanish with many borrowings from Arabic, known from the kharjas {cite:britannica-spanish-language}. The United Nations page names these languages in its list {cite:un-world-arabic-language-day}.

## What this page does not reach
No example words of Arabic origin in Spanish, Persian, Turkish or Urdu were found in the sources read, so the page gives none. A list of English words of Arabic origin was read in Wikipedia pages and is not set out here because it does not rest on a registered source. No linguistic studies of borrowing were read, and these pages do not show how large the influence was or how much of it entered each language directly or through an intermediary.
