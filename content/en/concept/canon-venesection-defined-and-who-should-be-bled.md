## Summary
Ibn Sina opens the chapter on venesection by defining it as a general evacuation that drains plethora, then limits those to be bled to two kinds, the one prone to diseases and the one already in them, lists those prone to diseases of the blood, and notes that the complexion of some of them does not show that bleeding is due {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.289-290}.

## Definition
Venesection is a general evacuation that drains plethora, and plethora is the increase of the humours beyond the standard in their even presence in the vessels. Only one of two persons should be bled: the one prone to diseases who will fall into them if his blood grows many, and the one already fallen into them. Each of the two is bled either for the abundance of the blood, or for its badness, or for both {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.289-290}.

## Those prone to diseases of the blood
The one prone to these diseases is such as the person disposed to sciatica, blood gout and blood pains of the joints; one who spits blood from the splitting of a thin-walled vessel in his lung, which splits whenever his blood grows many; those disposed to epilepsy, apoplexy, melancholia, quinsy with a surge of blood, swellings of the viscera and hot ophthalmia; men from whom the blood of piles that used to flow in their custom has stopped; and women whose menstrual blood is withheld {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.290}.

## Complexion and precaution
The complexions of these last two groups do not show that bleeding is due, because they are dull, white and greenish. Those with weakness in the inner organs together with a hot temperament are best bled in the spring, even if they have not yet fallen into these diseases {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.290}.
