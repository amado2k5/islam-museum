## Overview
A *hadith qudsi*, "sacred hadith", is a saying that the Prophet transmitted from his Lord. In the chains of transmission it is known by the formula "what he relates from his Lord." {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.511-512} The Encyclopaedia of Islam defines it as a class of traditions that give words spoken by God, also called *hadith ilahi* or *rabbani*, "divine tradition", and distinguishes it from the *hadith nabawi*, the prophetic tradition, which gives the words of the Prophet. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 37}

## How it differs from the Qur'an
The article sets out the differences as they are usually stated. The Qur'an was revealed through [[person/jibril|Jibril]], is inimitable, is recited in the prayer, and may not be touched or recited by someone in a state of ritual impurity. A hadith qudsi does not necessarily come through Jibril: it may have come by inspiration, in Arabic *ilham*, or in a dream. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 37} The article adds that one statement, which it says is not generally accepted, holds that God revealed these traditions to the Prophet on the night of the Miraj. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 37}

On the wording, the article reports that the words are not God's exact words but express their meaning. Such traditions may not be used in the prayer, and there is no harm if someone in a state of ritual impurity touches them. It also reports that disbelief in the Qur'an is unbelief, whereas this does not apply to a hadith qudsi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 37}

## How a hadith qudsi is quoted
When quoting a hadith qudsi, the article says, one must not say simply "God said" as when quoting the Qur'an. The accepted forms are "God's messenger said in what he related from his Lord", or "God most high said in what God's messenger related from Him". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 37}

## Parallels with biblical texts
The article notes that some of these traditions clearly have their source in the Bible. One example is the saying that what eye has not seen, nor ear heard, has entered the heart of man, which it compares with passages in Isaiah and in the First Letter to the Corinthians. Another is a tradition in which God says on the Day of Resurrection, "O son of Adam, I was sick and you did not visit me", which the article says continues on the lines of a passage in the Gospel of Matthew. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 37}

## Place in the books and the collections
The article says that hadith qudsi do not form a separate group in the books of tradition. Some collections have been compiled from the six Sunni books and, more commonly, from others. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 37}

The largest collection it names is *al-Ithafat al-saniyya fi'l-ahadith al-qudsiyya* by Muhammad al-Madani, also written al-Madyani, who died in 881/1476; it was published at Hyderabad in 1323/1905. It holds 858 traditions in three groups: those beginning with *qala*, those beginning with *yaqulu*, and those arranged alphabetically, the last group containing 603. The chains of transmission are not given, but because the collection from which each tradition comes is named, readers can look up the chain there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 37} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 38}

A collection of 101 sacred traditions titled *Mishkat al-anwar* by [[person/ibn-arabi-muhyi-al-din|Muhyi al-Din Ibn al-Arabi]], who died in 638/1240, was published at Aleppo in 1346/1927 together with a collection of 40 compiled by [[person/ali-al-qari|Mulla Ali al-Qari]], who died in 1014/1605. Ibn al-Arabi divides his collection into three parts, two of 40 traditions and one of 21, and gives a full chain in the first, sometimes in the second and usually in the third. Ali al-Qari merely names the Companion reputed to have heard the tradition from the Prophet. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 38}

Another collection, unpublished, is by Muhammad ibn Taj al-Din al-Munawi, who died in 1031/1621. It has two parts: the first with traditions beginning with *qala* and the second in alphabetical order. The article judges that al-Munawi, whose smaller work has the same title as al-Madani's, largely depended on that work. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 38}

## An example
An example is the hadith of Abu Dharr: "O My servants, I have forbidden injustice to Myself," reported by Muslim, number 2577. It is the first sacred hadith in the Forty of al-Nawawi. In the wording of al-Tirmidhi the formula "God the Exalted says" is explicit. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.511-512}

## Sayings about the hadith
Imam Ahmad said it is the most noble hadith for the people of Syria. When Abu Idris al-Khawlani narrated it, he would kneel on his knees; Sa'id ibn Abd al-Aziz relates this from Rabi'a ibn Yazid. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.511-512}

## In al-Sha'rawi's programme In the Courts of the Hadith Qudsi
The first episode of [[work/fi-rihab-al-hadith-al-qudsiyyah|Fi rihab al-hadith al-qudsi]] opens with the presenter saying that speech attributed to God is of three kinds: the Qur'an, the noblest of them, the books of the earlier prophets before they were altered, and the hadith qudsi; a hadith qudsi is what the Prophet transmitted attributing it to his Lord, whereas he does not attribute a prophetic hadith to his Lord. {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy fi-rihab-al-hadith-al-qudsiyyah/ep01 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy fi-rihab-al-hadith-al-qudsiyyah/ep01 00:02:00}

[[person/muhammad-mutawalli-al-shaarawi|Al-Sha'rawi]] then explains that what the Prophet conveyed from his Lord is of two parts: one by which God challenged the deniers, to prove their inability in the eloquence they excelled in, and another not coupled with the challenge and not recited as worship. {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy fi-rihab-al-hadith-al-qudsiyyah/ep01 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy fi-rihab-al-hadith-al-qudsiyyah/ep01 00:05:00} He holds that a challenge is addressed only to the one who denies, that Abu Bakr and Ali believed without having heard anything of the Qur'an, and that the hadith qudsi came as a speech by which those who never denied are honoured. {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy fi-rihab-al-hadith-al-qudsiyyah/ep01 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy fi-rihab-al-hadith-al-qudsiyyah/ep01 00:07:00} He says it is not read in the prayer nor recited as worship, and is known by the Prophet saying in it "God said". {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy fi-rihab-al-hadith-al-qudsiyyah/ep01 00:07:30}

He argues that none of the masters of Arabic eloquence had three styles, while the Prophet's speech came in three: the style of the Qur'an, that of the hadith qudsi and that of the prophetic hadith. {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy fi-rihab-al-hadith-al-qudsiyyah/ep01 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy fi-rihab-al-hadith-al-qudsiyyah/ep01 00:09:30} He sums up the difference by saying that the Qur'an is from God in wording and meaning, the hadith qudsi from God in meaning, and the prophetic hadith from the Prophet. {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy fi-rihab-al-hadith-al-qudsiyyah/ep01 00:09:30} In episode 23 he describes the chain of a hadith qudsi as "raised", *marfu*, to God. {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy fi-rihab-al-hadith-al-qudsiyyah/ep23 00:19:00}
