## Summary
This page sets out two parts of [[concept/ilm-al-asma-wal-alqab|onomastics]]: the nasab, the chain of ancestors, and the nisba, the affiliation to a place, tribe, trade or school, as a research file supplied to the museum presents them. The museum did not open the pages cited, and distinguishes this onomastic treatment from the juristic study of [[concept/nasab|lineage]].
## Nasab
The nasab records a person's descent, normally through the paternal line, though names identifying a person through the mother also occur. Its elements are ibn or bin, son of; bint, daughter of; banu, sons or descendants of; and bani, a grammatical variant of banu {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names}. Its example is the genealogy of Muhammad: Muhammad ibn Abd Allah ibn Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim, in which each ibn connects a person to his father.

| Name | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Umar ibn al-Khattab | Umar, son of al-Khattab |
| Khadija bint Khuwaylid | Khadija, daughter of Khuwaylid |
| Abd Allah ibn Umar | Abd Allah, son of Umar |

## A nasab that is not the immediate father
A designation beginning with Ibn can refer to a distant ancestor and not the immediate father; an example is [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]]: Khaldun was an ancestor of the family, not his father's personal name. The summary's references for nasab are Ibn Hisham's Sira, Ibn Hazm's Jamharat Ansab al-Arab, Ibn Khaldun's al-Ta'rif bi-Ibn Khaldun and Beeston's Arabic Nomenclature {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names}.
## Nisba
The nisba connects a person with a place, tribe, family, occupation, religious school or other affiliation. It is commonly formed as an attributive adjective ending in -i for men and -iyya for women, and a person could carry several nisbas at once {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names}.

The nisba of the mathematician al-Khwarizmi connects him to Khwarazm, but a geographical nisba does not necessarily establish birthplace; it can also indicate ancestral origin, a former residence or another link. See [[place/bukhara|Bukhara]], [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]], [[place/damascus|Damascus]], [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]], [[place/fez|Fez]] and [[person/al-khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]].
## Tribal and occupational nisbas
| Nisba | Affiliation |
|---|---|
| al-Qurashi | Quraysh |
| al-Hashimi | Banu Hashim |
| al-Tamimi | Banu Tamim |
| al-Kindi | Kinda |
| al-Ansari | The Ansar of Medina |
| al-Ayyubi | The Ayyubid family |
| al-Hadrami | Hadramawt origin or association |

A tribal nisba may indicate ancestry but can also reflect historical clientage or affiliation, so it is not always proof of biological descent. The occupations are al-Haddad, al-Najjar, al-Khayyat, al-Attar, al-Warraq, al-Jawhari, al-Sayrafi and al-Qabbani, and some are classed as laqabs and not grammatical nisbas, so the two functions overlap {cite:bnf-kitcat-arabic-names}.
## Religious and legal nisbas
These include al-Hanafi, al-Maliki, al-Shafi'i and al-Hanbali for the legal schools and al-Mu'tazili for Mu'tazilite theology, while al-Ash'ari may indicate Ash'ari theology or a tribal affiliation, depending on context {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names}. The summary's principal reference for nisbas is al-Sam'ani's Kitab al-Ansab, and among modern ones Jacqueline Sublet's Le voile du nom and the guidance of the Bibliotheque nationale de France {cite:persee-home} {cite:nyu-arabic-collections-online}. The book has its record at [[work/al-ansab-samani|al-Ansab by al-Sam'ani]].

| Nisba | Occupation |
|---|---|
| al-Haddad | Blacksmith |
| al-Najjar | Carpenter |
| al-Khayyat | Tailor |
| al-Attar | Perfumer or spice merchant |

| al-Jawhari | Jeweller |

The summary holds that occupational names pass into nisba, laqab or family name, and that the name al-Haddad does not prove the ancestors worked as smiths, as will be seen in [[concept/nasha-at-asma-al-usar-al-arabiyya|how Arab family names developed]].
