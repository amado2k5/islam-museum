## Summary
A compulsory public loan is what the state borrows from the wealthy by obligation {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 324}. The author classifies it under compulsory redistribution because interest is not permissible on a loan in Islam and because its purpose is relief and assistance {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 324}.

## Origin of the Idea
The author notes that the Prophet borrowed from his uncle al-'Abbas by taking two years' zakat in advance—or three years' in some reports—saying, "We were in need, so we took two years' charity in advance from al-'Abbas" {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 324}. According to the footnote, this report was recorded by al-Bayhaqi; the book does not rule on its chain of transmission, nor does this entry {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 324}.

## Authors on the Issue
The author states that those who discussed the compulsory public loan are the very same scholars who discussed extraordinary taxation (tawzif): al-Juwayni, al-Ghazali, al-Shatibi, and Ibn Taymiyyah {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 324}. The author quotes al-Juwayni as permitting borrowing when circumstances demand it and funds have run out {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 324}.

## The Condition of al-Ghazali and al-Shatibi
Al-Ghazali says he does not deny the permissibility of borrowing and the obligation to restrict oneself to it when public interest calls for it, yet if the ruler does not expect funds to flow into the public treasury (bayt al-mal) beyond the soldiers' provisions, on what is one to rely? {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 325}. Al-Shatibi says that borrowing in crises is only done where future revenue is expected, and if revenue is absent, the ruling of extraordinary taxation (tawzif) must apply, noting that Ibn al-'Arabi followed al-Ghazali in this {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 325}. The author observes that al-Shatibi had not seen al-Juwayni's earlier statement before al-Ghazali {cite:usul-iqtisad-islami-0660 p. 325}.
