## Summary
Algebra is the branch of mathematics that solves problems by positing an unknown and handling equations by general methods. The editors of the 1939 Cairo edition of the [[work/al-jabr-wal-muqabala|Book of al-Jabr and al-Muqabala]] note that the name of the science in every language comes from the Arabic word *al-jabr*, which [[person/al-khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]] used as the title of his book. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.25}

## Al-jabr and al-muqabala in al-Khwarizmi's book
Al-Khwarizmi states that reckoning by al-jabr and al-muqabala needs three kinds of number, roots, squares and simple number, {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.30} which combine into [[concept/al-durub-al-sitta|six types]] of problem, and that everything worked by this reckoning "must bring you" to one of them. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.35} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.43} In his problems *al-jabr* is completing a quantity that has a deficiency or deduction by adding its like to both sides, as in "restore the thirty by the thing and add it to the two things", {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.121} and *al-muqabala* is casting away like terms from both sides, as in "you cast away a square by a square". {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.69} He applies this reckoning to transactions, [[concept/ilm-al-misaha|mensuration]], and [[concept/hisab-al-dawr|bequests and circular reckoning]]. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.30}

## Algebra before al-Khwarizmi, according to the Cairo editors
The editors Ali Mustafa Mosharafa and Muhammad Mursi Ahmad give a survey of algebra before al-Khwarizmi, saying that modern research in the history of science has revealed the importance of the Egyptian and Islamic ages in the development of science. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.13} Among their points:
- The oldest surviving school text is the Ahmes (Rhind) papyrus of about 1700 BCE, with a first-degree equation in one unknown and a special sign for the unknown. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.14}
- Later Egyptian remains contain second-degree equations, and the Babylonians made tables of squares and cubes. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.14} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.16}
- Greek mathematicians knew the geometric solution of second-degree equations in Pythagoras's time, and the first analytic solution that can be affirmed goes back to Heron of Alexandria. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.16}
- Diophantus knew the analytic solution of second-degree equations with positive coefficients but did not study their types systematically, as al-Khwarizmi did. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.18}
- Indian mathematicians from Aryabhata to Brahmagupta in the seventh century CE, and then Mahavira, al-Khwarizmi's contemporary, set out rules for solving the quadratic equation. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.19} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.20}

They conclude that all these developments led towards algebra in its proper sense, which arose naturally from people's interest in geometry and the properties of numbers, each country being influenced by its neighbours. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.20} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.21}

## After al-Khwarizmi
The editors hold that al-Khwarizmi is rightly regarded as the founder of algebra; that [[person/abu-kamil-shuja-ibn-aslam|Abu Kamil Shuja ibn Aslam]] openly acknowledged him as an authority, [[person/umar-khayyam|Umar al-Khayyam]] quoted him, and his equations were repeated in algebraic works into the early modern age. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.24} They report that Ibn Khaldun made al-Khwarizmi the first to write on algebra with Abu Kamil after him, and that [[person/al-qazwini|Zakariyya al-Qazwini]] said he was the first to translate algebra for the Muslims. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.25} Following Ibn al-Nadim, they name Sinan ibn al-Fath, Abd Allah ibn al-Hasan and [[person/abu-al-wafa-al-buzjani|Abu al-Wafa al-Buzjani]] among the commentators on his book. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.23}
