## Summary
Sha'rawi reads Q. 4:97 to 4:100 as migration from a land where the believer is oppressed to a land where he is safe in his religion: Abyssinia was a land of safety and Medina a land of faith. He holds that after the conquest there is no hijra except for one who seeks help in obedience {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.2578}{cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.2583}.

## What Sha'rawi says
He discusses those who stayed behind when they could have migrated, and holds that one who wrongs himself by remaining in a land where he is kept from obedience faces a bad end, since the earth is God's and belongs to no one {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.2578}{cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.2579}. He holds that the truly weak are those who have no means and cannot find a way, and that cunning widens the chances of strength {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.2580}{cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.2581}.

## Hijras that remain
He says the early Muslims migrated to Abyssinia because no one was wronged there, so they called it a land of safety though not a land of faith, while migration to Medina was to a land of faith {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.2583}. He holds that after the conquest migration is only for one who seeks help in obedience, and counts the pilgrimage, the pursuit of knowledge, and moving to a place where the duties can be performed as hijras to God {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.2583}. He reads the verse on one who dies on the way as meaning that his reward falls on God {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan p.2587}. The jurists' rulings on the duty of migration and its end are not yet set out from a named source.
