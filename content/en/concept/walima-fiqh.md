Walima lexically comes from walm, gathering, because the spouses come together; it names the food of a wedding or a betrothal, or any food made for a wedding or another occasion. Technically it is any food made for a happy event, such as a wedding or betrothal, though its use for a wedding is most common. This entry covers the rules of the wedding feast; the other feasts are under the entry on invitations (da'wa). {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17946}

Related terms: da'wa (invitation, hospitality) is wider than walima. The ma'daba (a meal made for an invitation) is wider than walima, which is the narrower term. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17946}

Ruling: the majority (Hanafis, the school's Shafi'is and Hanbalis) hold the wedding feast is a sunna, the Hanafis adding that it brings great reward; the Malikis hold it recommended in their school. They argue from the hadith "there is no right in wealth beyond zakat", that its cause, the marriage contract, is not obligatory, and that if it were obligatory it would have a fixed measure and a substitute in poverty, as expiations do. A Shafi'i view, a Maliki view and a view of Ahmad reported by Ibn 'Aqil hold it obligatory, citing the Prophet's words to 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf, when he saw a trace of perfume on him: "give a feast, even with a sheep"; that the Prophet never married without a feast; and that a feast announces the marriage and separates it from fornication. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17947}

Enforcement: the Malikis hold that in their preferred view the feast is not enforced, since it is recommended in their school, and they read the command in the hadith of 'Abd al-Rahman as recommendation. Khalil approved enforcement, and Abu al-Asbagh ibn Sahl said the correct view is enforcement, because a command is basically obligatory. The dispute applies unless the feast was made a condition on the husband or custom requires it, in which case it is enforced by agreement. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17948}

Purpose: the Malikis say the feast is to make the marriage known, since witnesses may die, and they tie it to a report that the Prophet distinguished a marriage from fornication by the drum or the announcement. The Shafi'is say its apparent purpose is hope that the wife will be made good by its blessing. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17948}

Accepting the invitation: the majority (Malikis, Shafi'is, Hanbalis and some Hanafis) hold that accepting is obligatory, if the guest is named, explicitly or by implication, even by letter or a trusted messenger. It is not obligatory for a general invitation, the open call (jafla), although it is permitted. They cite the hadith of Ibn 'Umar, "when one of you is invited to a feast let him go", and the hadith of Abu Hurayra, "the worst food is the food of a feast to which the rich are invited and the poor left out". {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17949}

Others, the general Hanafi and Shafi'i view and a Hanbali view (chosen by Ibn Taymiyya), hold accepting is a sunna, not obligatory, since it means eating food and taking a benefit, and no one must take a benefit against his will. A Hanbali view and a Shafi'i view hold it a collective duty: once enough guests accept, the obligation falls from the rest. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17950}

Fasting guests: a guest fulfils the invitation by attending. If his fast is obligatory he does not break it, prays for the household's blessing and says he is fasting. If it is voluntary, he may complete it. The Shafis and Hanbalis hold it recommended to break the fast if that pleases the host, and if it does not burden the host, to keep the fast is better. Ibn Taymiyya held that this is the most balanced view, and that the host should not press the guest to eat. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17950} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17951}

Guests who are not fasting: the majority hold that a non-fasting guest is recommended to eat but not required, citing "if one of you is invited to food let him go; if he wishes he eats, and if he wishes he leaves it". A Shafi'i view and a Maliki view hold eating obligatory, citing the hadith "if he is not fasting let him eat", since eating is the purpose of attending. Another Shafi'i view treats eating in the feast as a collective duty. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17951}
