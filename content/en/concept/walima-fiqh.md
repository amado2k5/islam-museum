Walima lexically comes from walm, gathering, because the spouses come together; it names the food of a wedding or a betrothal, or any food made for a wedding or another occasion. Technically it is any food made for a happy event, such as a wedding or betrothal, though its use for a wedding is most common. This entry covers the rules of the wedding feast; the other feasts are under the entry on invitations (da'wa). {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17946}

Related terms: da'wa (invitation, hospitality) is wider than walima. The ma'daba (a meal made for an invitation) is wider than walima, which is the narrower term. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17946}

Ruling: the majority (Hanafis, the school's Shafi'is and Hanbalis) hold the wedding feast is a sunna, the Hanafis adding that it brings great reward; the Malikis hold it recommended in their school. They argue from the hadith "there is no right in wealth beyond zakat", that its cause, the marriage contract, is not obligatory, and that if it were obligatory it would have a fixed measure and a substitute in poverty, as expiations do. A Shafi'i view, a Maliki view and a view of Ahmad reported by Ibn 'Aqil hold it obligatory, citing the Prophet's words to 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf, when he saw a trace of perfume on him: "give a feast, even with a sheep"; that the Prophet never married without a feast; and that a feast announces the marriage and separates it from fornication. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17947}

Enforcement: the Malikis hold that in their preferred view the feast is not enforced, since it is recommended in their school, and they read the command in the hadith of 'Abd al-Rahman as recommendation. Khalil approved enforcement, and Abu al-Asbagh ibn Sahl said the correct view is enforcement, because a command is basically obligatory. The dispute applies unless the feast was made a condition on the husband or custom requires it, in which case it is enforced by agreement. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17948}

Purpose: the Malikis say the feast is to make the marriage known, since witnesses may die, and they tie it to a report that the Prophet distinguished a marriage from fornication by the drum or the announcement. The Shafi'is say its apparent purpose is hope that the wife will be made good by its blessing. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17948}

Accepting the invitation: the majority (Malikis, Shafi'is, Hanbalis and some Hanafis) hold that accepting is obligatory, if the guest is named, explicitly or by implication, even by letter or a trusted messenger. It is not obligatory for a general invitation, the open call (jafla), although it is permitted. They cite the hadith of Ibn 'Umar, "when one of you is invited to a feast let him go", and the hadith of Abu Hurayra, "the worst food is the food of a feast to which the rich are invited and the poor left out". {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17949}

Others, the general Hanafi and Shafi'i view and a Hanbali view (chosen by Ibn Taymiyya), hold accepting is a sunna, not obligatory, since it means eating food and taking a benefit, and no one must take a benefit against his will. A Hanbali view and a Shafi'i view hold it a collective duty: once enough guests accept, the obligation falls from the rest. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17950}

Fasting guests: a guest fulfils the invitation by attending. If his fast is obligatory he does not break it, prays for the household's blessing and says he is fasting. If it is voluntary, he may complete it. The Shafis and Hanbalis hold it recommended to break the fast if that pleases the host, and if it does not burden the host, to keep the fast is better. Ibn Taymiyya held that this is the most balanced view, and that the host should not press the guest to eat. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17950} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17951}

Guests who are not fasting: the majority hold that a non-fasting guest is recommended to eat but not required, citing "if one of you is invited to food let him go; if he wishes he eats, and if he wishes he leaves it". A Shafi'i view and a Maliki view hold eating obligatory, citing the hadith "if he is not fasting let him eat", since eating is the purpose of attending. Another Shafi'i view treats eating in the feast as a collective duty. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17951}

Conditions of the obligation: the jurists who hold acceptance obligatory attach conditions to the place of the feast, the host, the guest and the feast itself. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17952}

First, conditions on the place. (1) No one present must be someone the guest is harmed by, or whose company is unfit for him; if there is, he is excused. The Malikis require the harm to be for a religious cause; the Shafi'is and Hanbalis differ on enmity; Ibn Taymiyya says he had not seen this view in his school and holds that such company does not excuse, like the presence in prayer rows or at a funeral does not cancel the congregation. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17952} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17953}

(2) No wrongdoing. All agree that if a guest knows beforehand that wine or entertainment is present, and cannot stop it, the duty falls. The Shafi'is (the sounder view) and the Hanbalis hold that he must not attend, citing the hadith of Jabir, "whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him not sit at a table where wine is passed". The Hanafis say he does not accept if he knows of the entertainment in advance. A Shafi'i view says it is better not to attend, though one may attend without listening and disapprove in the heart. The Malikis hold that a wrong present always prevents acceptance. The Hanbalis say that if he can remove the wrong on arrival he does so and sits; if not, he leaves, citing a narration about Ibn 'Umar and a flute, which Abu Dawud calls munkar. For the Hanafis, if the wrong is in the house and the guest cannot stop it, he endures it with disapproval in his heart, unless he is a figure to be followed, in which case he leaves; if the wrong is on the table, he must not sit, citing a verse in al-An'am (verse 68). {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17953} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17954}

(3) No forbidden images. Images of a whole human or animal with its outward limbs, and a lasting shade, displayed in the place, prevent acceptance, on the hadiths cursing the image-maker and the report of 'Ali, when the Prophet turned back from his house because of a curtain with pictures. The jurists distinguish images that are humiliated or incomplete. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17955}

(4) No dog that may not be kept or that bites; the Shafi'is require this even for a blind guest. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17955}

(5) No dense crowd: the Malikis excuse the guest if the crowd is heavy. The Shafi'is say a crowd is no excuse if there is room to enter and sit and his honour is safe. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17955} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17956}

(6) The door must not be shut on arrival; the Malikis excuse a guest who knows it will be shut, since that is a humiliation, though shutting it against gate-crashers does not excuse. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17956}

(7) The place must not be so far that it is hard to reach (the Malikis). {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17956}

(8) No women who look on the guests from the road or the house; the Malikis and Shafi'is count this as an excuse, and the Shafi'is hold it is an excuse even if the guest could cover his head and face. (9) No mixing of women and men at the feast. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17956} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17957}

Conditions on the host. (1) He must have full legal capacity; a ward under interdiction for youth, madness or folly is not asked, even if his guardian allows, since he is to guard his property, not spend it. If the feast is made by a father or grandfather from the child's property, the Shafi'i al-Adhra'i holds attendance obligatory. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17957}

(2) He must be a Muslim. Most jurists hold a non-Muslim's invitation does not bind, though it may be accepted, citing a hadith of a Jewish man who invited the Prophet. The Hanafis in one view dislike accepting a Magian's or Christian's invitation, while Muhammad al-Shaybani said there is no harm in attending a dhimmi's feast; a Shafi'i view held it obligatory. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17957} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17958}

(3) He must not be a wrongdoer; the Shafi'is say a host who is known for sin is not to be accepted, and the Hanafis limit this to one who openly declares his sin. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17958}

(4) Most of his property must be lawful. The Shafi'is and Hanbalis dislike accepting from one whose property is mostly unlawful, unless he says it is lawful. The Malikis forbid both attending and eating if the food is doubtful. Al-Kharaqi and Ibn al-Jawzi held eating is forbidden if the unlawful part is larger; Sahib al-Ri'aya set the line at a third. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17958}

(5) He must not seek display or boasting; the Malikis and Shafi'is say so, and the Hanafis dislike eating food made for show. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17958}

(6) A woman may not host unless a mahram of the guest is present or she has a mahram; the Hanbalis say a woman who names a particular man must be obeyed, except where a forbidden seclusion is involved. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17959}

(7) The host must not have invited only the rich for their wealth. The hadith "the worst food is the food of a feast to which the rich are invited and the poor left out" is cited. Al-Qurtubi reports the scholars' dispute on whether such an invitation is accepted: Ibn Mas'ud says no, and the report of Abu Hurayra suggests yes. Ibn Rushd reads the hadith as blaming the host, not the guest. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17959} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17960}

Conditions on the guest. (1) Sanity and puberty (Shafi'is). {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17960}

(2) Freedom: a slave is bound only with his master's permission. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17960}

(3) Islam: according to the Shafi'is a dhimmi is not bound to accept a Muslim's invitation, as he is not bound by our law except by consent. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17960}

(4) No lawful excuse, such as those that excuse the absence from Friday or congregational prayer. The Malikis count missing Friday prayer, a very distant place, sickness, nursing a relative, mud or rain, and fear for property. The Shafi'is add that the guest must not be obliged to another duty, such as giving testimony or a funeral prayer. The Hanbalis excuse the sick, the nurse, the one guarding property, extreme heat or cold, rain soaking clothes, mud, and the private hired worker without his employer's leave. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17960} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17961}

(5) The host must be the first to invite. If two invite and the feast cannot take both, the first to invite is accepted, because the duty arose with his call. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17961}

Choosing between two hosts: if both invitations are equal, al-Buhuti prefers the more pious host, since piety has weight in precedence, as in leading prayer; then the nearer kin, then the nearer neighbour; then lot, unless time allows accepting both. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17962}

Accepting a judge's invitation: the majority (Hanafis, Malikis in the preferred view, Shafi'is in the sounder view) allow it, unless the host is in a dispute with the judge; the Shafi'is add that if feasts are so many they keep the judge from judging, he leaves them all. The Hanbalis hold it obligatory like any guest, and leave all if they are too many; one Shafi'i view holds it forbidden. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17962} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17963}

Conditions on the feast itself. (1) It must be on the first day. A feast held for three days is not obligatory on the second and is disliked on the third, according to the Shafi'is and Hanbalis, citing "the first day of the feast is a right, the second is a courtesy, and the third is show and display". The Shafi'is excuse a third-day feast held for want of room or to gather peers such as scholars or merchants. The Hanafis say that after the wedding night a man should invite neighbours, relatives and friends, and there is no harm in inviting on the following days. The Malikis dislike repeated feasts as waste, unless the second guest is new; a Shafi'i view adopted by al-Adhra'i makes it obligatory if the guest was not invited on the first day or was invited on the second and excused. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17962} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17963}

(2) Time: the Hanafis (the famous view), the Malikis (the famous view) and Ibn Taymiyya say the feast is after consummation; the Shafi'is say after consummation is better, and the time runs from the contract. Al-Mardawi says the desirable time runs from the contract to the end of the wedding days, though custom makes it a little before consummation. The Hanbalis, a Hanafi view and a Maliki view say it is sunna at the contract; some Hanafis say at both. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17963} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17964}

(3) Several wives: the Shafis and Hanbalis hold one feast suffices for several marriages, whether in one contract or several, if it is meant for all, since its causes merge; if meant for one, the others still owe a feast. The Shafi'i principle is that feasts multiply with the number of wives, even in one contract. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17964}

(4) Least amount: the four schools hold there is no minimum; the sunna is met by any food, even two mudds of barley, as in the report that the Prophet made a feast for one of his wives with two mudds of barley. Iyad reports consensus on this. The Shafi'is say the least for one who can afford it is a sheep, and for others what they can give, citing "even with a sheep". Some Hanbalis recommend not less than a sheep. Al-Zarkashi says the "sheep" there means something small; al-Mardawi says it is better to exceed a sheep. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17964} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17965}

(5) Expiry: the Malikis and Shafi'is say the feast has no end, so it is not lost by divorce, death or lapse of time, and most Shafi'i texts treat it as always performed. Al-Dumayri, as quoted by al-Bujayrimi, says the feast ends with the wedding period, seven days for a virgin and three for a previously married woman, and a feast after that is a make-up (qada'). {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.17965}
