## Summary
Kharaj is lexically the yield of a thing, and it can mean a rent or a levy or tax. Abu Ubayd and al-Layth treat kharj and kharaj as one word, while Abu Amr ibn al-Ala' distinguishes them: kharj is what one gives freely, kharaj what one must pay. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7575}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7576} As a technical term it has a general sense, the funds the state collects and spends, and a particular sense, the duty the Imam imposes on productive land; al-Mawardi and Abu Ya'la define it as the rights laid on the necks of land and paid for it. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7576} Other names for it are land jizya, land rent, and tasq, a Persian word that appears in Umar's letter to Uthman ibn Hunayf. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7576}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7577} It is distinct from ghanima, fay', jizya (a levy on persons, not land), khums and 'ushr (the tithe on land owned by a Muslim). {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7577} When conquests grew under Umar ibn al-Khattab, he decided not to divide land taken by force among the conquerors but to hold it as an endowment for all Muslims, with a fixed kharaj on its cultivators. Companions first differed; Abu Yusuf reports that Bilal ibn Rabah was among the strongest objectors; Umar argued from the verses on fay' in Surat al-Hashr and consulted the Emigrants and the Helpers until the matter was settled. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7578}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7579} Jurists base its legitimacy on the Qur'an, the Sunna and public interest. The Sunna cited includes the report 'Iraq withheld its dirham and qafiz' (a prophetic foretelling of the kharaj imposed on land), and the Prophet's division of half of Khaybar for the needs of the community. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7580}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7581} The interests cited are a stable revenue for future generations, keeping wealth from concentrating in one group, and keeping land cultivated by those who know it best; Mu'adh ibn Jabal's advice to Umar is quoted for this. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7581}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7582} Kharaj is due on every holder of productive kharaj land, Muslim or not, adult or minor, sane or insane, man or woman, since it is the cost of the land's yield. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7580} By form of levy there is kharaj al-wazifa, a fixed sum owed on the land's area and crop type whatever it yields, which Umar imposed on al-Sawad, Egypt and Syria; and kharaj al-muqasama, a share of the produce such as a quarter or a fifth, which al-Mahdi introduced in place of the fixed sum, a change that the text connects to falling prices. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7582}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7583}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7584} By status of the land there is sulhi kharaj, levied on land whose people made terms to keep it, and 'anwi kharaj, levied on land taken by force and held for all Muslims, including land its people left in fear without fighting. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7584} Kharaj lands are of two kinds: those settled by treaty, and those abandoned out of fear without battle, which by most jurists become an endowment for Muslims upon seizure. {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7584}{cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.7585}
