## Summary
The equinoctial circle — also called the equator — is the parallel to the equinoctial on the earth's surface, dividing the earth into northern and southern halves {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 151}. It is the first boundary of the inhabited quarter from the south {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 111}.

## Naming
It is called "al-istiwa" because night and day are equal on it, and "the equator" because when the sun runs along it night and day are equal over all the earth {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 116}. In the Latin translation it is "aequinoctialis," the circle of the night-and-day equinox {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 148}.

## What happens on it
Al-Farghani says the sun passes through the zenith on the equator twice a year, and its deviation from the zenith north and south is equal, so summer and winter are temperate {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 111}. The time from the sun's rising to its setting equals that from its setting to its rising on every day of the year {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 154}.

## The extent of the inhabited world
The span from the first inhabited places in the east to the farthest in the west does not exceed twelve hours of the zodiac's course {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 110}{cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 152}.
