## Summary
Chapter 18 begins with the distances of stars from the equator and the part of the ecliptic that comes to midheaven with them, by longitude and latitude. A star on the ecliptic with no latitude behaves like the Sun in its declination, but one with latitude has a distance from the equator that comes from an arc through the poles of the equator, whereas its latitude comes from an arc through the poles of the ecliptic, so it comes to midheaven with a degree other than its own except at the first of Cancer and the first of Capricorn. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion pp.50-51}.

## A star with no latitude and one with latitude
He says: if you want to know the distances of stars from the equator and the part of the zodiac that comes to midheaven according to their places in longitude and latitude: every star that lies on the ecliptic circle, meaning it has no latitude, follows the course of the Sun in its declination from the equator, which is its distance from it. But one that has latitude on either side has a distance from the equator that falls short of its latitude and the declination of the degree it is in, when the two are added together or one is subtracted from the other as is due. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion pp.50-51}.

## Why it culminates with another degree
He explains: the star's latitude comes from an arc through the ecliptic's poles and the star's place in longitude and latitude, while its distance from the equator comes from an arc through the equator's poles and the star's place, and so its midheaven is with a part of the ecliptic other than its own when it has latitude, except one whose course falls at the first of Cancer or the first of Capricorn, for only then do its distance from the equator and its latitude come from one arc, so that the distance equals the declination with the star's latitude added or taken away. The scan reads 'the night' (al-layl) where the sense calls for 'the declination' (al-mayl), and it is read so here. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.51}.
