## Summary
This page gathers the recurrent elements of Arabic names as a research file supplied to the museum presents them: Abd and the compound names with it; Ibn and its forms; Abu and Umm and their change of case; and the difference between al- and Al. The museum did not open the pages cited. It joins the rest of [[concept/ilm-al-asma-wal-alqab|onomastics]].
## Abd al-
The word abd means servant, slave or worshipper. In Islamic personal names it is commonly combined with one of the names or attributes of God, which are known as theophoric names, names incorporating a divine designation {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names}. Grammatically they are possessive constructions, idafa, and the whole expression constitutes a single personal name though it contains several words. Abdel Rahman, Abdul Rahman, Abdurrahman and Abd al-Rahman are different representations of one Arabic name.

## The construction before Islam
The construction predates Islam: pre-Islamic Arabs used names such as Abd Shams, Abd Manaf, Abd al-Uzza and Abd al-Lat, servant of the sun, of Manaf, of al-Uzza and of al-Lat. By the summary Islam redirected this established pattern toward the worship of God alone, and the name Abd Allah itself was already known before Islam. It cites Sahih Muslim, hadith 2132: "The names most beloved to God are Abd Allah and Abd al-Rahman", given in Arabic as إِنَّ أَحَبَّ أَسْمَائِكُمْ إِلَى اللَّهِ عَبْدُ اللَّهِ وَعَبْدُ الرَّحْمَنِ {cite:sunnah-com-muslim-2132}.
## Ibn, Bin and Ben
These are forms or transliterations of the same Arabic word for sonship. Ibn is used in classical Arabic and scholarly transliteration, Bin is widely used in the Arabian Peninsula, Ben is common in North African names and European transliterations, B. is an abbreviation in academic publications, and Bint is for daughter of {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names}. Examples are Ibn Sina, Muhammad bin Salman and the North African surname Ben Youssef. In conventional spelling the initial alif of ibn is generally omitted when the word stands between a person's name and their father's name, as in Umar ibn al-Khattab, while Ibn Khaldun keeps it.
## Abu and Umm
Though their literal meanings are father and mother, they can also indicate possession, association or a characteristic: Abu al-Fadl and Abu al-Qasim for sons; Abu Hurayra, an association with a kitten; Abu Turab, an association with dust and a designation of Ali; and Umm Abd Allah, Umm Salama and Umm Habiba, mothers of named children {cite:jstor-schimmel-islamic-names} {cite:sunnah-com-abu-dawud-4970}. Abu changes by case in classical Arabic: Abu in the nominative, Aba in the accusative, Abi in the genitive, which is why Abu Talib and Ali ibn Abi Talib appear, and they do not refer to two people. ## Al- and Al
These two elements have completely different meanings though sometimes written identically in English: al- is the definite article, as in al-Bukhari; Al means family or house of, as in Al Saud; Banu means descendants of, as in Banu Hashim; and Ahl means people or household of, as in Ahl al-Bayt {cite:bnf-kitcat-arabic-names}. The distinction matters in genealogical research: Al Saud is the House of Saud, whereas al-Saudi is a relational adjective.
## The limits of the summary
The summary relies on a single hadith on the most beloved names and does not set out the variants of its transmission or its explanation, and does not detail the grammar of ibn. It does not say how Bin is pronounced or when its forms spread. For references see [[concept/kutub-al-ansab-wal-asma-wal-tarajim|books of genealogy, names and biographies]].
