## Summary
Sha'rawi takes up the difference between the prophet and the messenger in his reading of the verse that denies that God would give a human the scripture, wisdom and prophethood and then tell people to become his servants instead of God's ([[passage/quran-3-79|Al Imran 79]]). He holds that both are sent by God, though their functions differ {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan-vol-2 p.1561}.

## The function of the messenger
Sha'rawi says the messenger is both an intermediary of conveyance and a model of conduct. He receives the law, applies it to himself, and then conveys it, so that people see the law applied and do not only hear it {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan-vol-2 p.1561}{cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan-vol-2 p.1562}.

## The function of the prophet
The prophet, in Sha'rawi's account, is a model of conduct and not an intermediary of conveyance. He says the need for such a model appears in a time when the law is known but the soul is not made to keep it, as in the age he describes {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan-vol-2 p.1562}.

## Wisdom and rule
Sha'rawi notes that the verse names rule after scripture and prophethood, which shows that wisdom may be given to someone who is neither prophet nor messenger. The advice of Luqman to his son is his example. A person may be convinced by the law, act by it and convey it without claiming to be sent by God {cite:tafsir-sharawi-khawatir-scan-vol-2 p.1562}.

## Note on this entry
This is Sha'rawi's reading alone, taken from a scanned copy whose edition is not yet verified, and it is presented under his name. The distinction between the two words is disputed in works of creed and exegesis, and no other view has yet been checked (needs_source). See also [[concept/mithaq-al-nabiyyin|The covenant of the prophets]] and [[concept/rabbaniyyun|The rabbaniyyun]].

## In The Oxford Dictionary of Islam
The Oxford Dictionary of Islam glosses rasul as messenger of God and calls it one of two Qur'anic terms for Muhammad and the other prophets, the second being nabi, usually translated prophet. It reports that some scholars hold a rasul to be a nabi who was given a written revelation, a scripture, and delivered it, though the Qur'an seems to use the two words without distinction. In its account the Qur'an sets out a connected series of prophets and messengers, each linked with scriptures and sent by God: Adam, Noah, Abraham, David (his scripture is the Psalms), Solomon, Moses (bringer of the Torah), Jesus (his scripture is the Gospels) and Muhammad (bringer of the Qur'an). It cites [[passage/quran-10-47|10:47]] for the statement that every community (ummah) has had a rasul sent to it, says that all the messengers invite people to the worship of the one God and to the renunciation of evil, and says Muslims are called on to believe in every one of them. The profession of faith (shahada), it adds, consists of bearing witness to God's oneness and to Muhammad as God's messenger. {cite:oxford-dictionary-of-islam e1984}
