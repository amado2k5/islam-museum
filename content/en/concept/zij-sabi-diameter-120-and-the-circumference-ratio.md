## Summary
Al-Battani begins chapter 3 by saying the ancients differed on the ratio of a circle's diameter to its circumference though they approximated it, and then states that he does not need the exact ratio to set out the chords, because chords are known through their arcs; so he fixes the diameter at 120 parts as Ptolemy did, for the ease of the computing, and works on it in his book, which makes the chord of a sixth sixty parts, the radius. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.17}.

## The ancients' differing values
He says some held that the circumference is three diameters and a seventh of one, others three and ten parts of seventy-one and something, and that the value Ptolemy and the astronomers used lies between these, which he expresses as three, a tenth of the unit and a quarter of a sixth of the unit. The wording of the second value is uncertain in the printed text. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.17}.

## Why the diameter may be assumed
Al-Battani says the exact ratio is not needed in setting chords, since chords have no known measure to one another except through their arcs, so there is no harm in assuming the diameter as large as we wish. The choice of 120 parts is therefore one of convenience and no verdict on the true ratio. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.17}.

## The chord of a sixth and the radius
He shows that the chord of a sixth of any circle equals its radius, so it is sixty parts when the circle is 360 and the diameter 120. This is the measure on which all the other chords in the tables are built. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.17}.
