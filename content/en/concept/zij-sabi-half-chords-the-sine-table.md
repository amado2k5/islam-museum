## Summary
Al-Battani announces that what the computations of intersecting arcs need is known from the chords of half the arcs, so in his tables he takes half the chord of double each arc and writes it under that arc's share in the quarter, at steps of half a degree up to ninety, so that half the chord of a degree stands under half a degree and half the chord of 180 under ninety, which is sixty parts. He states that whenever he mentions chords in the book he means these half-chords, except what he names a full chord. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.19}.

## The circle and the two perpendicular diameters
Al-Battani pictures a circle cut by two diameters crossing at its centre at right angles, so dividing into four equal quarters at four angles, each enclosed by ninety parts of the circumference and two lines from the centre to it, each the length of the radius. He shows that the ratio of each arc's chord to the whole diameter equals the ratio of half that chord, the one under half the arc, to the radius, so the half-chord of the doubled arc is the one used. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.19}.

## Why the half
He explains that the half-chord is used so that halving the arc is not needed in the operations, and that Ptolemy worked with full chords for establishing the proof; he himself took half the chord of double each arc of the quarter and placed it under that arc's share. This is the function Arabic later names the jayb, the sine, and is here described as the halved chord. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.19}.

## The table's limits and values
Al-Battani says he set the differences of arcs in the tables at half a degree up to the full ninety, so that half the chord of one degree falls under half a degree, half the chord of two under one, half the chord of 120 under sixty, and half the chord of 180, the semicircle whose chord is the whole diameter, under ninety, which is the radius, of sixty parts. All the half-chords in the book are measured against it. {cite:zij-sabi-nallino-byblion p.19}.
