## Summary
This page sets out what a research summary says of diglossia in Arabic, the coexistence in one society of two varieties of one language, each with its own place. It holds Charles Ferguson's definition of 1959, Educated Spoken Arabic as Mitchell and El-Hassan described it, the five levels El-Said Badawi drew in 1973, and Clive Holes's view of 2004 that standard Arabic and the dialects are not separate compartments. Each statement is attributed to its page as the summary reported it, and the page says at its end what it did not reach. For the general picture of standard Arabic and the dialects see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects today]].

## Ferguson's definition
The summary credits Ferguson with the article "Diglossia", published in the journal Word, volume 15, pages 325 to 340, in 1959 {cite:ferguson-1959-diglossia}. It says he defined diglossia as a stable linguistic situation in which two distinct varieties of the same language coexist, each with strictly complementary social functions {cite:eisele-2002-approaching-diglossia} {cite:brill-mechanisms-arabic-multiglossia}. In Arabic the High variety (H) is the fusha, which the summary says is learned through formal education and used in writing, religion, news broadcasts and formal speeches. The Low variety (L) is the regional spoken dialects, acquired naturally as a first language and used in everyday conversation {cite:eisele-2002-approaching-diglossia}. The summary rests this on two pages: Eisele's paper of 2002, which cites Ferguson, and a Brill chapter on the mechanisms of Arabic multiglossia.

## Educated Spoken Arabic
The summary says later linguists showed that speakers bridge the two varieties in formal settings and in talk between speakers of different dialects. It names T. F. Mitchell's study "What is Educated Spoken Arabic?" in the International Journal of the Sociology of Language, number 61, 1986 {cite:mitchell-1986-what-is-educated-spoken-arabic}, and S. A. El-Hassan's "Variation in the Educated Spoken Arabic of Jordan" in Archivum Linguisticum, 1978 {cite:el-hassan-1978-educated-spoken-arabic-jordan}, and says that within the Leeds University project they documented a register named Educated Spoken Arabic. According to the summary it is a spoken, koine-like register formed by the constant interplay of written standard Arabic and the regional dialects, avoiding narrow localisms so that speakers from different regions can understand one another {cite:brill-eall-educated-arabic} {cite:cambridge-handbook-arabic-linguistics-formal-spoken}.

## Badawi's five levels
The summary says El-Said Badawi did not stop at a rigid two-part split but drew, in his book Mustawayat al-arabiyya al-muasira fi Misr (Levels of Contemporary Arabic in Egypt), 1973, a socio-stylistic continuum of five levels {cite:badawi-1973-mustawayat-al-arabiyya-al-muasira} {cite:brill-eall-educated-arabic} {cite:cambridge-handbook-arabic-linguistics-formal-spoken}:

| Level | Arabic name | Transliteration | Gloss |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | فصحى التراث | Fuṣḥā al-turāth | Classical Arabic of the heritage and the Qur'an |
| 2 | فصحى العصر | Fuṣḥā al-ʿaṣr | Modern Standard Arabic |
| 3 | عامية المثقفين | ʿĀmmiyyat al-muthaqqafīn | Educated Spoken Arabic |
| 4 | عامية المتنورين | ʿĀmmiyyat al-mutanawwirīn | semi-literate (enlightened) spoken Arabic |
| 5 | عامية الأميين | ʿĀmmiyyat al-ummiyyīn | illiterate spoken Arabic |

## Clive Holes
The summary adds that Clive Holes, in Modern Arabic: Structures, Functions, and Varieties, 2004, shows that standard Arabic and the dialects are not compartmentalised boxes, and that speakers move between the levels and mix them along this spectrum {cite:google-books-holes-modern-arabic}. The subject links to the page on [[concept/al-fusha-al-turathiyya-wa-fusha-al-asr|Classical Arabic and Modern Standard Arabic]] and to the page on [[concept/taalim-al-arabiyya-lighayr-al-natiqin-biha|teaching Arabic to non-native speakers]].

## What this page does not reach
The museum opened none of the pages named; it did not read Ferguson's article, Badawi's book or the studies by Mitchell and El-Hassan, so what is said of them is what the summary reported. The summary gives no share of speakers at each level and no sharp boundaries between the levels. The Arabic names given for the five levels are the usual Arabic forms of the transliterated names the summary lists; the museum did not check that scholars agree on the boundaries between them. This page does not decide between linguists who describe the Arabic situation as diglossia and those who prefer multiglossia.
