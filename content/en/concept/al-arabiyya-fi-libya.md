## Summary
This page sets out the place of [[language/arabic|Arabic]] in [[place/libya|Libya]] as it appears in the People section of the Encyclopaedia Britannica article and in the Libya page of The World Factbook. It ascribes each statement to its page and estimates no number of speakers. The constitutional text is added in its original wording, as supplied in a research summary that the museum did not check against the page.

## Official status
The encyclopedia says almost all Libyans speak Arabic, the country's official language, that English is still widely used as a second language in international business and politics, and that the government's embrace of Arab nationalism reduced Western influences {cite:britannica-libya-people}. The Factbook says: Arabic (official), Italian, English (all widely understood in the major cities) {cite:cia-factbook-libya}. The encyclopedia also lists Libya, in its article on the Semitic languages, among the places where Arabic is official {cite:britannica-semitic-languages}. The three sources agree on official status, and the Factbook adds Italian among the understood languages.

## Constitutional text
The Constitutional Declaration of 2011, Article 1. The research summary supplied to the museum gives this Arabic wording: «واللغة الرسمية هي اللغة العربية و تضمن الدولة الليبية الحقوق الثقافية لكل مكونات المجتمع الليبي و تعتبر لغاتها لغات وطنية.», which the museum translates: "The official language is the Arabic language, and the Libyan State guarantees the cultural rights of all components of Libyan society and considers their languages national languages". The summary names the Supreme Judicial Council of Libya as the official source; the museum did not open that page, so the wording is taken from the summary as supplied, and the founder approved using it so on 21 November 2026 {cite:official-constitution-libya}. The summary also names the DCAF Libya Legal Database as a second place for the text, and describes the article as designating Arabic the official language while guaranteeing cultural rights for all components of Libyan society and recognising their languages as national languages {cite:dcaf-libya-constitutional-declaration-2011}.

## Language shares
No official figure. Language was not collected in the 2006 census, in the MICS 2024–25 or by UNSD. The summary lists Tamazight, Tebu, Tuareg and Arabic beside the entry, without shares. Issuing bodies and years named in the summary: BSC Libya / UNSD, 2006 / 2025. ## The standard and the spoken
The two pages say nothing on the Libyan dialect or its relation to the standard, so the museum sets no classification of it; for the duality in general see [[concept/al-fusha-wal-lahajat|Fusha and the dialects]]. What the encyclopedia says of origin is that Libyans claim descent from the Bedouin Arab tribes of the Banu Hilal and Banu Sulaym, said to have invaded the Maghrib in the 11th century {cite:britannica-libya-people}. The encyclopedia ascribes this account to those who hold it; it is not the museum's verdict. For the region as a whole it says that after the conquest of the 7th and 8th centuries the Arabs imposed on the native peoples of the Maghrib the religion of Islam and Arabic, the language of the Qur'an {cite:britannica-maghrib}.

## Other languages in the country
The encyclopedia says the Imazighen are believed to be Libya's earliest inhabitants, that their main groups were the Luata, Nefusa and Adassa, and that most have been assimilated into Arab society except on the Nafusah Plateau and at Awjilah, Hun, Socra and Zuwarah, spellings as the page prints them. Their languages are classified as Afro-Asiatic and have adopted the Arabic alphabet; many are bilingual in Nafusi and Arabic, and most are Sunni Muslims {cite:britannica-libya-people}. It also names a community of some 30,000 people known in North Africa as the Dom. The Factbook lists under Tamazight: Nafusi, Ghadamis, Suknah, Awjilah, Tamasheq {cite:cia-factbook-libya}, and gives the ethnic groups as Amazigh and Arab together 97 percent and others 3 percent. Compare [[concept/lughat-al-qabail|the languages of the tribes]].

## From the history of the language
This page read no detailed history of the language in Libya beyond the descent claimed from the Arab tribes and the encyclopedia's statement on the Maghrib as a whole, and its note that Amazigh speakers write in the Arabic alphabet. There is no history of the standard or of the Libyan colloquial in what was read. For Libya among the Arab states, and the suspension of its League participation in 2011 as the encyclopedia states it, see [[concept/al-alam-al-arabi|the Arab states and the League]].
