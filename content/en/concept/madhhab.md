## Overview
The madhhab in juristic usage is the method of ijtihad traced to an imam and his school; the jurists enumerate the 'four madhhabs' — Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3}.

## In the sources
The Kuwaiti Fiqh Encyclopedia applies 'madhhab' throughout to the imams and their four schools {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3}; Fiqh al-Sunna presents the rulings across the madhhabs {cite:sabiq-fiqh-al-sunnah}.

## For Sprouts
In legal usage a madhhab is a way of reasoning and deriving rulings, traced to an imam and his students. The four famous Sunni schools are the Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali.

## For Explorers
A madhhab is a way scholars use to understand religion and derive its rulings, named after a known imam and his students. The four best-known Sunni schools are the Hanafi, the Maliki, the Shafi'i and the Hanbali.

## For Scholars
In the legal sense a madhhab is a method of reasoning and derivation traced to an imam and his followers. Jurists speak of the four schools: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali. They differ on the principles of inference, on giving priority to analogy, juristic preference or public interest, on following the companions' opinions, and on many details of the branches, all of them efforts of reasoning based on the text. The museum presents the schools as methods of understanding and deriving rulings, does not prefer one over another, and states each school's known principles with their source.

## In al-Sha'rawi's programme "Liqa' al-Iman"
In episode 13 of [[work/likaa-al-iman|Liqa' al-Iman]] [[person/muhammad-mutawalli-al-shaarawi|al-Sha'rawi]] is asked whether a young man must follow a particular school. He says it depends on the person: whoever is qualified to be followed, knowing what God and the Messenger said and what was abrogated, has his own view like an imam, and whoever is not is a follower (muqallid) who asks those who know. {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep13 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep13 00:02:30} He illustrates this by saying a Muslim is not asked to read every book on the pilgrimage in order to make it once, but asks a scholar, as he goes to a doctor to keep his health, and he holds that studying the law is a collective duty discharged by a group from each community. {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep13 00:02:30} {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep13 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep13 00:03:30} He criticises a youth of seventeen or eighteen issuing rulings without knowledge, saying people would not accept anyone claiming to be a doctor or a lawyer, so how has religion become so cheap? {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep13 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep13 00:04:00}

In episode 17 he notes that for Abu Hanifa a man who fornicated with a woman may not marry her daughter, and even a man who looked at his mother-in-law with desire is barred from her daughter, while al-Shafi'i permitted it. {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep17 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep17 00:08:00} He then tells the questioner who asks for al-Shafi'i's proofs that he is a follower, and a follower does not ask for proofs, as a patient does not ask the doctor how the medicine is made up. {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep17 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep17 00:08:30} In episode 11 he notes that al-Shafi'i required forty for the Friday prayer, and says analogy is with Abu Hanifa and that this is the stronger view to him. {cite:assabile-muhammad-metwally-al-shaarawy likaa-al-iman/ep11 00:03:30}
