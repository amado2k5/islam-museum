## Summary
Chapter 6 of Fann 2 opens by setting out what hot, cold, moist and dry air does to the body, then separates turbid air from thick air and gives the signs and cause of each {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.118-119}.

## Hot and cold air
Hot air dissolves and relaxes. In moderation it reddens the colour by drawing blood outward; in excess it yellows the colour by dissolving what it draws, and it increases sweat, lessens urine, weakens digestion and brings thirst. Cold air tightens and strengthens digestion and increases urine, since the moistures are held in and little dissolved through sweat; it lessens stool because the muscles of the anus are squeezed, so stool stays and its watery part is dissolved into the urine {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.118-119}.

## Moist and dry air
Moist air softens the skin and moistens the body; dry air hardens the body and dries the skin {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.119}.

## Turbid and thick air
Turbid air makes the soul uneasy and stirs the humours. It is not the same as thick air: thick air is uniformly dense in its substance, while turbid air is mixed with coarse bodies. Both show in the few small stars seen and the dimmer glitter of the fixed stars, and both come from many vapours and smokes and few good winds {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.119}.

## Rulings of each season
Ibn Sina adds that each season has its own rulings, and that the end of one season and the start of the next share the rulings and diseases of both; he then takes the seasons one by one {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.119}.
