## Summary
The Zabad inscription is a trilingual text in Greek, Syriac and Old Arabic on a basalt door-lintel of the martyrium of Saint Sergios at Zabad, south-east of Aleppo in northern Syria, dated to 512 CE or 24 September 511 CE and now held in the Art and History Museum in Brussels. It is a Christian dedicatory inscription {cite:oxford-cult-of-saints-e01817-zabad} {cite:wikipedia-zabad-inscription-en}.

## Date and calendar
The summary states that the inscription is dated to 24 Gorpiaios, that is Elul, in year 823 of the Seleucid era, which equals 24 September 511 CE by the Macedonian calendar of northern Syria, and 512 CE in the other form it gives {cite:oxford-cult-of-saints-e01817-zabad} {cite:wikipedia-zabad-inscription-en}. The date is recorded here as 512 CE with 511 kept as a stated alternative; the museum does not choose between them, and it is derived from a local calendar.

## Find-place and where it is now
The inscription is on the door-lintel of a martyr shrine of Saint Sergios at Zabad near Anasartha, south-east of Aleppo in northern Syria, and the lintel is basalt {cite:oxford-cult-of-saints-e01817-zabad} {cite:wikipedia-zabad-inscription-en}. The summary says it is now in the Art and History Museum in Brussels; the museum adds nothing to that and did not open the two pages the summary draws on.

## Language and script
The inscription is trilingual: Greek, Syriac and Old Arabic, each in its own text on the lintel {cite:oxford-cult-of-saints-e01817-zabad} {cite:wikipedia-zabad-inscription-en}. The Arabic part is what the summary calls Paleo-Arabic, a single line beside the other two texts. Its presence among Greek and Syriac shows Arabic being written at a Christian shrine serving a community of several languages on the edge of the Levant.

## What it says
The Greek and Syriac texts record the founding of the martyrium of Saint Sergios and name its founders, architects and donors, while the Arabic line asks God to be mindful of five named people, among them Sirgu son of Amat-Manafu, Hani' or Hali' son of Mara' l-Qays, and Sirgu son of Sa'du, with two others {cite:oxford-cult-of-saints-e01817-zabad} {cite:wikipedia-zabad-inscription-en}. It is a text of prayer and of names, not a story or a chronicle. The names are given as the summary transliterates them, and in one of them a letter is not settled between two readings.

## Why it matters for the history of Arabic
The inscription comes nearly two centuries after the [[object/namara-inscription|Namara inscription]], and in the summary it is a witness to Arabic in a text that shares the stone with two other languages, so Arabic appears in a Christian setting and in the names of Arab people. Its value to the history of the language is that it sets Old Arabic beside Greek and Syriac in one religious building, about a century before Islam. It belongs to [[concept/aqdam-al-nuqush-al-arabiyya|the earliest Arabic inscriptions]], and the summary says no more than this.

## Basis and what is uncertain
This entry rests on a research summary supplied to the museum. The museum did not open the pages the summary cites; the founder approved using the summary as it stands on 21 November 2026, so every fact here is the summary's report of those pages, not something the museum checked itself. The account of the text is a paraphrase, not a transcription, and the reservations and alternative readings the summary states are kept as they are. What stays uncertain, as the summary reports it, is the date between 511 and 512 CE, the reading of one letter in one of the five names, and the identity of the two names not given. The museum has not opened it; its title is about the Qur'anic Jesus, and the file gives the dates 512, 528 and 568 CE for these inscriptions. Wikipedia is used here only for the entity facts.
