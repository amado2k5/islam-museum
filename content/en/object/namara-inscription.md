## Summary
The Namara inscription, also called the epitaph of Imru' al-Qays ibn 'Amr, is a funerary text of five lines in Old Arabic in the Nabataean script on a basalt lintel, dated 7 December 328 CE. It was found at al-Namara in southern Syria and is now in the Musée du Louvre, Paris, as AO 4083 {cite:louvre-collections-namara-ao-4083} {cite:wikipedia-namara-inscription-en}.

## Date and calendar
The inscription is dated to the seventh day of Kaslul in year 223 of the Nabataean era of Bostra, which the summary gives as 7 December 328 CE {cite:louvre-collections-namara-ao-4083} {cite:wikipedia-namara-inscription-en}. It is a complete date with day, month and year, but the conversion from the local era to the common calendar comes from the two sources the summary reports, so it is recorded as derived.

## Find-place and where it is now
The stone was found in 1901 at al-Namara, roughly 100 km south of Damascus in southern Syria, and is today held by the Musée du Louvre in Paris under the number AO 4083 {cite:louvre-collections-namara-ao-4083} {cite:wikipedia-namara-inscription-en}. The summary says no more about the circumstances of its discovery or its journey, and the museum adds nothing to it. Damascus has its own record at [[place/damascus|Damascus]].

## Language and script
The text is in Old Arabic in the Nabataean script, on a basalt lintel, in five lines {cite:wikipedia-namara-inscription-en}. That means the language is Arabic while the letters are still Nabataean, the stage the summary ties to the rise of the Arabic script; the Encyclopaedia Britannica says the Arabic alphabet probably developed in the 4th century CE as a direct descendant of the Nabataean {B}. The inscription belongs to the early fourth century, the same century.

## What it says
The text is the funerary epitaph of Imru' al-Qays ibn 'Amr, whom it calls "king of the Arabs"; it recounts his military campaigns against Arab tribes, among them Asad, Nizar, Madhhij and Ma'add, his role as an ally of Rome or one of its phylarchs, and the date of his death {cite:wikipedia-namara-inscription-en}. The name is Imru' al-Qays ibn 'Amr, a different man from the poet [[person/imru-al-qays-ibn-hujr|Imru' al-Qays ibn Hujr]], whose record the museum keeps; that is a note of the museum on the name and is not in the summary.

## Why it matters for the history of Arabic
The inscription comes about sixty years after the [[object/raqosh-epitaph-jsnab-17|Raqosh epitaph]], and it is a text in Old Arabic in Nabataean letters for a ruler who names the Arab tribes. Its value to the history of the language is that it is a dated witness to Arabic in the southern Levant about three centuries before Islam. It is one link in [[concept/aqdam-al-nuqush-al-arabiyya|the earliest Arabic inscriptions]], and the summary says no more than this.
