## Overview
This illustrated copy of the *Bustan* (The Orchard) of [[person/saadi-al-shirazi|Sa'di of Shiraz]] (ca. 1200-1290), of 229 folios and 43 miniatures, is in the National Museum, New Delhi (48.6/4), and was made at Mandu, capital of the sultanate of [[place/malwa|Malwa]], around 1500-1503. {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 134} The catalogue recalls that Sa'di's moral tales amused and enlightened readers throughout Islam and that several copies of his *Gulistan* and *Bustan* were brilliantly illustrated in India. {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 134}

## Scribe, patron and painter
It was written in an unusually bold but graceful nasta'liq by Shahsuvar al-Katib for [[person/nasir-al-din-khalji-malwa|Nasir al-Din Khalji]] of Malwa (r. 1500-1511), the second patron of the *Ni'matnama*; the sultan's name and titles are in a shamsa on folio 1r. {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 134} The colophon page carries an inspection date of AH 908 (1502-3), so it must have been finished early in his reign. {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 134} Inscriptions on folios 1 and 190r name the painter and illuminator as Haji Mahmud, who illustrated the *Ni'matnama* at Mandu; the other forty-one pictures appear to come from the same workshop if not the same hand. {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 134}

## Abraham and the fire-worshipper
One picture shows the tale of the hospitable patriarch Abraham, who invited a frail old wanderer to dine; noticing his silence at grace, he realised the guest was a Zoroastrian and drove him back into the desert hungry, whereupon an angel rebuked him: for a century God had fed this man, and now he presumed to withhold his hand. {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 134}

## Its style
The stumpy trees on the horizon, the logical handling of space and architecture, the ornament and the figure types show a faint but recognisable awareness of [[person/kamal-al-din-behzad|Bihzad]], the late Timurid master of Herat. {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 134} Since the Mandu patrons and artists are unlikely to have seen his paintings, his style must have reached them at second or third hand, which Welch takes as a tribute both to Bihzad's power and to the level of artistic appreciation at Mandu. {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 134}
