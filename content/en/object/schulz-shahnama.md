## Overview
The Schulz Shahnama is a dispersed manuscript of Firdawsi's epic named after the German scholar who once owned it; most of its known miniatures are in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, among them *Rustam Thrown into the Sea by the Demon Akvan* (1974.290.17). {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 128} Welch counts its pictures the best drawn, among the most vigorous and probably the earliest surviving from the Sultanate period. {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 129}

## Rustam and Akvan
The picture tells how Rustam, exhausted after searching for the demon Akvan, lay down to sleep; the demon cut out the turf around him, lifted him into the air and asked whether he would rather be flung onto the mountains or drowned in the sea; knowing the demon would do the opposite, Rustam chose the mountains, was thrown into the sea, fought off crocodiles and swam to freedom. {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 128} Welch describes the painting as done with the utmost humour and economy: the waves calm where Rustam swims, and the blossoms near him peep out in encouragement. {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 128}

## Date and attribution
Its date is hazarded by comparison with early fourteenth-century material usually associated with Iran, including two small-format Shahnama copies. {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 129} Because early Sultanate painting is so rare, the author can only speculate on its origin: its bright flat colours and large areas of vermilion look ahead to seventeenth-century pictures from [[place/malwa|Malwa]], which [[person/ala-al-din-khalji|Ala al-Din Khalji]] annexed in 1305 and which was governed from Delhi until it became independent in 1401. {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 129} He thinks the pictures belong to the tradition of the Khaljis or of the Tughluqs who followed them in 1320, painted at Delhi or in Malwa by an artist probably trained in Iran whose style grew bolder in India; already Indian are the vermilion sky, the large flowers and the well-observed elephants, unknown in Iranian painting. {cite:welch-india-art-and-culture-1985 p. 129}
