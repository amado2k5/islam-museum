## Overview
The kiswa is the black covering of the [[building/kaaba|Kaaba]], embroidered with gold; it is replaced each year {cite:wikidata Q1511204}. It used to be replaced during the hajj, on the morning of 9 Dhu al-Hijja; Arab News reported in July 2022 that the yearly event had been moved to the eve of 1 Muharram, and that the new cover was installed at the dawn of the year 1444 {cite:arabnews-kiswa-1444-2022}. [[person/ibn-fadl-allah-al-umari|Ibn Fadl Allah al-Umari]] gathers its history in his [[work/masalik-al-absar|Masalik al-absar]]: he quotes [[person/al-azraqi-muhammad-ibn-abd-allah|al-Azraqi]], from Ibn Jurayj, that Tubba' was the first to give the Kaaba a full covering; people then went on covering it in the pre-Islamic period {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.139}. He reports from al-Azraqi that [[person/muhammad|the Prophet]] covered the Kaaba, then Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, Mu'awiya, Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr and those after them {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.139}. Al-Azraqi also relates that [[person/abd-allah-al-mamun|al-Ma'mun]] covered it three times a year, in red brocade, Coptic linen and white brocade, beginning the white in 206 AH {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.138}.

Al-Umari describes the kiswa of his own day: put on once a year in the pilgrimage season, it came from the sultan's treasury in Egypt with the caravan; the Sharifs and the Banu Shayba took the old one and divided it, and its pieces were carried to other lands for blessing {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.138}. The ruler of Yemen used to send a covering worn beneath the Egyptian one, both of black silk with white writing {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.138}. By custom the Kaaba was washed with Zamzam water on 27 Dhu al-Qa'da, its hangings were tucked up, and it was dressed on the day of the Adha {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.139}. Al-Umari says that on his pilgrimage of 738 AH he took off the old covering and put on the new with his own hands {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.138}.

## Impact
Its traditions run from the pre-Islamic period to the modern Mecca workshop; Saudipedia's timeline records a workshop ordered in the Ajyad district of Mecca in 1927, moved to Jarwal in 1962, and a new factory in Umm al-Jud ordered in 1972 and inaugurated in 1977; in 2017 the factory was renamed the King Abdulaziz Complex for the Holy Kaaba Kiswa {cite:saudipedia-kiswa-complex-timeline}. Arab News reported that the cover installed at the start of 1444 used about 670 kilograms of raw silk, 120 kilograms of gold thread and 100 kilograms of silver thread {cite:arabnews-kiswa-1444-2022}.

## Form and customary ceremony
The Encyclopaedia of Islam has no separate article titled Kiswa; the material below is gathered from its articles on the Ka'ba, the mahmal, the pilgrimage, clothing and the minbar, and it is therefore shorter than a dedicated entry would be. Those articles describe the four walls of the [[building/kaaba|Ka'ba]] as hung with a curtain, the kiswa, which in Egypt is called the burqu', the veil. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 335} In front of the door hangs a separate curtain embroidered in gold and silver with many inscriptions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 335} The article describes the kiswa as usually of black brocade with the profession of faith worked into the weave, and about two thirds of the way up runs a gold-embroidered band, the hizam, bearing Qur'anic texts. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 335} The curtains are sometimes fixed to the ground with rings and cords, or the lower edge is raised by cords out of reach of human hands. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 335} At one period the black cloth was replaced by a white one from the 25th or 28th of Dhu al-Qa'da until the end of the pilgrimage, though witnesses told the author that this practice had stopped. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 335} Each year, when the curtains are replaced, the Banu Shayba cut the old ones into small pieces that are shared out and sold as relics. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 335}

## Earlier practice
The article on the Ka'ba reports that the custom of covering the building is said to have been introduced by the Tubba'. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 337} In its account, the annual re-covering became an established custom only in modern times. In the earliest Muslim period the day of Ashura is named as the day of covering, but the building also changed its covering in Rajab and in other months. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 337} The cloth was sometimes Yemeni and sometimes Egyptian or from elsewhere, and various colours are mentioned. During the caliphate of Umar the building is said to have been in danger of collapsing under the weight of the many coverings hung on it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 337} The same article adds that the Wahhabis once covered the Ka'ba with a red cloth. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 337}

A related practice concerned the Prophet's minbar in Medina. The article on the minbar says that under the Abbasids a new kiswa was sent for it every year from Baghdad, and that the sultans who came later did not renew it so often. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 95}

## Egypt as provider
According to the Ka'ba article, since Mamluk times, from the middle of the seventh century AH, the thirteenth century CE, the kiswa has traditionally been supplied by Egypt, except in periods of political tension, for which it gives 1926 to 1936 and the years after 1962. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 335} The article on the mahmal explains how this came about. In the Mamluk period Yemen and Egypt were rivals to offer the hangings of the Ka'ba, a gift that had always been a point of honour for the caliphs. The sharif of Mecca then promised [[person/qalawun|Qalawun]] in 681 AH/1282 CE that only the Egyptian hangings would be hung. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 54} The article reads this against the background of [[person/baybars|Baybars]]'s policy towards the Holy Places after the Abbasid caliphate had passed to [[place/cairo|Cairo]], presenting him as protector of the caliph, some of whose privileges of honour he inherited. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 54}

The pilgrim caravan from Egypt carried the new exterior hangings on camels apart from the palanquin, and the journey from Cairo to Mecca took about thirty-five days. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 43} The article names the [[concept/mahmal|mahmal]] as the palanquin that travelled with that caravan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 43}

## The kiswa and the mahmal
The article on the mahmal is careful to separate the two. It calls legends that the palanquin held Qur'ans or carried the Ka'ba hangings poorly founded, and states that the mahmal was normally empty. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 54} It also calls the Western phrase sacred cloth for the mahmal pure fantasy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 54} The mahmal in its political sense, an ornamented palanquin on a camel sent by a ruler with the pilgrim caravan, appears to be a Mamluk creation, first sent by Baybars in 664 AH/1266 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 53} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 54} The camel that bore the Egyptian mahmal rested all year at the dar al-kiswa on Khoronfish Street in Cairo. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 54}

Other rulers sent their own mahmals and hangings, among them Yemen from 1297 CE, Iraq from 718 AH/1319 CE and Syria from 692 AH/1293 CE, and there were disputes over precedence in which Egypt always claimed first place. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 54} After the Ottoman takeover of Egypt and Syria, Istanbul assumed this policy of prestige. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 54}

## Interruptions and the modern period
When the Wahhabis took the Holy Places in 1807, the Egyptian mahmal fell into their hands and was burnt, and its sending resumed only after their defeat. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 54} The Egyptian mahmal went on being sent until 1926, when a clash between its escort and the Ikhwan led to its despatch being stopped for good. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 54} The article presents a dispute over the ceremonial of its reception, first between the Egyptian government and King [[person/husayn-ibn-ali-sharif-makkah|Husayn]] of the Hijaz, then in negotiations with [[person/ibn-saud|Ibn Saud]], whose side objected to the accompanying music and to practices it judged superstitious. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 54} In 1937 the kiswa was again sent and accepted, although the mahmal was formally refused. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 55} The Egyptian mahmal processions inside Cairo, re-established that year, lasted until 1952, when they were abolished. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 55}

## A namesake: the Fatimid dar al-kiswa
The same word names a different institution. The article on clothing records that the Fatimid caliph [[person/al-muizz-li-din-allah|al-Mu'izz]], who died in 365 AH/975 CE, founded a government costume supply house called the dar al-kiswa or khizanat al-kiswa, with an outlay given as 600,000 dinars, to clothe officials and functionaries in ceremonial dress. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 186} This was a wardrobe for the court, not the covering of the Ka'ba, and the article does not link the two. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.2 p. 186}

## In al-Umari's Masalik al-absar
[[person/ibn-fadl-allah-al-umari|Ibn Fadl Allah al-Umari]] gathers reports on the covering of the Ka'ba: the historians' disagreement over who first covered it with brocade, al-Hajjaj according to Ibn Ishaq, Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr according to Ibn Bakkar, Khalid ibn Ja'far ibn Kilab according to al-Mawardi, and the vow of Nutayla, mother of al-Abbas ibn Abd al-Muttalib, reported by several including al-Daraqutni. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.137} Then al-Azraqi's report that Mu'awiya covered it with brocade, and that al-Ma'mun covered it three times a year: red brocade on the day of Tarwiya, Coptic linen at the new moon of Rajab and white brocade on 27 Ramadan, beginning the white in 206 AH. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.138} He quotes al-Azraqi from Ibn Jurayj that Tubba' was the first to give it a full covering. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.139}

He describes the covering in his own day: it was put on once a year in the pilgrimage season, brought from the sultan's treasury in Egypt with the caravan; the Sharifs and the Banu Shayba took the old one and divided it, and its pieces were carried to every land for blessing; the ruler of Yemen used to send a covering that was put on beneath the Egyptian one, both of black silk with white writing. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.138} The Ka'ba was washed with Zamzam water on 27 Dhu al-Qa'da, its hangings were tucked up, and it was dressed on the day of the Adha. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.139} He says that on his pilgrimage of 738 AH he took off the old covering and put on the new with his own hands. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.138}
