## Overview
A gilded Kufic Qur'an on indigo-blue parchment, from the ninth or tenth century CE, probably from the Maghrib or al-Andalus; its leaves were scattered among museums after it was broken up. {cite:wikidata}

## Description
According to [[person/sheila-blair|Sheila Blair]], it was a seven-part Qur'an on parchment dyed a deep blue with indigo, each page with fifteen lines of kufic in gold ink outlined in black, occasional pointing in gold, and vocalisation, verse markers and sura headings in silver that has often tarnished. The cost of its materials shows it was a very special codex. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 126} {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 166}

## Attribution
The earliest explanation, put forward by F. R. Martin in 1912, was that al-Ma'mun commissioned it for the tomb of his father Harun al-Rashid at Mashhad because blue was the colour of mourning; Blair finds little evidence for this "fantasy", which may have been created to conceal how Martin acquired the leaves in Istanbul or to raise their price. Jonathan Bloom used the manuscript itself to argue for the Maghrib: its verses are counted with abjad letters in the Maghribi rather than the eastern order. He then localised it to mid-tenth-century Tunisia, since it was the first item in an inventory of the library of the Great Mosque of Kairouan compiled in 693/1293, described as a Qur'an in seven sections in an aloeswood case decorated with copper inlaid with gold. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 166} {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 167}

## The Byzantine connection
Bloom suggested it imitated imperial Byzantine manuscripts written in gold on murex-purple parchment, Muslims substituting indigo for the dye they lacked, perhaps after the Fatimids received Byzantine embassies and began imitating Byzantine royal objects. A later dating is supported by the dry-point ruling of its folios, unlike most kufic Qur'ans attributed to the ninth century or earlier. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 126} {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 167}

## Impact
It is regarded as the most beautiful known Qur'an from the Islamic West; the use of blue parchment is almost unique among Islamic manuscripts. {cite:wikidata}
