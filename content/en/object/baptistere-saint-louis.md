## Overview
A Mamluk brass basin inlaid with gold and silver, made between 1325 and 1340 CE, in the second quarter of the fourteenth century, in Egypt or Syria, and attributed by the Louvre to the craftsman Muhammad ibn al-Zayn; the kings of France used it for centuries as a baptismal basin for their sons. {cite:louvre-collections cl010318774}

## Impact
In the Louvre; a unique example of an Islamic object that became a ritual in the French crown, and its decoration is among the finest Mamluk engraving. {cite:wikidata}

## Collection record
The Louvre holds this basin in its Department of Islamic Arts under the number LP 16 and dates it to 1325-1340. The museum's earlier summary above gives about 1300, taken from Wikidata; the Louvre's own catalogue date is the later one. The basin is hammered brass, engraved and inlaid with silver and gold. It is signed in several places in thuluth and naskh script: work of the master Muhammad ibn al-Zayn on the outer rim, as read by Reinaud in 1828, and work of Ibn al-Zayn on the cups and throne back, as read by Gaston Wiet in 1932. Short inscriptions name an inkwell and give a vessel's voice, I am a container for carrying food, read by de Longperier in 1866. The Louvre gives Egypt or Syria as the place of making, each with a question mark. It entered the Louvre by transfer on 26 January 1793. {cite:louvre-collections cl010318774}
