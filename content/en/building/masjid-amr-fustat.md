## Overview
A great mosque in the city of [[place/al-fustat|Fustat]], built by [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn al-As]] on the site of his tent and the land around it. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p. 209}

The site of his tent is where the mihrab and the minbar are. About eighty of the companions prayed in it. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p. 209}

## The mosque and the city in al-Maqrizi
Al-Maqrizi says that when Amr ibn al-As camped beside the fortress he laid out the mosque known as the Old Mosque, also called the Mosque of Amr ibn al-As, and the Arab tribes settled around it, so that a city known as Fustat came into being. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.831}

After the conquest, the Nile's waters withdrew for some years from the land opposite the fortress and the Old Mosque. The Muslims tethered their animals there, and then laid out houses on it little by little. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.831}

In another place al-Maqrizi says that the Muslims laid out Fustat around the mosque of Amr ibn al-As and the Qasr al-Sham'. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.990}

## In Ibn Abd al-Hakam
Amr built the mosque on ground surrounded by gardens and vines; they stretched ropes until the line was true, and Amr stood until the qibla was set, Amr and Companions of the Prophet setting it. {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 133} He made a minbar in it, and Umar wrote: "Is it not enough for you to stand while the Muslims are beneath your heels?", and ordered him to break it. {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 133} {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 134} Abu al-Khayr reports that Abu Muslim al-Ghafiqi, a Companion of the Prophet, was Amr's muezzin, and that he saw him perfuming the mosque with incense. {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 134} Amr wrote to Umar that he had laid out a house for him by the mosque; Umar answered, "How should a man in the Hijaz have a house in Egypt?", and ordered it made a market for the Muslims. {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 134}

## Fustat and its first mosque in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls al-Fustat the first city founded in Egypt by the Muslim conquerors and the first residence of the Arab governors. It rose on the east bank of the Nile beside the Greco-Coptic township of [[place/babylon-of-egypt|Babylon]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 999} Among the meanings proposed for the name is "tent", since the town was founded where Amr had pitched his tent during the siege of Babylon. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 999} When Amr returned from the first siege of [[place/alexandria|Alexandria]], probably early in 22/643, he laid the foundations of a permanent encampment that was gradually turned into a town; the nearness of Babylon let the Arabs employ and control Coptic officials. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1000}

After the distribution of the land came the building of the mosque, called the Mosque of Amr or the Old Mosque. The article describes it as the first mosque built in Egypt, measuring at first fifty by thirty cubits. It may have had a minbar from the start, while the niche-shaped mihrab seems to have been built only in 92/711, and after several reconstructions and enlargements the mosque reached its present dimensions in 212/827. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1000} It served at once as a place of prayer, a council chamber, a court room, a post office and lodging for travellers, and the main grants of leases of land were made there. Nearby stood Amr's house and the army stores, and an open-air prayer ground. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1000}

## The tribal quarters around the mosque
Each tribe was assigned a fixed zone, a khitta, which the article equates with the quarter or ward of later Cairo. Some quarters mixed tribes: the quarter of the Ahl al-Raya surrounded Amr's mosque, another lay just to its north, and a further one was kept for new arrivals who could not settle with their own tribes. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1000} Each quarter had its own mosque, and in 53/673 minarets were built for Amr's mosque and for those of the quarters, with two exceptions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1000} The article on mosques adds that Umar is said to have written to Amr in Egypt, as to the governors of Basra and Kufa, to build a congregational mosque and mosques for the tribes, and that the tribes of each quarter did have their own mosques around the Mosque of Amr. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 676}

## Orientation, minarets and fittings
The article on the qibla notes that the first mosque in Egypt was built in Fustat facing due east and a few years later was altered to face the winter sunrise, about 27 degrees south of east. The author suggests reasons linked to the corners and walls of the Kaaba and adds that the historical sources do not state them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 196} The same article observes that the Cairo of the Fatimids was later laid out in the orientation it calls "the qibla of the Companions". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 196}

On minarets the article on the minaret relies on al-Maqrizi, who says that Muawiya ordered four towers for the call to prayer at the mosque and that Maslama set four of them at its corners. The author considers that the word al-Maqrizi uses could instead mean that Maslama merely heightened the four corners, and judges that these towers were probably short, solid up to roof level and reached by ladders, resembling the corner towers of the Damascus temenos. He adds that, by al-Maqrizi's account, at dawn a muezzin stood at each one and their combined call resounded through the silent city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 382} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 383} The call was also made from a chamber on the roof, enlarged in 336/947-8 and left in place when Baybars cleared the roof of its many chambers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 690}

The article on mosques records that the courtyard of Amr's mosque was planted with trees, so that al-Maqrizi's remark that it had no courtyard probably means the space was very narrow; the floor was bare or covered with small stones until Maslama covered it with mats, and in the Mamluk period it was paved entirely in marble. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 688} The halls held several compartments used for teaching. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 689} The article on the minbar reports, citing al-Qudai, that Amr had a minbar made and that Umar ordered it removed, so that Amr should not raise himself above the Muslims; after Umar's death Amr is said to have used one, which stood until Qurra ibn Sharik rebuilt the mosque and set up a new one in 92/711. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 95} Qurra is also credited with the first concave mihrab in Egypt, built in 92/710-11 in this mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 19} In 403/1012-13 al-Hakim presented the mosque with 1,289 copies of the Quran, some written in gold lettering. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 690}

## The mosque in the later history of Fustat
In the Fatimid period Fustat remained a wealthy city of tall houses, with crowded markets around Amr's mosque, while Cairo became the city of the caliphs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1000} In 564 the vizier, fearing that the Frankish armies would use the undefended town as a base, had it evacuated and burned; the fire lasted fifty-four days. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1001} Afterwards [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Salah al-Din]] built a wall around Cairo, the citadel and Fustat, remains of which lie east and south-east of Amr's mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1001} The article adds that the mosque stayed a flourishing centre of religious instruction until the great plague of 749/1348; under the Mamluks Cairo drew the trade, the name Fustat was replaced by Misr, and the old city fell into obscurity. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1001}

## Enlargements of the mosque
[[person/maslama-ibn-mukhallad-al-ansari|Maslama ibn Mukhallad]] enlarged the congregational mosque after Amr's building; it was he who required the people of Egypt to build minarets for the mosques in the year 53, and a minaret was built with his name inscribed on it. {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 178} Abd al-Aziz ibn Marwan then pulled the mosque down in the year 77 and rebuilt it, and al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik wrote to Qurra ibn Sharik, governor of Egypt from the year 90, who pulled it down and rebuilt it. {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 179}

## The first building, as modern scholars see it
Yeomans dates the foundation to the winter of 641-642 and reports Kubiak's view that it was built as a neighbourhood mosque for the Ahl al-Raya quarter, not as a congregational mosque for the whole community: at fifty by thirty cubits it held at most seven hundred men, while the army then numbered about twelve thousand. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.20} Like the first [[building/prophets-mosque|Prophet's Mosque]] it was of mud-brick and palm trunks, free-standing, with six entrances on three sides; the qibla wall was solid with no mihrab niche, though al-Qalqashandi says four inserted columns marked the direction; the roof was split palm beams under mud-daubed fronds, with no minaret or courtyard and a pebble floor. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.20}

In 673 the governor Maslama enlarged it on Mu'awiya's orders to hold the whole Muslim population and added corner minarets barely higher than the roof, with external stairs; Creswell counts these the first recorded minarets and thinks they were modelled on the corner towers of the Roman temenos that formed the outer walls of the Great Mosque of Damascus. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.20} Amr preached from a stepped wooden minbar given him by a Nubian king, and Umar disapproved because it raised Amr above the people. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.21}

## Building phases
Nothing of the original mosque survives; what stands is an accumulation of many restorations. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.21} In the eastern corner of the prayer hall, under a dome, lies the tomb of [[person/abdullah-ibn-amr-ibn-al-as|Abd Allah ibn Amr]], who died in 664, and whose presence, Yeomans notes, lends the mosque blessing in the eyes of its visitors. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.21} Alterations and enlargements came in 698, 710, when the concave mihrab was installed, 750 and 791, but the main reconstruction, which gave the mosque its present size, was by [[person/abd-allah-ibn-tahir|Abd Allah ibn Tahir]] in the ninth century, and the oldest surviving parts date from his southern extension of the prayer hall in 827. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.21} These include stretches of his outer wall at the southern corner, about half the qibla wall, and deeply recessed windows flanked by narrow niches. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.21} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.22}

Al-Muqaddasi, visiting in 985, described glass mosaic on the walls, and the capitals and minbar were gilded. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.22} Under the Fatimids the mosaics were removed; [[person/al-hakim-bi-amr-allah|al-Hakim]] rebuilt the arcades in 1016 and gave a silver lamp of 100,000 drachms for which, Hillenbrand says, the doors had to be removed; five minarets added later probably fell in the earthquake of 1303. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.22} [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Salah al-Din]] repaired it after the burning of Fustat in 1168, restoring its heart, the great mihrab and its marble, inscribing his name, and building a pavilion under the great minaret and a water clock, as al-Maqrizi records. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.22} Then [[person/baybars|Baybars]] rebuilt the north-west wall, the amir Salar repaired it after the 1303 earthquake and added a stucco mihrab, and Qaytbay restored it further. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.23} The most radical change came in 1800, when Murad Bey rebuilt the arcades and prayer hall and added the two minarets that stand today. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.23}

## The mosque today
It is a hypostyle mosque, its prayer hall opening on a courtyard surrounded by arcades on the other three sides; the prayer-hall façade has twenty arches on piers alternating with double columns, and inside are twenty aisles perpendicular to the qibla, each arcade of six arches joined by tie-beams on antique Corinthian columns. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.23} The north-west arcade has recently been rebuilt on Ibn Tahir's plan of seven transverse arcades. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.21} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.23} In the south-west wall are wooden architraves of 827 with Hellenistic-style scrollwork, which Creswell compared with the Dome of the Rock; they are among the earliest Islamic woodwork in Egypt. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.24}

The mosque was a meeting place for the whole community, not for worship alone: civil matters were heard there, tribunals sat in the ziyada, it was the main centre of learning, and [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]] taught there for many years. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.25} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.26}
