## Overview
The khanqah of [[person/faraj-ibn-barquq|Faraj ibn Barquq]] (1400-1411) in the [[place/eastern-cemetery-cairo|Eastern Cemetery]] is the largest in Cairo; Faraj moved the body of his father [[person/barquq|Barquq]] there, fulfilling his wish to lie near the Sufi shaykhs. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.202} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.204} It began the urbanisation of the northern end of the necropolis and set the precedent for mausolea, madrasas and khanqahs there throughout the fifteenth century. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.202}

## Site and function
The site had been a hippodrome under Baybars; the desert setting allowed a free-standing symmetrical building unlike the cramped ones of al-Mu'izz street, and Faraj meant to add a hospice, market, baths and bakeries. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.204} Forty Sufis were appointed with lodgings, but whether it was also a madrasa is uncertain, since madrasa and khanqah merged in the Burji period. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.204} Yeomans notes that the domes of Cairo's khanqahs are dynastic tombs of sultans and amirs rather than shaykhs, which Rogers reads as a sign of state control over the orders. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.205}

## Façade and minarets
The north-west façade is flanked by two [[concept/sabil-kuttab|sabil-kuttabs]], and behind it rise two 52-metre minarets of three shafts: rectangular, cylindrical, and an open eight-columned pavilion with a bulbous finial. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.205} The entrance has alternating courses and a trilobed muqarnas hood framed by arabesque on a blue ground. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.205}

## Interior and domes
The plan is hypostyle: a prayer hall of seven tall arcades three bays deep under shallow brick domes, cells on the ground floor and lodgings above, and a three-bay arcade opposite the prayer hall recalling the suffa of the Prophet's mosque. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.205} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.207} The courtyard is plain, its only ornament Qaytbay's carved stone minbar. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.207} The two stone domes flanking the prayer hall, with crisp herring-bone patterns, are the largest in Cairo (over 14 metres across), and Yeomans sees them as opening a new stage in dome building and decoration; the men of the family lie in the eastern tomb and the women in the western. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.207} Inside are white marble dados, mihrabs framed in joggled voussoirs, gilded friezes on blue and stained glass. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.207} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.208}
