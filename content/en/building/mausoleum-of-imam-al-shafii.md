## Overview
The mausoleum of Imam Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i in the Qarafa of Cairo. Al-Maqrizi says that Saladin built a madrasa beside his grave and endowed it with the island of Fil {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1782}.

## The Nasiriyya madrasa
Al-Maqrizi says that the Nasiriyya madrasa in the Qarafa, beside the dome of Imam al-Shafi'i, was built by Sultan al-Malik al-Nasir Salah al-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub, who appointed a teacher there of the Shafi'i fiqh {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.2324}.

## The mosque beside it
A small mosque stood beside al-Shafi'i's tomb. When Salah al-Din built the madrasa beside his grave, al-Malik al-Kamil Muhammad ibn al-Adil enlarged the mosque, set a pulpit in it, and the Friday prayer was held there in 607 AH {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.2061}.

## The mausoleum in Yeomans
Yeomans writes that Salah al-Din, working to strengthen Sunni Islam, recognised the symbolic power of al-Shafi'i's tomb and promoted it as a place of pilgrimage, commissioning a cenotaph and some restoration, while the main rebuilding came under [[person/al-malik-al-kamil|al-Kamil]], who also made it a dynastic mausoleum. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.111} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.112} Ibn Jubayr in 1183 described the shrine as superb in beauty and size, facing a madrasa without equal in the country, its expenditure overseen by the shaykh Najm al-Din al-Khabushani and paid for by Salah al-Din. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.112}

## The building
It is one of the largest mausoleums in the Muslim world, 29 metres high, nearly the height of the Dome of the Rock, and is now reached through a nineteenth-century mosque. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.112} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.113} It is a domed cube: a two-storey rectangular block under a lead-sheathed dome with a boat-shaped finial; the upper storey is set back a metre, hiding the zone of transition, and its chamfered corners open in keel-arched doorways; as Behrens-Abouseif notes, the dome's profile bends directly from the zone of transition, unlike Fatimid domes. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.113} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.114} The lead sheeting of 1772 hides some of the green tiles with which Sultan al-Ghuri covered the dome in 1503-1504. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.114}

The interior is 15 metres across; the polychrome marble dado is Mamluk, almost certainly Qaytbay's; the qibla wall has three mihrabs on the Fatimid pattern, and a fourth was set in a corner because the tomb does not face Mecca. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.114} Above the dado run bands of arabesque and gilded, painted Naskhi cartouches and an original Kufic band, from which eight beams carry an octagonal timber frame hung with lamps; the zone of transition, three tiers of muqarnas, is attributed to Qaytbay. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.114}

## The cenotaph and the burials
Al-Shafi'i's teak cenotaph, inside a wooden enclosure, was made by Ubayd ibn Ma'ali in 1178 on Salah al-Din's order; Yeomans counts it a masterpiece of Ayyubid woodwork, with dense stellar patterns and Kufic and Naskhi bands, matched only by the cenotaph from the shrine of al-Husayn in the Museum of Islamic Art. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.114} Ubayd came from a family of Aleppo woodworkers: his father Ma'ali ibn Salam made the mihrab of the Maqam Ibrahim in the Aleppo citadel, and his brother Salman the minbar of the Aqsa Mosque (1168-1169). {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.115} The tomb also holds the cenotaph of Muhammad ibn Abd al-Hakim, in whose family vault al-Shafi'i was first buried, that of al-Kamil's mother, wife of al-Adil, and the tomb al-Kamil set aside for himself before being buried in Damascus; Shamsa, wife of Salah al-Din, and her son al-Aziz were buried there too. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.115}
