## Overview
A mosque and madrasa in [[place/cairo|Cairo]]. Sultan Hasan began its building in 757 AH. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.2111}

## According to al-Maqrizi
Al-Maqrizi says that the building went on for three years without a break, and that twenty thousand dirhams were set aside for its expense each day. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.2111}

He also says that no mosque of the Muslims is known to resemble this one, and that the great iwan measures sixty-five cubits in each direction. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.2111}

## The mosque in Yeomans
Yeomans counts the mosque of Sultan Hasan (1356-1361) the Mamluks' greatest architectural masterpiece and Cairo's largest and most magnificent mosque. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.154} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.155} Hasan was put on the throne at twelve in 1347; his reign was blighted by the Black Death and famine; he was deposed, reinstated in 1354 while power lay with the amirs Sarghitmish and Shaykhu, and finally deposed, imprisoned and murdered in 1361 after the amir Yilbugha rebelled; Yeomans sees the building's massive scale as masking its patron's weakness. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.154} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.155}

## The plan
It stands beneath the Citadel overlooking the Maydan Rumayla, on sloping ground on the site of a former palace; it combined mosque, madrasa and mausoleum, housed four hundred students, and had a qaysariyya whose rents supported it. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.155} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.156} At its heart is a mature cruciform plan of four colossal iwans round a deep cubic courtyard, with a madrasa for each school of law in the corners, each with its own court, fountain, qibla iwan and ranks of student rooms, the Hanafi and Shafi'i ones the largest. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.156} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.161} The qibla iwan, 21 metres wide, 27 deep and 26 high, exceeds the Taq-i Kisra at Ctesiphon, a challenge Yeomans thinks the builders knew they were meeting. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.159}

## The mausoleum
The mausoleum lies behind the qibla iwan, a position unprecedented in Egypt that, in Hillenbrand's words, "usurps the domed sanctuary in the classical Iranian mosque"; Yeomans favours an Anatolian Seljuk influence. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.156} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.157} It measures 21 by 30 metres, with a polychrome marble dado and a wooden frieze of gilded Naskhi on blue; under the marble cenotaph lie Hasan's sons Isma'il and al-Shihab Ahmad, while Hasan himself was never buried there and his remains are lost. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.161} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.162} The present dome is modern; the original, wooden and bulbous, fell two years after one of the minarets collapsed in 1659. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.157}

## Minarets and portal
The main minaret, 84 metres high, is the tallest in Cairo, with two octagonal shafts and a pavilion with a bulbous finial after the al-Maridani model; its twin collapsed in 1659 and was replaced by the present shorter one, and two minarets over the portal fell in 1361, killing three hundred people. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.157} The portal, 37 metres high, the tallest in Cairo, has a conical muqarnas hood and unfinished panels; it has been compared with the Gök Medrese at Sivas (1271-1272), and Chinese lotus and chrysanthemum motifs in its borders show Ilkhanid influence. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.157} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.159}

## The qibla iwan
Its decoration is concentrated in a dado of green, grey, white, pink and red marble; the mihrab is flanked by Crusader Gothic columns with gilded capitals, the minbar is of white marble, and above the dado runs a stucco Kufic band on an arabesque ground of Chinese lotus flowers. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.161}

## The synthesis
Yeomans sums up the mosque as Mamluk architecture come of age: the four-iwan plan from Persia, the qibla iwan built in the light of Sassanian architecture, the mausoleum behind the qibla and the portal from Anatolia, chinoiserie through Ilkhanid art, Syrian flavour in the ablaq, and a touch of Gothic in the mihrab columns, a synthesis he calls unselfconscious and unforced. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.162}
