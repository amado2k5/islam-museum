## Overview
A mosque and madrasa in [[place/cairo|Cairo]]. Sultan Hasan began its building in 757 AH. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.2111}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, treats the building in its article on the sultan and in its survey of the architecture of al-Kahira. It describes a vast cruciform [[concept/madrasa|madrasa]] for the four schools of law, joined to a congregational mosque and a tomb chamber, and calls the whole outstanding among fourteenth-century buildings for the vastness of its conception and above all for its height. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 452}

## Sultan Hasan and his reigns
The Encyclopaedia names the patron as al-Malik al-Nasir Nasir al-Din Abu al-Ma'ali Hasan, a sultan of the [[polity/mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk sultanate]] and the most prominent of eight sons of [[person/al-nasir-muhammad-ibn-qalawun|al-Nasir Muhammad b. Qalawun]] who ruled in turn between 741/1340 and 763/1362. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 259} He first became sultan on 14 Ramadan 748/18 December 1347, aged eleven according to the article, and did not in fact rule during that reign of just under four years, because power lay with rival factions of amirs left from earlier reigns. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 259} Nine months after reaching his majority he is reported to have abdicated under pressure from the amirs Taz and Minkali, who arranged the succession of his older brother Salih; Salih was deposed on 2 Shawwal 755/20 October 1354, and the amirs Sarghitmish and Shaykhun helped bring Hasan back. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 259} Shaykhun was soon killed in a quarrel with a fellow mamluk, and Sarghitmish, who had gained more power than the sultan liked, was imprisoned in Alexandria, where he died. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 259}

The second and last reign ended when one of his own mamluks, Yalbugha, murdered him in the [[building/citadel-of-cairo|Citadel]] on 12 Jumada I 763/9 March 1362, as the sultan, who had learned of the plot, was preparing to flee to Syria in bedouin dress. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 259} The article judges that his periods of rule are conspicuous neither for their length nor for evidence of political competence. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 259} It places in his first reign the plague of 749/1348, whose cause it says can hardly be laid at the sultan's door, and it notes documentary evidence of dealings with the Byzantine Empire, the monks of Mount Sinai and Venice. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 259} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 260} An article on the Mamluk sultanate adds that this plague, the most serious of the epidemics of the period, fell in his first reign and that mortality was especially high among the mamluks. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 344} The article names the madrasa of Sultan Hasan in Cairo, begun in 757/1356, as another trace of his reign. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 260}


## According to al-Maqrizi
Al-Maqrizi says that the building went on for three years without a break, and that twenty thousand dirhams were set aside for its expense each day. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.2111}

He also says that no mosque of the Muslims is known to resemble this one, and that the great iwan measures sixty-five cubits in each direction. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.2111}

## The mosque in Yeomans
Yeomans counts the mosque of Sultan Hasan (1356-1361) the Mamluks' greatest architectural masterpiece and Cairo's largest and most magnificent mosque. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.154} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.155} Hasan was put on the throne at twelve in 1347; his reign was blighted by the Black Death and famine; he was deposed, reinstated in 1354 while power lay with the amirs Sarghitmish and Shaykhu, and finally deposed, imprisoned and murdered in 1361 after the amir Yilbugha rebelled; Yeomans sees the building's massive scale as masking its patron's weakness. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.154} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.155}

## The plan
It stands beneath the Citadel overlooking the Maydan Rumayla, on sloping ground on the site of a former palace; it combined mosque, madrasa and mausoleum, housed four hundred students, and had a qaysariyya whose rents supported it. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.155} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.156} At its heart is a mature cruciform plan of four colossal iwans round a deep cubic courtyard, with a madrasa for each school of law in the corners, each with its own court, fountain, qibla iwan and ranks of student rooms, the Hanafi and Shafi'i ones the largest. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.156} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.161} The qibla iwan, 21 metres wide, 27 deep and 26 high, exceeds the Taq-i Kisra at Ctesiphon, a challenge Yeomans thinks the builders knew they were meeting. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.159}

## The mausoleum
The mausoleum lies behind the qibla iwan, a position unprecedented in Egypt that, in Hillenbrand's words, "usurps the domed sanctuary in the classical Iranian mosque"; Yeomans favours an Anatolian Seljuk influence. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.156} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.157} It measures 21 by 30 metres, with a polychrome marble dado and a wooden frieze of gilded Naskhi on blue; under the marble cenotaph lie Hasan's sons Isma'il and al-Shihab Ahmad, while Hasan himself was never buried there and his remains are lost. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.161} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.162} The present dome is modern; the original, wooden and bulbous, fell two years after one of the minarets collapsed in 1659. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.157}

## Minarets and portal
The main minaret, 84 metres high, is the tallest in Cairo, with two octagonal shafts and a pavilion with a bulbous finial after the al-Maridani model; its twin collapsed in 1659 and was replaced by the present shorter one, and two minarets over the portal fell in 1361, killing three hundred people. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.157} The portal, 37 metres high, the tallest in Cairo, has a conical muqarnas hood and unfinished panels; it has been compared with the Gök Medrese at Sivas (1271-1272), and Chinese lotus and chrysanthemum motifs in its borders show Ilkhanid influence. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.157} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.159}

## The qibla iwan
Its decoration is concentrated in a dado of green, grey, white, pink and red marble; the mihrab is flanked by Crusader Gothic columns with gilded capitals, the minbar is of white marble, and above the dado runs a stucco Kufic band on an arabesque ground of Chinese lotus flowers. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.161}

## The synthesis
Yeomans sums up the mosque as Mamluk architecture come of age: the four-iwan plan from Persia, the qibla iwan built in the light of Sassanian architecture, the mausoleum behind the qibla and the portal from Anatolia, chinoiserie through Ilkhanid art, Syrian flavour in the ablaq, and a touch of Gothic in the mihrab columns, a synthesis he calls unselfconscious and unforced. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.162}

## Name and functions in the Encyclopaedia
The founding deed of the endowment, as the article on Cairo reports, describes the building as a congregational mosque together with madrasas, and al-Maqrizi calls it variously a madrasa and a congregational mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 452} Its central feature is a vast cruciform madrasa for the four schools of law around an open court with a domed fountain. The principal [[concept/iwan|iwan]], assigned to the Hanafis for teaching, holds a marble [[concept/minbar|minbar]] and a monumental [[concept/mihrab|mihrab]], which the article takes as leaving no doubt that the functions of madrasa and congregational mosque were not exclusive. The qibla iwan opens onto a palatial tomb chamber that was first covered by a wooden dome. The four madrasas occupy the corners of the court and consist of many storeys of cells around a small court of their own. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 452}

Attached to the complex are an ablution court, a high water tower, a qaysariyya with the remains of shops, and a rab' that may also have served as a hospital. The article remarks that it is unusual in Cairo for court fountains to be used for ablutions before the late Ottoman period, and says that a full list of the appurtenances, many of them lost in the last hundred years, must await publication of the endowment deed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 452} On the history of the plan, it says the first Cairene cruciform madrasa built for all four rites was that of al-Nasir Muhammad, dated 695/1295 to 703/1304, and that al-Maqrizi records only two others, that of Sultan Hasan and that of Jamal al-Din al-Ustadar, dated 811/1408. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 453} It adds that mosques in Cairo from the time of Sultan Hasan onward, though grander, are fewer, and that even royal foundations usually form part of a more complex institution. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 452}

## Scale, the Anatolian porch and later history
The article calls the mosque outstanding in the whole architecture of the eighth/fourteenth century, and not only in al-Kahira, for the vastness of its conception and scale, and particularly remarkable for its height. It says that the interpretation of its architectural history had not advanced significantly since the monograph of Max Herz Bey, except that earlier speculation about an Anatolian origin for the porch has been confirmed by comparison with the two-minaret porch of the Seljuk [[building/gok-medrese-sivas|Gök Medrese]] at Sivas, dated 670/1271-2. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 452} An article on portals describes the full Egyptian form of that portal as 37 metres high at the mosque of Sultan Hasan, dated 760/1359, apparently derived from the Gök Medrese; its hood climbs to a cusped semidome at the apex, a Syrian detail already used in the great mosque of Baybars. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 337}

The article notes that the building remained unfinished at the death of Sultan Hasan, had an eventful history under the early Burji Mamluk sultans, and was so vast that it was rather unsuitable for general imitation. Its facade was nevertheless imitated by al-Mu'ayyad Shaykh at his [[building/al-muayyad-mosque-cairo|mosque]], built between 818/1415 and 823/1420 with two minarets placed above the Bab Zuwayla, and al-Mu'ayyad also took the great bronze doors from the entrance of Sultan Hasan's mosque and a bronze chandelier from its interior. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 452} The same article records that al-Maqrizi attributes a restoration of the [[building/mosque-of-al-hakim|mosque of al-Hakim]] to Sultan Hasan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 450} A survey of Cairo lists the mosque at the foot of the Citadel among the large Mamluk buildings and judges it of great importance in the history of art. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 175}

## Minarets, minbar and muqarnas in the Encyclopaedia
An article on minarets mentions the south-eastern corner minaret of the mosque as soaring to 90 metres and as the tallest in Cairo; Yeomans, quoted above, gives 84 metres for the main minaret, and the two figures are reported here as the sources state them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 387} An article on the minbar calls perhaps the most famous of the stone minbars of the Islamic world the one in the mosque of Sultan Hasan, dated 757-64/1356-63. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 98}

An article on [[concept/muqarnas|muqarnas]] describes the muqarnas along the facade of the mosque as an architectural tour de force, built as a stone cornice that projects boldly from the top of the facade. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 558} It adds that muqarnas domes never played a major role in Egypt, with a few small stone examples in portal vestibules, the most prominent at the mosque of Sultan Hasan and the palace of Yashbak, both of the mid-eighth/fourteenth century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 560} The same passage says that al-Maqrizi, describing a palace of Sultan Hasan at the Citadel, writes that it had a one-piece muqarnas, which suggests stone. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 560}
