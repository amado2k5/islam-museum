## Overview
The Selimiye Mosque is the imperial mosque complex that the Ottoman architect [[person/mimar-sinan|Sinan]] built at [[place/edirne|Edirne]] for Sultan [[person/selim-ii-uthmani|Selim II]]. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose articles this page adds to, calls it the glory of Edirne and the last royal mosque in the city, and counts it among the most beautiful monuments built there in the tenth/sixteenth century by Sinan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 712} In its survey of mosque design it names the Selimiye, dated 982/1574, as Sinan's final masterpiece and the climax of mature Ottoman architecture. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 714}

The Edirne article gives a building span of 972/1564-5 to 982/1574-5, a different start from the 1569 given by the Freely account further down this page, and reports a cost of 27,760 purses, drawn from booty captured in Cyprus. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 712}

## Place in Sinan's career
The article on Sinan lists three great complexes as his largest, the Shehzade, the Suleymaniyye and the Selimiyye, each a step in his maturity. In all three he kept to the form that had been standard for major Ottoman mosques since the [[building/uc-serefeli-mosque|Uc Serefeli]] at Edirne of 841-51/1438-47, a large domed chamber joined to a square or rectangular courtyard. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 644} The same article presents the Selimiye as a response to the challenge of the dome of [[building/hagia-sophia|Hagia Sophia]], and notes that the dome as built has about the same diameter. Measured from the ground it is lower, but because its profile is steeper it is higher when measured from the base of the dome. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 644}

Another article in the same work remarks that, in the years around 987/1579, Sinan must have given most of his energy to Selim II's mosque at Edirne, and suggests that a medrese built at that time at Eyup in Istanbul may be partly the work of another architect. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 209}

## The domed hall
According to the survey of mosque architecture, the Selimiye carries the largest of the Ottoman central domes, 31.28 metres across. Four minarets, the loftiest quartet in Ottoman building at 70.89 metres, stand around it. The dome rests on eight piers pushed as close to the walls as safety allows, so as to leave the largest possible open space. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 714}

The article on Sinan explains the plan as an octagon, which makes eight piers rather than four the main load-bearing elements. This allowed the curtain walls to be reduced so that light enters through more windows than in his earlier mosques. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 644} It judges the mihrab area to be the one drawback. The mihrab sits in a deep recess between the great piers, with few windows, so that despite the Iznik tile panels beside it, it remains in relative obscurity, and the article finds the outside of this recess less satisfactory, with the small paired columns of the arcade that links it to the corner minarets too slight for their setting. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 644} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 645} The same article calls the minarets the tallest in Ottoman architecture and sees their slenderness as a counterpart to the mass of the dome. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 645}

## Interior fittings
In the Edirne article, the muezzin's gallery under the great dome stands on twelve marble columns two metres high, with a small fountain below it. The mosque library is on the right and the royal gallery on the left; the royal gallery rests on four marble pillars and once had tile decoration, which the Russians removed in 1878. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 712}

The article on inscriptions singles out the Selimiye, completed in 982/1574-5, for very fine examples of tile inscriptions inside the mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 242} The article on the minbar describes the Selimiye's stone minbar as a notable one, superior in size, beauty and workmanship, carved from a single block, with an equilateral triangle on its side containing a sun disc, a fretted border and balustrade in polygonal patterns, and a conical canopy decorated with ceramic tiles. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 99}

## Courtyard, minarets and repairs
The courtyard is surrounded by cloisters carrying eighteen domes on sixteen large pillars, brought, the article says, from the Kapi-Dagh peninsula and from ruins in Syria. The Freely account in the sections below counts nineteen domes. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 712} Four minarets with three balconies each stand at the corners of the mosque and have often been repaired. The mosque itself was repaired after the earthquake of 1752 and again in 1808, in 1884 and in recent years. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 712}

## The wider complex
The Edirne article treats the mosque as one architectural whole with the adjoining medrese, the dar al-qurra, where Qur'an reciters lived, a school and a clock-house. The teacher of the Selimiye medrese ranked as the chief teacher of the city. The medrese was later used as a military detention centre and is now a museum of antiquities, while the dar al-qurra houses an ethnographic museum. The library was enriched by endowed books, though some valuable volumes were lost during the Bulgarian occupation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 712}

A covered market with 73 arches and 124 shops, called the Arasta, was built by Sinan under Murad III to provide revenue for the mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 713} When the mosque was completed in 982/1574, the Edirne preacher and poet Walihi, known as Kurd-zade, became its first preacher and kept the post until his death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 139}

## Summary
The Selimiye in Edirne is the great imperial külliye that Sinan built for Selim II in 1569–75; the sultan died a year before its completion, and Sinan himself considered it the crowning achievement of his career, surpassing even his Süleymaniye in Istanbul {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.88}.

## Precinct, courtyard, minarets
The külliye rises on the Kavak Meydanı platform where Beyazit I had a palace; the mosque and its courtyard — each about 60 by 44 metres — sit within a precinct walled by the medrese, the darülhadis and the Kavaflar Arastası market whose rents supported the foundation {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.89-90}. The courtyard is ringed by a colonnade under nineteen domes with a sixteen-sided white-marble şadırvan at its centre; four minarets at the mosque's corners each rise 70.89 metres — the tallest in Islam per Freely — with three balconies apiece, and the two northern minarets uniquely carry three separate spiral staircases {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.90}.

## The dome
The prayer hall centres on eight huge ribbed piers bearing the great dome, ringed by small semidomes over the exedrae and a larger semidome over the mihrab apse, the cascading masses giving the mosque its rounded pyramidal profile framed by the minarets {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.90-91}.

## For young visitors
The greatest mosque the architect Sinan ever built is not in Istanbul but in Edirne: the Selimiye, whose huge dome and four minarets — once the tallest in the Islamic world — crown the skyline.
