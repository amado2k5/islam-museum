## Overview
The chief contribution to Cairo of [[person/barquq|al-Zahir Barquq]], first of the Burji (Circassian) Mamluk sultans, is his khanqah-madrasa (1384-1386) on al-Mu'izz street beside the [[building/madrasa-of-al-nasir-muhammad|madrasa of al-Nasir Muhammad]] and the [[building/qalawun-complex|Qalawun complex]]. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.200} Its plan resembles its Bahri neighbours, but the symmetry of its court and four iwans facing the fountain, the trilobed muqarnas portal and the tall domed vestibule relate it more closely to [[building/masjid-sultan-hasan|Sultan Hasan's mosque]]. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.200} It combines a madrasa for the four schools with a khanqah, a sign in Yeomans's view of Barquq's esteem for the Sufis, which in the end led him to choose burial near the Sufi shaykhs of the Eastern Cemetery; the mausoleum became the tomb of his father, sister and children. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.200}

## The waqf
Yeomans cites Michael Rogers: attaching mausolea to religious foundations appeased ulama who disapproved of ostentatious tombs, and the [[concept/waqf|waqf]] system, which Yeomans traces to Byzantine pious foundations, explains in Rogers's view the number of such institutions and the excellence of Mamluk architecture. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.201}

## Façade and minaret
The façade, taller than its neighbours, is crowned with fleur-de-lis crenellation and divided by the portal and six square-headed recesses; its oculi have wooden ("matchwood") rather than stucco grilles, and the tiraz band runs above the second-storey windows. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.201} The minaret has three octagonal shafts, the second carved in high relief with interlaced circles and inlaid stone, which Yeomans sees as anticipating late fifteenth-century virtuosity; the dome is a nineteenth-century brick rebuilding of the wood and plaster original. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.201}

## Interior
The qibla iwan, the Hanafis' and the largest, is divided basilica-fashion by triple arcades on Pharaonic pink granite columns from Memphis, salvaged from the ruins of al-Salih's palace on Rawda, and covered by a painted, gilded flat roof; the other iwans are vaulted with zigzag two-coloured stone. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.201} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.202} The qibla wall and mihrab combine polychrome marble with mother-of-pearl, blue faience, bitumen and glass paste, substitutes for marble in a time of shortage that, Rogers argues, explain the building's decorative ingenuity. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.202} The mausoleum, entered by a mashrabiyya door, has a three-register polychrome marble dado, a gilded Naskhi frieze and wooden muqarnas pendentives carrying a sixteen-sided drum. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.202}
