## Overview
Humayun's tomb at Delhi is the mausoleum of the second Mughal emperor. [[person/syed-ali-nadeem-rezavi|Syed Ali Nadeem Rezavi]] ranks it, with the Taj Mahal, as the most famous Mughal building on the Timurid [[concept/hasht-bihisht-plan|nine-part plan]]. {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.138}

## Architect and plan
It was designed by Mirza Ghiyas, the master architect who had come to India with Babur. Rezavi argues that its plan copies the "boat-house" Humayun had made on the Yamuna, as described by his court historian Khwand Amir: four boats, each carrying a double-storeyed [[concept/chahartaq|chahartaq]], set facing one another and joined by four apartments, leaving an octagonal tank in the middle. In the tomb the chahartaqs became double-storeyed vaulted octagonal corner chambers in stone, the connecting apartments became rectangular side chambers, and the tank became the domed octagonal burial chamber at the centre. {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.138} {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.139}

## Features
The tomb stands in a [[concept/chaharbagh|chaharbagh]], which Rezavi calls the standard setting of Mughal tombs. {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.129} The bulbous double dome appears here for the first time, in a hesitant form, before it was perfected at the Taj Mahal, {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.131} and the building shows the red-and-white bichromy that replaced Iranian tile mosaic in Akbari architecture. {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.132} The plaster "arch-net" of the Kabuli Bagh Mosque reappears in its central dome. {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.133} In its garden stands the chahartaq "Barber's tomb", and nearby are the Sabz Burj and the Nili Gumbad. {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.133} {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.138}

## Building and garden, in Brookes
[[person/john-brookes|John Brookes]] writes that Humayun's widow [[person/hamida-banu-begum|Hamida Banu Begum]], a Persian from Khurasan, supervised the building of the tomb, begun around 1560 and completed in 1573, the first of the great Mughal garden tombs; elsewhere in the book he dates it to 1564. {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.131} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.132} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154} It stands on a large square platform under an immense marble dome that marks its Persian origins, and its garden is, in his view, the only extant Mughal garden to keep its original form: a [[concept/chaharbagh|chaharbagh]] enlarged and elaborated into a water parterre of narrow channels with shallow tanks at their crossings, its central watercourse small because water was precious. {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.130} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.132} From it grew the garden of [[building/tomb-of-akbar-sikandra|Akbar's tomb]] and then the reflecting canal of the [[building/taj-mahal|Taj Mahal]]. {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.136} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154}

## Builders and dates, in Nath
R. Nath writes that all authorities agree the tomb was built by Haji Begum ([[person/bega-begum|Bega Begum]]), Humayun's chief queen, who had been captured at Chausa and sent back with honour by Sher Shah, came to India again in AH 964/1557 with Hamida Banu Begum and Gulbadan Begum, began the work about 1558, went on pilgrimage in AH 972 — hence her title — and died in AH 989/1581 and was buried there. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 243} Abu al-Fadl records that Akbar visited the shrine in Rajab 976 (December 1568) and gave largesse to its attendants, and Bada'uni that the building was completed in AH 977/1569-70 after eight or nine years' work by Mirak Mirza Ghiyath; Nath therefore dates it about 1558-1570 and rejects the view that it took sixteen years, from 1565 to 1581. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 243} Tombstones in its chambers are dated between 988 and 1001, Haji Begum's bears 990, the earliest is 978/1570-71 on the grave of Muhammad Sultan son of Roshan Koka on the terrace, and neither of Humayun's tombstones is dated. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 244} Nath reads Bada'uni's words as naming Mirak Mirza Ghiyath the architect, and links him with the "Mirak Mir Ghiyath" whom Babur names as superintendent of the stone-cutters on his own works. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 244}
