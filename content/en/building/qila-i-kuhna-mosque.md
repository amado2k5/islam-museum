## Overview
The Qila-i-Kuhna mosque stands in the south-east of the [[building/purana-qila-delhi|Purana Qila]] at Delhi, close to the [[building/sher-mandal-delhi|Sher Mandal]]. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 160} It is a prayer hall of five bays with five arches on the facade, each in its own portal, without cloisters, monumental gateways or minarets; for Nath it is both the most perfectly developed mosque of this type and a new phenomenon in India. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 161} {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 168}

## Architecture and ornament
The grey-stone qibla wall is divided into two storeys by a cornice carrying a band of white marble inlaid in red stone, with three oriel windows in the upper storey and three-storeyed octagonal towers at the rear corners. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 160} {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 161} The central portal is covered with exquisite inlay — white marble and black slate set in red stone, and red stone set in marble, in geometric designs — with a fringe of lotus buds along the intrados of the arch. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 162} The white marble main mihrab is a composition of three nested semi-vaults framed by carved Qur'anic verses. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 163} The single dome rests on a sixteen-sided drum ringed by sixteen pinnacles, with traces of green, blue and yellow glazed tile. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 165} The mosque carries an extraordinary number of Qur'anic inscriptions, mostly in naskh with some Kufic, among them Surat al-Fath round the central arch and Surat al-Mulk round another. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 171}

## Attribution
The mosque is usually ascribed to Sher Shah, on the strength of Abbas Khan Sarwani's statement that he built in the governor's fort at Delhi a Friday mosque of stone ornamented with gold and lapis lazuli, and of Abdullah's *Tarikh-i Daudi*. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 169} Nath objects that the mosque has no gold or lapis, and that its style is unlike Sher Shah's buildings at Sasaram; he argues that Humayun commissioned it with the citadel in 1533, that it was unfinished when he fled India in 1540, and that it was completed early in Akbar's reign — which is why it shares with the tombs of Atgah Khan and Humayun the use of white marble with red stone and coloured inlay. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 169} {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 170} {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 171} He thinks the craftsmen probably came from Gwalior, bringing features of the Man Mandir palace. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 171}
