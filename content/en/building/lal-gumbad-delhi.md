## Overview
A square tomb in the garden of [[building/humayun-tomb|Humayun's tomb]] at Delhi, built of red sandstone with local grey quartzite, popularly known as the "Barber's Tomb"; Nath finds nothing to support that name and proposes calling it the Lal Gumbad, the Red Dome, after its stone. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 193} {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 195}

## The building
Each side has a central portal with two arches one above the other, closed by jalis except on the south where the lower arch is the entrance; a small panel cut into the western jali marks the qibla. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 193} The arches over the openings are ornamental: a single slab carved to look like an arch, so that the load rests horizontally. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 193} Four square chhatris on slender pillars flank the dome, and the double dome rises on a high sixteen-sided drum with a pinnacle at each angle, so that it sits among four chhatris and twenty-eight pinnacles. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 193}

## Its roofing
The roof uses a system hitherto unknown in the region: the dome is carried on four intersecting arches of stone ribs that cross to the opposite side and interlock, resting on eight pillars, so that there is technically no zone of transition. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 193} {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 194} Nath suggests that the architect of the Gol Gumbaz at Bijapur, about a century later, may have taken from it the idea of a frame of intersecting arches in place of squinches or stalactites, though at a vastly greater scale and in brick. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 194}

## Date
It has no Persian inscription; inside are two marble tombstones, a man's and a woman's, inscribed with Qur'anic verses, the woman's bearing the figure 999, perhaps AH 999 (1590-91). {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 195} Nath dates it on style to about 1570 and suggests that the man was a friend or relative of Humayun held in esteem by Haji Begum, and that his wife died later and was buried beside him. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 195}
