## Overview
The lofty column outside Alexandria, with a base beneath it and a base above, said to have no equal among columns for height or roundness {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.240}.

## The stone
Al-Maqrizi says that the column is of red speckled stone, a hard flint-like stone {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.487}.

## The broken columns
Al-Maqrizi says that about four hundred columns once stood around it. Qaraja broke them in the days of Saladin and threw them onto the shore, to make the way hard for the enemy {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.487}.

## Aristotle's portico
Al-Maqrizi says that the column was one of the columns that carried the portico of Aristotle, where he taught wisdom, and that it was a house of learning with a library in it {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.487}.

## In Ibn Battuta's Rihla
[[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]] counts the Pillar of the Columns among the wonders of Alexandria: a huge marble column outside the city, standing in a palm grove and rising far above its trees, a single, finely cut piece set on bases of square stones like great benches, "and no one knows how it was set there, nor is it established who set it up". {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.21}

[[person/ibn-juzayy-muhammad|Ibn Juzayy]] adds a story he heard from one of his travelling teachers: an archer of Alexandria climbed to the top of the column with his bow and quiver, and crowds gathered to see him; his trick was to shoot an arrow tied to a long thread, with a strong rope tied to the thread's end, so that the thread lay across the top of the column until the rope took its place; he fixed one end in the ground and climbed the other. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.21}
