## Overview
The al-Aqmar Mosque in Cairo stands at the end of the market of the candle-makers, near the market of the poultry-sellers. Its site was formerly the market of the wheat-dealers {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1063}.

## The gate and its inscriptions
Al-Maqrizi reports that Amir Yalbugha al-Salimi told him he had read words in the lines inscribed on the threshold of the al-Aqmar mosque's gate {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1132}.

## The Mukhalliq corner
The Mukhalliq corner lies opposite the basin of the al-Aqmar mosque, on the right of anyone entering the mosque known as Mu'bad Musa {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1131}.

## The façade in Yeomans
Yeomans dates al-Aqmar to 1125 and counts its façade on al-Mu'izz street an architectural masterpiece showing Fatimid stone carving at its best; its right-hand flank, long hidden by neighbouring buildings, has been reconstructed and its original symmetry restored. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.62} The façade is divided vertically by three keel-arched niches; the central one, taller and deeper in a projecting portal, has a fluted hood whose ribs rise and radiate like a sunrise around a central medallion. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.62} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.63} The medallion is a set of concentric rings alternating calligraphy and scrollwork: at its centre the name of Ali repeated in a drilled Kufic lattice, then a ring repeating the name of Muhammad, then a broad foliate scroll; medallion and fluted hood recur more simply in the side niches and became a feature of later Fatimid buildings such as the mashhad of Sayyida Ruqayya and the mosque of al-Salih Tala'i. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.63}

At the top of the tall recessed panels either side of the entrance, [[concept/muqarnas|muqarnas]] appear for the first time in Egyptian architecture as decoration on a flat surface; a band of foliated Kufic forms the cornice, and smaller panels and lozenges carry vases and interlace. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.63}

## The plan
Al-Aqmar is a neighbourhood mosque (masjid) rather than a congregational mosque, in Yeomans's view the earliest surviving example of the type. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.63} Its plan is bent because the axis of al-Mu'izz street does not face Mecca: the façade masks a wedge-shaped structure with a barrel-vaulted entrance, a room and a staircase. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.63} A square courtyard is surrounded on three sides by single-aisled keel-arched arcades and the prayer hall is three aisles deep; the bays are covered by shallow brick domes except the qibla aisle, which has a flat timber roof, and the domes are thought to come from the amir Yalbugha al-Salimi's restoration of 1397, as they resemble those of the khanqah of Faraj ibn Barquq. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.63} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.64} Behrens-Abouseif notes that, like al-Salih Tala'i's mosque, it stands over shops whose rents supported it, though these have not yet been excavated. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.64}
