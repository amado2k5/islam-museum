## Overview
[[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|Al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik]] began building the Umayyad Mosque in [[place/damascus|Damascus]] in 87 AH. Al-Suyuti counts it among the greatest things built in his reign. He reports from Ibn Abi Abla that al-Walid conquered India and al-Andalus and built the mosque of Damascus. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.367}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam, in its article on Damascus, treats the Great Mosque of the Umayyads as the first and one of the most impressive masterpieces of Muslim architecture, and as the work of al-Walid I. It describes the change of regime as leaving little mark on the city plan, apart from two closely linked buildings, the caliph's palace and the mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 294} The article is about the city as a whole, so what it says of the mosque is a section within it and not a full monograph on the building.

## Before al-Walid: church and prayer hall
After the conquest, the Christians of Damascus kept their churches under a tribute and poll tax. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 294} The article says the Church of St. John kept standing under the Sufyanid caliphs, and that [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya]] did not insist on including it in the mosque. It cites the Gallic bishop Arculf, who passed through about 50/670 and noted two separate sanctuaries, one for each community. The Muslim place of prayer was then only a musalla set against the eastern part of the south wall of the old enclosure. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 294}

As Muslim numbers grew, the prayer space became too small. [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik]] laid claim to the church and proposed buying it, but the talks failed. By the time al-Walid decided to enlarge the mosque, the article says, no free place was left in the city: houses had spread into the old sacred precinct and only the agora, where the Sunday markets were held, remained. In spite of earlier agreements, al-Walid took the Church of St. John the Baptist from the Christians and gave them several other places of worship, which had fallen out of use, in exchange. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 294} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 295}

## Construction
Al-Sallabi says that al-Walid converted the church that stood on the site after negotiating with the Christians, spent large sums on the building, and that the construction was completed in 96 AH. {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 p.131-132}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam gives the year 86/705 for the moment when al-Walid had everything inside the enclosure of the ancient temple pulled down, citing a poem collection of [[person/farazdaq|al-Farazdaq]]. This covered the Church of St. John and the small chapel over a crypt, three cubits square, said to hold a casket with the head of John the Baptist. Only the surrounding walls of large stones and the square corner towers were left. The article adds that the mosque was built in six years. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 295} The article's year for the demolition and the 87 AH start given above come from different works, and the museum records both without choosing between them.

## Plan and fittings
Inside a frame of about 120 by 80 metres, the article says, the builders placed a courtyard to the north, ringed by a wide covered portico with double arcades. Along the whole south wall they set an immense prayer hall for the community, with a central aisle under a large cupola. The article attributes the cupola to al-Walid. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 295}

Other features it lists are these. In the east, the "[[concept/mihrab|mihrab]] of the Companions" recalled the earliest prayer place. In the west, a new door, Bab al-Ziyada, replaced the central portico, which had been blocked. In the centre of the north wall rose a high square minaret. The walls were covered in some places with marble inlay and in others with mosaics of glass paste. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 295} The article reports that Thiersch, Strygowski, Sauvaget and Creswell, who studied the building on the spot, agree with only slight differences that it is a Muslim achievement. It calls it Muslim in conception and purpose, and quotes a phrase describing it as a symbol of the political supremacy and moral prestige of Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 295}

## The mosaics
A separate Encyclopaedia of Islam article on mural and floor mosaics places the Damascus mosque beside the [[building/dome-of-the-rock|Dome of the Rock]] in Jerusalem, built in 72/691 by Abd al-Malik, and the Great Mosque of Cordoba, as the three main Umayyad religious buildings adorned with mosaic. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 998}

At Damascus only fragments survive of pictures showing a great gilded vine and whole towns, the most striking of them on parts of the arcade around the courtyard. The article says that about 85 percent of the cubes are coloured glass paste, many of them a brown base with gold leaf under a protective glass layer. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 998} The courtyard panels, revealed when plaster was removed, show tall green trees among groups of buildings: fortresses, balconied houses, pavilions with colonnades and exedras, and nearer the front lively water. Others have noted a Pompeian character in the architecture. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 998}

Some have taken the water to be the river Barada. The article's author doubts this, finding a familiar local landscape hard to square with Muslim aesthetics, and suggests the courtyard scenes may complement geographical pictures on the prayer hall walls, with the water standing for the encircling Ocean of the Arab geographers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 998}

## Was the church divided?
A story says the church was shared between Christians and Muslims. The article calls this a legend that grew from a translation error. It notes that al-Tabari, al-Baladhuri and al-Mas'udi do not mention a division. The text of Ibn al-Mufalla, spread by Ibn Asakir and Ibn Jubayr, speaks of a division of the kanisa, but there it should be read in a broad sense, as a place of prayer, meaning the open-air haram of the old sanctuary and not the church building. The article adds that some authors, Dussaud among them, hoped to find an old basilica in the plan and so said the Christian hall of prayer was split between the two communities. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 295} This is the article's reading. The accounts above from Arculf and al-Sallabi are reported separately.

## Later history
The article does not trace the building's whole life, but mentions several moments. A brawl between Damascenes and Berber soldiers in 461/1069 started a fire that did serious damage to the Great Mosque and the city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 296} The museum has a separate card for the [[event/umayyad-mosque-fire-461|fire of 461]]. In 699/1300 the Mongol army entered Damascus and seized the Great Mosque, though it failed to take the citadel. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 299} Some years after 717/1317 Tankiz had work done on the mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 299}

In describing the old city's fabric, the article names the Great Mosque as the religious and political centre of the old town, where official decrees were proclaimed and displayed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 296} In Ottoman times, it says, the big religious foundations were built outside the walls, which shows the mosque was no longer the one gathering place of the Muslim community. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 302}

## The Gate of the Clocks and the talismans
Al-Umari reports that the Gate of the Clocks was so named because a clock compass was made there, by which every passing hour was known. On it were copper birds, a snake and a crow. When an hour was complete, the snake came out, the birds whistled, the crow called out, and a pebble fell into a basin. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.197-198}

He also reports that before the mosque was burned, talismans were hung from its ceiling against insects, mice, snakes, scorpions and spiders, and nothing was found in it until it burned. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.197-198}

The start date is derived from the Hijri date, and is approximate in the museum's calendar.

## Ibn al-Shatir's dial: original and copy
King describes the [[invention/shatir-polar-sundial|monumental sundial]] designed and constructed by [[person/ibn-al-shatir|Ibn al-Shatir]] on the northern minaret: horizontal marble approximately two metres by one, polar gnomon and multiple time scales. Dizer dates the instrument to 773 AH/1371–1372. This does not redate the mosque's construction or identify it with the Gate of the Clocks device in the earlier text {cite:king-ibn-shatir-bea sundial} {cite:tdv-ibn-shatir-dizer instruments}.

According to King, the minaret instrument is a late-nineteenth-century copy and original fragments are in a Damascus museum garden; Dizer names al-Tantawi in the remaking account. These are source reports, not current site inspection. Cam corroborates surviving original pieces. They must not be conflated with the small Aleppo jewel-box dial or a modern exterior view lacking a visible dial face {cite:king-ibn-shatir-bea sundial-and-compendium} {cite:tdv-ibn-shatir-dizer instruments} {cite:tdv-gunes-saati-cam surviving-dials}.
