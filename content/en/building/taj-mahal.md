## Overview
The Taj Mahal is a mausoleum in [[place/agra|Agra]] built by [[person/shah-jahan-mughal|Shah Jahan]] for his wife Mumtaz Mahal between 1632 and 1653: a white marble dome above the Yamuna river, a formal garden crossed by channels, and calligraphy and white marble inlay {cite:wikidata} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.152} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154} {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.131} {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.132}. John Brookes writes that she died giving birth to her fourteenth child three years after Shah Jahan's accession, and that building began in 1632 and ended in 1654 {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.151} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.152} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154}. He sees it as concluding earlier strands in Mughal building: slender minarets anticipated at the gateway of [[building/tomb-of-akbar-sikandra|Akbar's tomb]], white marble inlay used earlier at the [[building/itimad-ud-daulah-tomb|tomb of I'timad al-Dawla]], and the tomb form first seen at [[building/humayun-tomb|Humayun's tomb]] {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.152} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154}.

The tomb sits on a plinth between two sandstone and marble pavilions, a mosque and a reception hall, above the level of the [[concept/chaharbagh|quartered garden]] {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154}. It stands not at the garden's centre but at its edge above the Yamuna, and Syed Ali Nadeem Rezavi notes that here the focus was shifted from the centre to the river front {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154} {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.129}. Rezavi adds that the bulbous double dome, first seen at Humayun's tomb, was perfected here, and that its facades are the most representative Mughal example of calligraphic decoration {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.131} {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.132}. He ranks it with Humayun's tomb as the most famous Mughal tombs built on the Timurid plan, and finds the blend of Iranian and indigenous elements at its best in it {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.138} {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.140}. Shah Jahan was buried beside his wife here in 1666, and the tomb was inscribed on the UNESCO list in 1983 {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.159} {cite:wikidata}.

## Principles of its design
A mausoleum in [[place/agra|Agra]] built by [[person/shah-jahan-mughal|Shah Jahan]] for his wife Mumtaz Mahal between 1632 and 1653: a white marble dome above the Yamuna river, a formal garden crossed by channels, and inlaid calligraphy and precious stones, which make it the most famous Islamic building in the world. {cite:wikidata} The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls it the grandest of the monumental dynastic mausoleums that have become synonyms of Mughal architecture, and says that Mughal imperial tombs creatively combined the funerary tradition of their Timurid heritage with local Indian building conventions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 68}

The article holds that the Mughals left no written architectural theory, and that the Taj Mahal states their principles in built form. It lists four: a strict geometry from modular planning on grids based on the Shah Jahan gaz, a unit of roughly 80 to 82 centimetres; consistent bilateral symmetry about a central axis, with centralised schemes built into it; a graded hierarchy of material, form and colour down to the smallest ornament; and a refined symbolism running through the whole programme. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 68}

## Layout of the complex
The mausoleum stands at the northern end of the main axis of a large walled rectangle of about 1,114.5 by 373 gaz. The enclosure had three units: the tomb with its garden and water works, and two courtyard complexes to the south, only one of which survives. The preserved complex measures about 561 by 300 metres. The article treats the tomb garden as the grand version of the waterfront garden, a type it calls specific to Mughal architecture: a raised rectangular terrace carries the main buildings beside a lower four-part garden, a [[concept/chaharbagh|chahar bagh]], whose square of 368 gaz, about 296 metres, formed the basis of the grid. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 68} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 69}

The two southern complexes follow the same scheme of a rectangle joined to a centrally planned unit, but are built as open courtyards of narrow wings and arcades, as in the residential and utilitarian architecture of the time. The courtyard next to the tomb garden also holds smaller tombs for other women of Shah Jahan's household, laid out as miniature copies of the main garden; outside the walls stand a further small tomb complex, another tomb and a mosque. The income of the bazaars and caravanserais of the subsidiary complexes, together with that of thirty villages of the Agra district, was assigned by imperial order to the upkeep of the mausoleum. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 69}

## The mausoleum and its materials
On the central axis the great group of the mausoleum and its four minarets is flanked by a mosque and an assembly hall, which the article also describes as a guest house. The gatehouse and the tomb itself both follow a ninefold plan, a favourite of Mughal architecture with Timurid antecedents. The tomb plan is inscribed in a square with chamfered corners, an irregular octagon that contemporary texts call a Baghdadi octagon. Inside, two tomb chambers are stacked beneath a high double dome, a Timurid idea; outside, large portals flanked by double-storey niches bring the cubic tomb of the Delhi tradition, with the bulbous dome profile of the Deccan, to what the author judges an unparalleled elegance and harmony. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 69}

The brick structure is faced in white marble inlaid with semi-precious stones, which the article says responds to changes in the atmosphere. The subsidiary buildings are faced in red sandstone, with white marble reserved for features such as domes. The author links this graded colour pairing to older Indian texts that assign white stone to brahmans and red stone to kshatriyas. The marble was brought from Makrana in Rajasthan and the sandstone from quarries in the region of [[place/fatehpur-sikri|Fatehpur Sikri]] and Rupbas. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 69}

Relief carving of plants and flowers and the Italianate inlay known in Mughal usage as parchin kari reach their richest form in the central chamber. The article reads this decoration as a sign of eternal bloom that supports the programme of the building as an earthly copy of the garden of Paradise where Mumtaz, once pardoned, is imagined to dwell. The Qur'anic inscriptions, designed by [[person/abd-al-haqq-amanat-khan|Amanat Khan al-Shirazi]], accordingly dwell on the Day of Resurrection, the Last Judgement and the reward of the faithful. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 69}

## The question of the architect
The article says the identity of the architect is not yet fully settled, because contemporary sources play down the architects and stress the part of the patron. Mir Abd al-Karim, Jahangir's leading architect, and an official named Makramat are named in connection with the work, and [[person/ustad-ahmad-lahori|Ustad Ahmad Lahori]] is also reported to have been connected with it. The craftsmen made their contribution known in numerous mason marks, which still await systematic study. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 69}

## Construction history
Mumtaz died on 17 Dhu l-Qa'da 1040, 17 June 1631, in [[place/burhanpur|Burhanpur]] and was buried there for a time. Construction began in Jumada II 1041, January 1632, after the site was acquired from its owner, Raja Jai Singh Kachhwaha of Amber. Her body was brought to Agra in December 1631 and reburied temporarily on the building site in January 1632. In June 1632 Shah Jahan marked the first death anniversary, the urs, with rites meant to win divine pardon for her. The second urs, in May 1633, was held on the great platform of the terrace, over her third and final grave, where a screen of enamelled gold made by the imperial goldsmiths surrounded the tombstone; the present inlaid marble screen replaced it in 1643. At that time the domed tomb structure had not yet been raised. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 69}

Two inscriptions inside the mausoleum and one on the west portal give 1048, 1638 to 1639, as the year the main mausoleum was completed. The histories report the whole complex finished in 1052, 1643, though an inscription on the garden face of the main gateway shows decoration continuing until at least 1057, 1647. Muhammad Salih Kanbo even reports that the complex took twenty years. The cost is given as 50 lakhs of rupees, which the article puts at 4 to 5 million. The twelfth urs, on 17 Dhu l-Qa'da 1052, 6 February 1643, is the date on which the tomb was officially reported complete; the descriptions written then by Lahori and Kanbo are called unparalleled, among Mughal writing on architecture, in exactness and consistent terminology. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 69}

After the fourteenth urs Shah Jahan spent over two years in the north of his empire, and in 1648 he moved his capital to the newly built Shahjahanabad at [[place/delhi|Delhi]]. The last documented imperial visit to the Taj Mahal was in Safar 1065, December 1654. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 69} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 70} Shah Jahan died in 1076, 1666, after spending his last years in captivity at Agra, and was buried in the tomb beside his wife. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 70}

## Legacy
It was inscribed on the UNESCO list in 1983; it has become the symbol of India, of love and of death alike, and an example in every book on Islamic architecture. {cite:wikidata}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam says that little is known of the mausoleum after Shah Jahan's burial until the later eighteenth century, when British visitors in search of the picturesque described and drew it and brought it to Western notice. The British conquered Agra in 1803, and the tomb became the focus of their selective restoration of monuments, put on a more systematic footing early in the twentieth century when the Archaeological Survey of India, founded in 1860, took up conservation. The article records the Taj Mahal's place among the World Heritage monuments of India and its appearance on the 1996 list of the world's hundred most endangered historic sites of the World Monuments Watch, which it attributes to tourism and uncontrolled industrial growth around it. It adds that, despite India's uneasiness with its Islamic past, the Taj Mahal has become India's national symbol, used above all in tourism advertising. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 70}

## Building and garden, in Brookes
[[person/john-brookes|John Brookes]] writes that Shah Jahan built the Taj in memory of his wife Mumtaz Mahal, who died giving birth to her fourteenth child three years after his accession, and that it was begun in 1632 and completed in 1654. Various names are credited with its design; Brookes thinks Shah Jahan used the proposals he commissioned to crystallise his own ideas. {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.151} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.152} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154} He sees it as the conclusion of earlier strands in Mughal building: the swelling dome over the pointed iwan from Persia, slender minarets anticipated at the gateway of [[building/tomb-of-akbar-sikandra|Akbar's tomb]], white marble inlay already used at the [[building/itimad-ud-daulah-tomb|tomb of I'timad al-Dawla]], and the tomb form first seen at [[building/humayun-tomb|Humayun's tomb]]. {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154}

## Its place in the garden
The tomb sits on a plinth between two sandstone and marble pavilions, a mosque and a reception hall, above the level of the quartered garden; a raised marble platform marks the meeting of the long reflecting canal with its side arms, now dry. Unusually, the tomb is not central to its site but stands at its edge above the Yamuna; the crenellated enclosure wall was restored by Lord Curzon in 1903, when the garden was largely replanted in park-like fashion. {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.152} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154} Brookes reports the story that Shah Jahan meant to build a black marble mausoleum for himself across the river, which was never realised; [[person/syed-ali-nadeem-rezavi|Rezavi]] likewise sees the shift of focus from the centre to the river front as what sets the Taj apart from other garden tombs. {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154} {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.129} Shah Jahan was buried beside his wife here in 1666. {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.159}
