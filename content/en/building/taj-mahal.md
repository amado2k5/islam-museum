## Overview
A mausoleum in [[place/agra|Agra]] built by Shah Jahan for his wife Mumtaz Mahal between 1632 and 1653: a white marble dome above the Yamuna river, a formal garden crossed by channels, and inlaid calligraphy and precious stones, which make it the most famous Islamic building in the world. {cite:wikidata}

## Legacy
It was inscribed on the UNESCO list in 1983; it has become the symbol of India, of love and of death alike, and an example in every book on Islamic architecture. {cite:wikidata}

## Building and garden, in Brookes
[[person/john-brookes|John Brookes]] writes that [[person/shah-jahan-mughal|Shah Jahan]] built the Taj in memory of his wife Mumtaz Mahal, who died giving birth to her fourteenth child three years after his accession, and that it was begun in 1632 and completed in 1654. Various names are credited with its design; Brookes thinks Shah Jahan used the proposals he commissioned to crystallise his own ideas. {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.151} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.152} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154} He sees it as the conclusion of earlier strands in Mughal building: the swelling dome over the pointed iwan from Persia, slender minarets anticipated at the gateway of [[building/tomb-of-akbar-sikandra|Akbar's tomb]], white marble inlay already used at the [[building/itimad-ud-daulah-tomb|tomb of I'timad al-Dawla]], and the tomb form first seen at [[building/humayun-tomb|Humayun's tomb]]. {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154}

## Its place in the garden
The tomb sits on a plinth between two sandstone and marble pavilions, a mosque and a reception hall, above the level of the [[concept/chaharbagh|quartered garden]]; a raised marble platform marks the meeting of the long reflecting canal with its side arms, now dry. Unusually, the tomb is not central to its site but stands at its edge above the Yamuna; the crenellated enclosure wall was restored by Lord Curzon in 1903, when the garden was largely replanted in park-like fashion. {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.152} {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154} Brookes reports the story that Shah Jahan meant to build a black marble mausoleum for himself across the river, which was never realised; [[person/syed-ali-nadeem-rezavi|Rezavi]] likewise sees the shift of focus from the centre to the river front as what sets the Taj apart from other garden tombs. {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.154} {cite:rezavi-iranian-influence-indian-architecture-2002 p.129} Shah Jahan was buried beside his wife here in 1666. {cite:brookes-gardens-of-paradise-1987 p.159}
