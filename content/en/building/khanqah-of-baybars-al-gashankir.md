## Overview
The Khanqah of Rukn al-Din Baybars al-Jashnakir in Cairo, within the Dar al-Wizara, faces the Dirb al-Asfar, the slaughter ground where the caliphs sacrificed animals {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1065} {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1200}.

## Its neighbours
Al-Maqrizi says that the Qaraqanshiyya madrasa and the Khanqah of Rukn al-Din Baybars were both part of the Dar al-Wizara, and that the lands beside the khanqah extended to Bab al-Jawaniyya {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1065}.

He also says that the house of Amir Qazman and the house of the vizier Sanqur al-A'sar stood beside the khanqah {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1065}.

## The khanqah in Yeomans
Yeomans notes that Salah al-Din founded Cairo's first khanqah, the Dar Sa'id al-Su'ada, of which nothing remains, so the oldest surviving [[concept/khanqah|khanqah]] in Cairo is that of [[person/baybars-al-jashankir|Baybars al-Jashankir]] (1307-1310) in the Jamaliyya district, on the site of the Fatimid Dar al-Wizara. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.148} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.149} A window in the tomb vestibule is said to have come from the Abbasid caliph's palace in Baghdad and been set in the Dar al-Wizara under the Fatimids. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.149} The complex comprised a tomb, khanqah, ribat, ablutions court, kitchen and shops; only the tomb and parts of the khanqah survive. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.149}

## The building
The entrance, a large semicircular arch of cushion voussoirs, frames a marble recess with a muqarnas half-dome and bronze-clad doors; the minaret rises from a rectangular base through two cylindrical shafts to a ribbed dome, its first-storey muqarnas billowing out like a capital. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.149} Reused material from the Dar al-Wizara and other palaces was lavished on the mausoleum, with a black and white marble dado and a mihrab almost as large as Qalawun's, while the khanqah was built austerely as befits a Sufi house. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.149} The plan is cruciform with unequal iwans, the largest, the qibla iwan, serving as oratory, and cells in three tiers. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.149} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.150}

## Endowment and residents
The endowment deeds provide for a hundred resident bachelor Sufis and up to three hundred non-resident ones, married men among them, with a shaykh, two imams, repeaters, muezzins, an eye doctor, a cook and others; the ribat had a hundred members, thirty of them housed, needy Muslims with preference for the founder's freedmen and retired soldiers; the tomb had an imam and thirty-six Qur'an readers, and in its vestibule a shaykh taught thirty students hadith. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.150} Its most distinguished director was [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]], which Yeomans takes as evidence of how closely the Sufi orders were then integrated with orthodox teaching. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.150} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.151}

## The tomb
Baybars was buried in his tomb only after the amirs' persistent petitioning: after his execution he had been buried ignominiously in the Southern Cemetery; al-Nasir Muhammad grudgingly agreed, and removed Baybars's titles from the tiraz inscription on the khanqah's façade. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.149}
