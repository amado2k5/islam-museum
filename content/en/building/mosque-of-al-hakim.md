## Overview
The Fatimid caliph [[person/al-aziz-fatimid|al-Aziz]] began this mosque in 990 and his son [[person/al-hakim-bi-amr-allah|al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah]] completed it between 1003 and 1013, near [[building/bab-al-nasr|Bab al-Nasr]] and [[building/bab-al-futuh|Bab al-Futuh]]. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.59} It first stood outside the city wall; [[person/badr-al-jamali|Badr al-Jamali]] later brought it inside his walls, making it part of the northern fortifications. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.59} Ettinghausen and Grabar suggest it served chiefly as a royal sanctuary and imperial ceremonial mosque set apart from the centres of population. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.59}

## Plan
The mosque combines features of the [[building/masjid-ibn-tulun|Mosque of Ibn Tulun]], [[building/al-azhar|al-Azhar]] and the [[building/great-mosque-mahdia|mosque of Mahdiya]] in Tunisia. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.59} It has Ibn Tulun's monumental simplicity and is largely of brick, with pointed arches on rectangular piers, but its proportions are taller. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.59} The prayer hall follows the Tunisian model: a wider, taller central aisle perpendicular to the mihrab, and three domes on the transverse qibla aisle, one over the mihrab and two in the corners; unlike al-Azhar there is no dome at the front of the central aisle on the courtyard side. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.59} The central arch of the qibla arcade is taller than the rest, set in a tall crenellated portal and flanked inside by paired marble columns. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.60}

## Façade and minarets
The façade is faced with dressed stone, its long sweep recalling Mahdiya, broken at the centre by a projecting cubic portal in the Tunisian manner, with niches holding arabesques like those of the al-Azhar mihrab hood. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.60} At either end are huge battered salients added by al-Hakim and Badr al-Jamali to strengthen the mosque and fold it into the fortifications; they are not load-bearing but encase the lower shafts of the original minarets, revealed when the rubble between them was removed during restoration. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.60} The northern shaft is a tapering cylinder on a square block, the southern a taller rectangular shaft with an octagonal second tier; the southern bears a foliated Kufic inscription with al-Hakim's name, and the northern's bands and lozenges resemble those of the minaret of Sfax, according to Behrens-Abouseif. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.60} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.61}

The present minaret tops are the work of [[person/baybars-al-jashankir|Baybars al-Jashankir]] after the earthquake of 1303 (c. 1309-1310), in the "mabkhara" (incense-burner) style named for their perforated stonework: an octagonal shaft on a rectangular base crowned by a ribbed, helmet-shaped dome; the northern one has tall keel-arched niches and three bands of muqarnas. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.61} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.62} Hillenbrand suggests that the tiered division of the Egyptian minaret may derive from the Pharos of Alexandria; Yeomans notes that Creswell dismissed this influence. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.60} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.61}

## Interior and modern rebuilding
The mosque lay abandoned for centuries, its ruins painted by Prosper Marilhat in 1840; in the early 1980s it was substantially rebuilt and now serves as an Isma'ili mosque, a reconstruction Yeomans calls controversial because it obscured much of the original fabric. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.62} The inner arcade piers resemble Ibn Tulun's, with engaged corner columns, the arches have a slight horseshoe return, and decoration is sparse, mostly bands of Kufic in stucco. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.62} The most unexpected Fatimid survivals in place are wooden tie-beams carved with bevelled volutes in [[concept/samarran-stucco-styles|Samarra style C]]. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.62} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.39}
