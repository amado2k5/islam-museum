## Overview
The Khirki Masjid is a two-storey mosque on the southern edge of Khirki village in south Delhi, attributed to Khan-i Jahan, vizier of [[person/firuz-shah-tughluq|Firuz Shah Tughluq]]. {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 76} No inscription or text fixes its date: Syed Ahmad Khan gives AH 789/1387, Abha Rani and R. Nath about 1375, while Welch and Crane suggest that it predates the foundation of Firuzabad and was Firuz Shah's contribution to the capital where he lived from 1351 to 1354, which would make it the earliest work of his patronage. {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 71} {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 76} The author thinks [[person/khan-i-jahan-maqbul-tilangani|Khan-i Jahan Maqbul Tilangani]] may have planned it before his death in 1368. {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 72}

## Architecture
The mosque is square, about 52 metres a side, on a plinth three metres high, divided into four equal quarters each with an open court thirty feet square, the type R. Nath calls *chaturangana*. {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 76} {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 77} The lower storey (*tahkhana*) holds over a hundred vaulted cells, and the upper storey has pointed-arched windows filled with pierced stone screens (*khirki*), which gave the building its name. {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 77} Battered corner towers some forty-five feet high give it the look of a fortress; projecting domed gateways flanked by tapering minarets stand on the north, south and east, the east gate being the main entrance, and the central mihrab is on the west. {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 77} The walls thin from five feet at the base to two feet at the parapet. {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 77}

## Roof and columns
The roof rests on 180 stone columns, single, double and quadruple, and sixty pilasters; it is made of nine groups of nine small domes, which with the domes of the gateways and the mihrab chamber come to 85, the remaining twelve squares being vaulted. {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 78} Four domes of the north-east group are said to have fallen in a fire started in a heap of grass stored there. {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 79} The west wall holds fifteen plain mihrabs, seven on each side of the central one. {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 79} The prayer hall is not architecturally distinct from the cloisters, the whole roof being a cluster of cupolas. {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 77}

## Its state
Built of rubble with plaster now blackened by age, the mosque is a protected ruin and not in use; in her survey the author found no trace of a sanctuary hall, pulpit or qibla wall and no inscription on the east gate, and two staircases to the roof on either side of it. {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 79} {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 80} She notes that roofing part of the court while leaving four open courts for light and air was tried only once more, at Gulbarga in the south. {cite:seema-khan-mosque-architecture-firuzshah-tughlaq-2011 p. 79}
