## Overview
[[person/qansuh-al-ghawri|Al-Ghuri]]'s wakala and rab' (1504-1505) is the best preserved in Cairo, an imposing cubic mass on the narrow Sharia Muhammad Abduh linking [[building/al-azhar|al-Azhar]] with [[building/madrasa-of-sultan-al-ghuri|al-Ghuri's madrasa]] and khanqah. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.230} A wakala is an urban caravanserai for merchants; a rab' is an apartment building. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.224} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.231}

## The building
A central trilobed portal with a groin-vaulted hood and muqarnas squinches is flanked by rows of shuttered windows, grilles and mashrabiyyas of the rab' above. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.230} It leads to a court ringed by pointed arcades on octagonal ablaq piers, behind which are two storeys: storage on the ground floor and merchants' rooms on the second, with a continuous mashrabiyya balcony above the first floor. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.230} The rab' apartments are triplex, unlike the duplexes of Qaytbay's wakalas, with latrines, water-jug niches and a reception hall below and sleeping rooms and a private roof above. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.230} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.231}

## Life in the rab'
Yeomans quotes E. W. Lane's nineteenth-century description: separate lodgings let to families who cannot rent a whole house, each with one or two rooms and usually a kitchen and latrine, reached by a shared entrance and stair; single men were seldom allowed and lodged instead in a wakala. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.231} Behrens-Abouseif notes that such apartments usually had no kitchens because fuel was dear, so people relied on take-away food from cook-shops and street vendors, as Lane also observed. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.231}
