## Overview
The al-Aqsa Mosque is the large congregational mosque of the sanctuary enclosure of [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]], the area the Encyclopaedia of Islam calls the Haram. The article on the monuments of the city treats it together with the [[building/dome-of-the-rock|Dome of the Rock]], the two main buildings of the [[building/al-haram-al-sharif|Haram al-Sharif]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 377} The author, Oleg Grabar, notes that the building was rebuilt several times, that several stages of its early history are still debated among scholars, and that the standard studies are those of Hamilton and Stern. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 379} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 382}

The geographer [[person/al-muqaddasi|al-Maqdisi]] described the mosque as an eyewitness. His covered hall lies at the eastern corner of Jerusalem, toward the qibla. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p.143-144}

## The ground it stands on
According to the article, the Muslims took over the ruined area of the Herodian Temple soon after the conquest of the city. It was a large empty space, the treaty did not allow the conquerors to take over Christian buildings, and the Christians had left the area unused. The article lists further possible reasons, among them the influence of Jewish converts and a wish to oppose the Christian belief that the area should stay empty, and it adds that an earlier Muslim attachment to Jerusalem is possible but hard to demonstrate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 378}

The first Muslim building was, in the words of the pilgrim Arculf around 680, a small and roughly built quadrangular prayer place, mostly of wood and set among the ruins. Nothing is known of its inner arrangement, though it was probably a hypostyle mosque, and its exact site is unknown, although it was likely not far from the present Aqsa. It probably stood until the first decade of the 8th century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 378}

## The Umayyad mosque
The article says the first clearly documented Aqsa mosque is owed to [[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|al-Walid]], who ruled from 86/705 to 96/715. It also records a disagreement: H. Stern believes that a new mosque may already have been begun under [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik]], while Creswell and Hamilton do not. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 378} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 379}

The building had an uncertain number of naves running at right angles to the qibla wall, with a domed central nave. The number of naves and the central dome follow Stern, against Creswell. The plan was unusual for its time, and the article suggests it reflects the substructures of the platform, which consisted of north-south arcades that had to carry the building above. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 379}

It was decorated with mosaics and marble, and had carved and painted woodwork, now kept in the Palestine Archaeological Museum and in the Aqsa Museum. Although its inner organisation was a variation on the hypostyle type common then, it was placed deliberately on the same axis as the earlier Dome of the Rock. The article reads the two as one planned ensemble of a congregational building and a commemorative building, comparable with the complex of the Holy Sepulchre in the western part of the city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 379}

The article adds that the Haram was then probably called the masjid bayt al-maqdis, the mosque of Jerusalem, and that Muslim associations with the sanctuary connected to the life of the Prophet and to earlier prophets begin to appear in this period, though they are difficult to date. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 379}

## The Abbasid centuries
The two centuries after the Umayyads are, in the article, the least documented in the monumental history of Jerusalem. They consisted mostly of repairs and restorations, including major reconstructions of the Aqsa under [[person/al-mansur|al-Mansur]] and [[person/al-mahdi|al-Mahdi]]. The author values them chiefly as a sign of the continuing concern of the Muslim community for the sanctuary. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 379}

## The building as al-Maqdisi saw it
Al-Maqdisi says its foundation was the work of David, with large carved stones. Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan built on it with smaller, fine stones, and it was finer than the mosque of Damascus. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p.143-144}

The article takes the time of al-Maqdisi, around 385/985, as the end of the first period of the city's monumental history. By then the sanctuary, dominated by the Dome of the Rock and holding a large domed mosque, was already accepted as the third most important sanctuary of the faith. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 380}

## The earthquake and the rebuilding
An earthquake in the Abbasid period brought down the covered hall, except for the area around the mihrab. The caliph was told that the treasury of the Muslims could not pay for rebuilding, so he wrote to the governors of the provinces asking each to build one colonnade. They built them sturdier and of heavier workmanship than before, and that section remained as a mark on the mosque. Abd Allah ibn Tahir added fifteen colonnades on marble columns. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p.143-144}

## Doors and courtyard
The covered hall has twenty-six doors, the greatest of which is the Bronze Gate, plated with gilded brass. Its courtyard has a raised platform reached by steps from all four sides, with the domes of the Chain, the Ascension and the Prophet. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p.143-144}

The mosque measures a thousand cubits in length by the king's cubit and seven hundred in width. Its covered hall does not join the eastern wall, which is why it was said that a full row can never be completed in it. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p.143-144}

## The Fatimid rebuilding
The article places a second major rebuilding under the Fatimid caliph [[person/al-zahir-fatimid|al-Zahir]], the same ruler under whom, probably around 421-4/1030-3, the city walls were rebuilt and shortened on the south. It infers that the city had lost population, and that the [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]] mosque was therefore reduced to approximately its present dimensions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 380}

Its most notable feature was its mosaic decoration, studied by Henri Stern, who showed that the Fatimids used Umayyad models. The imperial mosaic inscription on the triumphal arch is the first in Jerusalem to begin with the verse of the Night Journey, the [[event/isra-and-miraj|Isra]]. From this and from other Fatimid building on the Haram, the author proposes that the dynasty formally tried to build up the holiness of the Jerusalem sanctuaries. He presents this as a hypothesis, and says the effort was cut short by political difficulties in the second half of the 5th/11th century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 380}

The Persian traveller [[person/nasir-khusraw|Nasir-i Khusraw]], who visited in 438/1047, described the mosaics of the western gate, now the Bab al-Silsila, as apparently similar to those of the Fatimid Aqsa. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 380}

## Crusader occupation and the Ayyubid restoration
The article says the Crusaders turned the Haram into a palace and later into the military and religious centre of the Knights Templar. Their building left the transept of the Aqsa as a completed unit that survives. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 380}

After the reconquest, according to the article, the two main sanctuaries were systematically cleared of traces of Christian occupation. In the Aqsa, [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Saladin]] set up a new mihrab with a rare mosaic decoration and brought to it the celebrated minbar that [[person/nur-al-din-mahmud-ibn-zangi|Nur al-Din]] had made for Jerusalem. The article notes that this minbar was destroyed in 1969. It adds that the restoration of the holy places was carried out in some confusion, and that the old sanctuary seems to have returned quickly to its former shape, though not necessarily to its former splendour. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 381}

## Mamluk and Ottoman times
The article describes a long period of building around the Haram under the [[polity/mamluk-sultanate|Mamluks]], mostly on its western and northern sides. In the author's judgement, the many new buildings detracted by their number from the main sanctuaries. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 381} Under the [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottomans]], after the 10th/16th century, activity consisted of constant repairs to the main sanctuaries of the Haram, which the article says declined in quality as Ottoman wealth and the city's importance declined. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 382}

## The fire of 1969 and recent work
In the historical section of the same article, the author reports a fire that damaged the Aqsa on 21 August 1969. He attributes it to a deranged Christian tourist from Australia, and writes that it caused great stirrings in the Muslim world and that it took some time before the truth was understood. About a year later, the Muslim Council began repairs. These lasted several years and were practically complete when the article was written, and many parts of the mosque, including areas the fire had not damaged, were built anew. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 376}

The article also notes that excavations south and south-west of the Haram have shown that the Umayyads used, and probably rebuilt, the Herodian staircases leading to the sanctuary. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 382}
