## Overview
When the city spread under [[person/al-nasir-muhammad-ibn-qalawun|al-Nasir Muhammad]] between the Citadel and the Fatimid walls near [[building/bab-zuwayla|Bab Zuwayla]], the growing population needed two new congregational mosques, built by his sons-in-law al-Maridani and Aqsunqur. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.151} Al-Maridani's mosque (1340) is hypostyle like al-Nasir's Citadel mosque, though its exterior has none of that austerity. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.151}

## The building
The arcades are two aisles deep and the prayer hall four, with a prominent dome over the mihrab; the courtyard arcading is very high, antique columns carrying tall piers from which pointed arches spring. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.151} The most distinctive feature of the courtyard is the mashrabiyya screen filling the prayer-hall arches; all the columns are antique, from Pharaonic, Roman and Christian sources, the finest being the red granite columns with gilded Ptolemaic capitals under the dome. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.152} The prayer hall has a marble dado inlaid with mother-of-pearl and square-Kufic inscriptions of the name Muhammad, and a mihrab with joggled voussoirs, marble marquetry, mother-of-pearl and turquoise glass, under a stucco tree panel, one of many that once covered the qibla wall. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.152}

## The minaret
Yeomans finds no conceptual innovation in the mosque, but its minaret marks a real departure from the mabkhara style: two octagonal shafts with balconies on muqarnas corbels, then an open octagonal pavilion crowned by a bulbous finial, a composition that became a defining feature of later Mamluk architecture. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.152}
