## Overview
The Ablaq Palace was part of the palace complex [[person/al-nasir-muhammad-ibn-qalawun|al-Nasir Muhammad]] built on the [[building/citadel-of-cairo|Citadel]] in 1313-1315; its remains were destroyed in Muhammad Ali's nineteenth-century rebuilding, and the engravings of the *Description de l'Égypte* keep vivid images of its ruins. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.143} It was named for its yellow and black stone and had two iwans on a courtyard. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.143}

## Al-Umari's description
Yeomans quotes the fourteenth-century al-Umari: corridors led to a lofty palace with two iwans, the larger, northern one overlooking the sultan's stables, the horse market, Cairo and its suburbs to the Nile and Giza; the other had a door by which the sultan left for the Hall of Justice, and the palace connected with three others. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.143} Within were marble dados and gold mosaics of trees and houses, gilded ceilings painted with lapis lazuli, windows of coloured glass like necklaces of gems, and floors of marble from every country. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.144}

## The royal residence
Nearby stood the Dar al-Adl, a domed pavilion with red Aswan granite columns, the ceremonial heart where the sultan received ambassadors and held audience; the private Dar al-Sultaniyya had a mosque, pavilions and small gardens, and the harem a separate palace for each wife. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.143} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.144} Yeomans links the mosaics of trees and houses with those of the courtyard of the Great Mosque of Damascus, which inspired the decoration of Baybars's mausoleum there (1278). {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.144}
