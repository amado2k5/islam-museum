## Overview
The mosque of the amir Aqsunqur (1347), son-in-law of [[person/al-nasir-muhammad-ibn-qalawun|al-Nasir Muhammad]], is known as the Blue Mosque for its profusion of Ottoman tiles, one of two mosques built for the quarter spreading between the Citadel and Bab Zuwayla. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.151} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.152} It is full of irregularities from successive restorations. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.152}

## The building
Its minaret once had four storeys, reduced to three in early twentieth-century restoration: a plain cylindrical shaft, a ribbed one, and an octagonal pavilion with a bulbous finial. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.152} Unusually, heavy octagonal piers carry cross-vaulted bays, possibly a Syrian influence since Aqsunqur had governed Tripoli and took part in the building; much of the vaulting was replaced by columns and flat roofs in Ibrahim Agha's Ottoman restoration of 1652. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.152} The mausoleum of the child sultan Kujuk, older than the mosque and aligned to the street rather than Mecca, was built in from the start, so the plan is bent and the north-west arcade wedge-shaped. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.152} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.153}

## Mihrab, minbar and tiles
The prayer hall has a fine mihrab framed by a double arch of joggled voussoirs with a white marble hood, and a white marble minbar with a bulbous canopy and coloured marble panels. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.153} Iznik tiles with flowers, vases and cypresses now cover the qibla wall, overshadowing, in Yeomans's view, the warmth of the Mamluk marble; the mix works better in the tomb Ibrahim Agha built for himself, captured in Ludwig Deutsch's painting *The Prayer at the Tomb*. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.153} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.154}
