## Overview
The khanqah-madrasa of the amir [[person/sanjar-al-jawli|Sanjar al-Jawli]] (1303-1304) stands near the [[building/masjid-ibn-tulun|Mosque of Ibn Tulun]], beside Salar's palace on the site of the old barracks, a monument to the memory of two friends, Sanjar and Salar. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.145} The two were lifelong friends who held the highest offices; Sanjar avoided the intrigues that brought Salar down, and when Salar starved to death in al-Nasir Muhammad's prison he was buried here; Sanjar went on to govern Gaza and later Hama and died in 1344. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.145}

## Exterior
The plain front is relieved only by six iron-grilled windows on the upper storey; above the roof rise two ribbed brick domes of unequal size on one axis behind a tall mabkhara minaret, forms that, as Hillenbrand observes, give little hint of a madrasa and khanqah. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.145} The drums are polygons of twenty-four and twenty sides, each face pierced with a keel-arched window, with a stucco Naskhi band under each dome's rim. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.145} The three-tier minaret, rectangular, octagonal and cylindrical, has double lights and oculi in trilobed arches on muqarnas sills. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.145} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.146}

## Interior
Built on a steep slope, its plan is asymmetrical and works on several levels: steps lead to one vestibule and then another giving onto the madrasa and the tombs. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.146} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.147} The madrasa is a roofed court with a small iwan and cells, and a large vaulted iwan to the east forms the prayer hall. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.147} A long corridor leads to a small domed tomb chamber with the earliest stone dome built in Cairo, and on its left an arcade looks onto a court through finely carved stone screens, evidence with the dome of growing skill in stone cutting. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.147} The two mausolea are domed cubes on three tiers of muqarnas squinches; Salar's is the larger and richer, and each has an ornate mihrab. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.147}

## An ambiguous function
The tombs face the qibla but the prayer hall does not, its mihrab a later, awkwardly angled addition, so the iwan may not first have been meant for prayer; the foundation inscriptions call the building only "a place", and al-Maqrizi says the madrasa taught Shafi'i law and the khanqah lodged Sufis without saying which space was which; Yeomans sees this ambiguity growing in Mamluk architecture. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.147}
