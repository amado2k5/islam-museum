## Overview
Topkapı Sarayı stands on the summit of the First Hill of Istanbul, on the site of ancient Byzantium's acropolis; Mehmet the Conqueror built it as his seat after the conquest — Kritovoulos says he ordered it in 1459 and described it complete in his entry for 1465 — and it remained the principal imperial residence until 1855, when Abdül Mecit I moved to Dolmabahçe {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 pp.104,120,139}.

## Function
The palace was far more than the sultan's home: it housed the Divan, the empire's supreme executive and judicial council, and the Palace School, the largest and most select training school of the imperial civil service, its institutions arranged around four main courtyards. The residential quarter ran along the west side of the inner courts, the Harem to the south and the Selamlık, the sultan's and princes' residence, to the north; in the empire's great days its population was between three and four thousand {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.141}. The Harem was not part of Fatih's original design, for he conceived the palace first as an administrative centre {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.141}.

## Walls and gardens
Fatih enclosed the grounds landward with a high towered wall running between the Golden Horn and the Marmara to join the medieval Byzantine sea-walls, and laid out gardens descending to the shore; the western lower gardens toward the Golden Horn are today Gülhane Park {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.145}.

## Fate
In the empire's last years the palace decayed and its seaside kiosks were destroyed for the Orient Express railway; in 1923 it became the property of the Turkish nation under the Istanbul museums administration, was restored for years before opening as a museum, and the Harem was the last part opened, in the 1960s {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 pp.139-140}.
