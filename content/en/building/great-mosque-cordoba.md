## Overview
The Great Mosque of Cordoba, known in Spanish as La Mezquita, is the cathedral mosque of the capital of the [[group/umayyads-of-cordoba|Umayyads of Cordoba]], in [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]]. The Encyclopaedia of Islam has no article on the building alone: its account is one passage inside the article on Cordoba, which calls the mosque the Kaba of the west and the lasting monument of that dynasty's age of splendour. The article says that [[person/abd-al-rahman-al-dakhil|Abd al-Rahman I]] began the mosque in 171-172/787-788 and that his successors enlarged it in turn, down to [[person/al-mansur-ibn-abi-amir|al-Mansur]]. The sections below follow that passage, and the museum's older accounts, from al-Maqqari and al-Umari, come after them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 548} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 549}

The Great Mosque of Cordoba is the well-known mosque in Cordoba. The museum's sources describe it in two accounts: one transmitted by al-Maqqari, and a detailed description by al-Umari. {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari p. 556} {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p. 212-214}

## The city and the first mosque
Cordoba lies on the right bank of the Guadalquivir. The article relates that [[person/al-hurr-ibn-abd-al-rahman-al-thaqafi|al-Hurr ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Thaqafi]], governor of al-Andalus, moved the capital from [[place/seville|Seville]] to Cordoba between 97 and 100 AH, 716-719 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 548}

It states that around 133/750 the governor [[person/yusuf-ibn-abd-al-rahman-al-fihri|Yusuf ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Fihri]] took over the church of San Vicente and made it the first congregational mosque of the city. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 548} He was overthrown by the Umayyad prince Abd al-Rahman I, the only member of his house to escape the massacre in Syria, and the city's great period of prosperity began. The article says it lasted through the whole of the Umayyad dynasty of Cordoba, which was independent of the Abbasids of Baghdad from 138 to 403 or 422 AH, 756 to 1013 or 1031 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 548}

## Abd al-Rahman I and Hisham I
The article describes Abd al-Rahman I as having laid the foundations of his dynasty's supremacy in very hard circumstances, with rivalries between northern and southern Arabs, Berbers, Spanish renegades and Mozarabs still unsettled. He began the great mosque in the last two years of his life, 171-172/787-788. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 548}

His son and successor [[person/hisham-i-andalusi|Hisham I]], who ruled from 172 to 180/788 to 796, completed it and built the minaret, which the article says was often called sawma'a or manar in Spain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 548}

## Abd al-Rahman II and Muhammad I
[[person/abd-al-rahman-ii-andalusi|Abd al-Rahman II]], who ruled from 206 to 238/822 to 852 as the son and successor of al-Hakam I, found himself obliged to enlarge the building. The article says he extended the naves southwards, adding seven transepts, and built a second mihrab in the south wall, where the chapel of Nuestra Senora de Villaviciosa was constructed later. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 548}

His son and successor [[person/muhammad-i-andalusi|Muhammad I]], who ruled from 238 to 273/852 to 886, had the older building thoroughly overhauled in 852-856, since it had been put up too hurriedly. The article says he gave particular attention to the decoration of the doors and walls, closed off with a wooden screen the maqsura reserved for the amir and the court in front of the mihrab, and built a covered passage, a sabat, from the Alcazar, the palace west of the mosque, so that the ruler could enter the maqsura directly and privately for the daily prayers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 548}

## Abd al-Rahman III
[[person/abd-al-rahman-iii-al-nasir|Abd al-Rahman III al-Nasir]], who ruled from 300 to 350/912 to 962 and whom the article presents as the zenith of the Arab period in Spain, rebuilt in splendid fashion the minaret that the earthquake of 880 had badly damaged. He enlarged the courtyard northwards, demolished the old sawma'a and built another. The article calls the new tower the forerunner of the great Hispano-Moorish minarets of the 6th/12th century, which carry an actual bell hidden behind a stone facing. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 548}

The same ruler built the country estate of [[place/madinat-al-zahra|Madinat al-Zahra]], about an hour and a half from Cordoba to the north-west. The article adds that its remains were identified in 1853, that the whole enclosure was declared a national monument in 1923, and that excavations have since restored part of its splendour, notably the great hall called the Salon Rico. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 548} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 549}

## Al-Hakam II
The article judges the most beautiful extension of the mosque, which almost doubled it, to be the work of the learned caliph [[person/al-hakam-ii|al-Hakam II al-Mustansir bi-llah]], who ruled from 350 to 366/961 to 976, son and successor of Abd al-Rahman III. It says he ordered his chief minister Jafar al-Saqlabi to extend the colonnades southwards by fourteen transepts, and that he built a splendid new maqsura, a new sabat and the third noble mihrab, which alone has survived in its entirety. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 549}

## Al-Mansur's enlargement
The last great extension was made by al-Mansur, called Almanzor in the West, the vizier and regent of the caliph [[person/hisham-ii-al-muayyad|Hisham II al-Mu'ayyad]], who ruled from 366 to 399/976 to 1009. Al-Mansur died in 392/1002. He added seven colonnades along the whole length of the building on its eastern side, raising the number of naves from eleven to nineteen. The article notes that this moved the mihrab away from the end of the central axis of the prayer hall, and that the steep slope down to the Guadalquivir made it impossible to extend the building further south. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 549}

The article on al-Mansur dates this final expansion to 377/987. It places the work in a passage about his ostentatious piety, alongside his copying of a Qur'an in his own hand, and treats it as part of his dealings with the scholars who criticised his conduct. That is the author's reading of his motives. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 453} The article on Cordoba adds that al-Mansur founded the city of al-Madina al-Zahira east of Cordoba as the seat of government, and that it was destroyed in the revolution at the start of the 5th/11th century and has quite disappeared. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 549}

## After the Umayyads
The article records that after the Umayyads ended with [[person/hisham-iii-al-mutadd|Hisham III al-Mu'tadd]], who ruled from 418 to 422/1027 to 1031, Cordoba became a republic under three members of the Jahwarid family, and passed in 1070 to the Abbadids of Seville, then to the Almoravids and in 1148 to the Almohads. Its conquest by Ferdinand III of Castile in 1236 doomed it, the article says, to decline. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 549}

At that conquest the mosque became a Christian cathedral. The article judges that it was disfigured by alterations but has on the whole kept its Arabic character: its forest of pillars, its outer court, the Patio de los Naranjos, the wall that encircles it like a fortress or monastery, and the bell tower, a 16th-century work built from the remains of the 4th/10th-century minaret. It adds that La Mezquita, the Mosque, has remained the popular name, and that the building stands in front of the old Moorish bridgehead and the tower of La Calahorra. In the author's view, almost every other building of that age has disappeared except for a few poor fragments. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 548}

## Al-Maqqari's account
Al-Maqqari reports, from the author of "Nashq al-Azhar", that the mosque had a yellow-brass chandelier carrying a thousand lamps, and that it was said its making took seven years. He also reports that it had three columns of red marble: on one was written the name of Muhammad, on the second an image of Moses' staff and the People of the Cave, and on the third an image of Noah's raven. Al-Maqqari comments that he did not find this among the careful historians of al-Andalus, and that it seems far-fetched to him. {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari p. 556}

## Al-Umari's description
Ibn Fadl Allah al-Umari says the mosque is a hundred cubits long and eighty wide, half of it roofed and half a courtyard. Its roofed part has nineteen arches, and among its pillars there are a thousand, small and large. The ceiling panels are of Tartushi pine. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p. 212-214}

In its storeroom is a Qur'an that two men lift because of its weight. Al-Umari says that it contains four leaves of the Qur'an copied by Uthman with his own hand, with spots of his blood on them. Its minbar is of ebony and box-wood. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p. 212-214}

There is a censer, on which six craftsmen worked for seven years, and al-Umari considers that it has no equal in craftsmanship anywhere in the inhabited world. Its minaret is climbed by two staircases set at odds, so that the two climbers meet only at the top, and it holds three hundred columns and three apples, one of gold and two of silver. Sixty men serve the mosque. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p. 212-214}

## For Sprouts
The Great Mosque of Cordoba is a vast and grand mosque in al-Andalus, famed for its marble columns and red-and-white arches.

## For Explorers
The Great Mosque of Cordoba is a masterpiece of Islamic architecture in al-Andalus, commenced by Emir Abd al-Rahman I in 170 AH and expanded by subsequent rulers. It features a breathtaking hypostyle prayer hall with hundreds of marble columns supporting double-tiered red-and-white arches and a dazzling golden mihrab.

## For Scholars
A monumental masterpiece of Islamic architecture and the crowning jewel of the Umayyad Caliphate in al-Andalus, founded by Emir Abd al-Rahman I in 170 AH (786 CE). Successively expanded under Abd al-Rahman II, al-Hakam II, and al-Mansur, the mosque could accommodate tens of thousands of worshipers. It is architecturally celebrated for its iconic hypostyle forest of jasper, onyx, and marble columns crowned by two-tiered horseshoe arches with alternating red brick and white stone voussoirs, ribbed melon domes, and a dazzling octagonal mihrab adorned with Byzantine glass mosaics.
