## Overview
Rawda Island had been a major military base since before the Muslim conquest, and Salah al-Din fortified it alongside the Citadel. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.105} Between 1239 and 1244 Sultan [[person/al-malik-al-salih-ayyub|al-Salih Najm al-Din Ayyub]] left the Citadel and built a new citadel on Rawda with a palace, a seat of government and barracks for a thousand Mamluks, who became known as the Bahriyya ("of the river") to distinguish them from the Burjiyya garrisoned in the Citadel. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.118}

## The building
Yeomans cites al-Maqrizi: al-Salih built sixty towers on its wall, planted trees inside and reused Pharaonic granite and marble columns for houses, palaces and a mosque. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.118} Ibn Sa'id, quoted by al-Maqrizi, described a throne room of gilt surfaces and ebony, camphor and veined marble without equal, and a fenced game park with meadows beyond leading to the Nile. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.118}

## Why Rawda?
Yeomans notes that the site was weaker for defence than the Citadel, so taste may have been the guide: space and gardens by the Nile on an island that had long been a promenade with pavilions; he also sees the sultan's choice to live beside his Mamluks as a sign of trust, a bond with far-reaching effects on Egyptian history. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.119}
