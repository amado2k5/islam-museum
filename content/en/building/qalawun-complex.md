## Overview
A complex on [[place/cairo|Cairo]]'s al-Mu'izz Street built by Sultan Qalawun in 1285: a madrasa, a hospital and a mausoleum with a gilded dome, among the finest Mamluk architecture. {cite:wikidata}

## Legacy
The complex's hospital was the most distinguished hospital in the Islamic world of its time; it is still visited today on the historic street of Cairo. {cite:wikidata}

## A new kind of complex
Yeomans dates the complex of mosque, madrasa, maristan (hospital) and mausoleum to 1284-1285 and counts it a masterpiece and an innovation uniting the community's spiritual, intellectual and medical needs under one roof, the mosque serving as an oratory integrated with other purpose-built units. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.130} [[person/qalawun|Qalawun]] had fallen ill with colic in Damascus and was cured at the maristan of Nur al-Din, and vowed to build such a hospital in Cairo if he recovered. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.130}

## The maristan
The hospital was built on the site of the smaller Fatimid western palace, loosely after Nur al-Din's; it was larger than mosque, madrasa and mausoleum together, and little survives beyond remains of the iwans; an ophthalmic hospital now occupies the site. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.131} At its heart was a four-iwan cruciform court with fountains in the east and west iwans, wards off the corners, and a library, lecture rooms, laboratories, kitchens, pharmacy, mortuary, baths and stores. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.131} Behrens-Abouseif, summarising its deed, says it served all Muslims of both sexes, all ages and conditions, the neediest first; each patient had a bed, proper food and medicine and his own vessel, physicians discussed each case, and all was free. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.131} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.132} There were men's and women's sections and wards by disease, story-tellers and music for the patients, a library with a librarian and five assistants, and a nearby school for sixty orphans. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.132}

## Façade and minaret
The façade on al-Mu'izz street is striking for the Western Gothic character of its pointed recesses, buttresses and three tiers of windows, the top tier paired under an oculus; only the stucco grilles, stepped crenellation and a tiraz band of Naskhi mark it as Islamic, and Creswell suggests Sicilian influence given Qalawun's ties with Sicily. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.132} The minaret has two rectangular stone shafts carrying a brick cylinder and helmet; Lajin restored the first two storeys and [[person/al-nasir-muhammad-ibn-qalawun|al-Nasir Muhammad]] rebuilt the third after the 1303 earthquake; its horseshoe arches show North African and Andalusian influence. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.134}

## The mausoleum
The mausoleum is almost as large as mosque and madrasa together, and Yeomans counts it one of the great masterpieces of medieval Cairo: an octagon within a rough square, the tall dome on an octagonal drum with no zone of transition, carried on arches over antique pink granite columns with gilded Corinthian capitals alternating with piers. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.135} He likens it to the Dome of the Rock, but taller in its "Gothic" proportions and more sombre, lit by green, yellow and blue stained glass; the dado of marble, mother-of-pearl and mosaic repeats the name Muhammad in square Kufic. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.135} The seven-metre mihrab is among the largest and finest in Cairo, and the great mashrabiyya screen was installed by al-Nasir Muhammad around the cenotaph covering Qalawun and al-Nasir himself, who chose to be buried here rather than in his own mausoleum next door. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.135} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.137}

## Madrasa and oratory
Of the madrasa, its four-iwan plan and three-storey student cells on the north-east survive. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.137} The oratory is the qibla iwan, divided like a basilica by an arcade of four arches perpendicular to the qibla; Behrens-Abouseif sees Byzantine influence in the mosaic of its mihrabs, the façade and the plan, and Yeomans quotes Hillenbrand: the whole Mediterranean "systematically trawled for ideas" to make the foundation truly representative of its time. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.138}
