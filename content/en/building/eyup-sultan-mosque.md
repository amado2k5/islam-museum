## Summary
The shrine of Eyüp on the upper reaches of the Golden Horn is the holiest Muslim site in Istanbul — after Mecca and Medina, in the source author's words, the third most sacred place of pilgrimage in the Islamic world {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.125}. Five years after the Conquest Fatih built a külliye around the discovered grave, comprising the türbe, a mosque, medrese, han, hamam, imaret and market {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.126}.

## The sword of the sultans
Here the Ottoman sultans were girded with the sword of their ancestor Osman Gazi on accession — a ceremony equivalent to coronation first performed for Beyazit II in 1481 {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.126}. By the end of the eighteenth century the mosque had fallen into ruins, perhaps in the 1766 earthquake; in 1798 Selim III had the remains torn down and the present mosque, finished in 1800, erected — only the minarets, a gift of Ahmet III, survive from the older building {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.126}.

## Courtyard and türbe
The approach passes through an irregular outer courtyard with two undulating baroque gateways, aged plane trees and pampered pigeons, then an inner courtyard ringed on three sides by an unusually tall colonnade {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.126-127}. The mosque is an octagon inscribed in a rectangle, with baroque decoration and pale honey-coloured stone {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.127}. A wall opposite, sheathed in tile panels of many periods, gives onto the vestibule of Eyüp's octagonal türbe, itself clad in the best İznik tiles and visible through an ornate grille {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.127}. Of the hamam, the soğukluk and hararet survive in use; a fine panel of 24 İznik tiles of c.1570 from this hamam is in the Victoria and Albert Museum {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.127}. Around the mosque lies a veritable suburb of the dead with grandee türbes and a vast hillside graveyard crowned by the café made famous by Pierre Loti {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.128}.

## For young visitors
An old Companion who defended Constantinople rests here — around his grave grew the mosque where every new sultan received the empire's sword.

## For Sprouts
A beautiful mosque in Istanbul built in honor of the noble companion Abu Ayyub al-Ansari, who hosted the Prophet in Medina.

## For Explorers
The Eyüp Sultan Mosque is situated at the Golden Horn in Istanbul, considered one of the most venerated sanctuaries of the Ottoman realm. Built adjacent to the tomb of the companion Abu Ayyub al-Ansari, Ottoman sultans visited it upon their accession to gird the sword of Osman in a solemn ceremony of justice and service.

## For Scholars
A historic imperial mosque complex and pilgrimage shrine located near the Golden Horn in Istanbul. Established in 863 AH (1458 CE) by Sultan Mehmed II following the discovery of the burial site of the venerable companion Abu Ayyub al-Ansari (Khalid ibn Zayd), who died during the early Umayyad expedition in 52 AH. The complex encompasses a mosque, mausoleum, madrasa, soup kitchen, and cemetery, completely rebuilt in classical Ottoman Baroque style under Sultan Selim III in 1800 CE. It held supreme ceremonial significance as the site where new Ottoman sultans were girded with the Sword of Osman.
