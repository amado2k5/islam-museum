## Overview
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, calls the Kaaba the most famous sanctuary of Islam, the House of God, set almost at the centre of the great mosque of [[place/mecca|Mecca]]. Muslims everywhere direct their prayers toward it, and every year hundreds of thousands of pilgrims make the greater or lesser pilgrimage and walk their ritual circuits around it. The article adds that the young Muslim community spent its early years around the Kaaba, and that it holds a place for Muslims comparable to that of the temple in [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]] for ancient Jewry {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 335}. The sections below follow the article. The Muslim understanding is given first and labelled; academic and historical views are labelled separately, and the museum gives no verdict between them.

## The building and its surroundings
The article links the name to the cube-like look of the building and says the word was once used for other sanctuaries of similar shape. The structure is about fifty feet high, with shorter walls of about thirty-five feet and a façade of about forty. The façade faces north-east and holds the door, set about seven feet above the ground. The Black Stone is built into the eastern corner, and the Hijr begins beyond the northern corner {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 335}. The walls are layers of grey-blue stone from the hills around Mecca, on a low marble base. The four corners roughly face the four points of the compass and are named the Black Stone corner in the east, the Iraqi corner in the north, the Syrian in the west and the Yemeni in the south {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 335}.

The walls are covered by the [[concept/kiswa|kiswa]], usually black brocade with the profession of faith woven in and a gold-embroidered band carrying Qur'anic texts. According to the article, Egypt has traditionally supplied it since Mamluk times, except in periods of political tension, and each year the Banu Shayba cut the replaced coverings into pieces that are distributed and sold as relics. Inside, three wooden pillars hold the roof, lamps hang from it, and the walls carry building inscriptions {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 335}.

The Black Stone sits about five feet above the ground. It was once broken into three pieces and several fragments and is now held together by a ring of stone in a silver band. The article says its real nature is hard to determine, because touching and kissing have worn its surface smooth; it has been called both lava and basalt, and it is reddish black with red and yellow particles {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 335}. The wall between the stone and the door is the multazam, where visitors press their chests while praying. A gilt spout, the spout of mercy, juts from the north-west wall, and the stretch between it and the west corner marks the exact direction of prayer. Opposite that wall stands a low semicircular wall of white marble, the hatim; the space inside it, the Hijr, once belonged to the Kaaba, so worshippers circle outside it. Tradition says Ismail and his mother [[person/hajar|Hajar]] are buried there {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 336}. See also [[concept/hijr-kaaba|the Hijr]].

In front of the façade stand the arched gate of the Banu Shayba, the small domed building of the Station of Ibrahim and the Zamzam well. The Station keeps a stone with the prints of two feet, which tradition ascribes to [[person/ibrahim|Ibrahim]] while building, preserved by miracle {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 336}. The article also describes the works begun in 1956: the circuit pavement relaid, the Zamzam well opened to a staircase and an underground hall divided for men and women, the small buildings that sheltered the imams of the four schools removed, and the walk between [[place/al-safa|al-Safa]] and Marwa made a spacious covered passage {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 336}.

## The Muslim account of the Kaaba's origins
The article says Muslim tradition is the chief source on the early story, and that modern Muslims tend to hold to the letter of the Qur'an and to discount other accounts more and more. In the Medinan suras, it says, [[person/ibrahim|Ibrahim]] and [[person/ismail|Ismail]] are said to have laid the foundations; the Station of Ibrahim is named as a place of prayer; Ibrahim is told to proclaim the pilgrimage; and the Kaaba is called the first sanctuary established on earth, the sacred house and the ancient house. The article states that for Muslims these accounts rest on the Qur'an alone {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 336}. It also records later legends: the Kaaba's creation at the beginning of the world, its fate in the flood, Ibrahim's guidance to Arabia, and Gabriel bringing the Black Stone, kept since the flood on [[place/jabal-abi-qubays|Abu Qubays]]. In these legends the stone was white and took its colour from the sins of the pagan era. [[person/muhammad-abduh|Muhammad Abduh]] cited such legends when protesting against his coreligionists' imaginative excesses {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 339}.

## What the article says historians can establish
Apart from Muslim traditions, the article says, practically nothing is known of the Kaaba's history. The sole reason to think it existed in the second century CE is Ptolemy's mention of Mecca as Macoraba, which Glaser thought might mean a temple. The accounts of the campaign of [[person/abraha|Abraha]], elaborated with legend, suggest that it existed and was worshipped in the sixth century but say nothing of its appearance. The distribution of offices among the sons of [[person/qusayy-ibn-kilab|Qusayy]] shows, the article says, a carefully regulated cult several generations before Muhammad. Whether Qusayy demolished and rebuilt the edifice, as historians say, it calls impossible to determine. It asks, without answering, whether the Ibrahim stories reflect Coptic traditions of the Holy Family's flight to Egypt, transposed by Judaized circles in Arabia, and concludes that this will never be known {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 336}.

## Rebuilding and later history
The article's firmer history begins with [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]]. When he had reached manhood, a fire from a woman's censer is said to have damaged the building. The Meccans used the wood of a Byzantine ship cast ashore at Jidda, with a man called Baqum remembered as its captain or as the carpenter who advised them. The old Kaaba was man-high and roofless, with its threshold at ground level, so floods entered easily. The new one had alternating layers of stone and wood, double the height, a roof, and a raised door reached by a ladder. The Meccans quarrelled over who should set the Black Stone; Muhammad laid it on a cloak, had the tribal heads each hold an edge, and placed it himself {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 337}. See also [[event/rebuilding-of-the-kaaba|the rebuilding of the Kaaba]].

At the conquest of Mecca in 8/629 Muhammad left the building unaltered. Tradition says he later remarked that only the Meccans' recent conversion stopped him from making changes. These intentions were realized in 64/683 by [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr]], who was besieged in Mecca by al-Husayn ibn Numayr; catapult stones damaged the house badly, and a fire split the Black Stone in three. After the Umayyad army withdrew, he resolved to rebuild. Most people, led by Ibn Abbas, left the town in fear of punishment from heaven, and none dared begin the demolition until Ibn al-Zubayr climbed up with an axe himself. The new house used Meccan stone and Yemeni mortar and stood twenty-seven ells high. According to a tradition of the Prophet it included the Hijr, with two ground-level doors, one for entering and one for leaving {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 337}.

This lasted briefly. In 74/693 [[person/al-hajjaj-ibn-yusuf|al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf]] took Mecca and killed Ibn al-Zubayr; in agreement with the caliph [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik]] he separated the Hijr again and walled up the west door, so the building largely regained its pre-Islamic form, which survives. Popular piety, the article says, has always resisted considerable innovation, and floods stayed a danger: a copper girdle was used in 1611, and renovations were decided in 1630, reusing old stones as far as possible. The Kaaba survived the Qarmatian invasion of 317/929, when only the Black Stone was carried off, to be returned after about twenty years {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 337}. See [[event/black-stone-theft-317|the theft of the Black Stone]] and [[event/black-stone-return-339|its return]].

## The Kaaba in early Islam
The article notes that the Qur'an is silent on Muhammad's own relation to the Kaaba in his Meccan period. The early Medinan community prayed toward Jerusalem; about a year and a half after the emigration it turned toward Mecca {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 337}. At about that time, the article says, the Qur'an began to stress the religion of Ibrahim, presenting Islam as a return to a primitive purity obscured by Judaism and Christianity; it notes that scholars disagree about how far to read this historically. It judges it incontrovertible that a whole pre-Islamic ritual was taken over by Islam after being purified and given a strictly monotheistic orientation {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 338}.

The pact of [[event/treaty-of-hudaybiyya|al-Hudaybiya]] in 6/627 opened a prospect of taking part in the Mecca cult, and the lesser pilgrimage took place in the year 7. Then came the [[event/conquest-of-mecca|conquest of Mecca]]. Tradition says that 360 idols stood around the building and fell when touched with the Prophet's rod, and that the statue of [[object/hubal|Hubal]], said to have been set up by [[person/amr-ibn-luhayy|Amr ibn Luhayy]], and the pictures of prophets were removed. When the pictures were being washed, the article reports, Muhammad covered those of Jesus and Mary with his hands and asked that the rest be washed out {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 338}.

The offices of Mecca had been held by one man's line before Islam, on the historians' account: the council house and standard, the feeding and watering of pilgrims, and the keeping and doorkeeping of the Kaaba. When Muhammad took Mecca he said he had abolished all but the watering, left with [[person/abbas-ibn-abd-al-muttalib|al-Abbas]], and the guardianship, which he gave to [[person/uthman-ibn-talha|Uthman ibn Talha]], who let his cousin [[person/shayba-ibn-uthman|Shayba]] act for him. The Banu Shayba are doorkeepers to this day {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 338}. At the pilgrimage of the year 9 [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]], as Muhammad's representative, announced new arrangements, given in the form of a revelation, barring idolaters from the Meccan festival after a period of four months. In 10/631 Muhammad led the pilgrimage himself, and tradition says no idolater was present: the Kaaba had become an exclusively Muslim sanctuary {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 338}.

## Veneration and devotion
The article describes two special ceremonies, the opening of the Kaaba on set days, men first and then women, with prayer inside regarded as particularly meritorious, and the washing of its interior after the pilgrimage {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 338}. Veneration, it says, extends to all that touches the building: the Black Stone, the spout, the multazam and above all Zamzam water. It records a saying of [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]], probably authentic in its view, that the stone neither helps nor harms and that he kissed it only because the Messenger of God did {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 338}. It cites al-Batanuni on the awe felt in prayer there, and says that even the Shia and the Wahhabis left the Kaaba its place; only the Qarmatians were an evident exception {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 339}.

Many modern writers, it reports, stress that God chose Mecca as the place of revelation and that the Kaaba symbolizes the unity of the Muslim world. Mystics differ. For law-minded Sufis such as [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]], the circuit gains its worth when it lifts a person spiritually. [[person/ibn-arabi-muhyi-al-din|Ibn al-Arabi]] is cited as saying the true Kaaba is our own being, while [[person/al-hujwiri|al-Hujwiri]] quotes mystics who no longer need it, and [[person/bayazid-al-bistami|Abu Yazid al-Bistami]] is cited as describing three pilgrimages, seeing first the house, then the house and its Lord, then the Lord alone {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 339}.

## Religion before Islam: the article's historical reading
The article presents this as hypothesis. Ptolemy's name suggests a sanctuary of one or more deities. Hubal, whom it says might be called the god of Mecca and the Kaaba, stood inside, while al-Lat, al-Uzza and Manat, named in the Qur'an, were also worshipped; Hubal is not named there, and what place God held among them is unclear. No tradition ties the Black Stone to a particular god {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 339} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 340}. The Kaaba made the surrounding area consecrated ground, a sacred zone in which the truce of God held, arms were forbidden, killers by accident found refuge, and blood, trees and animals were protected; the article says Islam confirmed these rules. See [[concept/haram-sanctuary|the sanctuary]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 340}.

It judges it most probable that the Kaaba rites and the halt at [[place/arafat|Arafat]] were fused before Islam, and notes traces of astral symbolism, such as votive golden suns and moons and the 360 idols, while cautioning that this view cannot safely be put first. It calls the pagan cult certainly syncretistic, and says that how far North Semitic cults were present in Mecca cannot be told. It adds that animals were sacrificed at the Kaaba in the pagan period, whereas in Islam the slaughter is at [[place/mina|Mina]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 340}.

## Summary
The Kaaba is the House whose foundations Abraham and Ismail raised. Its custodianship passed to the descendants of Ismail, then to Jurhum, then to Khuza'a, and then to Quraysh.

## The story
Abraham settled Hajar and her son in a barren valley beside the Sacred House of God, which at that time was only a raised mound of earth, like a hillock, to which the floods came and divided around its right and left sides {cite:sahih-al-bukhari}. On his third journey, he and Ismail built the Kaaba and raised its foundations {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}.

Ismail held the leadership of Mecca and the custody of the House all his life. It then passed to Jurhum for about twenty centuries, then to Khuza'a for three hundred years, and then to Qusayy and Quraysh from about 440 CE {cite:qalb-jazirat-al-arab}. The idolaters filled the Sacred Mosque with idols. When the Prophet conquered Mecca, he found three hundred and sixty idols around the House, and he ordered them removed and burned {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}.

## For young visitors
The Kaaba is God's House in Mecca. Abraham and his son Ismail built it, and people have continued to make pilgrimage to it through the ages.

## From the introduction to The Sealed Nectar
When Abraham settled Hajar and Ismail, the site of the House was a raised mound of earth, like a hillock, to which floods came and divided around its right and left sides. Then Abraham and Ismail built the Kaaba and raised its foundations, during Abraham's third journey, which is preserved in the record {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.18} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.20} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/474-476}. Ismail held charge of the House all his life; then Jurhum, for about twenty centuries; then Khuza'a, for three hundred years; then Qusayy and Quraysh from about 440 CE {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.27} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.29} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.30} {cite:qalb-jazirat-al-arab p.230-237}. When Jurhum was expelled, [[person/amr-ibn-al-harith-al-jurhumi|'Amr ibn al-Harith al-Jurhumi]] buried the two gazelles of the Kaaba and the Black Stone in the well of Zamzam {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.28} {cite:ibn-hisham-sira 1/114-115}. The custodianship of the Kaaba was among Qusayy's five offices, and it later passed to the Banu Abd al-Dar {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.31} (see [[concept/manasib-quraysh|the offices of Quraysh]]). Abraha wanted to divert the Arabs' pilgrimage to his church and to demolish the House {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.41}. 'Amr ibn Luhayy placed Hubal inside the Kaaba, and the idolaters filled the Sacred Mosque with idols, until on the day of the Conquest the Prophet found three hundred and sixty idols around the House {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.35} {cite:mukhtasar-sirat-al-rasul p.50-54}.

## Quraysh's rebuilding in The Sealed Nectar (pp. 61–62)
The Kaaba had been a heap of rubble rising above a man's height, nine cubits high since the time of Ismail, and it had no roof. Its treasure inside was stolen, and the assaults of time had weakened its structure. Then a flood swept through Mecca five years before the mission, and the building was close to collapse, so Quraysh rebuilt it in the thirty-fifth year after the Prophet's birth ([[event/rebuilding-of-the-kaaba|the rebuilding of the Kaaba]]) {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.61} {cite:ibn-hisham-sira 1/192-197}. Pure funds fell short for them, so from the northern side they left out about six cubits, which are the Hijr and the Hatim. They raised its door above the ground, and they roofed it on six pillars when the structure had reached fifteen cubits {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.62} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/215} {cite:muhadarat-tarikh-al-umam-al-islamiyya 1/64-65}.

After the rebuilding, the author describes it as roughly square and fifteen metres high. The side containing the Black Stone and the side opposite it are ten metres and ten centimetres long; the Stone is one and a half metres above the floor of the tawaf. The side containing the door and the side opposite it are twelve metres long, and the door is two metres high. A ledge of masonry called the shadhirwan runs around it from the outside, averaging a quarter of a metre in height and about a third of a metre in width. It is part of the original House, but Quraysh left it out {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.62}.

## For Sprouts
The Kaaba is God's house in Mecca; Ibrahim and his son Isma'il raised its foundations, and the house's custody passed through Isma'il's descendants, then Jurhum, then Khuza'a, then Quraysh. People have made pilgrimage to it through the ages, and on the day of the Opening the Prophet had three hundred and sixty idols removed from around it.

## For Scholars
The Kaaba: the house whose foundations Ibrahim and Isma'il raised; its custody passed through Isma'il's descendants, then Jurhum, then Khuza'a, then Quraysh. Ibrahim settled Hajar and her child in a barren valley beside the Sacred House, then no more than a rise in the ground like a hillock that floods passed right and left; on his third recorded journey the two built the Kaaba and raised its foundations. Isma'il held the leadership of Mecca and the house's custody all his life, then Jurhum for about twenty centuries, then Khuza'a for three hundred years, then Qusayy and Quraysh from about 440 CE. When Jurhum was expelled, Amr ibn al-Harith buried the Kaaba's two gazelles and the Black Stone's stone in the well of Zamzam; the custodianship was one of Qusayy's five offices, passing then to Banu Abd al-Dar. Abraha sought to divert the Arabs' pilgrimage to his church and destroy the house; Amr ibn Luhayy set Hubal inside the Kaaba; and the polytheists filled the Sacred Mosque with idols until the Prophet found three hundred and sixty around the house on the day of the Opening — brought out and burned.
