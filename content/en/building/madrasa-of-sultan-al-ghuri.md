## Overview
When [[person/qansuh-al-ghawri|al-Ghuri]] came to the throne at sixty in 1501 he restored stability by force of character and refilled a bankrupt treasury with heavy taxes, spending on major works including his madrasa, khanqah and wakala in the quarter that bears his name, the Ghuriyya. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.229} The madrasa and mosque stand on the west side of al-Mu'izz street facing [[building/mausoleum-sabil-kuttab-al-ghouri|his khanqah and mausoleum]]. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.229}

## The minaret
The minaret dominates, the second tallest in Cairo, of four square-sectioned shafts crowned by five bulbous finials; Yeomans counts the square section an innovation, shared by the minaret of the amir Qanibay al-Rammah's complex (1503), and cites Henri Stierlin's suggestion of Syrian masons who had fled to Cairo fearing an Ottoman invasion. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.229} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.230}

## The prayer hall
The prayer hall is of the [[concept/qaa|qa'a]] type, much larger than Qaytbay's and close in concept to the mosque of Qijmas, but its qibla iwan is wider than court and north-west iwan together, almost screened off by a great pointed horseshoe arch. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.230} It has a black and white marble dado and much carved stone; Yeomans finds the craftsmanship coarser than that of al-Ghuri's predecessors, but with confidence and boldness of scale. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.230}
