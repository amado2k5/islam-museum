## For Sprouts
Hagia Sophia is a grand historic landmark in Istanbul, famous for its massive dome and ancient artistry through the ages.

## For Explorers
Hagia Sophia is among the most famous historical monuments in Istanbul, originally built as a Byzantine cathedral in the 6th century CE and converted into a mosque following the city's opening in 857 AH. Preserving the ancient structure, Muslims added elegant minarets and grand calligraphic medallions, making it an enduring symbol of architectural dialogue.

## For Scholars
A world-renowned architectural monument overlooking the Bosphorus in historic Istanbul. Originally consecrated in 537 CE under Byzantine Emperor Justinian I as the supreme cathedral of Eastern Christendom, it represented the pinnacle of late antique engineering with its daring pendentive dome. Following the 1453 CE conquest, Sultan Mehmed II endowed it as the city's imperial mosque, preserving its interior structure while instituting Islamic liturgical features. Later reinforced by Mimar Sinan with buttresses and four minarets, and adorned with monumental calligraphic roundels, it stands as an architectural nexus between civilizations.

## Encyclopaedia of Islam: the foundation and the rebuilding under Justinian

Aya Sofya, the largest mosque in Constantinople (Istanbul), was once the leading metropolitan church of eastern Christendom. It was known generally as the Megale Ekklesia up to 1453. Around 400 it was called Zoola, without the article, and from the fifth century it was known as Hagia Sophia. According to the most recent research, the original church was not built by Constantine the Great himself, but by his son, in accordance with the emperor's last wishes, after the son's victory over his brother-in-law Licinius. It was built in the form of a basilica and consecrated on 15 February. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 823}

The first basilica, with a wooden roof, went up in flames in June 404 on the occasion of the expulsion of John Chrysostom. The rebuilt church stood undamaged for more than a century, until the night of 13 January 532, when it burned again, together with much of the city and the imperial archives, during the riots of the rival hippodrome factions. The emperor Justinian at once resolved to rebuild it in a splendour never seen before. Even before the fire, materials from old monuments in the provinces, where heathen works of art had been left to decay, were to be sent to the imperial residence; after the fire these materials were largely used in the rebuilding. Two architects were put in charge, the first of them named Anthemius. The emperor required that the new building be proof against both fire and earthquake, so they chose a dome-and-cupola design as the surest means of escaping these dangers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 823-824}

The magnificent building opened on 27 December 537 with enormous pomp, and the proud Justinian is said to have exclaimed, "Solomon, I have surpassed you!" Even during his own reign, however, the eastern part of the dome collapsed in an earthquake on 7 May 558, and the ambo, the tabernacle and the altar were smashed. The dome had been designed too flat, so it was raised by more than 20 feet and the supports of the great pillars were strengthened. The church reopened on 24 December 562. Its setting was grand: to the south lay the Augusteum, with an equestrian statue of Justinian, used for national festivities; to the north, within the walls of today's Saray, stood court churches, noble monasteries and the palaces of court officials; and to the east, towards the sea, stood the imperial palace. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 824}

## Plan, interior and the Ottoman changes

The church covered an area that is almost square: its internal length is about 75 metres, excluding the main apse to the east, and its breadth is about 70 metres. The floor is laid out in the form of a cross, and above it rises the almost hemispherical pendentive dome, to a height of 56 metres. The outside walls alone could not have carried it, so it was supported by four great pillars, which were braced by smaller arches and their pillars, structurally important though less conspicuous. To the east and west of the dome stand two further semi-domes, each covering three semicircular chambers. Of greatest importance for the shaping of the interior was the two-storey arrangement of the side chambers next to the central aisle, where the galleries, as was customary in Byzantine churches, were reserved for women. The weight of the building is carried by 107 columns, of green marble in most cases and in some of red porphyry.

The walls and vault of the original building were of brick throughout. The sanctuary, the bema, lay east of the central part of the church and was divided from it by a tall screen adorned with pictures and openwork pillars; it held the altar and its canopy and led into the main apse. In the days of Justinian there were 425 priests, who also served three other churches, and 100 doorkeepers.

For the medieval visitor the interior made an overwhelming impression through its wealth of ornament: marble used lavishly everywhere, pictures of Christ and of the Mother of God, of the Prophets, Apostles and other saints that turned the walls into a sea of colour, the mighty seraphim in the spherical triangles of the main dome, and the gold mosaic that covered dome and walls with a splendour never seen before. The mosaic decoration was probably not finished until the last years of Justinian and into the reign of Justin II.

The first major repairs were made under Basil II. Part of the dome collapsed in an earthquake on 26 October 986, and the emperor had the damage repaired; the flying buttresses on the western façade probably date from that time. In 1204 the church was severely damaged during the Latin sack of Constantinople. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 824-825}

The conquest brought large changes to the interior, following the rules of the victorious religion. The mosaics that had covered the walls and vaults, and which their Greek makers had intended to last for ever, were hidden under a grey lime-wash. Evliya Celebi, writing in the seventeenth century, still mentions the mosaics, a few of which must have remained visible in his time. The screen between the clergy and the laity was torn down, and the rich decoration of the bema was stripped. Byzantine churches faced Jerusalem, whereas the prayer had to be performed facing Mecca; since the conquest the Turks have prayed towards the south, not towards the eastern wing of the mosque. The article describes the moment of entry, when a leader of the conquering army pronounced the confession of faith and, with his followers, prostrated himself before the one God, and so the church was dedicated to Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 825}

From the time of Mehmed II a preacher, carrying a wooden sword, ascended the pulpit on Fridays, on every afternoon of Ramadan and at the Bayram festivals, and two flags always stood beside the pulpit. Mehmed II built the great buttresses against the south wall and the first of the tall, slender minarets. Selim II built the two buttresses in the north and the second minaret at the north-east corner; his son Murad III was responsible for the other two. Murad III also carried out thorough repairs, corrected the minor defects that had come to light with time, and embellished the bare chamber. He placed two huge alabaster urns near the main entrance, each holding 1,250 litres, and donated two large estrades (mastaba): one from which the Qur'an was recited through most of the day, and one for the prayer leaders. At great expense he gilded the half-moon that crowned the dome, which had replaced the cross, so that the Muslim subjects of the Porte could behold the emblem of their faith from as far off as the summit of Bithynian Olympus. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 825}

In the second half of the sixteenth century the churchyard immediately south of the mosque was converted into a mausoleum for the sultans. The oldest tomb is that of Selim II. His son Murad III and his grandson Mehmed III are buried there too, together with the nineteen brothers whom Mehmed III had killed on his accession. A few decades later the dethroned sultan Mustafa I died suddenly, and no suitable grave could be found at once; the old baptistry on the southern side of the narthex, which the Turks had used for oil storage since the conquest, was taken over for the purpose. Later the nephew of Mustafa I, Sultan Ibrahim, was buried there as well. The large oil stores were then kept in the hall and courtyard on the north side of the baptistry.

Sultan Murad IV (1623-1640), whose reign saw a certain measure of general revival, had the bare walls embellished in a memorable way by the great calligrapher Bicakci-zade Mustafa Celebi with large gold-lettered quotations from the Qur'an; some letters, such as the alif, were as much as ten ells long. Those painted verses are outshone by the clear, boldly drawn names of the first four caliphs, written by Teknedji-zade Ibrahim Efendi. A magnificent minbar dates from those years. Ahmed III erected the enclosed raised throne for the ruler, the maqsura, on the north side of the main apse. Mahmud I (1730-1754) donated the large sultan's loggia on the first floor of the gallery, a fountain and a school in the courtyard on the south side, the large soup kitchen (imaret) in the north and, above all, the valuable library in the mosque itself, which was built on an older foundation already in the building. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 825}

From the time of Murad IV, the conqueror of Baghdad, there was a perceptible decline in the maintenance of the mosque, which coincided with the general decline of the empire. In 1847 Sultan Abd al-Medjid commissioned the Italian brothers Fossati as architects to renovate the building, to avoid the threatened collapse of some parts and to give the whole a more dignified appearance. The work took two years. The lime-wash was left only in the places that had depicted human forms; elsewhere the walls came back into prominence with the disclosure of their old splendour. The red and yellow striped paint on the outside dates from that restoration. The restoration covered all the minarets except that of Mehmed II, which had dealt the final and decisive blow against the Byzantine empire; the Italian architects were eventually allowed to make this minaret as high as the others. The eight round tablets inscribed by the calligrapher Mustafa Izzet Efendi were put into the mosque under Abd al-Medjid. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 825}

The mosque has not suffered from earthquakes since the tenth century. The article credits this largely to the buttresses which the last Byzantines and the Turks put up against three sides of the walls, so that this gigantic building, standing on seismic ground, has served mankind longer than any other building in Europe. The storms that blow from the Balkans or from the sea, on the other hand, seem to be increasingly dangerous to the mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 825-826}
