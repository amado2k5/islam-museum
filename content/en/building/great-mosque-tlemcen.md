## Overview
The Great Mosque of [[place/tlemcen|Tlemcen]], round which the quarters of the city are grouped, is dated by [[person/rexford-newcomb|Rexford Newcomb]] to 1136. {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p.27}

## Newcomb's description
Its brick minaret rises to 112 feet, adorned with marble columns and encased in faience mosaic of varied designs. An alabaster (Algerian onyx) fountain stands in the centre of the court, seventy-two columns carry the arches of the prayer hall, and the mihrab is worked with fine arabesques. {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p.27} {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p.28}

## Tlemcen and ceramics
Newcomb holds that faience revetments became popular in North Africa about the fourteenth century and that no part of western Islam used architectural ceramics with better taste than the builders of Tlemcen; following the Marçais brothers he cites a text of Ibn Khaldun on the borrowings of [[person/abu-tashfin-i-zayyanid|Abu Tashfin]] from the art of Granada. {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p.20}
