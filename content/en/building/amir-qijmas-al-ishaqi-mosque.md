## Overview
The mosque of the amir Qijmas al-Ishaqi is one of the most beautiful of [[person/qaytbay|Qaytbay]]'s age; Yeomans dates it 1478-1479, while his map gives 1480-1481. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.151} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.225} Qijmas was a distinguished man who became viceroy of Damascus, and his many offices are blazoned on the window grilles of his sabil. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.227}

## The building
It stands on the triangular meeting of Darb al-Ahmar and Abu Hurayba streets near [[building/bab-zuwayla|Bab Zuwayla]]; the cramped site put the sabil-kuttab across the street and linked the mosque to its ablution court by a raised passage and bridge. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.227} It is built over shops now below street level, and its stepped façades catch the eye with ablaq masonry, joggled lintels, heart-shaped arabesques and roundels recalling Qaytbay's sabil-kuttab, while dome and minaret are relatively plain. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.227}

## Interior
The interior is of the [[concept/qaa|qa'a]] type: qibla iwan, court and north-west iwan diminishing in that order, the court covered by a gilded octagonal lantern and the side iwans shallow recesses. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.227} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.228} Yeomans calls it a gem, with much stained glass, rich door panelling, gilded calligraphy and a black, white and brown marble dado; the white marble mihrab is cut with shallow arabesques under a Kufic band with touches of blue faience. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.228} Its chief treasure is the inlaid wooden minbar, matching Qaytbay's, in which bone replaces costly ivory. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.228}
