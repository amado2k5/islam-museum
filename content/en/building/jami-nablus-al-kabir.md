## Summary
The Great Mosque of Nablus (the Salahi Mosque) is the largest mosque in [[place/nablus|Nablus]], about 1,460 square metres, standing in the old town on the remains of a sixth-century Byzantine basilica. Its conversion into the congregational mosque is traditionally assigned to the Ayyubid period after 1187, though the attribution is traditional — al-Muqaddasi already described it in the tenth century as a well-paved mosque in the middle of the city. {cite:unesco-tl-6956-nablus} {cite:ircica-islamic-architectural-heritage}

## Building history
The Franks rebuilt it as a church in 1167; it became a mosque again after Saladin retook the city in 1187. The Templars burned it in the 1242 sack of Nablus, and it was rebuilt under the Mamluks by the late thirteenth century, as al-Dimashqi's description around 1300 attests. Under the Ottomans it received a marble minbar in 1016 AH (1607) and the Waziri iwans in 1149 AH (1736); it was restored after the 1927 earthquake and again in 1996. {cite:unesco-tl-6956-nablus} {cite:taawon-yalla-falasteen}

## Inscriptions
Reused Kufic fragments bearing the name of Allah survive under the northern minaret's base, and two Naskh decree plaques dated to early Jumada I 713 AH (August 1313) order the restitution of wrongs and fair dealing. Ottoman inscriptions record the 1016 AH minbar and the iwans of 1149 and 1153 AH. {cite:taawon-yalla-falasteen}
