## Overview
The mosque of Muhammad Ali (1830-1848) dominates the [[building/citadel-of-cairo|Citadel]] and much of the city; Muhammad Ali built it in his rebuilding of the Citadel as the seat of government, for which notable medieval buildings, among them the [[building/ablaq-palace-cairo|Ablaq Palace]] and the Hall of Justice, were demolished. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.237} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.239}

## The building
Its broad shape and composition are Ottoman, after the Yeni Cami in Istanbul (1663): a central dome with four half-domes; its decoration is European baroque. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.238} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.239} The architect first chosen was the Frenchman Pascal Coste; the final choice fell on an Armenian. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.238} In the courtyard stands a clock-tower (1845), a gift of King Louis Philippe of France, in return for which Muhammad Ali gave the obelisk now in the Place de la Concorde; below it is an octagonal alabaster fountain, and the alabaster facing the arcades and first storey, which gives it its popular name, the "Alabaster Mosque", came from Upper Egypt. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.238} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.239}

## Yeomans's judgement and those he quotes
Yeomans holds that the mosque is not Egyptian but an imported European-Ottoman synthesis, and that its interior is profane in taste and unrelated to Islamic tradition; he quotes Flaubert (1849) on the "deplorable taste" of the family tombs, Florence Nightingale calling it "tawdrily ornamented", and Robert Curzon lamenting the demolition of the granite-columned Hall of Justice to make way for it. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.238} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.239} He ends his book by noting that Western tourists often see nothing of Cairo's Islamic heritage but this mosque, one of his reasons for writing; these are his views and those of the writers he cites. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.239}
