## Overview
Yeomans notes the claim that [[person/baybars|al-Zahir Baybars]] put up some 269 buildings across the Mamluk empire, but all that survives of substance in Cairo is his mosque (1266-1269), standing ruined in the northern suburbs beyond [[building/bab-al-nasr|Bab al-Nasr]] and [[building/bab-al-futuh|Bab al-Futuh]]. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.127} It was the first major mosque built since [[building/mosque-of-al-hakim|al-Hakim's]], which the Ayyubids had used as their main congregational mosque, preferring to refurbish rather than build anew. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.127} Baybars supervised its planning himself and organised the reuse of materials from Crusader buildings at Jaffa. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.127}

## Plan
Its long elevation with projecting gates and corner buttresses recalls the mosque of Mahdiya in Tunisia, while its plan with a large domed sanctuary suggests Seljuk influence. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.128} It is hypostyle: a courtyard with arcades three aisles deep on the north-east and south-west and two aisles deep on the north-west; the prayer hall is six aisles deep, parallel to the qibla, except three taller central aisles perpendicular to the mihrab leading to the dome. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.128} The wooden dome, of material salvaged from the Crusader citadel of Jaffa, covered nine bays on heavy piers, close in size to the [[building/mausoleum-of-imam-al-shafii|mausoleum of al-Shafi'i]]; Yeomans counts it an innovation creating a discrete sanctuary as in Persian and Anatolian mosques, and Hillenbrand thinks it also served as a maqsura for the sultan. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.128}

## Façades and gates
Buttresses break the walls where the inner arcades meet them, with pointed windows between that once held stucco grilles; outer decoration is confined to the three gates and the stepped crenellation. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.128} The main gate is a cubic block projecting from the centre of the north-west wall, domed on pendentives, its arch with cushion voussoirs like Bab Zuwayla and the Holy Sepulchre; it formed the base of the minaret, whose rectangular stump survived into the time of the *Description de l'Égypte*. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.128} The side gates have a lobed arch and an arch with Romanesque-like chevrons. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.128} Yeomans sees its fortified severity as reflecting its builder's personality, and notes that Napoleon garrisoned troops in it. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.128} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.129}
