## Overview
The Nila Gumbad ("Blue Dome") stands just outside the south-east corner of the compound of [[building/humayun-tomb|Humayun's tomb]], overlooking the railway; square with chamfered angles, it is covered by a single dome of less than hemispherical shape on a cylindrical drum, its four archways open and without a mihrab. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 197} It takes its name from the blue tilework that once covered it, mostly intact on the north facade, in green, blue, yellow and white with blue predominating, in geometric designs. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 197}

## Interior and dome
The interior hall is artificially divided into two storeys; its arches carry bold stucco medallions with arabesques, and the soffit of the dome has a beautiful arabesque in incised stucco, blue and deep red predominating, with eight tablets round it that hang like coloured chandeliers. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 197} {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 198} The brick dome sits on the rubble substructure so that dome and drum merge into each other, which Nath sees as the way the bulbous dome later evolved. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 198} A course of finely dressed red stone bricks at the springing gives a colour contrast, and the whole dome was covered in blue tile, much of it now peeled away. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 198}

## Attribution and date
There is no tombstone and no Persian inscription; popularly it is said to hold Fahim Khan, a servant of Abd al-Rahim Khan-i Khanan in Jahangir's reign, which Nath calls a misnomer without support, arguing that its single dome, incised stucco, tilework and lack of stonework mark it as much earlier, about 1560. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 198}
