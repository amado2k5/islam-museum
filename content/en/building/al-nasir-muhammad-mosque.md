## Overview
The mosque of [[person/al-nasir-muhammad-ibn-qalawun|al-Nasir Muhammad]] in the [[building/citadel-of-cairo|Citadel]] is one of the few congregational mosques of the Bahri Mamluk era and served as the Citadel's royal mosque until Ottoman times. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.140} It is a free-standing hypostyle building with two minarets, begun in 1318 and rebuilt in 1335 with much higher walls. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.141}

## Exterior and gates
Its plain dressed-stone exterior is austere, broken only by rows of pointed windows, round-headed crenellation and the projections of three entrances. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.141} The north-west entrance, with a trilobed muqarnas hood, faced the palace and served the sultan's officials; the north-east one faced the Bab al-Qulla and the military compound and served the army; and al-Nasir's private entrance at the southern corner of the prayer hall has a sunburst motif and ablaq masonry. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.141} Yeomans sees the mosque as a sacred intermediary between two secular domains, court and barracks, drawing its ethos from the military rather than the luxury of the court. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.143}

## The minarets
The two stone minarets are unusual: the taller, over the north-west entrance, has three cylindrical shafts, the first with vertical chevrons, the second with horizontal ones, the third tapered and ribbed under a bulbous ribbed dome, with a band of white and blue faience and green tile mosaic over the rest. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.141} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.142} The other rises from a rectangular to a cylindrical shaft and an open hexagonal kiosk. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.142} Al-Maqrizi records that craftsmen from Tabriz set up workshops in Cairo at this time, and Michael Meinecke identified a thirteenth-century Tabriz miniature with similar bulbous-domed minarets. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.142}

## Interior
The plan is traditional, with little innovation: a prayer hall four aisles deep parallel to the qibla, three arcades two aisles deep, and a dome over the mihrab covering nine bays as at Baybars's mosque but smaller, fifteen metres across; the original, of wood covered with Persian green and blue tiles, collapsed in 1531. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.142} Its most distinctive features are the ablaq voussoirs of arcades and windows and the variety of antique columns with Pharaonic, Roman and Coptic capitals. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.142} Its five-metre marble dado was taken to Istanbul by Sultan Selim after the Ottoman conquest. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.143}
