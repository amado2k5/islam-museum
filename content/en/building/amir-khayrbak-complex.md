## Overview
The Ottoman conquest did not end Mamluk architecture at once, and one of the finest buildings of the period is the mosque of the amir Khayrbak (1520-1521) on Sharia Bab al-Wazir near the Citadel; Yeomans's map gives 1502. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.151} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.233} Khayrbak, governor of Aleppo, knew the Ottoman court as a diplomat; al-Ghuri gave him the left wing at [[event/marj-dabiq-922|Marj Dabiq]], and at a decisive moment he went over to Selim, who made him governor of Egypt, an act Yeomans calls treachery; Khayrbak adopted Ottoman dress, language and culture. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.233}

## The building
He did not build in the Ottoman style: the mosque is one of the most distinctive late Mamluk buildings, its dome of interlaced arabesque equal to Qaytbay's work, its minaret on a square base with a keel-niched octagonal shaft and a brick cylinder covered in geometric stucco, its pavilion top removed. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.233} The mausoleum's irregular, sharp-angled plan strikes the eye from Bab al-Wazir, and an arch joins it to the palace of the [[building/amir-alin-aq-palace-cairo|amir Alin Aq]], which Khayrbak refurbished and lived in, an unusual link of tomb and palace. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.233}

## The prayer hall
The prayer hall is unique, departing from the qa'a plan: three cross-vaulted bays in ablaq stone with an octagonal lantern over the centre; a wooden roof may have been intended but timber shortage forced stone vaults, as the arches blocking the windows over the mihrab suggest, and the vaults may also reflect Syrian influence from Khayrbak's years at Aleppo. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.234}
