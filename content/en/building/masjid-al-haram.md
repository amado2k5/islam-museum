## Overview
The Masjid al-Haram is a mosque in [[place/mecca|Mecca]]. {cite:wikidata} The Encyclopaedia of Islam treats it inside its article on Mecca, not in an entry of its own. It says the open space that always surrounded the [[building/kaaba|Kaaba]] was enlarged over the centuries into the present mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 154}

## From open ground to mosque
The article places the Kaaba in al-Batha', the lowest part of the Meccan valley, where the first settlement lay. Houses once stood close by, but a free space always remained round it, the sacred area called the Haram, and the well of Zamzam beside the Kaaba was the best known of the city's water sources. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 154} After the [[event/conquest-of-mecca|conquest]] the Kaaba, long the direction of prayer, was cleared of idols and became a centre of Islamic worship. The Black Stone was kept as an object of reverence, and the annual [[practice/hajj|Hajj]] was kept as an Islamic ceremony. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 157}

## The Umayyad age
The article says [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] and [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]] built barrages and dykes against flooding, including dykes to protect the area round the Kaaba, and that work against the torrent went on under the [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyads]], with buildings beside the Kaaba taken down. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 157}

In 63/682 the Kaaba was partly burnt while an Umayyad army was in Mecca, probably through the carelessness of a supporter of [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Ibn al-Zubayr]]. He had it rebuilt with the Hijr included within it, and [[person/al-hajjaj-ibn-yusuf|al-Hajjaj]] reversed that change. The caliph [[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|al-Walid I]] is credited with galleries encircling the large courtyard, which, the article says, gave the mosque its distinctive form. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 157} The article also notes that the completion of Ibn al-Zubayr's building was marked on 27 Rajab by a lesser pilgrimage. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 159}

## After the Umayyads
In 318/930, the article reports, a force of Karmatians raided Mecca, killed many of its people and carried the Black Stone to Bahrayn, and in 339/950 they brought it back. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 158} Earlier, under al-Ma'mun, Alid rebels who held Mecca plundered the treasures of the Kaaba. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 158}

## Al-Maqdisi's description
Al-Maqdisi, around 375 AH, described it as built by al-Mahdi. Its marble columns were carried by sea from Alexandria to Jeddah. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim pp. 81-82}

The walls of its three colonnades are covered in mosaic made by craftsmen of Syria and Egypt, and their names are on them. It has nineteen gates, among them the Gate of Banu Shayba, the Gate of al-Safa and the Gate of the Assembly House. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim pp. 81-82}

Around the circuit are miles of brass with hanging lamps, on which candles are lit for the kings of Egypt and Yemen and the ruler of Gharjistan. In Mecca there are three pools that are filled from a channel which Zubayda built from the garden of Banu Amir. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim pp. 81-82}

## Al-Umari's description
Some four centuries later, Ibn Fadl Allah al-Umari recorded the measurements of the Ka'ba in cubits. The distance between the door and the Black Stone is four cubits, and this is the Multazam. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 pp. 137, 141-142}

On the back of the Ka'ba is a closed door called the Mustajar of Sins. Its gilded door was renewed in the Nasirid period, after its plates had been removed, and its cost was estimated at eighteen thousand dinars. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 pp. 137, 141-142}

## Learning in the mosque
After the conquest of Mecca the mosque became a school of learning. The book records that the Prophet left Mu'adh ibn Jabal to teach the people the basics of their religion there, and that Ibn Abbas sat to give fatwas and lessons, training Mujahid, Ata and Tawus. {cite:al-masjid-fi-al-islam-azhar p. 30} The report of Mu'adh staying in Mecca needs verification, since his biography mentions his being sent to the Yemen. {cite:al-masjid-fi-al-islam-azhar p. 30}

The Hajj season gave students the chance to meet scholars in the mosque. {cite:al-masjid-fi-al-islam-azhar p. 30} Drawing on the travels of Ibn Jubayr, the book describes the nights of completing the Qur'an in the last ten days of Ramadan, and the presence of four imams for the schools of law then current. {cite:al-masjid-fi-al-islam-azhar p. 31} {cite:al-masjid-fi-al-islam-azhar p. 32}

## Life in the Haram in the early twentieth century
For Meccan custom the article draws on the traveller Rutter. On the fortieth day after birth a child is taken to the Haram and placed for a moment on the threshold of the Kaaba. A dead person is carried on a bier to the pavement of the mataf before the Kaaba door, where the mourners stand and one recites the burial prayers; the bier then leaves by the Gate of Funerals for the Ma'la cemetery. Shrouds were commonly soaked in Zamzam water. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 170}

Friends met in the quadrangle, read, walked the tawaf together and fed the pigeons kept there by an endowment. Twice a year, in Rajab and Dhu al-Qa'da, the inside of the Kaaba is washed in a ceremony attended by notables and pilgrims, with water and brooms provided by al-Shaybi. The article calls the washing of oneself in the used water an un-Islamic folk practice. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 170} Before the great enlargement, houses that Philby called oratory houses ringed the mosque and rested on the roof of its colonnade, and they commanded high rents. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 171}

## Saudi repairs and equipment
After Abd al-Aziz took Mecca, the article lists a general tidying in 1344/1925 and, in 1346/1927, a repair programme at the king's expense under Abd Allah al-Dihlawi. It lasted about a year, cost 2,000 gold pounds and covered tiles, marble, domes, doors, pillars, the Zamzam building, the pointing of the stones of the Kaaba and the widening of the Gate of Ibrahim. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 174} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 175} A canvas awning on a wooden frame shaded the cloister for the pilgrimage of 1346, and a committee in 1354/1935-6 recommended rebuilding walls with cement mortar. Electric lighting begun under the Hashimite al-Husayn was extended with generators donated from India and Burma, and microphones and loudspeakers were first used in 1368/1948-9. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 175}

Al-Husayn was the first person in Islamic history to improve al-Mas'a, the running place between al-Safa and al-Marwa, when he had it roofed in 1339/1920-1. Abd al-Aziz had it paved with granite in 1345/1926-7, and Prince Faysal laid the corner stone. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 175} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 176} New drinking fountains were built near Zamzam, and in 1383/1963-4 the building over the well was torn down so that the well is now reached down a marble staircase. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 176} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 177}

## The kiswa and the Kaaba's repair
The kiswa, the covering of the Kaaba, came from Egypt for many years. After a dispute in 1340/1922 between the Sharif al-Husayn and Egypt, a kiswa made in Istanbul and held at Medina was brought in time for the dressing of the Kaaba on 10 Dhu al-Hijja. In 1345 a black kiswa brocaded with silver and gold was made in Mecca at short notice, with the Saudi ruler's name as donor for the first time, and a factory was later set up in Mecca. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 176}

On 1 Muharram 1377/29 July 1957 King Saud found the Kaaba's outer roof needing repair, its inner wooden roof rotting and its walls crumbling. Technical and religious commissions oversaw the work: a new upper roof, a concrete beam between the roofs, repaired walls and stairs, and restored marble lining. Makkan workers did it with local materials, and the roof was left undecorated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 176}

## The great enlargement
The article calls the expansion that began in the 1950s the most stupendous in the mosque's history, since pilgrims were praying in streets far outside it. A royal decree of 6 Safar 1375/24 September 1955 set up committees. The design kept the old mosque intact, surrounded it with new construction and brought al-Mas'a fully inside. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 176} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 177} Work began in November 1955 and was completed in 1398/1978. The mas'a is 394.5 m long, with a ground floor 12 m high and a lane for the handicapped, and a conduit 5 m wide carries floods round the mosque. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 177}

The new walls are faced with local marble from Wadi Fatima, Madraka and Farsan. The article reports room for about 300,000 worshippers with a clear view of the Kaaba, old gate names kept, seven new minarets 90 m high, and an estimated cost of 155 million dollars. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 177}

## Servants and administration
In late Ottoman days the wali of Mecca was nominal head of the Haram and a deputy appointed by the sultan ran it. Below him came the "opener" of the House, always from the Shayba family since pre-Islamic times, who had the right to sell pieces of the old kiswa after the Hajj. Citing Rutter, the article gives a staff of up to 800, among them imams, muezzins, sweepers, door keepers and Zamzam water drawers, and about fifty eunuchs who cared for the mataf and served as mosque police. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 177} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 178}

## For Explorers
The Sacred Mosque is the mosque of Mecca holding the Ka'ba. Al-Muqaddasi described it about 375 AH as al-Mahdi's building: marble columns carried by sea from Alexandria to Jeddah, the walls of its three porticoes clad in mosaic by craftsmen of Syria and Egypt whose names remain on it, nineteen doors including Banu Shayba, Safa and Nadwa gates, and around the tawaf miles of brass with candles burning on them. Four centuries later al-Umari recorded the Ka'ba's measures: four cubits between door and Black Stone — the Multazam — and on its back the sealed 'Refuge from sins' door; its gilded door was renewed in the Nasiri days. After the conquest it became a school of learning: the book records the Prophet leaving Mu'adh to teach and Ibn Abbas holding fatwa and lessons, training Mujahid, Ata and Tawus; Hajj season was students' chance to meet scholars, and Ramadan held the Qur'an-finishing nights with the imams of the four schools.

## For Scholars
The Sacred Mosque is the mosque of Mecca holding the Ka'ba. Al-Muqaddasi describes it c. 375 AH as al-Mahdi's building: marble columns carried by sea from Alexandria to Jeddah; the walls of its three porticoes clad in mosaic made by craftsmen of Syria and Egypt whose names survive on it; nineteen doors including the Banu Shayba, Safa and Nadwa gates; and around the tawaf miles of brass bearing candles lit for the kings of Egypt, Yemen and the lord of Ghurjistan — Mecca also holding three pools filled from the channel Zubayda edged from Banu Amir's garden. Four centuries on, Ibn Fadl Allah al-Umari recorded the Ka'ba's cubit measures: four cubits between the door and the Black Stone — the Multazam — the sealed 'Refuge from sins' door on its back, and its gilded door renewed in the Nasiri days at eighteen thousand dinars. After the conquest it became a school of learning: the book records the Prophet leaving Mu'adh to teach (a report needing verification), Ibn Abbas holding fatwa and lessons with Mujahid, Ata and Tawus graduating at his hands; the Hajj season as the meeting of students with scholars; the Ramadan nights of Qur'an completion; and the imams of the four schools, per Ibn Jubayr's journey.
