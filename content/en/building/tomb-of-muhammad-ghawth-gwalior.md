## Overview
The tomb of Shaykh [[person/muhammad-ghawth-gwaliori|Muhammad Ghawth]] at Gwalior is a regular square without chamfered angles, with two-storeyed hexagonal towers at its corners, a square projection in the middle of each side crowned by a square chhatri, and a broad sloping chhajja on massive carved brackets all round. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 218} {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 224} Its broad single dome rises on an octagonal drum above a high square platform ringed by four hexagonal chhatris — in Nath's words, like a peacock resting among his hens. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 218}

## Interior and jalis
The square hall, 13.11 metres a side, carries its dome on corner squinches; at its centre is an oblong white marble chhatri closed by finely chiselled jalis, a replica of a canopied bed, holding the shaykh's tombstone. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 218} {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 219} Round the hall runs a lofty verandah 5.87 metres wide on pillars 8.53 metres high, its almost semicircular arches filled with jalis on the north and east: about a hundred panels in some twenty designs, mostly geometric. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 219} Nath calls it the first Mughal building to use jalis on such a scale, a decade before Fatehpur Sikri and four before Akbar's tomb at Sikandra. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 219} A Persian inscription on the south-west abutment records Akbar's Deccan expedition of AH 1008/1599; there is no other inscription, not even an epitaph. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 219}

## Who built it?
The sources do not say who built the tomb: Bada'uni mentions a khanqah the shaykh built at Gwalior, and Abu al-Fadl is silent. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 220} Nath thinks it unlikely that the austere shaykh, his sons or Akbar paid for it, and argues that its builder was Tansen, the greatest musician of the age, a native of Gwalior and the shaykh's disciple, who came to Akbar's court in AH 970/1562, the year of his master's death, and was buried at his feet in Gwalior around 1590. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 221} {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 222} {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 223} He concedes that there is no direct epigraphic or documentary evidence. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 223}

## Its influence
For Nath, jalis, bracketed chhajjas and chhatris are local elements used here decisively, and Akbar followed them at Fatehpur Sikri and in his tomb at Sikandra; the dome is close to the "Pathan" domes of Delhi, while the jalis and brackets came from Gujarat by way of Rajasthan and the Man Mandir at Gwalior. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 224} {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 225} He compares it with the tomb of Shaykh Ahmad Khattu at Sarkhej near Ahmadabad (1416-51), which he thinks the shaykh saw during his years in Gujarat. {cite:nath-history-of-mughal-architecture-1-1982 p. 226}
