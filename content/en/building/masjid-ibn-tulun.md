## Overview
[[person/ahmad-ibn-tulun|Ahmad ibn Tulun]] built the mosque in al-Askar, beside the upper police headquarters (al-Shurta al-Ulya). {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.881} Yeomans calls it the largest and oldest mosque standing in Egypt, and in his judgement the finest in grandeur and simplicity; he dates it between 876 and 896 and notes that, apart from thirteenth-century restoration, it keeps the coherence of its original design. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.32}

## Site and building
He built his mosque on the hill of Yashkur, and there he carried out a great building programme. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.882} The mosque was the heart of [[place/al-qatai|al-Qata'i]], the city Ibn Tulun laid out on the model of Samarra; when the caliph's army sacked the city in 905 and destroyed its palaces, only the mosque survived. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.29} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.41}

## The divan
When al-Askar became the residence of Egypt's emirs, the divan (the government office) moved to Ibn Tulun's mosque. Under al-Aziz billah it moved again, to the house of the vizier Ya'qub ibn Killis. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.279}

## The story of the Coptic architect
Al-Balawi, a tenth-century historian, tells that the Copt who built the Aqueduct of Basatin also designed the mosque: Ibn Tulun imprisoned him over a fault in the aqueduct, then freed him when he sought a way to build a mosque without columns, since at least three hundred columns were reckoned necessary and acquiring them would mean stripping churches; the Copt proposed brick piers. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.30} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.31} Yeomans finds the story very unlikely: in his view Copts took part in the building and gave technical advice, but the design was a Samarran import carried out by many Iraqi builders, and brick piers are a feature of Samarran architecture, not a Coptic influence. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.31} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.32}

## Plan and materials
The mosque is of the hypostyle type: a square courtyard (sahn) surrounded by four arcaded halls, five aisles deep on the qibla side and two aisles deep on each of the other three sides. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.32} Each side of the sahn has thirteen pointed arches; the arcades of the prayer hall run parallel to the qibla wall in seventeen arches; the mihrab aisle is like the others, and no dome or raised aisle marks the façade. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.32} The minaret is of stone, while the rest of the mosque is red brick faced with stucco, which sets it apart from most Cairene mosques. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.32}

An outer wall surrounds the mosque and forms the ziyada, a feature of the Samarran mosques; Creswell suggested it may go back to the Roman temenos, the wall around a sacred enclosure, and that the ninth-century streets probably converged on the ziyada's doors. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.32} The ablution facilities were in the ziyada. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.32} The walls hold over 120 pointed windows with stucco grilles of many periods, of which Creswell identified only four as original. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.36}

## The fountain
An ornamental fountain in the middle of the sahn burned down in 986; Ibn Duqmaq describes a gilt dome on ten marble columns, with sixteen marble columns around it, a marble basin four cubits across beneath, and a sundial on the roof. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.34} The present fountain is the thirteenth-century work of Sultan [[person/lajin|Lajin]]: a domed cube opened by pointed arches, with an octagonal basin inside and five tiers of muqarnas carrying the dome. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.34} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.35}

## The minaret and the debate on its date
The stone minaret stands between the north-west wall of the mosque and the ziyada wall, in four storeys: a square base with an external staircase winding anti-clockwise up to a cylindrical tower, topped by two octagonal shafts and a small ribbed dome. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.32} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.33} Its roots lie in the spiral minarets of Samarra and Abu Dulaf in Iraq. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.33} Ibn Duqmaq, al-Maqrizi and al-Ya'qubi relate that Ibn Tulun twisted a piece of paper round his finger and ordered his architects to build the minaret in that shape. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.33}

Scholars disagree on the age of the present minaret. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.33}
- **Creswell**: first thought it a mixture of Ibn Tulun's work and Lajin's restoration, then, after the consistency of the stonework was revealed in 1920, held it to be entirely Lajin's. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.33}
- **Doris Behrens-Abouseif**: a total reconstruction would have carried a commemorative inscription. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.34}
- **M. Tarek Swelim**: the rectangular base with its Andalusian horseshoe windows was probably built in the Ayyubid period with the help of Maghribi builders who settled in the mosque under Salah al-Din; the cylinder kept the original shape in acknowledgement, and the octagonal shafts are thirteenth-century additions with no proof that they are Lajin's. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.34}
- **Yeomans**: asks why Lajin, had he rebuilt the whole, would build only the top shafts in the Mamluk style, and notes that the arched bridge joining the minaret to the mosque blocks a window and was no part of the original design. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.34}

## Lajin's restoration
Lajin took sanctuary in the abandoned mosque in 1296 after the assassination of Sultan al-Ashraf Khalil, and vowed to restore it if he survived to take the throne; he became sultan the following year and kept his vow. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.33} The fountain, the minaret's octagonal shafts, the minbar and repairs to the mihrab are attributed to him. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.33}

## The stucco decoration
In the arcades facing the sahn each brick pier has an engaged colonnette at each corner with vine-leaf capitals; a band of stucco scrollwork frames each arch, windows pierce the spandrels, and a string course of rosettes in octagonal frames runs beneath the cornice. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.36} Along the outer walls runs a band of crenellation that Yeomans likens to ranks of soldiers standing shoulder to shoulder. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.36}

Yeomans regards the stucco here as a milestone in Islamic art, because it survives in place, whereas very little of the Samarran stucco survives in situ. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.36} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.37} The decoration of the arch soffits is in [[concept/samarran-stucco-styles|Samarra style B]]: vine leaf, acanthus, palmettes and rosettes in new combinations filling geometric networks of interlacing circles, triangles and squares. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.38} Only fragments of woodwork in the door soffits derive from style C. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.39} The English designer Owen Jones devoted a whole page of his *Grammar of Ornament* (1856) to the mosque, seeing it as a critical stage in the evolution of Islamic ornament that peaked at the Alhambra. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.39}

## A note on the date
Yeomans places the building between 876 and 896, while the date field of this record gives 879 from Wikidata; the difference is shown here and not resolved. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.32}
