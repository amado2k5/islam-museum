## Overview
The Mosque of Bayezid II, the Beyazidiye, stands on the summit of the Third Hill by Beyazit Square in Istanbul; it was the second imperial foundation raised in the city after the mosque of the Conqueror, its külliye built between 1501 and 1506 {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.183}.

## Foundation
Besides the mosque, the külliye comprises a medrese, a primary school, a public kitchen, a hamam and several tombs. The architect's name was long given as Hayrettin or Kemalettin until Rıfkı Melül Meriç's study showed the complex is due to Yakub-şah bin Sultan-şah, who also built a caravanserai at Bursa {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 pp.183-185}. Freely reads the mosque as opening the classical period of Ottoman architecture, and — since the original Mosque of the Conqueror was destroyed by an eighteenth-century earthquake — the Beyazidiye is the earliest surviving example of what the great imperial mosques of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries would be {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.185}.

## Architecture
The courtyard is a peristyle of twenty ancient columns of porphyry, verd antique and Syenitic marble carrying an arcade roofed by twenty-four small domes, with a decorated şadırvan at its centre {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.185}. The prayer hall is a greatly simplified, smaller Hagia Sophia: a central dome about 17 metres in diameter over a central area roughly 40 metres square, continued by semidomes north and south, while its two minarets rise at the ends of the north corridor in a unique position {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.186}.

## Tombs and dependencies
Behind the mosque lies the türbe garden with the limestone-and-verd-antique tomb of Bayezid II, the simpler tomb of his daughter Selçuk Hatun, and a third in Empire style for the grand vezir Koca Reşit Pasha, who died in 1857; below the garden's east side, an arcade of shops first erected by Sinan in 1580 was reconstructed in the 1960s {cite:freely-ottoman-architecture-2011 p.187}.
