## Overview
The great mosque of al-Qayrawan stands on a site called the Great Market, in the middle of the markets, in the city of [[place/qayrawan|al-Qayrawan]]. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim pp. 180-181}

## Al-Maqdisi's description
Al-Maqdisi saw it around 375 AH and counted it larger than the mosque of Ibn Tulun. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim pp. 180-181}

Its columns are of marble, its floor is paved with marble, its courtyard is of marble, and its gutters are of lead. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim pp. 180-181}

It has doors, among them the Gate of the Market, the Gate of the Money-changers, the Gate of the Pilgrims, the Gate of the Idlers, the Gate of the Minaret, the Gate of the Dyers, the Gate of the Disciples, the Gate of the Thursday Market, the Gate of the Ablutions and the Gate of the Elite. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim pp. 180-181}

## Founding
The museum's data says that Uqba ibn Nafi founded it along with the laying out of al-Qayrawan, that the Aghlabids rebuilt it, and it dates its founding to about 670 CE. This date needs an additional source.

## Minaret and phases, in Newcomb
[[person/rexford-newcomb|Rexford Newcomb]] describes the minaret as a sturdy crenellated tower about 35 feet square and 85 feet high, topped by a smaller square stage and a cube-like cupola with a melon dome; he quotes Rivoira that it is the oldest minaret still standing, and holds that the plain square minarets of the Maghreb stand closest in idea to the first minarets. {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p.16} {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p.17} Following Rivoira he summarises its phases: founded by [[person/uqba-ibn-nafi|Uqba ibn Nafi]], rebuilt by Hassan, pulled down and rebuilt by [[person/hisham-ibn-abd-al-malik|Hisham]] at the request of Bishr, governor of Kairouan, restored again under Yazid, then razed and rebuilt by the Aghlabid [[person/ziyadat-allah-i|Ziyadat Allah I]], whose building essentially survives, with the dome over the mihrab and a front portico added by Ibrahim II. {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p.18}

## The mihrab tiles
Newcomb cites Rivoira that when the dome was built the mihrab wall was faced with metallic lustre tiles brought from Baghdad, and Migeon that a tradition resting on Arab authors credits an Aghlabid ruler with bringing these tiles, and plane-tree wood for the minbar, from Baghdad; Migeon urged that they be examined against pieces of probable Mesopotamian origin. Either way they attest [[concept/islamic-lustreware|lustre tiles]] in Tunisia by the ninth century. {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p.18} {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p.19}
