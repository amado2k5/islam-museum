## Overview
Omar Khalidi takes the Islamic Cultural Center of Manhattan as an example of "adapted design", a reinterpretation of tradition in American architectural terms. {cite:khalidi-donnellon-mosques-usa-canada-catalogue pp. 4-5} Designed by Skidmore, Owings & Merrill, it was completed in 1991 at Third Avenue and 96th Street for the Muslims of metropolitan New York, among them diplomats and staff of the United Nations and consulates. {cite:khalidi-donnellon-mosques-usa-canada-catalogue pp. 4-5}

## The debate over its image
During the design the centre's board appointed two advisory committees, one of "prominent members" of New York's Muslim community and one of architects, mostly non-Muslims; the architects wanted "a mosque that belonged to the 21st century", the Muslims a traditional mosque with literal versions of historic motifs. {cite:khalidi-donnellon-mosques-usa-canada-catalogue pp. 4-5} They settled on a "modernist" building, the Muslim committee insisting on both a minaret and a dome, which Khalidi reads as the pull of the "old and familiar" for many Muslims. {cite:khalidi-donnellon-mosques-usa-canada-catalogue pp. 4-5} {cite:khalidi-donnellon-mosques-usa-canada-catalogue pp. 6-7}

## Architecture
The building expresses traditional Ottoman forms, Islamic geometric figures and calligraphy in a contemporary architectural language, the slender, abstract minaret contrasting with the mass of the mosque, and it has become a Manhattan landmark since it opened. {cite:khalidi-donnellon-mosques-usa-canada-catalogue pp. 22-23}
