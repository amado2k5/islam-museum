## Overview
The Dome of the Rock is an octagonal building crowned by a dome, standing in the courtyard of the [[building/al-aqsa-mosque|al-Aqsa Mosque]] in [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]] {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p. 143} {cite:wikidata}. Wikidata dates its construction to 691 CE {cite:wikidata}. Muhammad Qabbani writes that [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan]] ordered, in 72 AH, a mosque to be built over the Rock from which, he says, the Prophet was taken up on the night of the [[event/isra-and-miraj|Isra and Mi'raj]], and he counts it among the best-known mosques the Umayyads raised {cite:umayyad-birth-to-fall-2006 p.163} {cite:umayyad-birth-to-fall-2006 p.161}. The Thesaurus d'Epigraphie Islamique records for it a building inscription dated 72 AH {cite:tei-epigraphie 9}.

[[person/al-muqaddasi|Al-Maqdisi]] described it in [[work/ahsan-al-taqasim|Ahsan al-Taqasim]], a book he completed in 375 AH, as an octagonal building with four gilded doors, each facing a flight of steps {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p. 12} {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p. 143}. Inside are three ambulatories circling on columns that he calls finer than marble, and above them a dome sheathed in gilded brass that rises a hundred cubits into the air {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p. 144}. He says that when the sun rises on it the dome shines and its drum glitters, and that he had seen nothing like it in Islam {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p. 144}. Later [[person/ibn-fadl-allah-al-umari|Ibn Fadl Allah al-Umari]], in [[work/masalik-al-absar|Masalik al-absar]], described the al-Aqsa Mosque and the Rock as the building stood in the year 743 AH, drawing on a short book by Taj al-Din Ahmad ibn Amin al-Mulk that set down the measurements in cubits {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.177} {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.178}. There the octagonal building rises eighteen cubits above the paved court, and its northern door is called the Gate of Paradise {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.178} {cite:umari-masalik-1924 pp. 181-186}.

## Al-Maqdisi's description
Al-Maqdisi described it when he saw it around 375 AH. It is an octagonal building with four doors, each facing a flight of steps: the southern, Israfil, the Sur and the women's. Inside are three ambulatories circling on columns. Al-Maqdisi considers it finer than marble and without equal. Above its area is a dome covered in gilded brass, a hundred cubits high including its base. He says that when the sun rises on it, it shines and the whole area glitters, and that he has seen nothing like it in the East. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p. 144}

## Al-Umari's measurements
In the Mamluk period Ibn Fadl Allah al-Umari measured it door by door. The eastern door is doubled, with an arch between its two leaves set with a gilded stone; the northern is called the Gate of Paradise, and the western is like them. Both leaves are plated with carved yellow brass. He gave the dome, the ambulatories and the courtyard in cubits. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 pp. 181-186}

## Its description in 743 AH according to al-Umari
In [[work/masalik-al-absar|Masalik al-absar]], [[person/ibn-fadl-allah-al-umari|Ibn Fadl Allah al-Umari]] describes al-Aqsa and the Rock "as its building stood in the year 743", drawing on a short book by al-Sahib Taj al-Din Abu al-Fada'il Ahmad ibn Amin al-Mulk called *Silsilat al-asjad fi sifat al-sakhra wal-masjid*, relying on its measurements in cubits. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.177} {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.178}

There the octagonal building around the Rock rises eighteen cubits above the paved court; above it the drum of the dome is ten and a quarter cubits high and a hundred and three and two-thirds cubits round, with sixteen gilded glass windows screened by grilles outside; each side of the octagon is twenty-nine and two-thirds cubits, faced for seven cubits with veined white marble and for seven more up to the waterspouts with gilded mosaic; each side has seven window bays, and the building has four doors. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.178} He describes the piers and columns of the first octagon and the colours of their marble: eight piers and sixteen columns. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.179} The book's editor Ahmad Zaki Pasha says he went to Palestine to compare these figures with the building. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.10}
