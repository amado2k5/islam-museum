## Overview
The Dome of the Rock stands in the courtyard of the al-Aqsa Mosque in [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]]. Wikidata dates its construction to 691 CE, from a single source that needs a second. {cite:wikidata}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls it the oldest surviving monument of Islamic architecture and probably the first conscious work of art of Islamic civilisation. It is sometimes called the Mosque of Umar, although the article's author treats the stories linking the caliph [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] to the site as legends. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 336} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362} The same encyclopaedia says that the history of Islamic Jerusalem was solemnly opened by the erection of this building, and the article reports that the purpose of the building is still debated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 360} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 337}

## Site and layout
The Dome stands on an artificial platform roughly, but not exactly, in the middle of the [[building/al-haram-al-sharif|Haram al-Sharif]]. The article suggests that the shape and position of the platform were probably set by the ruined state of the old Jewish Temple area and by whatever Roman construction survived, and adds that pious or legendary associations with parts of the area are possible but hard to demonstrate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 336} The article on al-Kuds notes that the platform lies off-centre to the rest of the former Temple area and was reached by stairs, some of which must already have existed under [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 379}

The al-Kuds article describes how, soon after the conquest, the Muslims took over the empty Herodian Temple area. It lists the reasons that may have counted: it was a large vacant space in a city where the treaty barred taking Christian buildings, the early Muslims were influenced by Jewish converts who were thought to know its sacred meaning, they may have wished to show opposition to the Christian view that it should stay empty, and they may have had a spiritual attachment to Jerusalem already, which the author calls hard to demonstrate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 378}

## The building
Following K. A. C. Creswell's reconstruction, the article describes a dome 20.44 m across and about 30 m high, set within two octagonal ambulatories, each side of the outer one being around 20.50 m. Piers and columns carry the interior, the dome stands on a high drum, and the drum and the octagons hold fifty-six windows, with a parapet outside and simple porches. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 336} The al-Kuds article gives slightly different figures, a cylinder 30.30 m high and 20.30 m across, set over a large rocky outcrop with an underground chamber, and four axial gates with porches that have often been rebuilt. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 378}

The article stresses two features. One is the geometry of the plan, based on two squares inscribed in the circle of the rotunda, which gives the building its airy harmony and lets it be taken in at a glance. The other is that its plan and composition belong to a high Byzantine tradition seen at Ravenna, in Syria at St Simeon and Bosra, and in Jerusalem at the Holy Sepulchre and the Church of the Ascension. The author calls it almost certain that the builders and planners were Christians from Syria or Palestine. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 336} The al-Kuds article places its conception and interior arrangement in the martyrium tradition of Jerusalem's churches. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 378}

## Decoration and inscription
The Dome was richly decorated with marble, mosaics and gilt bronze plaques. The cupola was gilded outside and the outer walls were largely covered in mosaic. Most of the exterior decoration has gone, but the interior still resembles its first glittering sheath of mosaic, with vegetal and geometric designs and the unusual motif of royal crowns and jewels. The article says scholars debate whether the mosaicists were Syrian or Byzantine, while the style comes mostly from Late Antique and early Byzantine art with some Iranian elements. It points to the absence of figural representation as a sign of a conscious new Muslim taste in the decoration. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 336}

A long inscription in gold mosaic cubes runs along both sides of the octagonal arcades. It gives the date of the building as 72/691-2, and it contains many Qur'anic passages, from statements of the faith of Islam to the main Christological passages. The article calls these the earliest dated fragments of the Qur'an and reports C. Kessler's finding that many letters already carried clear diacritical marks. It also notes that [[person/abd-allah-al-mamun|al-Ma'mun]] replaced Abd al-Malik's name in the inscription with his own. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 336} The al-Kuds article, by contrast, says the building was completed in 71/691. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 378}

## Changes and repairs
The article says the building has been remarkably well preserved, though the exact chronology of its alterations is hard to fix. The cupola was redone in [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]] times and under the [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottomans]], the roof of the octagons was repaired in Abbasid times, and their ceilings were transformed under the [[polity/mamluk-sultanate|Mamluks]]. The Crusaders added an iron screen around the Rock, since removed, and in the tenth/sixteenth century the Ottomans covered the outer walls with Turkish ceramics. Between 1956 and 1964 a Restoration Committee of Egyptian and Jordanian architects and authorities restored the monument, guided by the aim of returning every part to its earliest documented shape; the article judges the work one of the most successful restorations known. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 336}

The al-Kuds article adds that the dome of the Sakhra collapsed in the earthquake of 407/1016, which a Geniza letter dates to 25 July, and that the mother of the caliph al-Muqtadir paid for repairs to its cupola. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 365} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 366} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 379} It records that when Atsiz retook Jerusalem in 463/1071, those who had taken refuge in the Dome of the Rock were spared. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 366} It attributes the tile covering of the exterior to the Ottoman period and to [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Sulayman the Magnificent]], and mentions Jordanian restoration of the golden dome and the ceramic inscriptions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 382} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 375}

## Significance
The article says the Dome has drawn more scholarly attention than any other Islamic monument. It was rarely copied for its shape, perhaps by a few later mausoleums such as the Sulaybiyya at Samarra or Qalawun's tomb in Cairo, and never for its function. It fits no architectural series. It stands on the site of the Jewish Temple, does not look like a mosque, and was not needed as one, since the nearby [[building/al-aqsa-mosque|al-Aqsa]] met the congregation's needs; the scan loses part of the article's remark on how the building relates to the two older faiths. The article adds that the literary sources are confused and contradictory, although the inscription shows a major Umayyad effort. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 336} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 337}

## Abd al-Malik and the building
Abd al-Malik, the fifth caliph of the [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad]] line, reigned 65-86/685-705. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 95} The Encyclopaedia names him as the builder of the Dome. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 96} His reign began in turmoil: an Umayyad force under [[person/al-hajjaj-ibn-yusuf|al-Hajjaj]] besieged [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Ibn al-Zubayr]] at [[place/mecca|Mecca]] from 72/692, and Ibn al-Zubayr was killed in 73/692. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 95} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 96} The al-Kuds article also describes the Umayyad contribution to Jerusalem: under [[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|al-Walid]], who reigned 86-96/705-15, the first clearly documented al-Aqsa mosque was built on the same axis as the Dome, forming a planned ensemble. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 379}

## Debated purposes
The article on the Dome says the many explanations of its purpose fall into three groups, and the al-Kuds article names the same three. The museum reports each as the sources give it and gives no verdict.

The first is the traditional pious Muslim view, which takes the Rock as the place from which the Prophet made his Night Journey and ascension, the [[event/isra-and-miraj|isra and miraj]]. The article says that this became the building's holy meaning in the end, since the whole Haram came to be understood as the farthest mosque of sura 17, verse 1. It adds that although the link may have been made early, nothing of it appears in the building's own long inscription. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 337} The al-Kuds article judges that this explanation matches later Muslim piety but that there is much doubt it existed in Abd al-Malik's time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 379}

The second is historical and goes back to a passage in al-Ya'qubi. It holds that, during the struggle with Ibn al-Zubayr, Abd al-Malik tried to create in Jerusalem a shrine to rival the [[building/kaaba|Kaaba]] and the pilgrimage to Mecca. The article says W. Caskel revived the theory, arguing that the Umayyads as a dynasty did seek a cult centre in Syria or Palestine and that Jerusalem alone fitted. The article gives as the main objection the doubt that so basic a requirement as the pilgrimage to Mecca could be altered, and continues with a remark, partly lost in the scan, about a violently anti-Umayyad polemicist whose interpretations it calls open to criticism. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 337} The al-Kuds article traces the theory to Goldziher, who held that the Dome was meant to divert pilgrims from Mecca, then the capital of Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr, and that the traditions for and against the sanctity of Jerusalem reflect that contest. Its author says the thesis cannot be maintained: no major historian of the third/ninth century, nor any early geographer including al-Muqaddasi, hints at such an intention, and Tabari and others report that Abd al-Malik's soldiers took part in the pilgrimage of 68/687-8. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 363} The same article notes a report of a ritual standing like that at [[place/arafat|Arafat]], and reads it as a parallel found in other provincial cities that shared in the pilgrimage rather than replaced it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 363} The al-Kuds article's section on monuments says that too many arguments exist against this second explanation for it to be maintained. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 379}

The third is cultural-historical. Starting from the inscriptions and mosaics, it sees a monument proclaiming the new faith and empire in the city of the two older religions. It sanctified a Jewish sanctuary anew and slowly took in memories of Abraham and Joseph, among others, set the crowns of Byzantine and Persian kings around the centre like an offering and raised its own Christology above them. On this view it was a missionary monument of victory, built when Abd al-Malik was concerned with Christian hostility and wished to assert Islamic uniqueness within a shared tradition, as his coinage also did. As the need to hold the community together faded, pious associations grew and made it the unique sanctuary of today. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 337} The article's author says this explanation seems to him to fit the sources and the spirit of the time better than the other two, while stating that the debate is not closed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 337} The al-Kuds article likewise says it agrees with the political and psychological setting, with the royal symbols strung like trophies around the centre, and with the inscriptions that contain the whole Christology of the Qur'an. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 379}

The author of the al-Kuds history offers a further view of his own. The author thinks the plan was probably drawn up under [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya]], whose long rule allowed the immense preparations, and who is known to have built extensively at Mecca; doubts that the turbulent early years of Abd al-Malik could have both conceived and finished it. The author holds that the real urge was religious, together with the Arabs' natural adjustment to a setting of grand buildings, and that [[person/raja-ibn-haywa|Raja ibn Haywa]] of Baysan, a pious traditionist in charge of the building operations, probably on the financial side only, and men like him may have been its spiritual originators. By choosing the site, the author says, Islam showed itself as the exclusive heir of the older religions; the jewel-like mosaics answer prophetic descriptions of the future Jerusalem, and the inscriptions show a spirit of mission towards Christians, since Jesus' prophethood is stressed and his sonship denied. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 363}

The Dome article ends by saying that whatever its first purpose, the building gathered ever more complex associations over time, so that it may be a rare sanctuary whose holy meaning grew after it was built. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 337}

## The early Muslim use of the Temple area
In the al-Kuds article's account, stories of Umar's visit to Jerusalem are legends. In one, Ka'b al-Ahbar urges Umar to pray behind the Rock so that two qiblas would be before him, and Umar refuses because Muslims should face the Kaaba alone. The author reads this as one of many traditions against over-valuing Jerusalem. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362} The author thinks it likely, from the terms of the surrender, that the largely empty Temple area served as a Muslim place of prayer from the beginning, on Umar's order, and that a Muslim place of prayer there, described about 680 by the pilgrim Arculfus, took shape under Mu'awiya. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 362} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 363} The article also mentions the name al-Kuds as the most common Arabic name of Jerusalem. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 360}

## Al-Maqdisi's description
Al-Maqdisi described it when he saw it around 375 AH. It is an octagonal building with four doors, each facing a flight of steps: the southern, Israfil, the Sur and the women's. Inside are three ambulatories circling on columns. Al-Maqdisi considers it finer than marble and without equal. Above its area is a dome covered in gilded brass, a hundred cubits high including its base. He says that when the sun rises on it, it shines and the whole area glitters, and that he has seen nothing like it in the East. {cite:ahsan-al-taqasim p. 144}

## Al-Umari's measurements
In the Mamluk period Ibn Fadl Allah al-Umari measured it door by door. The eastern door is doubled, with an arch between its two leaves set with a gilded stone; the northern is called the Gate of Paradise, and the western is like them. Both leaves are plated with carved yellow brass. He gave the dome, the ambulatories and the courtyard in cubits. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 pp. 181-186}

## Its description in 743 AH according to al-Umari
In [[work/masalik-al-absar|Masalik al-absar]], [[person/ibn-fadl-allah-al-umari|Ibn Fadl Allah al-Umari]] describes al-Aqsa and the Rock "as its building stood in the year 743", drawing on a short book by al-Sahib Taj al-Din Abu al-Fada'il Ahmad ibn Amin al-Mulk called *Silsilat al-asjad fi sifat al-sakhra wal-masjid*, relying on its measurements in cubits. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.177} {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.178}

There the octagonal building around the Rock rises eighteen cubits above the paved court; above it the drum of the dome is ten and a quarter cubits high and a hundred and three and two-thirds cubits round, with sixteen gilded glass windows screened by grilles outside; each side of the octagon is twenty-nine and two-thirds cubits, faced for seven cubits with veined white marble and for seven more up to the waterspouts with gilded mosaic; each side has seven window bays, and the building has four doors. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.178} He describes the piers and columns of the first octagon and the colours of their marble: eight piers and sixteen columns. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.179} The book's editor Ahmad Zaki Pasha says he went to Palestine to compare these figures with the building. {cite:umari-masalik-1924 p.10}
