## Overview
The Encyclopaedia of Islam describes the Sajids as a line of military commanders who governed the northwestern provinces of the caliphate, Adharbaydjan, Arran and Armenia, in the later 3rd/9th and early 4th/10th centuries on behalf of the Abbasids. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 798} The family seems to have come from Ushrusana on the middle Syr Darya in Transoxania and was probably of Sogdian stock, though by the time it came to prominence it had become largely Arabicised in culture. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 798}

## Abu l-Saj and his sons
Abu l-Saj Dewdad b. Yusuf Dewdasht fought against the rebels Babak and Mazyar, served as governor of Aleppo and Qinnasrin and as police commander in Baghdad, and in 262/876 joined the Saffarid Ya'qub b. al-Layth, so that his estates in Iraq were confiscated; he died in the service of Amr b. al-Layth in 266/879. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 pp. 798-801} His son Muhammad was appointed governor of Adharbaydjan in 276/889-90, the province that became the family's power base; he took Maragha in 280/893, fought the Armenian Bagratid ruler Smbat I, and appears to have assumed the title Afshin, which appears on a dirham of his struck at Bardha'a in 285/898, but he made peace again with al-Mu'tadid and died of plague at Bardha'a in Rabi I 288/March 901. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 pp. 798-801}

## Yusuf and the end of the line
Muhammad's brother Yusuf moved the capital to Ardabil, had his governorship of Adharbaydjan, Arran and Armenia confirmed under al-Muqtadir, and campaigned in Armenia, capturing Smbat and executing him in 301/914. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 pp. 798-801} He later conquered Zanjan, Abhar, Qazvin and Rayy, was defeated and imprisoned by the caliph's army under Mu'nis al-Muzaffar in 307/919, was released in 310/922 and reappointed, and was killed near Kufa in 315/928 by the Qarmatis. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 pp. 798-801} His nephew Abu l-Musafir Fath was made governor but was poisoned at Ardabil in Sha'ban 317/September 929 by one of his slaves, and the short line of Sajid governors ended; the Sajiyya regiment in Baghdad was named after Abu l-Saj. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 801}

## The article's assessment
The article stresses that the Sajids did not form an independent line of rulers, any more than the Tahirids before them, and are not to be equated with an openly anti-Abbasid power such as the Saffarids; the dinars and dirhams they minted in Adharbaydjan, Arran, Armenia and briefly at Rayy all acknowledge the Abbasid caliph as suzerain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 801} Their governorship was important for the extension of Arab control over the Armenian kingdoms, and after it ended those regions came under native Iranian rather than Arab control. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 801}
