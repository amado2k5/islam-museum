## Origins and the Saljuq period
Artuk, son of Eksab, belonged to the Turkoman tribe of the Doger. In 1073 he was in Asia Minor, fighting for and against the Byzantine emperor Michael VII, but he later appears mainly as an officer of the Great Saljuq Malikshah. In 1077 he brought the Qarmatians of Bahrayn under Malikshah's rule; in 1079 Malikshah placed him under his brother Tutush in the Syrian campaign, and in 1084 under Ibn Dahir in the Diyar Bakr campaign; in 1085 he was sent to Khurasan against the sultan's brother Tukush. He received the iqta of Halwan, a strategic point in southern Kurdistan. From 1085 he intrigued in Diyar Bakr with the Arab prince Muslim, lord of Mosul and Aleppo, who had quarrelled with Malikshah. Muslim's death forced him back into Tutush's service, and Tutush gave him Palestine in 1086. The date of his death is unknown; he left several sons. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 663-664}

After Malikshah's death the Artukids, with Tutush, helped him dispute the throne with his nephews in Diyar Bakr (1092-1095). When Tutush died they supported his son Ridwan of Aleppo against another son, Dukak of Damascus. They lost Palestine, which Egypt reconquered in 1098 and the Crusaders then occupied, so that they never returned there. One of the two Artukid leaders, Ilghazi, entered for a time the service of Muhammad, a son of Malikshah, whom he had supported against his brother Barkyaruk, and who made him governor of Iraq, but the family's real strength remained among the Turkomans of Diyar Bakr. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 663-664}

## Mardin and Hisn Kayfa
In 1097 a nephew of Sukman seized Mardin. Sukman himself, who had taken Sarudj, was expelled from there by the Crusaders in 1097, but after quarrels among the chiefs of the Jazira he won Hisn Kayfa in 1102, controlled many districts further north, and then inherited Mardin. He fought the Franks, and in 1104 before Harran he captured Count Baldwin of Edessa; he died soon afterwards. After the death of Barkyaruk, the sultan sent Ilghazi back to Diyar Bakr. In 1107 he helped defeat Kilij Arslan of Rum, who had been summoned by Muhammad's enemies, and in 1108 he took Mardin from one of Sukman's sons; another son, Dawud, kept Hisn Kayfa. Other chiefs at Amid, Akhlat and Arzan carved out lordships for themselves, and Muhammad tried to unite them for a holy war against the Franks, without success. From 1110 relations between Ilghazi and Muhammad grew strained, and Ilghazi increasingly avoided the expeditions against the Franks, since only the Saljuq authority would profit from them. In 1114 he formed a Turkoman coalition against the governor of Mosul, Aqsunqur al-Bursuqi, and won, but feared Muhammad's reprisal and fled; he came to terms with Tughtakin of Damascus, and escaped (1115). In 1118, when Muhammad died, Ilghazi seized Mayyafarikin, the last Saljuq post in Diyar Bakr. He was now a power to be reckoned with. Aleppo, threatened by the Franks and torn by anarchy, appealed to him, though its leading men disliked surrendering power to him, and in agreement with Tughtakin he answered the appeal in 1118. In 1119 his Turkomans crushed the Franks of Antioch. Their base remained in Diyar Bakr, and Ilghazi was inclined to make peace; he then fought the Georgians and was defeated. His prestige remained unharmed at his death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 663-664}

## Balak and the Khartpert line
From 1113 onward Ilghazi's nephew Balak had been building a stable principality north-east of Diyar Bakr, astride the eastern Euphrates, whose chief town from about 1115 was Khartpert. As tutor of the minor Saljuq of Malatya he gained fame by an alliance with the Danishmendid Gumushtegin, Ibn Mangudjak of Erzincan and the Byzantine governor of Trebizond, Gavras (1120). While in Ilghazi's service he captured Joscelin of Edessa in 1122, and after Ilghazi's death he defeated Baldwin of Jerusalem, who had come to protect the Franco-Armenians of the Euphrates border (1123). He then took Aleppo from another nephew of Ilghazi, but was killed besieging Manbij in 1124, and Aleppo passed out of Artukid hands. In Diyar Bakr, where they stayed firmly entrenched, Shams al-Dawla Sulayman, son of Ilghazi and ruler at Mayyafarikin, died at the end of 524 AH (1129-1130). His brother Timurtash, already lord of Mardin, succeeded him. Balak's principality had passed to Dawud, lord of Hisn Kayfa since 1104. From then on the two branches existed separately for two centuries. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 663-664}

## Zengi and Nur al-Din
The period of expansion was over. From 1127 Zengi ruled at Mosul, and from 1128 at Aleppo too, where he built a strong kingdom. Timurtash acted as Zengi's vassal, but took hostile action against Dawud and then against Dawud's son Arslan; he absorbed the small lordships bordering his own, especially east of Hisn Kayfa, and on an anti-Georgian expedition he took Amid. Zengi pressed him hard, conquering Buhtan east of Diyar Bakr and, on the accession of Kara Arslan, the districts between Hisn Kayfa and Khartpert. Kara Arslan had to draw closer to the Franco-Armenians of Edessa, against whom he had sometimes fought; Zengi's capture of Edessa in 1144 was a disaster for him too, but he was saved by his enemy's death in 1146. Timurtash and Kara Arslan divided Diyar Bakr between them not without difficulty. Zengi's dominions were divided between Nur al-Din at Aleppo and a line of princes at Mosul, his brothers and nephews, whom Nur al-Din increasingly brought under his tutelage. Nur al-Din did not contest Diyar Bakr with the Artukids and let them take their share of the spoils of the count of Edessa north of the Euphrates, but he drew them into his holy wars against the Franks and Byzantines. Relations with them were excellent, especially with Kara Arslan. Alpi, son and successor of Timurtash, sought to secure his position by the protection of the Shah-i Arman of Akhlat, whom in return he had to help against the Georgians. In 1163 Kara Arslan tried to take Amid from the Inalids and Nisanids but was prevented by a Danishmendid attack; his son Muhammad, with Nur al-Din, went to the aid of the Danishmendids, who were threatened by the expansionist policy of the Saljuqs of Konya. The growing power of Nur al-Din had without notice made the Artukids his vassals; when he died (the year is garbled in the scan) the Artukids had become in effect his vassals. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 663-664}
