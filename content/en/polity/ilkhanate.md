## Overview
The Ilkhanate was the Mongol dynasty that ruled Persia in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, from the middle of the seventh/thirteenth century until the death of its last effective ruler Abu Said in 736/1335. The museum's data records it as a state founded in 1256 and ended in 1335, and as the successor of [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|the Abbasid caliphate]]. {cite:wikidata} The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose article this page follows, describes it as essentially a Persian state that also took in Mesopotamia, and so rather like the Sasanian empire in its shape. It adds that historians customarily call the whole Mongol period in Persia, down to 756/1355, the Ilkhanid period. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1154}

## The Mongol advance under Hulegu
The first Mongol advance towards the Middle East, in 1218 to 1221, had touched only the north of the Iranian lands, and only [[place/khurasan|Khurasan]] had come under any real Mongol control. When the Great Khan Mongke, who reigned from 1251 to 1259, divided the territories, the task of extending control over Persia, Mesopotamia and, if possible, Syria and Egypt fell to his brother [[person/hulagu|Hulegu]]. According to Barthold, about 129,000 men were levied for the purpose from the armies of the empire. Hulegu took the fortress of [[place/alamut|Alamut]] but for the time being left the eastern and southern Iranian principalities, such as Herat and Fars, alone in order to press on to Baghdad after talks with the caliph broke down. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1154}

[[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] fell on 10 February 1258. Hulegu then held southern Mesopotamia, the north of the country fell to him the following year, and only [[place/mayyafariqin|Mayyafariqin]] went on resisting. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1154} The museum keeps a separate page on [[event/sack-of-baghdad-656|the sack of Baghdad]], drawn from other sources.

## Ayn Jalut and the frontiers
Mongke died in 1259 and Hulegu left the army, which was to carry on through Syria against the Mamluks. Damascus and other towns were taken, but at Ayn Jalut on 3 September 1260 [[person/qutuz|Qutuz]], with a Mamluk army trained for cavalry fighting, forced the Mongols to halt. The new sultan [[person/baybars|Baybars]], who took the sultanate immediately after the victory, proved an opponent equal to the challenge. The article says this encounter gave the Mongol empire of Persia its final boundaries. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1154} See also [[event/ayn-jalut-658|the battle of Ayn Jalut]].

Syria and Palestine stayed Mamluk, with the western edge of the Euphrates valley as the frontier. To the north the Ilkhans counted as dependent states Lesser Armenia and Seljuk Asia Minor, which threw off the overlordship of the [[polity/golden-horde|Golden Horde]], together with the Caucasus, which had been loosely subject to the Horde. Later attacks from the north did not loosen the Ilkhan hold on the Caucasus, even when the Georgians rose repeatedly. The Oxus formed the frontier with the territories of Chaghatay in Central Asia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1154}

Several areas stayed largely independent. The [[polity/kartid-dynasty|Kart dynasty]] of Herat remained more or less free of Mongol power, Makran was for a time a frontier zone of uncertain ownership, and the island state of Hormuz and the small principalities of Luristan, Gilan and Mazandaran kept to themselves. Fars passed to the Ilkhans only through the marriage of a Mongol prince. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1154}

## The title Ilkhan
The rulers of Persia bore the title to show that they depended on the Great Khan in Peking, and this lasted until Kublai died in 1294. The final adoption of Islam by the Persian rulers in 1295 cut the close tie; from then on the Great Khan's name vanished from the Persian coinage and the title Khan replaced Ilkhan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1154}

## Religion and diplomacy among the early Ilkhans
The article reports that some of the Mongols who invaded Persia were Nestorian Christians but most were Shamanists, and that a leaning towards Buddhism appeared in the royal house and ruling class even under Hulegu, possibly through contact with China. It judges that this strengthened a hostile attitude towards Sunni Islam, an attitude it explains partly by reasons of state during the decades of rivalry with Egypt, while Islam linked Egypt and the Golden Horde, who formed a lasting coalition against the Ilkhans. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1154} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1155}

The same hostility, the article says, brought tolerance of the [[group/shia|Shia]], achieved after the capture of Baghdad under the lead of the mathematician and astronomer [[person/nasir-al-din-al-tusi|Nasir al-Din al-Tusi]], and it favoured their social position and entry into the administration. Christians, especially Nestorians, were also favoured, among them Hulegu's favourite wife Doquz Khatun, who died in 1265 and also helped the Jacobites and won Syrian Christians to the Ilkhan side. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1155}

This policy opened diplomatic relations with the Christian West, begun before Hulegu and especially active under [[person/abaga-ibn-hulagu|Abaqa]], who reigned from 663/1265 to 680/1282 and was himself a Buddhist. Links grew with the Papal See, with the France of Louis IX and with some Crusader states, all opponents of the Mamluks. A joint campaign against Egypt proposed in 1269 failed because the two sides could not agree on a date, and a simultaneous blow planned by the rival coalition in the Caucasus and on the Oxus in 1268 to 1269 also failed. The article calls Abaqa the true organiser of the state his father founded. He built many Buddhist temples while staying tolerant towards Christians, and in 1281 the Nestorians chose a Christian Uyghur as Catholicos. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1155}

## From Ahmad to Baydu
Abaqa died of a fever in 1282 and a time of confusion followed. His brother at once embraced Islam and took the name Ahmad, which eased tension with Egypt, but his nephew [[person/arghun-khan|Arghun]], Abaqa's son, overthrew him in 1284. The article describes Arghun as zealous for Buddhism but without a sense of his state's finances; he left the Jewish wazir Sa'd al-Dawla a free hand, and the wazir's heavy money demands stirred repeated unrest and brought him to a violent end right after Arghun died in 1291. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1155}

Arghun's brother Gaykhatu faced a financial crisis and in 1294 tried to introduce paper money on the Chinese model. The innovation caused an immediate and widespread breakdown of trade; he withdrew the measure after a few months but was overthrown in March 1295. His successor Baydu, from another branch of the family, could not keep his position. The article lists the consequences of the collapse of the financial system: more brigandage, disruption of the Mongol postal system, ruin of agriculture in many places, inflation and a trade crisis. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1155}

## Ghazan and the turn to Islam
[[person/ghazan-khan|Ghazan]], Arghun's son, succeeded on 9 November 1295 at the age of 24 and made peace and order his first task. Guided by the wazir [[person/rashid-al-din-hamadani|Rashid al-Din]] Fadl Allah and by Ali Shah, he introduced reforms in administration, agriculture, trade and public welfare, though many never fully bore fruit in the short time left. The article stresses that he also embraced Sunni Islam, which most leading men had already adopted. This did not wipe out Mongol customs such as the respected public place of women, but it sealed the fusion of Mongols and Turks in Persia and shaped settlement on the Iranian plateau, notably in Azerbaijan, where [[place/tabriz|Tabriz]], [[place/maragha|Maragha]] and, from 1307, Sultaniyya near Qazvin were the capitals. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1155}

Ghazan was careful to be conciliatory to the Shia, visiting their shrines and funding their cause. He was therefore strong enough to reject the Golden Horde's demand that he leave the Caucasus, and he made an unsuccessful attempt to conquer Syria. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1155}

## Oljeitu, Abu Said and the end
When Ghazan died in 703/1304 the article judges that the state had passed its peak. His brother Oljeitu did not continue the reforms but governed and fought capably. His turn to Shiism in 1310 brought, in the article's words, great affliction, since he applied severe measures against the Sunnis, who were still the majority; Christians suffered more under him than under Ghazan. Civil war threatened when he died in 716/1316. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1155}

His son [[person/abu-said-ilkhan|Abu Said]], the first ruler with a purely Islamic name, returned to Sunni Islam. Being young, he was surrounded by factions. The vizier Rashid al-Din, also important as a historian, was executed in 1318. The general Choban then rose, working with the vizier Ali Shah until Ali Shah died in 724/1324, and fell to a plot three years later. After that Choban's son Hasan Kuchuk and his former son-in-law Hasan Buzurg fought each other almost continually. Abu Said played little part and died on a campaign in the Caucasus in 736/1335. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1155}

## After the Ilkhans
The dynasty practically ended with Abu Said, though until 756/1355 various princes of the house, and a princess in 1339 to 1340, were set up and deposed as khans. Real power lay with the two Hasans. The younger was murdered in 1344; the elder withdrew to Baghdad and founded the Jalayirids, whose rule stayed limited to Mesopotamia. Asia Minor, Georgia, Little Armenia and the Kurtids had broken away, the [[polity/muzaffarid-dynasty|Muzaffarids]] took Fars and the Sarbadarids took Mazandaran and further east, and in 1357 to 1358 the Golden Horde briefly occupied Azerbaijan. The article says only the campaigns of [[person/timur-lang|Timur]] ended the collapse, and then only briefly. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1156}

The article judges that under the Ilkhans Persia was for the first time in centuries brought together as a territorial and political unit, though through tolerating independent minor states, which makes the period highly significant for the country. It notes the notable growth of the arts and some branches of science, namely astronomy, medicine and historiography. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1156}

## Architecture
The article's art section says Ilkhanid art is the art of Mongol Iran, with the strongest Far Eastern influence in the country, seen mainly in textiles, ceramics and miniature painting; it brought in motifs of Chinese origin such as the lotus, the phoenix and square Kufic writing. In many respects it continues and refines Seljuk art. A building boom followed nearly five decades of inactivity, which the article attributes to the Islamization, Iranization and urbanization of the dynasty under Ghazan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1156} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1157}

Religious building meant mosques, madrasas, mausolea, shrines and khanqahs. New tendencies stressed height, through taller and often egg-shaped domes and narrower iwans, and used more colour, with faience mosaic covering whole walls after about 710/1310. Mausolea were numerous, 39 of the 119 monuments Wilber listed being imamzadas. The mausoleum of Oljeitu at Sultaniyya, built in 705-13/1305-13, is described by the article as an enormous octagon about 39 metres wide with a single-shelled brick dome of 24.5 metres span; it quotes Godard's verdict that it is the finest known example of Mongol architecture. The article adds that Oljeitu, a Shia after 709/1309, meant to move the remains of Ali and Husayn there, but this was not carried out. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1157} Further examples include the mosque of [[building/jami-varamin|Varamin]] of 722-6/1322-6 and the mosque at [[place/nayin|Nayin]] of 700/1300. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1157}

Few secular buildings survive, partly because Ilkhan rulers preferred luxurious tents and used perishable materials. German excavations from 1960 to 1964 at [[place/takht-e-soleyman|Takht-i Sulayman]] revealed a palace, which Hamd Allah Mustawfi says Abaqa rebuilt and Wilber dates to about 1275. The observatory at Maragha was built about 656/1258 on Hulegu's orders from plans by Nasir al-Din al-Tusi. The article also describes whole quarters of public buildings near Tabriz, Ghazan's Ghazaniyya and the Rab-i Rashidi of Rashid al-Din, and suggests they foreshadow Ottoman kulliyye foundations. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1158}

## Ceramics, metalwork, textiles and the book
The main ceramic centre was [[place/kashan|Kashan]], which produced mihrabs, tiles and pottery throughout the period; the article describes new underglaze and cobalt wares and notes a decline in quality in the eighth/fourteenth century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1158} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1159} Metalwork gradually became drier and more rigid than in Seljuk times. Textiles, which Western sources called panni tartarici, show Chinese schemes and motifs, and a burial robe in Vienna carries the name of Abu Said. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1159}

The article also treats bookbinding, illumination and painting. Oljeitu was a great patron, with a thirty-volume Quran set made in Hamadan in 713/1313. Miniature painting made a complete break with earlier Arab and Seljuk Iranian workshops under strong Chinese influence, as seen in a manuscript of Juwayni's history dated 689/1290 and in manuscripts of Rashid al-Din's Jami al-tawarikh. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1159} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1160}

## Sources for the period
The article says information on the Ilkhans is very extensive: Persian historiography, above all the works of Juwayni, Rashid al-Din and Wassaf; Syriac chronicles such as that of Barhebraeus, with a Christian view; Arabic works, including those of [[person/ibn-al-fuwati|Ibn al-Fuwati]] and early Mamluk historians, who give the Egyptian view; and notices in Georgian, Armenian, Byzantine and Western writing. Coins largely make up for the near absence of original documents. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1156}
