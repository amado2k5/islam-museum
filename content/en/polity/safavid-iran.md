## Overview
The Safavids ruled Persia as sovereigns from 907/1501 to 1135/1722 and in name only from 1142/1729 to 1148/1736. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose account this page follows, counts their rise a turning point for two reasons. They restored a single Persian sovereignty over the lands regarded as the heart of Persia for the first time since the Arab conquest eight and a half centuries earlier. And their founder, [[person/ismail-i-safavi|Shah Isma'il I]], declared Twelver Shi'ism the official religion of the state, the first time a major Islamic state had done so. The article supposes his motives were a mix of religious conviction and the wish to give the new state an identity distinct from its powerful Sunni neighbour, the [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottoman empire]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 820}

## A Sufi order at Ardabil
The family's origins are obscure, and the article notes that an official genealogy was fabricated for it; scholars now broadly agree that it came from Persian Kurdistan and settled at [[place/ardabil|Ardabil]] in the eleventh century. Its history proper begins with [[person/safi-al-din-ardabili|Shaykh Safi al-Din]], born in 650/1252-3, who in 700/1301 took the leadership of a local Sufi order. Under him and his successor it became a religious movement preaching across Persia, Syria and Asia Minor. There was as yet no sign of the militant Shi'ism of later times; Safi al-Din himself, the article says, was apparently a Sunni of the Shafi'i school. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 821}

A new phase opened when [[person/shaykh-junayd-safavi|Shaykh Junayd]] became head of the order in 851/1447-8 and sought worldly power as well as religious authority. He was killed on a raid into Shirwan in 864/1460; his son Haydar was killed in 893/1488. Their followers wore a red cap of twelve gores for the twelve Imams, from which the Ottomans called them qizilbash, redheads, a name they took up with pride. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 821}

The article finds the strength of the movement in three things: the unquestioning obedience of the Sufi disciples to the head of the order; the belief, which it classes with the doctrines of the extremist Shi'i sects, that the Safavid leader was a living emanation of the divine, a claim it reads in Isma'il's own Turkish poems; and, after the state was founded, the shahs' claim to represent the Twelfth Imam on earth, by which they took over the traditional role of the mujtahids. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 822}

## Isma'il and the founding of the state
In the summer of 905/1499 Isma'il, still a boy, left his refuge in [[place/gilan|Gilan]] to make his bid for power. By the summer of 906/1500 some 7,000 qizilbash from the Turcoman tribes of Anatolia and Syria had joined him. He avenged his father and grandfather in Shirwan, defeated an [[group/aq-qoyunlu|Aq Qoyunlu]] army of 30,000 at Sharur, and in 907/1501 entered [[place/tabriz|Tabriz]], where coins were struck in his name and the Friday sermon was read in the name of the Twelve Imams. Since he was barely fourteen, the article points out, the movement was in fact directed by a small group of devoted qizilbash chiefs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 822}

He spent the next decade extending his rule over the rest of Persia and over [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]], taken in 914/1508. Help sent to [[person/babur-mughal|Babur]] against the Uzbeks ended in 918/1512 with the destruction of a Safavid army, and the north-eastern frontier remained an unsolved problem. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 822}

## Chaldiran
In the north-west the spread of Safavid preaching among the Turcoman tribes of eastern Anatolia alarmed the Ottomans. [[person/selim-i-uthmani|Selim I]], on coming to the throne in 918/1512, proscribed Shi'ism in his dominions and marched east with a great army. At [[event/battle-chaldiran-920|Chaldiran]], on 2 Rajab 920/23 August 1514, the Safavid army was heavily defeated; the article judges the Ottoman artillery and handguns the decisive factor. The Ottomans entered Tabriz but withdrew eight days later when the Janissaries refused to winter there. The Ottomans annexed Diyar Bakr and neighbouring regions. The article lays as much weight on the effect on Isma'il himself: the defeat broke his belief in his own invincibility, he never again took the field in person, and after his death the rivalries of the qizilbash tribes led to ten years of civil war. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 823}

## Tahmasp
[[person/tahmasp-i-safavi|Tahmasp]] succeeded in 930/1524 at the age of ten and went on to reign fifty-two years. The article reports the unflattering portraits of him in the sources and sets against them his record: the Uzbeks made five great attacks on Khurasan and the Ottomans under [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Suleyman]] four invasions of Persia; Baghdad was lost in 942/1535 and the capital moved from Tabriz to [[place/qazvin|Qazvin]]; yet with small means he fought on two fronts and in 962/1555 negotiated the Treaty of Amasya, after which the peace held for the rest of his reign. In 951/1544 he received the fugitive Mughal emperor [[person/humayun-mughal|Humayun]]. He also began to bring Armenians, Georgians and Circassians from the Caucasus into royal service, a third element beside the Turcomans and the Persians. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 823} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 824}

## 'Abbas I
After Tahmasp's death in 984/1576, eleven years of faction left the state so weak that the Ottomans held the citadel of Tabriz and the Uzbeks took [[place/harat|Herat]]. [[person/abbas-i-safavi|'Abbas I]], placed on the throne in 996/1588, resolved to free himself from dependence on the qizilbash. He raised a standing army, a new thing in Persia, from the royal slaves of Caucasian origin, with regiments of musketeers, paid from his own treasury; to pay for it he brought more provinces under direct royal administration. The article calls the result a social and political revolution: by the end of his reign about a fifth of the high-ranking amirs were such men. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 824}

It took ten years to train the new army, and meanwhile he had to cede Azerbaijan, Georgia and other lands to the Ottomans. The tide turned in 1007/1598 with a victory over the Uzbeks and the recovery of Herat. From 1012/1603 he drove the Ottomans back, retaking Tabriz, and by 1016/1607 had cleared the territory fixed at Amasya; in 1033/1624 he recovered Baghdad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 825}

In 1007/1598 he moved the capital from Qazvin to [[place/isfahan|Isfahan]] and laid out a new city beside the old, centred on a great square 507 metres long and 158 wide, with the Chahar Bagh avenue running south across the river. The arts flourished, trade grew, and European envoys and religious orders came to the new capital. In 1031/1622 he expelled the Portuguese from Hormuz with the help of English ships. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 825}

## Decline and fall
The article quotes the traveller Chardin, that when this prince ceased to live Persia ceased to prosper, and finds a kernel of truth in it. It names three causes. 'Abbas, after the murder of his eldest son in 1024/1614, shut the royal princes in the harem, so that his successors came to the throne untrained and the women and eunuchs of the palace gained power. The conversion of provinces to direct royal rule was carried too far, weakening the army and bringing harsher administration by officials who were little more than tax-farmers. And under the last two shahs the mujtahids emerged as a political force: the tolerance of 'Abbas I was abandoned, and Sufis, who had brought the dynasty to power, philosophers, non-conforming Muslims, Christians and Jews suffered. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 825} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 826}

Baghdad and Qandahar were lost in 1047-8/1637-8, though [[person/shah-abbas-ii|'Abbas II]] was an abler ruler. In 1121/1709 the Ghalzay Afghans, until then vassals, rebelled and took Qandahar. Their leader Mahmud routed a Safavid force at Gulnabad near Isfahan on 20 Jumada I 1134/8 March 1722 and starved the capital into surrender; on 1 Muharram 1135/12 October 1722 [[person/sultan-husayn-safavi|Shah Sultan Husayn]] handed him the crown. His son [[person/tahmasp-ii-safavi|Tahmasp II]] proclaimed himself shah at Qazvin, and the dynasty lingered in name until 1148/1736. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 826} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 820}

## How the article frames the dynasty
The encyclopaedia writes as academic history and gives its judgements as such. It sees in the Safavid settlement a lasting tension between the shahs and the mujtahids, and it treats the early movement's claims for its leaders as extreme by the standards of Twelver Shi'ism itself. The museum's Sunni sources describe the dynasty chiefly through its wars with the Ottomans; that account is given in this record's summary, from al-Sallabi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 821}
