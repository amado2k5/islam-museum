## Overview
The Ottoman Empire, an empire founded in 1299 CE and ended in 1922 CE, with its capital at Constantinople. {cite:wikidata}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls it the territorially most extensive and most enduring Islamic state since the break-up of the Abbasid caliphate, and the greatest one founded by Turkish-speaking peoples. It arose after the Mongol devastations in the eastern and central Islamic lands, survived the onslaught of [[person/timur-lang|Timur]] at the start of the fifteenth century, and began on the edge of the Islamic world, in Anatolia. The article credits it with a leading role in the Turkicisation and Islamisation of a land that had been Greek and Armenian, a process it says was not finished until the dynasty ended in 1922. By the sixteenth century the Ottomans had overrun most of mainland Greece, Albania, the Slav lands of the Balkans and much of Hungary, leaving permanent traces in the Balkans in pockets of Muslim Turks and of local peoples who adopted Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 202}

The article presents the empire as a blend of native Turkish traditions with Persian and Arabic literary and artistic currents. It links the sultans' alliance with the Sunni ulama and with Sufi orders such as the [[group/mawlawiyya|Mevlevi]] to the dominance of the [[group/hanafi-madhhab|Hanafi]] school of law over the central Turkish lands and much of the Arab world. It adds that the sultans tacitly took the caliphate for themselves after the last Abbasid puppet caliphs of Cairo disappeared in 1517. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 202}

## The setting before the Ottomans
The article, in its section on the founding, explains that the nucleus of the state was a frontier outpost near the Sakarya river, on the old border between the Seljuk sultanate of Rum and the Byzantines. The Rum sultanate had slid into anarchy after its defeat by the Mongols at Kose Dagh in 1243. Anatolia was by then largely Turkicised, most of its Turks belonging to Oghuz tribes who had entered after Malazgird in 1071, with new waves of tribes and fugitives, many of them from the former Khwarazmian state, coming in the thirteenth century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 203} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 204}

The nomadic Turkomans, the article says, kept many pre-Islamic habits within a heterodox, mystical form of Islam preached by wandering dervishes, and the Baba'i revolt of 1239 came from such circles. The Seljuk government and upper classes followed Sunni tradition, Persian in culture and Hanafi in law, and townspeople joined fraternities such as the [[group/akhi-associations|Akhis]]. In the principalities that replaced the Seljuk state, now the orthodox element and now the heterodox Turkoman one stood in the lead. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 204}

## Osman and Orkhan
The Ottoman state arose in Bithynia, presumably around 1299, as one of several small Turkish principalities on the frontier, beside Karasi, Aydin, Menteshe, Karaman, Germiyan and others. Their lords were descendants of Turkoman frontier chiefs and could expand against the Byzantine coast and the islands held by the Italians; the article says this chance of westward expansion gave the house of [[person/uthman-i-uthmani|Osman]] its eventual superiority. Tradition makes Osman's father Ertoghrul a tribal leader around Sogut, of the Kayi division of the Oghuz. Because the reports are so legendary his very existence was doubted until a coin of Osman bearing his father's name was found. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 204}

Osman's small amirate, centred on the fortress of Karaca Hisar, grew by raids on Byzantine land, by stratagem and by personal ties. In 1302 he defeated the Byzantines at Bapheus and his men reached the shore opposite Constantinople; at his death in 1326 the Sakarya was in effect his eastern border. His son Orkhan took [[place/bursa|Bursa]], which became the capital in 1326, then Iznik in 1331 and Izmid in 1337, made his first big raid into Thrace the same year, and added the Karasi amirate around 1346, which gave him a seafaring power. The article stresses close relations with local Christian chiefs, such as Kose Mikhal, who joined Osman, became Muslim and founded a notable military family, and sees in this one route by which Byzantine customs entered the young state. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 204}

## Early institutions
According to the article, the early administration was military, in the Seljuk manner. The state belonged to the family and was ruled by its senior member, the ulu beg, who made treaties, struck coins and seems to have been named in the Friday prayer, while his sons governed districts and military chiefs received grants of land. Under Orkhan a cavalry force and an infantry were created, since the irregular akincis were no longer enough, and the title pasha, once used for military dervishes, began to be given to statesmen and commanders. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 204}

Orkhan's reach into Europe came with his alliance with the emperor John VI Cantacuzenus in the Byzantine civil war of 1341. His son Suleyman took a fortress on the European side in 1352 and the Ottomans occupied [[place/gallipoli|Gallipoli]] in 1354, while a commercial treaty was made with Genoa in 1352. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 204} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 205}

## Murad I, Bayezid I and Timur
After Orkhan died in 1362, [[person/murad-i-uthmani|Murad I]] conquered the Byzantine land west of Constantinople and took [[place/edirne|Adrianople]] in 1369, soon the European capital. The Serbians were crushed at the Maritsa in 1371, which gave the Ottomans most of present Bulgaria and turned Serbs and Byzantines into tribute-paying vassals, and again at [[event/battle-kosovo-791|Kosovo]] in 1389, where Murad was killed. [[person/bayazid-i-yildirim|Bayezid I]] campaigned from Hungary to southern Greece, won at [[event/battle-of-nicopolis-1396|Nicopolis]] in 1396 over a crusading force and began a siege of Constantinople. In Anatolia Murad gained Germiyan land as a wedding gift for his son and the Hamid amirate by purchase, while Bayezid annexed Saruhan, Aydin and Menteshe in 1390 and Isfendiyar in 1391, with the help of his Christian vassals and by methods the article calls brutal. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 205}

The article names Candarli Kara Halil as the leading statesman of the period and credits him with the origin of the [[group/janissaries|Janissaries]], linked to the sultan's fifth of the booty. They were usually taken from captured Christians, and a Greek source suggests the [[concept/devshirme|devshirme]] was already used in Bayezid's day. Their fraternity-like organisation, modelled on the Akhis and ghazis and tied to the [[group/bektashiyya|Bektashi]] order, shows, it says, the state's peculiar religious tradition. Murad I is the first to take the title sultan in inscriptions, and Orkhan was the first to marry a Byzantine princess. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 205}

Bayezid's policy brought in [[person/timur-lang|Timur]], who crushed his army at [[event/battle-ankara-804|Ankara]] in 1402 and captured him; Bayezid died in captivity. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 205}

## Interregnum and recovery
When Timur left, the Anatolian amirates were restored and the Ottoman state, divided among four sons of Bayezid, passed through the period of the interregnum until 1413, with Christian states backing one prince against another. Mehmed built a base in Anatolia centred on Amasya, Suleyman ruled in Europe and made a treaty with the Christian powers in 1403, and Musa also appeared there. Mehmed I won and restored unity in 1413. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 205}

Under Mehmed I and then Murad II, whose reign began in 1421, the main effort turned again to Europe. Hungary had been the chief rival since the later fourteenth century, and Murad II beat the Hungarians and their allies at [[event/battle-varna-848|Varna]] in 1444 and [[event/battle-kosovo-ii-852|Kosovo]] in 1448. Most European lands stayed under their old lords as vassals who paid tribute and gave military help. A second, gradual and mostly bloodless absorption of the Anatolian amirates took place, except for the Karaman, whom the Ottomans first treated conciliatorily. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 205} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 206}

## Constantinople and the consolidation of the state
[[person/mehmed-ii|Mehmed II]], who succeeded in 1451, set about ending the Byzantine empire, by then reduced to the city, a few islands and some Black Sea towns. [[place/constantinople|Constantinople]] fell on 29 May 1453 and the Ottoman empire succeeded the [[polity/byzantine-empire|Byzantine]] one, in what the article calls only part of a plan to bring the whole Balkan peninsula under direct Ottoman government. Bosnia and most large Aegean islands, except Rhodes, were taken, the Danube principalities and, since 1475, the [[polity/crimean-khanate|Crimean Khanate]] stayed vassals, Trebizond fell in 1461 and the Karamanid dynasty ended in 1475. Mehmed beat the Ak Koyunlu ruler Uzun Hasan at Otluk Beli in 1473. The article explains the permanence of the occupation by the absence of any strong Christian power in a divided Balkans and by the deep hatred between the Orthodox and Catholic churches. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 206}

The article describes a political system whose core was the person of the sultan, with the threat of revolt after nearly every death, the custom of fratricide, begun probably by Bayezid I and decreed by Mehmed II, and a ruling class made up mainly of recruits of Christian origin, from war prisoners, the devshirme or converts. These men were the sultan's slaves, which, it says, prevented a hereditary nobility; the learned class under the Sheikh al-Islam was less dependent. Administration and court showed Byzantine influence, the army rested on timar-holding sipahi cavalry and the Janissaries, and revenue came largely from the jizya and kharaj paid by non-Muslims, tribute and customs. Foreign merchants, mainly Italians, had self-governing communities under the privileges later called capitulations. The article also remarks that Mehmed II's links with Italian princes and artists give him a place among Renaissance rulers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 206} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 207}

## The empire at its height
After the peaceful reign of [[person/bayazid-ii|Bayezid II]], the article says there was no more question of Asia Minor or the Balkans. [[person/selim-i-uthmani|Selim I]] fought Persia, which it presents as an international extension of the earlier struggle against the Shi'i opposition inside Anatolia, and won Azerbaijan for a time and lasting control of Kurdistan and northern Mesopotamia. He then absorbed the [[polity/mamluk-sultanate|Mamluks]] of Syria and Egypt in one campaign, which extended Ottoman overlordship to the holy cities of Islam and soon to Yemen. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 202} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 207}

Under [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Suleyman the Magnificent]] the empire reached its greatest extent: most of Hungary was taken, after [[event/battle-mohacs-932|Mohacs]] in 1526, the army besieged [[place/vienna|Vienna]], Baghdad and Iraq were conquered, and Yemen was recovered in 1568 to 1570. The article notes that [[polity/republic-of-venice|Venice]] was never broken, and that the Ottomans never held clear naval superiority. At his death the empire stood between the Austrian monarchy and the Safavid empire, with half-independent buffer states such as Transylvania, Wallachia, Moldavia and Crimea, and with the sharif of Mecca as a vassal. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 207}

## Strain from the late sixteenth century
Under [[person/selim-ii-uthmani|Selim II]], in practice under the vizier [[person/sokollu-mehmed-pasha|Mehmed Sokollu]], Cyprus was conquered in 1570 to 1571, but the naval defeat at [[event/battle-lepanto-978|Lepanto]] in 1571 was the first great military blow. Lacking room for expansion, the article says, the empire weakened inside, with failed campaigns against Austria and Persia and the treaty of Zsitvatorok in 1606. The recovery under [[person/murad-iv-uthmani|Murad IV]], the last sultan to revive the military tradition, brought back Baghdad in 1638 and a long peace with Persia. After 1640 the Ottomans took Crete, completed in 1669, and in 1664 lost at St Gotthard to Austria; the failed siege of Vienna in 1683 led to the loss of Hungary and to the peace of Carlowitz in 1699, in which the empire also had to recognise Venetian rule in the Morea. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 208}

The article locates the cause mainly in domestic matters. An empire that could survive only by constant conquest had to adapt to peace, which exceeded the personal rule of the sultan, who became less involved in administration. Sultans were deposed, among them [[person/uthman-ii-uthmani|Osman II]] in 1628 and Ibrahim in 1648, the latter by a fatwa of the Sheikh al-Islam, and [[person/mehmed-iv-uthmani|Mehmed IV]] in 1688. Power passed to great ministers such as Sokollu and the [[person/koprulu-mehmed-pasha|Koprulu]] family, but also to the Janissaries and sipahis, the court circle under the sultan's mother or the chief black eunuch, and the ulama. The author sees these symptoms analysed in Koci Bey's Risala. Revolts in Anatolia, such as that of the Kalender-oghlu in 1606, showed the limits of central control. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 208} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 209}

## The eighteenth century
The article says the eighteenth century has been described too superficially as decadence. The peace of Passarowitz in 1718 cost the last of Hungary and Transylvania, though Belgrade was recovered in 1739, and the Morea was retaken from Venice in 1715. A new danger appeared in Russia. The war that began in 1768 ended with [[event/treaty-kucuk-kaynarca-1187|Kucuk Kaynarca]] in 1774, which made Crimea independent and gave the sultan a claim to protect the Muslims there, the start of religious claims that would matter in the nineteenth century. Further losses followed in 1792 and 1812, and the French occupied Egypt in 1798. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 209}

Inside the empire, [[person/ahmed-iii-uthmani|Ahmed III]]'s Istanbul was brilliant and gave its name, from the cultivation of tulips, to the Tulip Period; a new class of literati arose and a short-lived Muslim Turkish printing began in 1727 under [[person/ibrahim-muteferrika|Ibrahim Muteferrika]]. The Janissary rebellion of Patrona Khalil in 1730 cost Ahmed his throne. Military reforms were attempted with foreign help, [[person/selim-iii-uthmani|Selim III]]'s new troops provoked a large rebellion, and [[person/mahmud-ii-uthmani|Mahmud II]] destroyed the Janissaries on 16 June 1826 and persecuted the Bektashi order. The weakening of the centre had produced hereditary beys in North Africa, powerful ayan in the Balkans and derebeys in Anatolia. The article holds that the Ottoman administrative tradition still kept these regions together enough for Mahmud II and later statesmen to preserve political unity for another century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 209} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 210}

## Reform and the nineteenth century
Greece won independence by 1830, after a Greek insurrection that began in 1820. The Egyptian governor [[person/muhammad-ali-pasha|Muhammad Ali]] threatened to take Egypt, Syria and Cilicia from 1831 and in 1840 was recognised in Egypt under a hereditary dynasty, but only through the intervention of the European powers, which the article calls decisive for the empire's territorial status. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 210}

The Tanzimat measures, applied gradually on the French model, aimed at a modern state in which all citizens had equal rights, a goal the article says was unlike the democratic ideals then working in Europe. After the Crimean War the Treaty of Paris in 1856 seemed to secure the empire, but foreign intervention grew, including over the capitulations, which the Porte tried in vain to shed until 1914. The Bulgarian crisis brought war with Russia, and the preliminaries of San Stefano in 1878, softened at Berlin, brought the definite loss of Serbia, Montenegro and Romania, a semi-dependent Bulgaria, and the loss of Kars and Batum; Britain took the administration of Cyprus and, in 1882, occupied Egypt. The article lists later losses: Tripoli to Italy in 1912 and, in the Balkan War of 1912 to 1913, all of Europe except Eastern Thrace. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 210} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 211}

It judges that the Tanzimat helped dissolve the empire as much as foreign interest did. The first constitution, drafted by [[person/midhat-pasha|Midhat Pasha]], was set aside when [[person/abd-al-hamid-ii-uthmani|Abdulhamid II]] preferred to rule himself; the censorship of his reign was known as the period of despotism, though the article says he did not undo the Tanzimat institutions. The Tanzimat also produced a middle class of Turkish-speaking Muslim intellectuals, with a new patriotism voiced by [[person/namik-kemal|Namik Kemal]] and a Turkish daily press, and their separation from the Christian, Jewish and non-Turkish Muslim populations grew. Pan-Islamic feeling also rose, and Istanbul came to be seen as the political capital of Islam. The Young Turk movement, centred on Salonica and the Committee of Union and Progress, forced the sultan to restore the constitution on 24 June 1908; he was deposed on 27 April 1909. Ottomanism, meaning equality of all communities, soon gave way, in the article's account, to Pan-Turkism and finally to Turkish nationalism. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 211} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 212}

## The end of the dynasty
The empire entered the First World War on the side of the Central Powers. The Allies failed at the Dardanelles but conquered Palestine, Syria and Mesopotamia, with help in Syria from the sharif of Mecca, who had made himself king of the Hijaz in 1917. The armistice of Mudros on 30 October 1918 was followed by the Allied occupation of Istanbul and the Greek landing at Izmir in May 1919, and the Treaty of Sevres in August 1920 put large parts of the remaining land under foreign control. A national resistance rose in Anatolia under [[person/mustafa-kemal-ataturk|Mustafa Kemal]], and on 1 November 1922 the Grand National Assembly at Ankara abolished the Istanbul government and deposed the sultan Mehmed VI, which the article calls the extinction of the empire and its dynasty. Abdulmecid II stayed on as caliph until the Assembly abolished the caliphate on 2 March 1924 and banished the whole house of Osman, who were allowed back only some fifty years later. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 211} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 212}

## For Sprouts
The Ottoman Empire was a vast Islamic state that built thriving cities and mosques with grand domes and slender minarets.

## For Explorers
A major Islamic empire that lasted over six centuries, with its capital at Istanbul. Extending across three continents, it was renowned for charitable endowment systems, majestic domed mosques designed by Mimar Sinan, and the patronage of Arabic calligraphy and illuminated Qur'ans.

## For Scholars
An enduring Islamic empire founded in northwestern Anatolia in the late 7th century AH (c. 1299 CE) by Osman I, enduring until 1922 CE. Following the conquest of Constantinople in 857 AH (1453 CE), it expanded across Anatolia, Southeastern Europe, the Arab provinces, and North Africa. Characterized by centralized administration blending Islamic jurisprudence with customary imperial decrees (kanun), it achieved renowned architectural mastery through central domed complexes, fine calligraphy, and extensive socio-religious waqf endowments.
