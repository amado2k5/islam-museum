## Overview
The Abbasids were the dynasty of caliphs that ruled from 132/750 to 656/1258, succeeding the [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad state]]. The family takes its name from al-Abbas, the uncle of the Prophet. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose account this page follows, divides their sovereignty into two parts: from 132/750 to 334/945, when the authority of the caliphs gradually declined and military leaders rose to rule through their troops, and from about 334/945 to 656/1258, when the caliphs, with one exception, kept only a nominal suzerainty while real power lay with secular dynasties. The article calls this the great epoch of classical Islamic civilisation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 34} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 36} Their capital was [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]], and a later line of Abbasid caliphs sat in [[place/cairo|Cairo]]. {cite:wikidata}

## The movement that overthrew the Umayyads
The article says the story of the movement was long known only in the revised version circulated once the dynasty had gained power, and that a more critical reading by G. van Vloten, developed by J. Wellhausen, has been largely confirmed by later research. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 34}

The Abbasid party was known as the Hashimiyya. The article argues that the name did not simply assert kinship with the Prophet but came from a sect that followed [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-hanafiyya|Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya]] and, after him, his son [[person/abu-hashim-ibn-al-hanafiyya|Abu Hashim]]. When Abu Hashim died without issue in 98/716, one group held that he had bequeathed the imamate to Muhammad ibn Ali, of the line of al-Abbas. The article notes that the story of this bequest may be fictitious, but judges that Muhammad ibn Ali clearly took over the sect and its propaganda network and turned it into the instrument of the Abbasid cause. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 34}

According to the accounts the article summarises, intensive propaganda began about 100/718, directed from [[place/kufa|Kufa]] and aimed at [[place/khurasan|Khurasan]]. Muhammad ibn Ali died in 125/743 and his son [[person/ibrahim-al-imam|Ibrahim]] succeeded to the claims. In 128/745-6 Ibrahim sent his freedman [[person/abu-muslim-al-khurasani|Abu Muslim]] to Khurasan. The sources differ over Abu Muslim's origin, but agree that he was a Persian. His appeal was chiefly to the Persian mawali, though he also won Yemeni Arabs, and the article reads the black flags he raised as a messianic signal. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 34}

By Ramadan 129/May-June 747 Abu Muslim was ready to act. The article describes Khurasan as a well-chosen ground: its Persian population resented Umayyad inequalities, and the Arab settlers were divided among themselves. Abu Muslim took Marw, and with his general [[person/qahtaba-ibn-shabib|Qahtaba]] seized the whole province from the Umayyad governor [[person/nasr-ibn-sayyar|Nasr ibn Sayyar]]. The army then moved on [[place/rayy|Rayy]] and [[place/nihawand|Nihawand]], and in 132/749 crossed the Euphrates and defeated an Umayyad army under [[person/ibn-hubayra|Ibn Hubayra]]. Ibrahim had been captured by the caliph Marwan and died, so his brother [[person/abu-al-abbas-al-saffah|Abu al-Abbas]] was hailed as caliph at Kufa with the title al-Saffah. The battle of the Greater Zab, in 132/750, sealed the fate of the Umayyad caliphate. Marwan fled to Syria and then to Egypt, where he was killed, and the new caliph's authority was established across the Middle East. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 35}

## How the revolution has been read
The article reports that many nineteenth-century orientalists, influenced by racial theories, saw the revolution as a victory of Iran over Arabia, and that some Arabic sources contain similar statements. It then describes the revisions of more recent writers: Shi'ism had Arab origins and was strongest in areas of Arab settlement, Abu Muslim's revolt was aimed at Umayyad and Syrian rule rather than at Arabs as such, and many Arabs, Yemenis especially, took part. In the article's judgement Persians served an Arab dynasty, and the fates of Abu Muslim, Abu Salama and the Barmakids show they could be removed if they fell foul of their masters. What the Arabs lost, it says, was the exclusive right to the fruits of power. It suggests that the end of the Arab kingdom is better placed in the stopping of the old pensions and the rise of the Turkish guards from the time of al-Mu'tasim, and that the revolution's significance lies in what changed after it, above all the shift of the centre of gravity from Syria to Iraq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 35}

## Baghdad and the first consolidation
Al-Saffah first set up his capital at the small town of [[place/al-hashimiyya|al-Hashimiyya]], near Kufa, and later moved it to [[place/al-anbar|al-Anbar]]. His brother and successor [[person/al-mansur|al-Mansur]], whom the article calls in many ways the real founder of the caliphate, established the permanent capital on the west bank of the [[place/tigris|Tigris]] near the ruins of Ctesiphon. Its official name was Madinat al-Salam, though it is usually known by the name of the earlier town, Baghdad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 36}

The early years were insecure. Risings occurred on every side, Umayyad partisans gave trouble in Syria, the Alids soon reappeared as determined opponents, and the Kharijites stayed active. When al-Mansur succeeded, his uncle [[person/abdallah-ibn-ali-ibn-abdallah|Abd Allah ibn Ali]], commanding on the Byzantine frontier, proclaimed himself caliph, and the threat was met mainly through Abu Muslim. The article says the dynasty then chose continuity and orthodoxy over the aims of its revolutionary followers. Abu Salama had already been put to death, and Abu Muslim was killed once al-Mansur felt strong enough. The article holds that these steps, with the suppression of the Rawandiyya, alienated the extremist supporters, some of whom turned to religio-political revolts in Iran and others to the Isma'ilis, while reassuring the orthodox. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 36}

## Administration, Persian influence and orthodoxy
In building the centralised administration al-Mansur was assisted by the [[group/barmakids|Barmakids]], whom the article describes as Persians of a different kind from the Khurasani rebels: Buddhists by earlier religion, from the priestly landowning class of Balkh. Khalid al-Barmaki appeared after the foundation of Baghdad, and the family directed the administration until its dramatic and still unexplained fall under [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]] in 187/803. The article describes growing Persian influence in the court and government, with Sasanid models followed, and notes that the prejudice against employing mawali in high office gradually disappeared. As the Arab bond weakened, it says, the caliphs stressed Islamic orthodoxy, courted theologians, rebuilt Mecca and Medina, and organised the pilgrimage from Iraq. They also persecuted heretical movements, among them Manichaeism under the name of zandaqa. For a time they tried to impose the Mu'tazili doctrine, which the article, following H. S. Nyberg's hypothesis, takes to have been an official attempt at a compromise with the Shi'a. From the time of [[person/al-mutawakkil|al-Mutawakkil]] that attempt was abandoned and the Abbasids adhered, formally at least, to strict orthodoxy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 36} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 37}

## Loss of the provinces and civil war
The article records that Spain had broken away under an Umayyad prince as early as 138/756, and that after the death of Yazid ibn Hatim, the last effective Abbasid governor of North Africa, in 170/787, independent dynasties arose in the west. It presents the [[polity/aghlabid-emirate|Aghlabids]] as the pattern of hereditary local governorships under nominal suzerainty. It also says the campaigns of Harun were the last major Muslim offensives against Byzantium, after which Islam was on the defensive. After Harun's death war broke out between his sons [[person/al-amin|al-Amin]], strong in Baghdad and Iraq, and al-Ma'mun, strong in Persia. The article doubts the reading of this as an Arab-Persian conflict and prefers to see social struggles complicated by rivalry between Persia and Iraq. Al-Ma'mun for a time planned to move the capital to Marw but later returned to Baghdad. In 205/820 his general [[person/tahir-ibn-al-husayn|Tahir]] made himself virtually independent in Khurasan and founded a dynasty, and others followed his example. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 37}

## The Turkish guards and Samarra
The article ties the weakening of the caliphs to chronic financial disorder and to the farming out of state revenues, which put real power in the hands of army commanders. It says that from the time of [[person/al-mutasim|al-Mu'tasim]] and [[person/al-wathiq|al-Wathiq]] the caliphs became puppets of their own generals. Al-Mu'tasim is usually credited with using Turks from Central Asia as soldiers and officers, and in 221/836 he built a new residence at [[place/samarra|Samarra]], which remained the imperial seat until al-Mu'tamid returned to Baghdad in 279/892. Al-Mutawakkil tried to break the Turkish guards by rallying the theologians and civil population, renouncing Mu'tazili doctrine and enforcing regulations against Christians and Jews, but the attempt failed, and his murder in 247/861 was followed by nine years of anarchy and four caliphs. During this time the [[event/zanj-rebellion|Zanj]] revolt of slaves near Basra cut the road to the Gulf, and by 264/877 raiders came within 17 miles of Baghdad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 37}

## Partial recovery and the Shi'i challenge
Under al-Mu'tamid, his brother [[person/abu-ahmad-al-muwaffaq|al-Muwaffak]] became the real master and, by 270/883, crushed the Zanj and checked the Saffarids and Tulunids. His son [[person/al-mutadid|al-Mu'tadid]] and then al-Muktafi were able rulers who for a time reasserted caliphal authority in Persia and Egypt. The article then turns to a militant Shi'i challenge: the Isma'ili movement, the Qarmatians of Bahrayn and Mesopotamia, and in the west a Fatimid counter-caliphate in Tunisia. Al-Muktafi crushed the Qarmatian revolt in Syria and Mesopotamia, but under the boy-caliph al-Muqtadir the destructive tendencies returned, while the Hamdanids and the Buyids emerged. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 37} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 38}

Under al-Qahir and al-Radi the decay was completed. The event the article takes as symbolic was the grant of the title amir al-umara to the governor of Iraq, [[person/ibn-raiq|Ibn Ra'iq]], which recognised a supreme temporal authority and left the caliph as formal head of state and faith. In 334/945 the Buyid [[person/muizz-al-dawla|Mu'izz al-Dawla]] entered Baghdad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 38}

## Government, army and economy
The article says that the early Abbasids continued the late Umayyad lines with less break than once believed, but that the caliph became an autocrat claiming divine origin for his authority, exercising it through a vast bureaucracy. It judges the Abbasids stronger than the Umayyads yet weaker than old oriental despots, being theoretically subject to the Holy Law. It describes ministries under a wazir, provincial rule shared by an amir and a financial administrator, and an intelligence service under the director of posts. In the army, the Khurasanis, defined regionally, gave way to Turkish slave troops. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 38} The article adds that the accession was followed by a great economic revival, that the Arab warrior caste was replaced by landowners, bureaucrats, soldiers, merchants and men of learning, and that the political breakdown had no immediate ill effects on economic and cultural life. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 38} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 39}

## Under the Buyids and Seljuks
From 334/945 to the Mongol conquest the article sees the caliphate as a titular institution, the headship of Sunni Islam and a legitimating authority for secular rulers. The Shi'i [[polity/buyid-dynasty|Buyids]] did not install an Alid caliph but kept the Abbasids as an orthodox cover. The article judges the Isma'ili [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimids]], who conquered Egypt in 356/969, to have been the real threat, and says the occupation of Baghdad by the Turkish general [[person/al-basasiri|al-Basasiri]] in the Fatimid name was the high-water mark of their power. In 447/1055 [[person/tughril-beg|Tughril Beg]] of the [[polity/seljuk-empire|Seljuks]] entered Baghdad and was proclaimed sultan. The article says the Seljuks, as Sunni Turks, raised the prestige though not the power of the caliphs, and that they met the Shi'i danger through the suppression of Shi'i dynasties and through a network of [[concept/madrasa|madrasas]]. It adds that the Nizari movement, called Assassins there, challenged them and ultimately failed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 39}

## The last caliphs and the Mongols
After the Seljuks broke up, the caliph al-Nasir made a final attempt to restore caliphal authority, trying to create a kind of state for the caliphate in Baghdad and Iraq and drawing on the futuwwa organisations. His successors were weak. In 656/1258 the Mongol general [[person/hulagu|Hulagu]] appeared before Baghdad and the last caliph, al-Musta'sim, could offer no serious resistance. The article agrees that this was the end of an epoch, but judges that the immediate moral effects have been overrated, since the caliphate had long ceased to be an effective institution. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 39} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 40}

## The Abbasid caliphs in Cairo
In 659/1261 the [[polity/mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk]] sultan [[person/baybars|Baybars]] installed an Abbasid refugee as caliph in Cairo, the start of the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate-cairo|Abbasid line of Cairo]]. The article gives R. Hartmann's explanation: the disappearance of the Baghdad caliphate left the secular rulers in need of legitimacy. For two and a half centuries the caliphs there were nominal, with ceremonial duties on the accession of a new sultan, apart from a six-month interval in 815/1412 when al-Musta'in served as stop-gap ruler. In 1517 the last of them was deposed by [[person/selim-i-ottoman|Selim I]]. The article reports that the story of a transfer of the title to the Ottoman house first appeared in 1788 and that Barthold showed it to be without foundation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 40}

## For Sprouts
The Abbasid state arose in Baghdad, built the House of Wisdom library, and gathered scholars and books from everywhere to spread knowledge.

## For Explorers
The Abbasid Caliphate was a major caliphate that ruled after the Umayyads and built its famous capital Baghdad on the Tigris River. Science, translation, and mathematics flourished in its era, and its caliphs founded the House of Wisdom to gather knowledge and encourage scholars.

## For Scholars
A major Islamic caliphate established in 132 AH (750 CE) following the fall of the Umayyads, lasting until the Mongol sack of Baghdad in 656 AH (1258 CE), with a shadow caliphate surviving in Mamluk Cairo until the Ottoman conquest in 923 AH (1517 CE). From its capital Baghdad on the Tigris, its early era witnessed a flourishing of administration, jurisprudence, translation, medicine, and mathematics, anchored by institutions like the House of Wisdom.
