## Overview
The Delhi Sultanate was the principal Muslim kingdom of northern India from its establishment by [[person/iltutmish|Iltutmish]], who reigned 608-633/1211-1236, until it was absorbed into the [[polity/mughal-empire|Mughal empire]] under [[person/jalal-al-din-akbar|Akbar]]. Five lines of sultans held [[place/delhi|Delhi]] in turn: the Turkish slave commanders and their heirs, the [[polity/khalji-dynasty|Khaljis]], the [[polity/tughlaq-dynasty|Tughluqs]], the [[polity/sayyid-dynasty-delhi|Sayyids]] and the Afghan [[polity/lodi-dynasty|Lodis]], followed by a short Afghan restoration under the [[polity/sur-empire|Surs]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 280}

## The Ghurid conquests
The sultanate was made possible by the Indian campaigns of the [[polity/ghurid-dynasty|Ghurid]] Mu'izz al-Din Muhammad ibn Sam and his lieutenant [[person/qutb-al-din-aybak|Qutb al-Din Aybak]]. The capture of Peshawar in 575/1179, Sialkot in 581/1185 and [[place/lahore|Lahore]] in 582/1186 ended Ghaznavid rule in India. Defeated at Tarain in 587/1191 by the Chauhans under Prithviraja, Mu'izz al-Din [[event/battle-tarain-588|returned the next year and defeated them decisively]] on the same ground, and Delhi was occupied in 588/1192. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 280} Aybak took Bada'un, Kanauj and Gwalior in the following years. When Mu'izz al-Din was assassinated on the Indus in 602/1206, the article's author, P. Hardy, judges the Ghurid position in India to have been a precarious military occupation of the chief towns of the Punjab, Sind and the Ganges-Jumna plain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 281}

## Aybak and Iltutmish
Aybak then moved to Lahore and took power there. Hardy notes that no coin or inscription bears out the statement that he took the title of sultan, and that he made no recorded effort to extend the conquests before he died at Lahore in 607/1210 in an accident at polo. His son Aram Shah succeeded him at Lahore, but a group of officers at Delhi set up Iltutmish, Aybak's son-in-law and holder of Bada'un, and Aram Shah was killed marching on Delhi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 281}

Hardy counts Iltutmish the founder of an independent sultanate. In his reign the Turks and Afghans of India were cut off politically from Central Asia, the ruler of Delhi gained the first place in Muslim India, and the main strategic centres of the northern plain were firmly held. He defeated and captured his rival Yildiz at Tarain in 612/1215, kept out of the struggle between the Mongols and [[person/jalal-al-din-khwarazmshah|Jalal al-Din Khwarazmshah]], and in 625/1228 drove Qubacha from [[place/multan|Multan]] and Uch. In the east he invaded Lakhnauti in [[place/bengal|Bengal]] towards the end of 627/1230. In 626/1229 he received a robe of honour and a title from the Abbasid caliph al-Mustansir, the sign of an independent Muslim power in north India. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 281}

## From Raziyya to Balban
Within ten years of his death the officers and officials had accepted and deposed four of his children or grandchildren, among them his daughter [[person/razia-sultana|Raziyya]], who reigned 634-7/1236-40. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 281} Stability returned only under Nasir al-Din Mahmud, 644-64/1246-66, in whose time real authority lay with Ghiyath al-Din Balban. Balban's career as minister and then as sultan, 664-86/1266-87, shows how ceaseless was the fighting needed to keep Delhi the first power of the north: raids and campaigns almost every year, the clearing of the forests near Delhi, and garrisons of Afghans settled in the pacified districts. The historian Barani presents him as following a purely defensive policy against the Mongols; Hardy thinks this a later rationalisation, and adds that the Mongols were then too taken up with their own quarrels to be a serious threat. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 282}

## The Khaljis
Balban's grandson Kayqubad could not master the factions of Turks and Khaljis, and in 689/1290 the Khalji leader Jalal al-Din took the sultanate. Later Muslim historians saw in this the end of the Turkish sultanate; Hardy accepts it of the sultan's descent, but not of a ruling group that had already ceased to be purely Turkish. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 282}

Under [[person/ala-al-din-khalji|'Ala' al-Din Khalji]], 695-715/1296-1315, who came to the throne by having his uncle Jalal al-Din killed, the sultanate reached for a short time an imperial standing in the subcontinent. Serious attacks by the Chaghatay Mongols were beaten off; that of 702-3/1303 invested Delhi itself. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 282} [[place/gujarat|Gujarat]] was overrun in 698/1299, Ranthambhor taken in 700/1301 and Chitor in 702/1303, and south of the Narbada the kingdoms of Devagiri and Telingana were laid under tribute. In 710/1311 [[person/malik-kafur|Malik Kafur]] invaded the Pandya kingdom in the far south and returned to Delhi loaded with spoil. Hardy stresses that 'Ala' al-Din was content to make the Hindu kingdoms of the south tributaries, and that he received defeated Hindu rulers at court and married into their families. Barani describes his measures to control the prices of necessities at the capital and so lessen the cost of a large army. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 283}

## The Tughluqs
The Khalji line ended in murder within five years of his death. Ghazi Malik marched on Delhi and took the throne as [[person/ghiyath-al-din-tughluq|Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq]], reigning 720-5/1320-5; he died under a collapsing hunting pavilion, and Hardy, weighing the evidence, judges his son innocent of the death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 283}

Hardy calls the reign of [[person/muhammad-ibn-tughluq|Muhammad ibn Tughluq]], 725-52/1325-51, a watershed. At its start Delhi's authority was recognised, according to Barani, in twenty-three provinces, and [[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]] in the 1330s describes power and magnificence; by its end the lands south of the Narbada were lost and revolt was endemic elsewhere. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 283} The decision that did most harm, in Hardy's view, was the attempt to make Deogir, renamed [[place/daulatabad|Daulatabad]], a second capital and to settle members of the Muslim ruling group in the Deccan, which turned suzerainty over the south into settlement and gave ambitious officers their chance. A famine struck the Delhi region in 736/1335-6. The sultan had to put down twenty-two rebellions, and in 748/1347 an independent sultanate was proclaimed in the Deccan, that of the [[polity/bahmani-sultanate|Bahmanids]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 284}

His cousin [[person/firuz-shah-tughluq|Firuz Shah]], chosen by the army, is drawn in the surviving accounts as pious, kindly and devoted to building; Hardy observes that before old age he campaigned as other sultans did, though he declined an invitation to try to recover the south. He died in 790/1388, and his sons and grandsons fought for the throne. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 284}

## Timur and after
Into this disorder came [[person/timur-lang|Timur]], who in 801/1398 defeated the forces of Sultan Mahmud before Delhi and [[event/timur-delhi-801|occupied and sacked the city]]. The holder of Delhi became one military chief among many. Former officers proclaimed independent sultanates in Malwa in 808/1406 and Gujarat in 810/1407, and [[place/jaunpur|Jaunpur]] became the centre of another. Delhi, in Hardy's phrase a capital city without an empire, passed in 817/1414 to Khidr Khan, governor of Multan, and his Sayyid successors ruled as provincial princes. In 855/1451 the last of them gave Delhi up peacefully to the Afghan [[person/bahlul-lodi|Bahlul Lodi]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 284}

Bahlul widened Delhi's reach again, twice beat off the sultans of Jaunpur and occupied Jaunpur itself in 884/1479; his success is put down to his deft handling of his Afghan amirs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 284} His son [[person/sikandar-lodi|Sikandar]] completed the absorption of Jaunpur and founded [[place/agra|Agra]] in 910/1504. Under [[person/ibrahim-lodi|Ibrahim]] the governor of the Punjab and the sultan's own uncle appealed to [[person/babur-mughal|Babur]] at Kabul, who [[event/battle-of-panipat-932|defeated and killed Ibrahim at Panipat]] in Rajab 932/April 1526. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 285}

## The Sur restoration
The Afghan sultanate was restored for a time by [[person/shir-shah-suri|Shir Shah Sur]], who rose from the management of his father's lands in Bihar to the mastery of Bengal by 944/1538, defeated [[person/humayun-mughal|Humayun]] at Chausa in 946/1539 and near Kanauj in 947/1540, and was killed besieging Kalinjar in 952/1545. His son [[person/islam-shah-suri|Islam Shah]] held the dominions together until 960/1553, after which unity collapsed. Humayun re-entered in 962/1555, and the Mughal victory at Panipat after his death settled that the Mughals would not be driven out again. Under Akbar, Hardy writes, the sultanate merged imperceptibly into the Mughal empire, which built more on Khalji and Tughluq foundations than its panegyrists admit. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 285}

## Rulers and subjects
Hardy argues that the sultanate did not break with the older Rajput tradition by which rulers in Hindustan sought paramountcy and not full sovereignty: the Turks and Afghans never reduced the Hindu chiefs to disarmed impotence, and Rajasthan was independent of Delhi except for a short period. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 285} Muslim migration to India in these centuries was mostly of professional classes and did not displace the mass of the Hindu population; most working Muslims were converts or the children of mixed unions. Relations with Hindus, in his reading, were governed more by policy than by religion: Hindu chiefs were tributaries, Hindu cultivators taxpayers, and Hindu clerks and bankers indispensable. On whether the sultans normally levied the [[concept/jizya|jizya]] as a separate tax on non-Muslims, Hardy doubts it and reads the term in the chronicles as a conventional name for tribute or land revenue; he states plainly that the contrary view is the more usual one among historians. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 286}

## Government
The ruling group was mixed: Turks, Afghans and Khaljis at first, then Hindu converts in high office. Many of the early sultans and commanders began as slaves of the royal household, but Hardy holds that slavery was one road to power among several. The central offices followed Samanid and Ghaznavid models. A provincial governor commanded the local troops, was paid by a revenue assignment, the [[concept/iqta|iqta']], and was meant to send the surplus revenue to the capital; after the Tughluqs governors became half-independent tributaries with private armies. Throughout there was a tension between sultans such as 'Ala' al-Din, who wished to pay troops in cash from lands under direct administration, and a military class that preferred assignments it managed itself. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 286} In collecting the land revenue the sultanate kept to the practice of earlier times, taking a share of the produce by dividing the harvest, by estimating the yield or by measuring the land. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 287}
