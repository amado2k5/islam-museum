## Overview
The Umayyad caliphate, founded in 661 CE and ended in July 750 CE, with its capital at [[place/damascus|Damascus]]. It follows the [[polity/rashidun-caliphate|Rashidun caliphate]] in the data. {cite:wikidata} The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose account this page follows for the rest, describes the Umayyads as the dynasty of caliphs which ruled the whole of the Arab Islamic territories from a centre in Syria, from 41/661 to 132/750. All its caliphs descended from Umayya ibn Abd Shams, a pre-Islamic notable of the tribe of [[group/quraysh|Quraysh]] of [[place/mecca|Mecca]], but they form two lines within the clan of [[group/banu-umayya|Umayya]]. The first three, descended from [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-harb|Abu Sufyan ibn Harb]], are called Sufyanids; the remaining eleven, descendants of [[person/marwan-ibn-al-hakam|Marwan ibn al-Hakam]], are called Marwanids. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 895}

The article lists the caliphs with the dates generally given for them. Among the Marwanids are Marwan I in 64-5/684-5, [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik]] in 65-86/685-705, [[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|al-Walid I]] in 86-96/705-15, [[person/sulayman-ibn-abd-al-malik|Sulayman]] in 96-9/715-17, [[person/umar-ibn-abd-al-aziz|Umar II]] in 99-101/717-20 and [[person/yazid-ibn-abd-al-malik|Yazid II]] in 101-5/720-4. The list then names [[person/hisham-ibn-abd-al-malik|Hisham]], [[person/al-walid-ibn-yazid|al-Walid II]], [[person/yazid-ibn-al-walid|Yazid III]] and [[person/marwan-ibn-muhammad|Marwan II]]; their dates are not legible in the scan used here and are left out. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 895}

## The Umayyads in modern scholarship
The article dates serious modern study of the period to Wellhausen's book of 1902, which tried to recover the earliest layers of Muslim tradition. It conveyed a rather favourable image of the Umayyads and their officials as pragmatic politicians who built an empire on Arab domination over non-Arabs and were largely unconcerned with religion unless pietist opponents forced the matter. Lammens presented the same image in a more exaggerated form. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 895}

Later work, the article says, changed this picture. Goldziher and Schacht showed that religious and legal traditions about early Islam often project later ideas back onto the past, and Albrecht Noth stressed the literary character of the historical tradition, so some scholars doubt that the period can be recreated in detail from sources written after the dynasty fell. Others turned to coins, inscriptions, archaeology and non-Muslim writings. The article adds that "Arab" is now understood as a cultural as much as an ethnic category, and that the most significant feature of the period is how conquerors and conquered merged into a new Islamic society. It also holds that the idea that the caliphs cared little for religion came from accepting too readily the traditional view of the dynasty, and notes the caliphs' claim, stressed by Crone and Hinds, to be God's deputies on earth. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 895} The article judges that Islam as it took shape in the first two centuries of the Abbasid age owes much to circles opposed to the Umayyads, and that it is anachronistic to call the Umayyads representatives of Sunni Islam, since the Sunni tradition emerged in developed form only after the dynasty fell. It adds that the Umayyads' own understanding of their religion can be seen only indirectly, because the tradition reflects the views of their opponents. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 896}

## The Umayyads in Muslim tradition
The article reports that the image in Muslim tradition is generally negative, though softened by some more favourable material. Tradition traces the rivalry to the two branches of Quraysh, the Banu Abd Shams, to whom the Umayyads belonged, and the [[group/banu-hashim|Banu Hashim]], from whom came the Prophet, the Alids and the Abbasids. It shows Abu Sufyan leading the Meccan armies at [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]] and [[event/battle-of-the-trench|the Trench]], tells of the notorious act of [[person/hind-bint-utba|Hind bint Utba]] against the body of [[person/hamza|Hamza]], and presents the leading Umayyads as accepting Islam late and with doubtful sincerity around the [[event/conquest-of-mecca|conquest of Mecca]]. Some reports suggest that the killing of [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]] was exploited by his Umayyad relatives to weaken the claim of [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]], and that Muawiya's side outwitted Ali's supporters at [[event/battle-of-siffin|Siffin]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 896}

Its sharpest charge is that the dynasty ruled as kings, not true caliphs, linked above all to Muawiya's naming of his son Yazid as successor. Individual caliphs such as Yazid are credited with habits it finds repellent, though the article suggests that stress on these reflects the refusal to accept the dynasty as much as causes it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 896} The tradition also holds the dynasty responsible for the army sent to the Hijaz under Yazid, which sacked Medina and bombarded Mecca, damaging the Kaaba so that it was rebuilt, and for the killing of [[person/husayn-ibn-ali|al-Husayn ibn Ali]] at [[event/battle-of-karbala|Karbala]]. Works listing such misdeeds were written, among them by [[person/al-jahiz|al-Jahiz]] and [[person/al-maqrizi|al-Maqrizi]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 897}

The tradition has milder voices too. Umar II and, to a lesser extent, Yazid III are presented as legitimate, and Umar II is sometimes called the fifth Rightly-Guided Caliph. Even caliphs denied legitimacy are credited with achievements, and the phrase "year of unity" for the years when Muawiya and Abd al-Malik ended civil strife implies some approval. The article explains the overall picture by the hostile origin of the surviving historical tradition and by the changing nature of Islam, since the Umayyads were judged by criteria that developed during their rule. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 897}

## The Sufyanid period
The article treats the events reported in the literary tradition as reflecting a historical reality, noting that coins, inscriptions and non-Muslim writings usually confirm the basic details. It explains Syria's rise as the centre mainly by the fact that Muawiya had held it for over twenty years before becoming caliph, and by its large Arab population, dominated by one tribal group, the Kudaa. Muawiya and his successors governed through a few powerful governors, notably Ziyad ibn Abihi and his son [[person/ubayd-allah-ibn-ziyad|Ubayd Allah ibn Ziyad]]. The article cautions against picturing the caliphs as little more than tribal shaykhs, and says the Sufyanids largely kept Byzantine and Sasanian personnel and systems: records stayed in their earlier languages and earlier coins stayed in use. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 897}

On Yazid's accession a small group of descendants of leading Companions in Medina opposed it, chiefly al-Husayn and [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr]]. Al-Husayn's revolt was crushed at Karbala, and Ibn al-Zubayr was still refusing recognition when Yazid died. Yazid's son Muawiya II received only limited recognition and was short-lived. The article calls this the temporary collapse of the caliphate and the start of the Second Civil War. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 898}

## The Second Civil War and the Marwanids
Between Yazid's death and the final defeat of Ibn al-Zubayr in 73/692, the caliphate passed to Marwan ibn al-Hakam and then to his son Abd al-Malik, who gradually restored Umayyad control. The article says the transfer was partly obscure, but depended on the support of the Kudaa leader Hassan ibn Malik and on the Kudaa victory over the Qaysi tribes at [[event/battle-of-marj-rahit|Marj Rahit]] in 64/684. Abd al-Malik defeated [[person/musab-ibn-al-zubayr|Musab ibn al-Zubayr]] in Iraq in 71/691 and sent [[person/al-hajjaj-ibn-yusuf|al-Hajjaj]] against Ibn al-Zubayr, who was killed when Mecca was taken the next year. The article names three lasting effects: the Arabs divided for the first time into two hostile tribal groupings, such as Kalb and Qays in Syria; the movement of [[person/al-mukhtar-ibn-abi-ubayd|al-Mukhtar]] showed the potential weight of the [[concept/mawali|mawali]] and later gave rise to the Hashimiyya; and the collapse exposed the loose structure of Sufyanid rule, to which the more centralised Marwanid government was probably a reaction. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 898}

## Abd al-Malik, al-Hajjaj and the mawali
Under Abd al-Malik and his son al-Walid, linked by the governorship of al-Hajjaj over Iraq and the east in 75-95/694-714, the first properly Islamic coinage appeared and government records began to be kept in Arabic. The [[building/dome-of-the-rock|Dome of the Rock]], built by Abd al-Malik in 72/691-2, shows an emerging Islamic architecture, and its inscriptions are the first datable evidence of substantial Qur'anic material and of the word islam as the name of the caliph's religion. The mosque of Damascus and the mosque of the Prophet in Medina also date from the time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 898}

In Iraq al-Hajjaj defeated a Kharijite threat with the help of [[person/al-muhallab-ibn-abi-sufra|al-Muhallab]] and brought Syrian troops, for whom Wasit was built. The revolt of Abd al-Rahman ibn al-Ashath, which took on a religious tone and came close to removing both governor and caliph, was defeated, and government control grew stronger. The article says that reading it as mawali discontent no longer seems tenable, and records that al-Hajjaj sent non-Arab cultivators who had left the land for the garrison cities back to their villages, most likely because of tax revenue. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 898} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 899}

## The Third Civil War and the fall
In the Third Civil War, the article says, family members fought for the caliphate until Marwan II was identified with the interests of Qays. The fighting of 744-747 reduced Syria to the level of the other provinces, damaged Homs, Damascus and Jerusalem, and led Marwan II to hold his court at Harran, not in Syria. The scan does not allow the earlier events of this war to be read. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 900}

The article explains the fall, despite many obscurities, as the meeting of themes developed during Umayyad rule. Yamani soldiers in the Khurasani army, mistrusting Marwan II, revolted against the governor [[person/nasr-ibn-sayyar|Nasr ibn Sayyar]]. The Hashimiyya, backing the claims of the Alids and the ahl al-bayt, drew early support from Khurasan's mixed population, alienated by Umayyad policy toward non-Arabs wishing to accept Islam. After the revolt began in 129/747, [[person/abu-muslim-al-khurasani|Abu Muslim]] allied with the Yamanis, entered Marw and sent [[person/qahtaba-ibn-shabib|Qahtaba ibn Shabib]] west, and the army reached Kufa, where in Rabi II 132/November 749 [[person/abu-al-abbas-al-saffah|Abu al-Abbas al-Saffah]] was proclaimed the first Abbasid caliph. Early the next year the Umayyad defeat on the Upper Zab sent Marwan II to Egypt, where he and a few followers were killed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 900}

## Arabisation and Islamisation
The article places the importance of the period in the fact that lands from Central Asia to the Maghrib and al-Andalus were first significantly Islamised and Arabised. Few people at first regarded themselves as Muslims or spoke Arabic, and by the end both groups had grown considerably. The article prefers "Islamisation" to "conversion", since it covers a social change that may or may not have involved a conscious change of religious identity, and holds that neither process was simply imposed by conquerors or borrowed by subjects: Arabs and non-Arabs were both affected as one society took shape. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 900}

## For Sprouts
The Umayyad caliphate was founded in 661 CE after the Rashidun caliphate, its capital was Damascus, and it ended in 750 CE.

## For Explorers
After the Rashidun caliphate came the Umayyad caliphate, with its centre in Damascus. The Banu Umayya ruled until 750, and their state reached from al-Andalus to Central Asia.

## For Scholars
A caliphate founded in 661 CE and ended in 750 CE, with its capital at Damascus. It followed the Rashidun caliphate under Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan and became a hereditary rule within one family. Its conquests stretched from al-Andalus to Central Asia, and its administration was Arabised in its time. The sources record many events of strife and war among the Muslims, and each needs a precise source. This entry presents the caliphate by its history and centre, and does not judge its caliphs.
