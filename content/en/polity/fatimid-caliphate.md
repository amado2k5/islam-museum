## Overview
The Fatimid caliphate, founded in 909 CE and ended in 1171 CE, with its capital at [[place/cairo|Cairo]]. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1366} {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.290}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam describes it as a dynasty that reigned first in North Africa and then in Egypt, from 297 AH/909 CE until 567 AH/1171 CE. It lists fourteen caliphs, from [[person/ubayd-allah-al-mahdi|Ubayd Allah al-Mahdi]], who reigned 297 to 322 AH, to al-Adid, who reigned 555 to 567 AH. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 888}

## The name and its Ismaili roots
The dynasty takes its name from [[person/fatima|Fatima]], from whom its caliphs traced their descent. The article adds that another Fatima, a daughter of al-Husayn, may have played a part in the choice of the name, as Louis Massignon suggested. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 888} It also reports two views on the earlier use of the word. According to W. Ivanow, the name Fatimiyyun, taken by a Bedouin group in the Syrian desert in the time of al-Tabari, was the first name of the Ismailis. Massignon reminds readers that the word already appears in a pejorative sense in the poet Bashshar b. Burd. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 888}

The article places the origin of the movement in Ismailism, a Shii doctrine at once religious, philosophical and social, whose followers expected a Mahdi descended from the Prophet through Ali and Fatima, in the line of Ismail son of Jafar. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 888}

## The genealogy: positions as the article reports them
The origin of the Fatimids is, in the article's words, still wrapped in obscurity today. The names of the imams between Muhammad b. Ismail and Ubayd Allah were deliberately left unspoken during the period of concealment, and several genealogies circulated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 888} The positions it distinguishes are these.

The traditional Fatimid genealogy makes Ubayd Allah a son of Husayn b. Ahmad, descended from Ismail b. Jafar al-Sadiq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 888}

The general anti-Fatimid tradition holds that he was a descendant of [[person/abdallah-ibn-maymun-al-qaddah|Abd Allah b. Maymun al-Qaddah]], really named Said, who took the name Ubayd Allah and claimed Alid descent and the role of Mahdi only in North Africa. Opponents denied the Alid descent altogether and called the Fatimids impostors, and Ubayd Allah was even presented as the son of a Jew. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 888}

Ismaili sources themselves differ. Their lists of imams are not identical, the name of Ubayd Allah's father varies, and Ubayd Allah is said to have given other versions of his own origin. In a letter to the Ismailis of Yemen he claimed descent from another son of Jafar, Abd Allah. In a conversation reported by a strongly anti-Fatimid author, he claimed a Qaddahi descent. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 889}

Bernard Lewis, as the article presents him, resolves the contradiction through the Ismaili idea of spiritual paternity beside physical paternity. On this reading the Qaddahi line were trustees of the imamate, who veiled the true imams, so that the imamate returned to the Alid family in al-Qaim. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 889}

On the political side, the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasids]] contested the Alid origin of their rivals. Documents signed by jurists and Alids in 402 AH/1011 CE and 444 AH/1052 CE denied it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 889} The article says Sunni historians are in general not well disposed towards the Fatimids, and that hardly any besides al-Maqrizi and Ibn Khaldun accept their Alid descent. It also judges the argument those two give, that the Abbasids would not have pursued Ubayd Allah had they not believed it, to be unconvincing. Supporters called the dynasty Alid, while several Sunni writers called it Ubaydid. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 890}

## Foundation in North Africa
Ubayd Allah lived at Salamiyya in Syria, a centre of Ismaili propaganda. Missionaries had prepared the way, among them [[person/abu-abd-allah-al-shii|Abu Abd Allah al-Shii]], who had worked among the [[group/kutama|Kutama]] Berbers since 280 AH/893 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 890} Ubayd Allah travelled by way of Ramla and Egypt and was held under house arrest at [[place/sijilmasa|Sijilmasa]]. Abu Abd Allah took the capital of the [[polity/aghlabid-emirate|Aghlabids]], [[place/raqqada|Raqqada]] in Rajab 296 AH/March 909 CE, then fetched him, and on 29 Rabi II 297 AH/15 January 910 CE Ubayd Allah entered Raqqada and publicly took the titles Mahdi and Amir al-Muminin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 890}

## The African period
The first four caliphs, Ubayd Allah al-Mahdi, al-Qaim, al-Mansur and [[person/al-muizz-li-din-allah|al-Muizz]], lived in North Africa until 362 AH/973 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 890} The article lists their difficulties: a country divided between Sunnism, chiefly Maliki, and Kharijism, rivalry between Zenata and Sanhaja Berbers, the Kharijite [[polity/rustamid-imamate|Rustamids]] of Tahert and the Alid [[polity/idrisid-dynasty|Idrisids]] of Fez, the [[polity/umayyads-of-cordoba|Umayyads]] of Spain, and the cost of an army and fleet kept ready for a later move east. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 890}

Within the movement, Ubayd Allah had Abu Abd Allah and his brother killed, which led to a Kutama revolt that was put down with much bloodshed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 890} The most serious challenge was the revolt of [[person/abu-yazid-makhlad|Abu Yazid]], which began in 332 AH/943 CE, besieged [[place/al-mahdiyya|al-Mahdiyya]] for a year and ended in 336 AH/947 CE. The article says it brought the dynasty to the brink of ruin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 890}

Ubayd Allah founded al-Mahdiyya on the east coast and made it his capital in 308 AH/920 CE. Expeditions sent against Egypt by al-Qaim failed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 891} Under al-Muizz, his general [[person/jawhar-al-siqilli|Jawhar]] subdued the west as far as the Atlantic. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 891} The article also records dealings over Sicily and with the [[polity/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]], including tribute, embassies, a naval victory in 345 AH/956 CE and a peace treaty in 356 AH/967 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 891}

## The conquest of Egypt and the founding of Cairo
Al-Muizz prepared the conquest of Egypt with skilful propaganda in a country the article describes as in internal chaos and ravaged by famine. Jawhar entered [[place/al-fustat|al-Fustat]] in Shaban 358 AH/July 969 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 891} He removed the Abbasid caliph's name from the sermon but introduced Shii formulae only gradually. To house his troops he built Cairo and laid the first stone of [[building/al-azhar|al-Azhar]] on 24 Jumada I 359 AH/4 April 970 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 891} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 892} Al-Muizz, who had left Ifriqiya in 361 AH/972 CE, entered Cairo in Ramadan 362 AH/June 973 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 892}

## Territorial reach in the east
Mecca and Medina accepted Fatimid suzerainty in 359 AH/970 CE and kept it, with interruptions, until the reign of [[person/al-mustansir-fatimid|al-Mustansir]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 892} Syria, in the article's judgement, was never a solidly Fatimid possession. It lists repeated contests for Damascus and Aleppo with the Qarmatis, Turkish commanders, the [[polity/mirdasid-dynasty|Mirdasids]] and later the [[polity/seljuk-empire|Seljuks]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 892} Under [[person/al-aziz-fatimid|al-Aziz]], Fatimid sovereignty was recognised from the Atlantic to the Red Sea, in the Hijaz, in Yemen and for a time as far as Mosul. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 892} In the fifth century AH, Turcoman and Seljuk commanders took Damascus and Jerusalem, and only Ascalon and a few coastal towns stayed Fatimid in Palestine. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 892}

## Neighbours
In North Africa, the [[polity/zirid-dynasty|Zirid]] ruler al-Muizz b. Badis broke with Cairo in 443 AH/1051 CE and took investiture from Baghdad. The article says the Banu Hilal invasion is attributed to a Fatimid vizier's wish for reprisal. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 892} Sicily became virtually independent, though coins carried the caliph's name for a time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 892} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 893}

With Byzantium, relations alternated between war and truce. A thirty-year peace was signed in 429 AH/1038 CE, with permission to rebuild the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, and friendly relations followed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 893} [[person/al-hakim-bi-amr-allah|Al-Hakim]]'s destruction of that church is named by the article as probably one cause of Basil's ending of trade in 406 AH/1015 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 893}

In the Abbasid east, al-Aziz sought recognition from the [[polity/buyid-dynasty|Buyid]] ruler without result. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 894} Al-Mustansir's missionaries reached as far as Sind, and the Turkish commander al-Basasiri had Fatimid sovereignty recognised at Mosul in 448 AH/1057 CE and at Baghdad for a year in 451 AH/1059 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 894} The Seljuks, as Sunnis, were hostile, and the Fatimid name was dropped from the sermon at Mecca, finally in 473 AH/1088 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 894} In Yemen, the [[polity/sulayhid-dynasty|Sulayhid]] dynasty of Sanaa, ruling from 429 AH/1038 CE to 534 AH/1139 CE, gave fervent support. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 894}

## The Fatimids and the Crusades
When the Crusaders reached northern Syria, the Fatimids held no Syrian territory. The article calls them less interested in the struggle than the Turkish amirs, and says an alliance project of 1098 came to nothing. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 894} The Franks took Jerusalem in July 1099, Tyre in 518 AH/1124 CE and Ascalon in 548 AH/1153 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 894}

## The end of the dynasty
Rivalry between the viziers [[person/shawar|Shawar]] and Dirgham led to intervention by Nur al-Din and the king of Jerusalem. [[person/asad-al-din-shirkuh|Shirkuh]] and his nephew [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Saladin]] came with a Syrian army, Shirkuh became vizier, and Saladin succeeded him and ended the caliphate in 567 AH/1171 CE, restoring Sunnism and Abbasid sovereignty. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 894} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 895}

## Succession, viziers and the court
In the Ismaili view, as the article presents it, the imamate passes by designation, nass, from father to son, not by election, and the nominee need not be the eldest. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 895} Several caliphs came to the throne as children, so that regents, viziers or generals often held real power. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 895} The designation of [[person/al-mustali-fatimid|al-Mustali]] over his elder brother [[person/nizar-ibn-al-mustansir|Nizar]] caused a lasting schism in the Ismaili community. After [[person/al-amir-fatimid|al-Amir]]'s death in 524 AH/1130 CE, [[person/al-hafiz-fatimid|al-Hafiz]] acted first as regent and then proclaimed himself caliph, and a Tayyibi schism also arose. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 895} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 896}

The first to hold the title of vizier in Egypt was [[person/yaqub-ibn-killis|Yaqub b. Killis]] under al-Aziz. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 895} From the time of [[person/badr-al-jamali|Badr al-Jamali]], viziers held full powers as Viziers of the Sword. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 895} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 896} Several viziers were Christians, and Jews held high posts, though the article says it does not seem that Jews became viziers without embracing Islam. The career was dangerous, with many viziers deposed or killed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 896}

## Disturbances and famine
The article lists revolts under al-Hakim and al-Mustansir, clashes among Berber, Turkish, Daylami and black Sudanese troops, and disturbances in 411 AH/1020 CE when some missionaries wanted the divinity of al-Hakim recognised. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 896} Famines struck in 414 to 415 AH and, worst of all, under al-Mustansir, when a famine of seven years from 457 AH/1065 CE to 464 AH/1072 CE is said, citing al-Maqrizi, to have reduced people to eating dogs, cats and even human flesh. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 896} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 897}

## Religious policy
The article describes the early caliphs as facing an Ismaili community whose hopes for the Mahdi had not been realised, and says state policy led them to keep the basic duties of Islam beside the inner meaning. It judges the caliphate in general opportunist and moderate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 897} The jurist [[person/al-qadi-al-numan|al-Numan]], later his sons, and the vizier Ibn Killis worked to establish Fatimid law, and the [[building/dar-al-hikma-cairo|Dar al-Hikma]] of al-Hakim served as a centre of teaching. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 897}

Policy towards Sunnis fluctuated between tolerance and strictness. The tarawih prayer was forbidden at times and allowed again by al-Hakim in 399 AH/1009 CE, and [[person/al-zahir-fatimid|al-Zahir]] expelled Maliki jurists from Egypt in 416 AH/1025 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 897} The article says Christians and Jews held a relatively favourable position throughout, and cites the Armenian Abu Salih on the building of churches. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 897} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 898}

## State, economy and culture
The article describes a strongly centralised administration working through diwans, with a strict hierarchy of civil and military officials. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 898} Egypt in general enjoyed great prosperity, from taxes, trade, Nubian gold and the Nile flood. Textiles led industry, followed by glass, pottery, metalwork and other crafts. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 898} Trade reached India through the Red Sea, and also Italy, Sicily, North Africa and Spain, and Jews played a large part. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 899}

Cultural life flourished in both periods. Poets such as [[person/ibn-hani-al-andalusi|Ibn Hani]] wrote for the court, al-Numan composed legal and historical works, and al-Mahdiyya and Sabra were built. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 899} In Egypt the article lists works on doctrine, philosophy and science and compares the era's curiosity to Europe's eighteenth century. It names [[person/ibn-al-haytham|Ibn al-Haytham]] among the scholars, together with the astronomer Ali b. Yunus and physicians. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 899} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 900}

## Assessment in the article
The author, citing G. Wiet, calls the period one of the most absorbing in the history of Muslim Egypt, notes that Sunni writers have not always described the dynasty with understanding, and sees a history of contrasts, with periods of greatness and also of famine, factional struggle and a disastrous end. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 900}

## According to al-Maqrizi
Al-Maqrizi says that the Fatimid state began with Abu Abd Allah al-Shi'i, who became one of the leading companions of the mission in the Maghrib. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1005}

He says that al-Mahdi reached Rafada at the end of Rabi' II 297 AH. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1366}

He says that Cairo became the residence of the caliph with his household and his closest people, and that al-Mu'izz lodged in his palace there. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1001} {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.2174}

He says that the state ended when the Ghuzz came from Syria and Saladin Yusuf ibn Ayyub took the kingdom of Egypt; he gives al-Qadi al-Fadil's notes for 567 AH. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.290}

## For Sprouts
The Fatimids founded the city of Cairo in Egypt and built the noble al-Azhar mosque to teach learning and reading.

## For Explorers
The Fatimid Caliphate was an Islamic caliphate that originated in North Africa and moved its capital to Egypt after founding Cairo in 358 AH. Its leaders established al-Azhar Mosque and Dar al-Ilm, and trade, glassmaking, textiles, Arabic calligraphy, and grand libraries flourished under their rule.

## For Scholars
An Ismaili caliphate established in North Africa in 297 AH (909 CE) by Ubayd Allah al-Mahdi, which expanded into Egypt, the Levant, and the Hijaz, making Cairo its capital from 362 AH (973 CE) until its end under Salah al-Din in 567 AH (1171 CE). Notable for establishing al-Azhar, Dar al-Ilm, and vibrant Red Sea and Mediterranean trade routes, it left a distinctive legacy in architecture, ceramics, and arts.
