## Overview
The Seljuks, in Arabic Saldjuks, were a Turkish dynasty of medieval Islam. At the height of their power in the eleventh and twelfth centuries they ruled, directly or through vassal princes, a wide area of Western Asia from Transoxania and Khwarazm in the east to Anatolia, Syria and the Hijaz in the west. From the core of the Great Seljuk empire, junior lines of the family held on in Kirman until near the end of the twelfth century, in Syria until its early years, and in Anatolia, as the [[polity/rum-seljuk-sultanate|sultanate of Rum]], until the beginning of the fourteenth. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose account this page follows, treats them as a change in the course of the central and eastern Islamic lands, and notes that the nature and extent of that change are disputed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1002} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1003}

## The world they entered
The Seljuks appeared only a few decades after the practical and moral authority of the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid caliphs]] at Baghdad had reached its lowest point under the tutelage of the Shi'i [[polity/buyid-dynasty|Buyids]]. Many small Arab principalities in Iraq and northern Syria were Shi'i too, and the gravest threat came from the Isma'ili [[polity/fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid caliphate]] in Egypt and southern Syria, then still expansionist, whose capital Cairo was beginning to outshine Baghdad. The article judges that the Seljuk settlement in Persia, Iraq and northern Syria reversed this march of political Shi'ism, and that it was some four centuries before Shi'ism could again change the religious character of Persia and eastern Anatolia, through the Safavids. The Seljuks were Sunnis of the Hanafi school. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1002}

## Origins
They began as a family group of the Oghuz Turks, from the Kinik tribe. In the tenth century their leader Seljuk held the post of military commander beside the Oghuz chief, and after a quarrel withdrew with his herds to the lower Syr Darya, where he died and where his family and followers became Muslim. On the borders of [[place/khwarizm|Khwarazm]] and Transoxania the Seljuks hired out their cavalry to the Samanids, the Karakhanids and local rulers. Their early leaders were Seljuk's sons Musa, Mika'il and Arslan Isra'il, and then Mika'il's sons [[person/tughril-beg|Tughril Beg]] and [[person/chaghri-beg|Chaghri Beg]]. After a defeat by [[person/mahmud-ghaznawi|Mahmud of Ghazna]] in 428/1029, Oghuz bands were scattered through Khurasan and northern Persia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1004}

By 426/1035 the Seljuks were asking Mahmud's son [[person/masud-ibn-mahmud|Mas'ud]] for pasturage at Nasa, Farawa and [[place/sarakhs|Sarakhs]]. Their lightly armed, mobile horsemen proved more than a match for the heavier Ghaznavid army, and with Mas'ud's defeat at [[event/battle-of-dandanqan-1040|Dandanqan]] in 431/1040 they overran Khurasan and then the rest of Persia. The article doubts that the numbers were large, and suggests, from the sixteen thousand Oghuz a Ghaznavid historian places at the battle, something like sixty-four thousand Turkmen entering Khurasan once their families are counted. It credits their success to leadership and to a steppe background not wholly unfamiliar with towns. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1004}

## Tughril and the caliph
[[place/nishapur|Nishapur]] was taken for good in 431/1040. Tughril turned west against the Daylami and Kurdish princes, making [[place/rayy|Rayy]] his base, while Chaghri held the east from [[place/marw|Marw]], which stayed the eastern Seljuk capital through Sanjar's time. In 447/1055 Tughril marched on [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] and ended the weak rule of the last Buyid there; other Buyid princes survived only as Seljuk puppets in Fars. The caliph al-Qa'im gave him honorific titles and robes of the Abbasid black and addressed him formally as sultan, a title already in informal use. Tughril pressed to marry a daughter of the caliph, and the article places this inside the new division of authority: the caliph as spiritual and moral head of Sunni Islam, the Seljuk sultan as its secular ruler. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1005} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1006}

The article adds that the sultans never saw themselves as despots over a single state. They had risen as war leaders of Oghuz tribesmen and depended at first on tribal followers, unlike the Samanids, who relied on slave troops, or the Ghaznavids, who kept a salaried army. They went on observing tribal custom where it did not clash with their new role, and shared out provinces as appanages among male relatives. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1005}

## Alp Arslan and Malik Shah
Tughril died childless, and [[person/alp-arslan|Alp Arslan]], Chaghri's son, became sultan of all the Seljuk lands against the claim of another relative, a result the article attributes to the commanders and to his vizier [[person/nizam-al-mulk|Nizam al-Mulk]] preferring strong unified rule. His reign, 455-65/1063-73, and that of his son [[person/malikshah|Malik Shah]], 465-85/1073-92, mark the height of the Great Seljuk sultanate. The sultans kept hold of their far-flung lands by constant travel and campaigning, and the pressure of the Turkmen and their herds on Persia's farmland was eased by sending them west against the Christian princes of the Caucasus and Anatolia and against the Fatimids in Syria. Persia and Iraq enjoyed considerable agricultural and commercial prosperity. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1007}

Behind both sultans stood Nizam al-Mulk, whose influence was so pervasive that Ibn al-Athir calls his thirty years in office the Nizamiyya state. Alp Arslan's great reputation as a Muslim hero rests on his defeat of the Byzantine emperor Romanus Diogenes at [[event/battle-of-manzikert-463|Manzikert]] in 463/1071. The article stresses that there are no signs this was part of a systematic plan to conquer Asia Minor; he imposed tribute and asked for the return of some border towns. The raids into Anatolia by the sons of Qutlumush, Sulayman and Mansur, who by 474/1081 had reached Iznik, were private ventures of cousins of the sultan, not official policy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1007}

Nizam al-Mulk hoped to mould the eighteen-year-old Malik Shah into the ideal ruler of his treatise on statecraft, the Siyasat-nama, and to build a centralised administration, but older Turkish attitudes persisted. His uncle Kawurd, long amir of Kirman, took up arms to claim the supreme power as the senior member of the family. Malik Shah rejected the claim on the ground that power now passed from father to son, and won at Hamadan through his professional slave soldiers, not tribal troops; the Turkmen of the northern and eastern provinces began to feel alienated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1007} Malik Shah excluded the caliph from secular affairs in Iraq, married a daughter to al-Muqtadi in 480/1087, and was beginning to make Baghdad his winter capital when he died. In his last years he secured the Friday sermon for the Abbasids from the Sharifs of Mecca and began to extend Seljuk power to the Hijaz, Yemen and al-Ahsa, plans cut short by his death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1007} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1008}

## After Malik Shah
Malik Shah's death in 485/1092 ended the best days of the Great Seljuks. Young princes and their ambitious mothers fought over the throne with no restraining hand like Nizam al-Mulk's, and his sons Berk-yaruk and [[person/muhammad-tapar|Muhammad Tapar]] fought each other until Berk-yaruk's death in 498/1105. The Crusaders' arrival in Syria in 1098 and Palestine the next year was not felt as a major threat to the Seljuk positions. But the strife forced the claimants to buy the support of the slave commanders and Turkmen begs, Turkmen principalities headed by atabegs began to form in provinces such as Khuzistan, Diyar Bakr and al-Jazira, more land was granted as iqta', and, the article notes, the weakening of central control let the Isma'ili group known as the Assassins strengthen its hold on strongholds taken towards the end of Malik Shah's reign. Muhammad, undisputed sultan in 498-511/1105-18, regained a good measure of control. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1008}

## Sanjar
[[person/sanjar|Sanjar]], whom Muhammad had left as viceroy in Khurasan, became the senior member of the dynasty on Muhammad's death and took the role of supreme sultan; his nephews who held western Persia and Iraq were reduced to vassals in all but title after he defeated one of them near Sawa in 513/1119. He campaigned against the Isma'ilis after the death of Hasan-i Sabbah in 518/1124 without seriously harming their power. Beyond the Oxus he kept the Karakhanids as vassals, placing a ruler on the throne at Samarqand in 495/1102, and put the Ghaznavid [[person/bahram-shah-ghaznavi|Bahram Shah]] on the throne at Ghazna in 510/1117, which spread a lasting Seljuk cultural influence there. The vassal Khwarazmshah [[person/atsiz-khwarazmshah|Atsiz]] rebelled in 533/1138. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1008} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1009}

The turning point came with the arrival of a new power from northern China, the pagan Kara Khitay. In a hard-fought battle on the Qatwan steppe east of Samarqand, the Muslim forces were routed, and Sanjar and the Karakhanid khan fled south; his prestige was badly damaged. Within Khurasan the cost of these wars meant heavier taxes, including on the nomadic Oghuz, who rose in open revolt in 548/1153. Sanjar himself was captured by the [[group/ghuzz|Ghuzz]] and kept in close tutelage, escaped after a long confinement, and died at the age of seventy-one in 552/1157. The commanders in Khurasan offered the sultanate to a Karakhanid, and the article reads this as the end of effective Seljuk rule there: to contemporaries it seemed the end of an epoch, for Sanjar had ruled, as malik and sultan, for over sixty years. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1009}

## The later lines
The line of sultans in western Persia and Iraq is dated by the article 511-90/1118-94; the Seljuks of Kirman are the subject of [[polity/seljuk-kirman|their own record]]. The caliphs recovered much independence in the twelfth century as the Great Seljuk sultans declined, a revival the article calls transitory, since both Abbasids and Seljuks were soon swept away by new forces from the east, notably the Khwarazmshahs and the Mongols. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1002} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1005}

## What the Seljuks changed
The article names several lasting effects. The Seljuk invasion was the start of a long movement of Turkish peoples from Inner Asia into the Middle East, with effects on land use, social organisation and the ethnic make-up of the lands affected, and with new systems of land tenure such as the iqta'. It consolidated Sunni authority as the leading ethos of rule in the central Islamic lands, and gave lasting shape to the idea of the caliph as spiritual head and the sultan as executive ruler. The Turks, unlettered at first, took over the existing Persian and Arab administration and its viziers, among them al-Kunduri, Nizam al-Mulk and al-Tughra'i, and having no literary heritage of their own took over Persia's, so that Persian became the language of administration and culture in Persia and Anatolia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1002} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1003}

In Anatolia the sultanate of Rum took over most of the former Byzantine and Armenian lands, and the article reports that the name Turchia first appears specifically in western European usage for this region at the time of the Third Crusade. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1003}

## How the dynasty has been judged
The article devotes a long passage to disagreement among historians. Nineteenth-century European views held that the Turks had contributed nothing to Islamic civilisation, and the article traces a long-standing contemptuous view of the Turks as unoriginal soldiers and administrators in parts of the Arab world, shaped by four centuries of Ottoman rule. Turkish historians of the twentieth century answered by presenting the Seljuks as heroes and founding fathers of Muslim Turkish culture and the precursors of the modern republic, a view the article ties to the building of a nation state and treats as itself the subject of study. It also mentions the argument, put by some nationalist historians, that the Turkish entry into Anatolia was a planned strategy from early in the century and not a series of plunder raids. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1003}
