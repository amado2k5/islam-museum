## Overview
The Almohads, in Arabic al-Muwahhidun, were the followers of a reform movement whose central teaching was the divine unity, [[concept/tawhid|tawhid]]. In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries they ruled an empire they had founded in North Africa and Spain, the first to bring the whole [[place/maghreb|Maghrib]] under a single state. Their capital was [[place/marrakesh|Marrakesh]], and their line of caliphs descended from [[person/abd-al-mumin-ibn-ali|'Abd al-Mu'min]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 855} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 856}

## Origins among the Masmuda
The movement was founded by the Mahdi [[person/ibn-tumart|Ibn Tumart]]. The article's author, M. Shatzmiller, places its political beginning not at his return from the East in 510/1116 or 511/1117 but at his return to his own Berber people, the [[group/masmuda|Masmuda]], in 517/1123. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 855} A mission first proclaimed as moral reform became a movement to overthrow the [[polity/almoravid-dynasty|Almoravids]] once he had settled at [[place/tinmal|Tinmal]], an isolated village in the Atlas. There, according to al-Marrakushi, he taught the Berbers and composed for them a creed in their own language; those who could pronounce the profession of unity in Berber replaced the preachers and imams of the mosques. Shatzmiller links the rise of the movement to the social, religious and intellectual tensions of Morocco at the time, and cites W. Montgomery Watt's question whether opposition to the Maliki school did not express the wish of the Berbers to assert themselves beside the Arabs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 856}

## The making of an empire
Military conquest began only after Ibn Tumart died in 524/1130. 'Abd al-Mu'min, chosen by the Mahdi as his successor although he was of [[group/zanata|Zanata]] origin, was proclaimed caliph in that year and directed the campaigns of the Masmuda tribes. The occupation of Morocco took some twenty years, from a failed attack on Marrakesh in 523/1129 to [[event/almohad-conquest-of-marrakesh-1147|its capture in 542/1147]]; the Almohads kept to the shelter of the mountains and avoided battle in open country. Shatzmiller calls the conquest brutal. Massacres and persecution of civilians led [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] to accuse the Almohads of having killed thousands of good Muslims, and a purge was carried out among the Almohads themselves. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 856}

'Abd al-Mu'min then built up an army at Sale and marched east. The central Maghrib and [[place/ifriqiya|Ifriqiya]] had been weakened by the incursions of nomadic Arab tribes and by Norman attacks on the coast, and the conquest was quick: Algiers, [[place/bejaia|Bougie]] and Constantine fell in 547/1152, and [[place/tunis|Tunis]], Mahdiyya, Sfax and Tripoli in 554/1159. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 856}

## Height of power
The empire reached its greatest material and cultural success under the three successors of 'Abd al-Mu'min, who died in 558/1163: [[person/abu-yaqub-yusuf-almohad|Abu Ya'qub Yusuf]], 558-80/1163-84, [[person/yaqub-al-mansur-al-muwahhidi|Abu Yusuf Ya'qub al-Mansur]], 580-95/1184-99, and [[person/muhammad-al-nasir-almohad|Muhammad al-Nasir]], 595-610/1199-1213. The army of Masmuda and Zanata Berbers was backed by a large fleet with dockyards on the Atlantic and the Mediterranean. [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Saladin]] asked for the help of that fleet during the siege of Acre in 585/1189; al-Mansur refused, though [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]] reports that he later sent 190 ships. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 856}

In Spain Almohad authority was never complete. Cadiz and Jerez recognised it in 541/1146, [[place/seville|Seville]] was taken in 542/1147, and the main effort came under Abu Ya'qub Yusuf, with [[event/almohad-wars-iberia-1172|an offensive into eastern Spain in 568/1172]]. The decisive victory over Castile was won at [[event/battle-of-alarcos-591|Alarcos in 591/1195]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 856} In North Africa the Banu Ghaniya, princes of the Almoravid house based in the Balearic Islands, extended their power over Ifriqiya and the central Maghrib until al-Nasir's army dislodged them in 602/1205. To fill their ranks the Almohads in 583/1187 moved nomadic Arab tribes into Morocco by force. Shatzmiller holds that this upset the balance between nomads and settled people and set in motion the rise of the [[polity/marinid-dynasty|Marinids]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 857}

## Decline and end
The defeat at [[event/al-uqab-609|Las Navas de Tolosa in 609/1212]], at the hands of a coalition of Spanish kings and knights, loosened the Almohad hold on Spain and opened the second period of their history. It was marked by rebellion, the defection of allies and, under the caliph [[person/al-mamun-almohad|al-Ma'mun]], the [[event/almohad-doctrine-repudiation-626|repudiation of the Almohad doctrine itself]]. Eight more caliphs of the house of 'Abd al-Mu'min followed one another between 610/1213 and 668/1269. Marrakesh nevertheless remained a centre of art and learning. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 857}

The provinces last won were the first lost, and the practice of setting members of the family over provinces with their own troops and revenues hastened the break-up. The [[polity/hafsid-dynasty|Hafsid]] governor of Ifriqiya declared independence in 627/1229-30, and the ruler of Tlemcen, of the [[polity/zayyanid-tlemcen|Banu 'Abd al-Wad]], in 637/1239. The Marinids ended the state by taking Marrakesh in 668/1269 and Tinmal in 676/1276. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 857}

## Government and religious policy
Ibn Tumart had ordered the community at Tinmal in ranks: an inner council of his own household, a committee of ten and another of fifty tribal representatives. Apart from the Council of Fifty, which stayed in the caliph's retinue, these structures were dropped, and with 'Abd al-Mu'min the government took more usual forms; by naming his son heir he founded the dynasty. He trained his own officials, who had religious schooling and were also swimmers, archers and horsemen. In each province a governor of the ruling family was assisted by an Almohad shaykh. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 857}

The official aim, from the Mahdi and 'Abd al-Mu'min onward, was to replace the Maliki school with the new doctrine. But the Mahdi had left no revised code of law, so Maliki law went on governing daily life and Maliki jurists, above all in Spain, went on working unhindered; at least three chief judges under the Almohads were known Malikis. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 858} The Almohads also reformed the coinage, introducing the square dirham and a gold dinar of new weight whose dimensions later dynasties kept. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 858}

## Christians and Jews
The Christians in Almohad Morocco were at first only the militia serving in the army, recruited mostly from prisoners and, from 625/1227, from free Castilians and Catalans; al-Ma'mun had a church built for them in Marrakesh. Letters between several popes and the caliphs survive. Franciscan missionaries who came to Marrakesh in 1220 and to [[place/ceuta|Ceuta]] in 1227 were put to death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 858}

The Jews, who lived in every town of the empire, suffered a first wave of persecution during the conquest, in 536-43/1141-8. Shatzmiller reports that letters of Jewish merchants found in the Cairo Geniza confirm that 'Abd al-Mu'min offered them the choice of conversion to Islam or death, and that massacres took place at [[place/sijilmasa|Sijilmasa]], Marrakesh and [[place/fes|Fez]]; many left for Christian Spain, Sicily, Italy and the East, among them the future philosopher Maimonides. A second wave came under Ya'qub al-Mansur, who according to al-Marrakushi made Jews and converts wear distinctive dress. His reign also saw the temporary imprisonment of [[person/ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd]] and the burning of his books. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 858}

## Economy
In North Africa 'Abd al-Mu'min brought the holding of land under one system in 554/1159 and laid the land tax on arable land. A list drawn up for Fez under al-Nasir counts 3,490 weaving shops, 86 tanneries, 116 dye-works, 472 water-mills, 400 paper-mills, 785 mosques and 93 public baths. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 859} The period coincided with the great growth of European trade with North Africa. Treaties of peace and commerce were signed with Pisa, Marseilles and other cities, and Ceuta dealt regularly with Barcelona and Montpellier; wax, leather and furs from Fez, cumin and sugar from Marrakesh and dates from Sijilmasa reached Flanders. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 859}

## Learning and building
The philosophers [[person/ibn-tufayl|Ibn Tufayl]] and Ibn Rushd lived and wrote at Marrakesh under the patronage of Abu Ya'qub Yusuf and his son. The caliphs came to protect Sufism, and the two central figures of Maghribi Sufism, [[person/abu-madyan|Abu Madyan]] and Ibn Mashish, had their favour. [[person/ibn-sabin|Ibn Sab'in]] wrote at Ceuta his answers to the questions of [[person/frederick-ii|Frederick II]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 859}

Great building was confined to the first fifty years. Mosques at Taza, Marrakesh, [[building/great-mosque-tinmal|Tinmal]], Seville, [[place/rabat|Rabat]] and Fez and the fortifications of Rabat survive in part; the palaces the chroniclers describe do not. Shatzmiller describes an architecture that joins a puritan plainness of ornament to walls and minarets of extraordinary size, built by craftsmen from Spain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 859}

## Why it failed
The article sets out several explanations and adopts none as final. Some historians hold that the capture of the state by one dynasty estranged the Berbers who had made it; another, that its wealth came from an empire and was not produced in Morocco. In Spain the regime lacked the Berber support it had in Africa, met the opposition of the Maliki scholars, and was seen as a foreign military occupation. The doctrine itself, Shatzmiller concludes, never took root: it offered neither a body of law to replace the Maliki one nor an answer to the spiritual needs that Sufism was then meeting, and the Maliki school remained dominant. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 860}
