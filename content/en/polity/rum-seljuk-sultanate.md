## Overview
The sultanate of Rum was the Seljuk state of Anatolia, built around Konya, which the sources call Iconium. The Encyclopaedia of Islam dates it from about 483 to 707 AH, roughly 1081 to 1307 CE, and treats it as a junior line of the wider [[polity/seljuk-empire|Seljuk family]], founded by descendants of Qutlumush son of Arslan Isra'il through his son Sulayman. The Seljuks of Anatolia called their land Rum and themselves the Seljuks of Rum, at least in informal usage. The medieval Arabic historian Ibn Khaldun, whose shorter account is kept below, describes the same house as the state of Qutlumush and his sons, kings of Konya and the land of Rum. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1014} {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.1331-1333}

## From the sons of Qutlumush to a principality
The article first meets the family soon after [[person/alp-arslan|Alp Arslan]]'s victory at [[event/battle-of-manzikert-463|Manzikert]], in the activities in Anatolia of the four sons of [[person/qutlumush-ibn-arslan-israil|Qutlumush]]. The descendants of one of them, [[person/sulayman-ibn-qutlumush|Sulayman]], went on to found the sultanate of Rum. The article stresses that, whatever some modern Turkish nationalist historians have argued, these activities look like individual enterprise. It adds that Seljuk court historiography of the seventh/thirteenth century later claimed that Sultan [[person/malikshah|Malik Shah]] had personally given the land of Rum to his cousins, but that in fact the sons of Qutlumush had earlier been under official surveillance and escaped to the loosely governed Anatolian frontier after Alp Arslan's death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1014}

Official disapproval continued under Malik Shah, who had Mansur son of Qutlumush killed. Sulayman escaped, but died in battle in 479/1086 while contending with his kinsman [[person/tutush|Tutush]] for control of Aleppo. Meanwhile Turkmen bands raided as far as the Sea of Marmara and the Aegean, and Sulayman reached Nicaea, modern Iznik, in the far north-west; the article takes this as the rough beginning of a Seljuk principality in Anatolia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1014}

After Malik Shah's death, Sulayman's son [[person/qilij-arslan-i|Kilij Arslan I]], who ruled 485-500/1092-1107, escaped from captivity and was raised to the leadership of the Turkmens of north-western Anatolia. He moved his capital to [[place/konya|Konya]] only after the armies of the First Crusade recaptured Nicaea in 1097. The article notes that Malik Shah had no definite plan to overrun Anatolia, though he regarded himself as head of all the Turks, including the Turkmens, and had been ready to reach an agreement with the Byzantine emperor Alexius Comnenus, whose empire suffered from Turkmen raiding. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1014}

## Rival principalities
The young principality at Konya was only one of several Turkmen beyliks of central and eastern Anatolia. The article names the Saltukids, the Mengujekids at Erzincan and neighbouring towns, the [[polity/shah-arman-akhlat|Shah-Armanids]] at Akhlat, west of Lake Van, and the [[polity/artukids|Artukids]] at Diyarbakir. The most serious rival, because the nearest, was the [[polity/danishmendid-emirate|Danishmendids]] of north-central Anatolia, who held the northern route through Sivas, Kayseri and Ankara and who, from 529/1134-5, bore the title of Malik granted by the Abbasid caliph for their zeal in raiding the Byzantines. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1014}

When Kilij Arslan I fell in battle the Seljuk lands were briefly divided. The First Crusade, a revived Byzantine power and the containment policy of the Great Seljuk sultans in Persia and Iraq had confined the Turkish groups to the interior of Asia Minor. Rukn al-Din [[person/masud-i-rumi|Mas'ud I]], who ruled 510-51/1117-56, sometimes cooperated with the Danishmendids against the Byzantines and the Armenians of Cilicia and Little Armenia. After the death of the Danishmendid Muhammad son of Gumushtigin in 536/1142, the Seljuks of Rum gradually gained the upper hand in central Anatolia. Mas'ud only narrowly survived a Byzantine attack on Konya led by Manuel I Comnenus in 541/1147, saved because the emperor heard that the Second Crusade had appeared further west. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1014}

## Myriocephalon
Mas'ud's son 'Izz al-Din [[person/qilij-arslan-ii|Kilij Arslan II]], who ruled from 551/1156 to about 581/1185, took revenge by inflicting a severe defeat on Manuel's army at the pass of Myriocephalon near Lake Egirdir in 572/1176, and so preventing a second attack on his capital. The article rates this victory as significant in its long effects as Manzikert had been. The Frankish and Byzantine project to recover Anatolia collapsed and Greek hopes of reconquest faded, a process sealed by the capture of Constantinople by the Fourth Crusade in 1204, after which Byzantine control in Anatolia shrank to the region around Nicaea and the principality of Trebizond. The Seljuk sultanate and the general Turkish presence were now, in the article's words, an unassailable reality. Kilij Arslan II, like Alp Arslan after Manzikert, is described as restrained towards the Greeks, content to unite the Turks of central and eastern Anatolia under his own rule. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1014}

## Titles and offices
The article says neither the Muslim nor the Byzantine sources are very informative on Seljuk royal practice at this time. Greek writers called Sulayman a sultan, but this can only reflect informal usage, since neither the Great Seljuk sultan nor the caliph can have conferred the title. Twelfth-century writers seem to call the rulers at Konya Maliks more often than sultans. From coins, inscriptions and quasi-official documents the article reports that Kilij Arslan called himself sultan of the Arabs and the 'Ajam, the latter meaning the Turks, and later sultan of the land of Rum and of the Armenians, Franks and Syria. The title Ghazi, used by the Danishmendids and the eastern Turkmen princes, does not appear among the Seljuks of Rum. Young princes had atabegs at their side into the thirteenth century, but in Rum the office never gained the importance, or the chance to seize real power, that it had in the Seljuk lands further east. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1014}

## Reaching the sea
Myriocephalon eventually opened the Mediterranean to the Seljuks. The article names Antalya, taken by 601/1207, and Alanya, which was named for Sultan 'Ala' al-Din Kay Kubadh I, who ruled 616-34/1220-37. Near the end of the sixth/twelfth century a Turkmen amir of Tokat took Samsun, bringing Turkish arms to the Black Sea, and 'Izz al-Din Kay Kawus I, ruling 608-16/1211-20, conquered Sinop from the Comneni of Trebizond in 611/1214. The Anatolian Turks, formerly confined to the interior plateau, now had access to the seas. The sultanate profited from its position across the north-south trade routes and from trade with the Venetians at Antalya, and so from Venetian commerce with Alexandria. Trade between the Black Sea ports and the Crimean entrepot of Sughdaq tended to be controlled by Trebizond, but from 1225 to 1239 Kay Kubadh I was able to establish his suzerainty over Sughdaq through Kastamonu and the Black Sea ports. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1014} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1015}

## Division, apogee and the Khwarazmians
Near the end of his life Kilij Arslan II, following the old Turkish principle of patrimonial division, shared his kingdom among ten sons and some other male relatives. A period of succession disputes and weakness followed. In 1190 the emperor Frederick Barbarossa and his crusading army plundered Konya while Kilij Arslan took refuge in the citadel. The crisis was overcome by the start of the seventh/thirteenth century, and the first forty years of that century were the high point of the sultanate under rulers such as Kay Kawus I and Kay Kubadh I. An agreement between Kay Kawus I and Theodore Lascaris brought half a century of peace with Byzantium, and the Seljuks turned their military effort to Cilicia, Syria, al-Jazira and Trebizond. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1015}

The eastern borders were now threatened by the expansion of the [[polity/khwarazmian-empire|Khwarazm Shahs]], who first appeared in eastern Anatolia in 623/1226, leading [[person/kayqubad-i|Kay Kubadh I]] to ally with the [[polity/ayyubid-dynasty|Ayyubids]] of Syria and Diyarbakir, who were equally menaced. The article sees him at the height of his power, master of most of Anatolia and suzerain of the smaller Christian Greek, Armenian and Georgian states around it. It cites as evidence of his resources and style the palaces he built, among them [[building/kubadabad-palace|Kubadabad]] on Lake Beysehir and the Kay Kubadhiyya near Kayseri, both only then being properly excavated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1015}

## The Mongols
The Mongols appeared in eastern Anatolia in 640/1242-3. Sultan Ghiyath al-Din Kay Khusraw II had only just put down, with difficulty, a prolonged rebellion in the east and north that began in 638/1240 under a Turkish popular holy man, [[person/baba-ishaq|Baba Ishaq]], who claimed to be a prophetic messenger. Kay Khusraw II, who ruled 634-44/1237-46, is described as distinctly less capable than his predecessors. He gathered an army that included Armenians, Greeks and Franks, but the Mongol commander Baydju defeated it at [[event/battle-of-kose-dag-641|Kose Dagh]] near Sivas in 641/1243. The Mongols let the sultan keep his throne at Konya, but as a vassal liable to heavy tribute. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1015}

The client state was then divided over the throne of the dead sultan, which officials and commanders contested on behalf of his minor sons. Only in 659/1261 did Rukn al-Din Kay Kawus II establish a measure of power in Konya. Until his execution in 676/1277 by the Il-Khan [[person/abaga-ibn-hulagu|Abaqa]], after a Mongol defeat by the invading Mamluk army of [[person/baybars|Baybars]], real power was exercised by the Parwana Mu'in al-Din Sulayman, son of a former Seljuk vizier, who worked closely with the Mongols and tried to reduce tensions between incoming Turco-Mongol soldiers and the established Turkmen groups. The article marks his death as the end of the sultans' semi-independence. The [[polity/ilkhanate|Il-Khans]] now ruled directly through Persian and Turco-Mongol officials and commanders, the Seljuk forces were disbanded and swelled the ranks of the discontented and of brigands, and Perso-Mongol fiscal practices were introduced. Mongol taxation was heavy, yet the article sees little visible harm to the economy: farming and foreign trade stayed buoyant and public and charitable buildings continued to be endowed and built. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1015}

## The end of the line
The Il-Khans sent expeditions to put down local rulers such as the [[polity/qaramanid-beylik|Karamanids]] and Ashrafids and to press their financial demands, among them the campaign of Gaykhatu in 690-1/1291-2 that spread terror and devastation through southern Anatolia as far as Mentese and the Aegean. Ambitious Turco-Mongol commanders also revolted, adding to a mood of disintegration. The weak sultan 'Ala' al-Din Kay Kubadh III was executed by [[person/ghazan-khan|Ghazan Khan]] in 702/1303, and the sultanate disappeared in obscure circumstances in 707/1307. After a period of control by the Il-Khanid commanders of the Chobanid family, Anatolia entered the age of the beyliks, a fragmentation into petty principalities that the article compares to the taifa kingdoms of eleventh-century Muslim Spain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1015}

Some descendants of the Seljuks of Rum seem to have survived. A Kilij Arslan son of Lutfi son of Sawchi, possibly a Seljuk, governed Alanya in the 1460s before the Ottoman annexation of 876/1471. A later Ottoman historian, 'Ali, says that after the deposition of the last Seljuk, Ghiyath al-Din Mas'ud III, Ghazan gave Sinop to a Seljuk prince, Ghazi Celebi, who campaigned from there against the Greeks of Trebizond and the Genoese on the Black Sea. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1015}

## Religion and learning
The article judges that the Turkmens who entered Anatolia no doubt brought with them traces of an Inner Asian shamanistic past, but became in large measure firm adherents of the Hanafi school, the madhhab of the Turks. It sees little original theological or legal writing in Rum until well into the seventh/thirteenth century, the Turks of Anatolia drawing instead on the Hanafi scholarship of Khurasan and Transoxiana, carried west by refugees before the Mongols. One example is the jurist Yusuf son of Abi Sa'id al-Sijistani, who composed at Sivas in 639/1241-2 a law book, the Munyat al-mufti, widely used in the central and eastern Islamic lands. Mosques and madrasas came late. The earliest mosques in Seljuk, Danishmendid, Mengujekid and Saltukid lands appear only after about 550/1155, and the earliest madrasa known from a foundation inscription was built at Kayseri in 589/1193, nearly two centuries after the type was flourishing further east. Only with the consolidation of the sultanate in the seventh/thirteenth century did they become numerous in Konya, Kayseri and other towns. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1018}

## Mysticism and Persian culture
Mysticism flourished early in Anatolia. The great mystic [[person/ibn-arabi-muhyi-al-din|Ibn al-'Arabi]], of Spanish Muslim origin, travelled to Malatya and as far as Sivas and Konya, and was followed by his disciple [[person/afif-al-din-al-tilimsani|'Afif al-Din al-Tilimsani]], who settled in Rum for some time. Ibn al-'Arabi's influence in Anatolia passed through Sadr al-Din Qunawi, so that his works later became standard texts in Ottoman madrasas. The article adds that contacts with North African pilgrims returning by way of Anatolia were enough for them to have a small mosque of their own in Konya. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1018}

Yet the Persian spiritual and literary tradition quickly became dominant, in step with Persian influence in administration and court life, and the sultans adopted Persian epic names such as Kay Kawus, Kay Khusraw and Kay Kubadh. In the thirteenth century refugees arrived at Konya from a family of preachers and mystics of Balkh: [[person/baha-al-din-walad|Baha' al-Din Walad]], invited by Kay Kubadh I but not long surviving there, and his son [[person/jalal-al-din-rumi|Jalal al-Din Rumi]], who spent most of his adult life at Konya and composed his [[work/mathnawi-rumi|Mathnawi]] there. The capital became the centre of the [[group/mawlawiyya|Mawlawi]] Sufi order as developed by Rumi's son [[person/sultan-walad|Sultan Walad]], with Rumi's tomb as its spiritual centre for centuries. [[person/najm-al-din-razi|Najm al-Din Razi Daya]], a former disciple of Najm al-Din Kubra, also came west to Rum but did not find it congenial and returned to Tabriz and Baghdad; even so his Mirsad al-'ibad became very popular in Anatolia and was later translated into Turkish. The article concludes that these traditions helped consolidate the dominant, official Sunnism of Anatolia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1018}

## Popular belief and mixed faiths
Below that level the article sees currents of belief that may go back to the Turks' animistic past, and probably vague pro-Shi'i feeling like that visible among the 'Alawi Turkmen of eastern Anatolia in early Ottoman times. They surfaced only in times of stress, as with the Baba'i movement, a religious movement with social overtones that disturbed much of Anatolia shortly before the Mongol invasion. Its leader Baba Ishaq defied the Seljuk armies for some time, and the movement was never wholly extinguished in the countryside. The article says it seems to have reappeared or merged soon after in the Bektashi movement, a link made explicitly by the fourteenth-century biographer Aflaki, and that Haji Bektash may have been a disciple of Baba Ishaq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1018} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1019}

On the meeting of faiths the author finds firm conclusions hard, but judges that some sultans, such as Kay Kubadh I, were enlightened and tolerant rulers aware of the mixture of peoples they governed. Konya had Armenians, Greeks with their monastery of St Chariton, and some Jews. Rumi seems to have had harmonious relations with local dhimmis, while remaining convinced of his own mission to convert them. At the popular level, Christians and Muslims shared veneration at many holy sites and often equated saints of the two faiths: St George with Khidr Ilyas, St Amphilochius at Konya with Aflatun, that is Plato, whose tomb was thought to lie there, and Sari Saltuk Dede with St Nicholas. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1019}

## Ibn Khaldun's account
Ibn Khaldun describes the state of Qutlumush and his sons from the Seljuks, kings of Konya and the land of Rum. Qutlumush entered the land of Rum and took Konya, Aksaray and their districts, and rebelled against Alp Arslan, who defeated him and killed him in 456 AH. His son Sulayman acted on his behalf and conquered Antioch from the Romans in 477 AH, then was killed in his war with Tutush in 479 AH. His son Kilij Arslan ruled, struggled with the Franks, and took Mosul and Diyar Bakr, until he drowned in the Khabur in his war with Jawali. His son Mas'ud then took the land of Rum, and the rule of his sons remained in Konya until their state died out at the hands of the Tatars. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.1331-1333}
