## Overview
The article NEMČE in the Encyclopaedia of Islam says that the Ottomans took the Slavonic word nemče, "mute", to mean the Germans; in a broader sense they used it for the territory of the Holy Roman Empire, which lasted until 1806, and in a narrower sense for the Habsburg lands within the boundaries of modern Austria. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 13}

## The empire in Arabic and Ottoman writing
Among the early Arabic traces the article lists, al-Mas'udi in the 10th century names the Namdjin as a Slavic tribe, and his contemporary Ibrahim b. Ya'qub travelled through the region, although the name of the territory cannot be established from his account. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 13} Al-Idrisi's Nuzhat al-mushtaq is, in the article's words, the most comprehensive medieval Arabic source concerning Austria; it names Carinthia and describes cities such as Villach, while Vienna appears only in an itinerary, and the article suggests that cities were chosen for their importance as trading places. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 13} From the middle of the 17th century Ottoman historiography carries references to events in the Holy Roman Empire, at first sparse and in the 18th century increasingly accurate, helped by embassy reports and travel accounts; Evliya Celebi's description of his journey to Vienna with the embassy of 1665 holds a particular place. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 pp. 13-14}

## Ottoman and Habsburg relations
The article dates the first diplomatic soundings between Sultan Bayezid II and the Emperor Maximilian I to 1497, 1504 and 1510-11, and continuous, hostile contact to the situation in Hungary after the battle of Mohacs in 1526. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 13} Sultan Suleyman I campaigned against Habsburg territory in 1529, against Vienna, and in 1532; after part of Hungary came under direct Ottoman rule in 1541, Ferdinand I concluded a treaty in 1547 that obliged him to pay the sultan a yearly tribute of 30,000 gold ducats, a liability that lasted, except in war, until the treaty of Zsitvatorok in 1606. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 13} The article calls the unsuccessful attack on Vienna in 1683 by Kara Mustafa Pasha the climax of a protracted war that led to the loss of Hungary to the Habsburgs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 13} It adds that the Treaties of Vienna (1615) and Karlowitz (1699) already carried articles on reciprocal trade, that a commercial treaty followed at Passarowitz in 1718, and that in 1790, at Reichenbach, Prussia forced Emperor Leopold II to renounce any conquest of Ottoman territory. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 13} The Habsburgs kept ambassadors at the Porte after 1547, while a permanent Ottoman representation in Vienna began only in 1797. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 13}
