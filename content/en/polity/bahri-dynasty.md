## Overview
The article MAMLUKS in the Encyclopaedia of Islam traces the Mamluk sultanate to the Bahriyya, a military household of Kipchak Turkish mamluks in the bodyguard of the Ayyubid al-Salih Ayyub, who reigned 637-47/1240-9. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 341} The Bahriyya replaced the Ayyubids in Egypt and Syria, the article says, less by deliberate usurpation than under the pressure of two military crises: the crusade of St Louis in 647-9/1249-50 and the Mongol invasion of Syria in 657-8/1259-60. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 341}

## Seizure of power and Baybars
A group of the Bahriyya murdered the new sultan Turanshah on 27 Muharram 648/1 May 1250, after he favoured his own household; they installed al-Salih Ayyub's widow Shajar al-Durr and then raised the commander Aybak al-Turkumani as al-Malik al-Mu'izz on 28 Rabi II 648/11 July 1250. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 341} The Mamluk commander Kutuz, with Baybars, defeated the Mongols at Ayn Jalut on 25 Ramadan 658/3 September 1260, and Baybars then usurped the sultanate; the article calls al-Zahir Baybars, who reigned 658-76/1260-77, rather than his predecessors the effective founder of the sultanate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 341} He installed an Abbasid prince as caliph in Cairo, who delegated plenary powers to him as universal sultan of Islam, and he took Caesarea, Haifa and Arsuf in 663/1265 and Jaffa and Antioch in 666/1268, demolishing those towns, while restoring and garrisoning inland fortresses such as Safad and Crac des Chevaliers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 342}

## Qalawun, Khalil and al-Nasir Muhammad
Qalawun usurped the throne in 678/1279 and introduced a Circassian mamluk regiment, the Burdjiyya; Tripoli fell in Rabi II 688/April 1289, and his son al-Ashraf Khalil took Acre in Jumada I 690/May 1291, extinguishing the Latin kingdom. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 342} Khalil was killed by a conspiracy of magnates in Muharram 693/December 1293, which opened seventeen years of instability in which his brother al-Nasir Muhammad b. Qalawun reigned twice as a figurehead, in 693-4/1293-4 and 698-708/1299-1309, before his long third reign of 709-41/1310-41. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 pp. 342-343} In that reign the article describes an autocracy: a cadastral survey of Egypt in 715/1315 raised the share of revenue assigned to the sultan's fisc from one-sixth to five-twelfths, uncanonical taxes were abolished, and the sultan went on the Pilgrimage in 712/1313, 719/1320 and 732/1332, which demonstrated his suzerainty over the Holy Cities. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 343}

## Later Qalawunids and the end of the line
After al-Nasir Muhammad died in Dhu l-Hijja 741/June 1341, twelve sultans reigned in less than half a century, mostly children, while the magnates controlled the state. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 343} The article singles out al-Nasir Hasan, who was deposed in 752/1351, restored in 755/1354 and put to death in Jumada I 762/March 1361 after he tried to build a new power base among the descendants of mamluks. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 343} Barquq b. Anas, a Circassian reared in the military household of Yalbugha, deposed the last Qalawunid and usurped the sultanate in 784/November 1382, which the article takes as the beginning of the Circassian Mamluk sultanate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 343}
