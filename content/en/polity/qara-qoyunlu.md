## Overview
The Encyclopaedia of Islam describes the Kara-Koyunlu as a Turkish dynasty that ruled parts of eastern Anatolia, Iraq and the Jazira and most of Iran; it was also known as the Barani, and the article says it is not known why the dynasty was given its name. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 621} The Kara-Koyunlu tribe was undoubtedly a sub-tribe of the Oghuz, and the article judges probable Minorsky's claim that it belonged to the Yiwa. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 621}

## Rise under Bayram Khodja and Kara Mehmed
In the Mongol period the Kara-Koyunlu wintered in the Mosul region and grazed in the Van region, and after the Sutayli occupied the eastern and south-eastern Anatolian lands in 737/1337 they became their vassals. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 621} Amir Bayram Khodja, who killed Husayn Beg in 752/1351 and took the leadership, captured Mosul; the Jalayirid Sultan Uways defeated him on the plain of Mush in 767/1366 and made him a tribute-paying vassal, but by his death in 782/1380 the territory from Mosul to Erzurum was directly subject to the Kara-Koyunlu. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 621} His nephew Kara Mehmed defeated the Jalayirid army near Nakhichevan in 784/1382, and the article reports that Pir Hasan killed him in Rabi II 791/April 1389. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 621}

## Kara Yusuf, Timur and Iskandar
Kara Yusuf, Kara Mehmed's son, opposed Timur as his father had done: he took refuge with the Ottoman Bayezid I in 802/1400, was held with the Jalayirid Ahmad at Damascus by order of the Mamluk sultan, and was released by the governor Shaykh in 807/1405. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 pp. 623-624} Returning to eastern Anatolia, he defeated Timur's grandson Abu Bakr Mirza in 809/1406 and secured Adharbaydjan; at the village of Asad near Tabriz on 28 Rabi II 813/30 August 1410 he defeated and captured Sultan Ahmad, who was later put to death, and Iraq-i Arab was incorporated into Kara-Koyunlu lands. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 623} He died on 7 Dhu l-Qa'da 823/13 November 1420 on his way to meet the Timurid Shah Rukh, leaving a large country from Erzincan to Qazvin and from Shirvan to Basra. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 624} His son Iskandar fought Shah Rukh at Alashgird on 29-30 Rajab and 1 Sha'ban 824/30-31 July and 1 August 1421, a battle won by Shah Rukh through numbers, though Shah Rukh then withdrew; Iskandar was killed by his own son Shah Kubad in 841/1438. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 pp. 624-625}

## Jihan Shah and the end of the dynasty
During the reign of Jihan Shah, 843-72/1439-67, the article says the territory grew into a large empire and enjoyed its most brilliant phase: it took Iraq-i Arab, Rayy, Isfahan, Fars and Kirman, and obtained from the Timurid Abu Sa'id confirmation of his gains; he bore the titles khan, khaqan and sultan, and encouraged culture and learning. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 625} He was killed with his son Muhammadi in 872/1467 on an expedition against Uzun Hasan of the Ak-Koyunlu; his son Hasan Ali, placed on the throne, killed himself in Shawwal 873/April 1469 when he could not avoid capture, and in 874/1469 all the Kara-Koyunlu territories passed to the Ak-Koyunlu. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 625}

## Religious leanings
The article notes that several members of the dynasty bore names such as Yar Ali, Pir Ali and Hasan Ali, which it takes as evidence of a tendency towards Shi'ism, and that Isfahan, a son of Kara Yusuf, is said to have struck coins in the name of the Twelve Imams; on the other side, the names of the Four Caliphs appear on coins of Jihan Shah's reign and no contemporary historian records any inclination to Shi'ism on his part. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 625}
