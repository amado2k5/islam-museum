## Overview
The Emirate of Granada, a principality founded in 1238 CE and ended in 1492 CE, with its capital at [[place/granada|Granada]]. The data record that it succeeded [[polity/almohad-caliphate|the Almohad state]]. {cite:wikidata}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam treats it under the Nasrids, in Arabic the Banu Nasr or Banu al-Ahmar, named after Muhammad b. Yusuf Ibn Nasr Ibn al-Ahmar, founder of what became the last major Muslim dynasty of the Iberian Peninsula, ruling a kingdom in the far south of Spain from Granada. The dynasty claimed as a remote ancestor the Khazraj leader Sa'd b. 'Ubada, a Companion of the Prophet. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1074} The article dates the dynasty's birth to 26 Ramadan 629 AH/18 April 1232 CE and its link with Granada to about Ramadan 634 AH/May 1237 CE, when the city passed to the founder; Granada became the seat of his power only from 644 AH/1246 CE. Nasrid rule formally ended on 2 January 1492 CE, in a public ceremony after an improvised capitulation the day before. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1074} The 1238 start in the data record above therefore differs from the dates the article gives, 1232 for the dynasty and 1237 for Granada. The article also warns that Arabic sources for the later period are scarce, so the Christian chronicles of Iberia, often partisan, have to be used with caution. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1074}

## The rulers
The article lists the Nasrid rulers in order. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1074} Muhammad I reigned from 1232 to 1273 CE, Muhammad II from 1273 to 1302, Muhammad III from 1302 to 1309, Nasr from 1309 to 1314, Ismail I from 1314 to 1325, Muhammad IV from 1325 to 1333 and Yusuf I from 1333 to 1354. Muhammad V ruled from 1354 to 1359 and again from 1362 to 1391, with Ismail II and Muhammad VI between. After Yusuf III, who died in 1417, comes a confusing series of changes of monarch, for which the article points readers to a schema by L. P. Harvey. For the last decade it notes that the subjects were deeply divided between Muhammad XII, called Boabdil, and his uncle al-Zaghal. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1074} The sections below follow the article's own order.

## The founding: Muhammad I, 1232 to 1273
The article sets the birth of the dynasty against the collapse of Almohad authority after the death of the caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf II in 620 AH/1224 CE without a son. Local dynasties in Valencia and Murcia exploited the confusion, and so, far more importantly, did Ferdinand III of [[polity/castile|Castile]], whose crown was joined with that of Leon in 1230, drove into the Muslim heartlands from 1231 and won [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]] in 1236 and [[place/seville|Seville]] in 1248. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1074} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1075}

The new dynasty took shape at [[place/arjona|Arjona]], a small frontier town northwest of [[place/jaen|Jaen]], where in 629 AH/1232 CE Muhammad b. Yusuf made himself the effective ruler of a region reaching from Jaen to [[place/guadix|Guadix]] and Baeza. He relied on the military help and marriage ties of the Banu Ashkilula, another family connected with Arjona, and in 634 AH/1237 CE accepted an invitation from the notables of Granada to take their city. After losing Arjona in 1244 and Jaen in 1246 he turned Granada into an enduring political entity. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1075}

The article stresses his religious side. He first rose as a charismatic leader of frontiersmen with a religious mission and, once in Granada, kept the image of an ascetic by leaving the exercise of government and command of the army to the head of the Ashkilula, while embracing and strictly enforcing the doctrines of the city's Maliki religious establishment. In 655 AH/1257 CE he named his sons Muhammad and Yusuf as his heirs, so restricting power to his own family, and in 662 AH/1264 CE he began using Maghribi troops. A rift with the Ashkilula followed, and they rebelled at Malaga in 1266; the conflict, with Castilian involvement on both sides, was still running when he died. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1075}

Toward Castile he accepted Ferdinand as overlord and an annual tribute when surrendering Jaen, took part in the campaign that won Seville in 1248, and stood aside from Castilian victories of 1261 and 1262. In 1264 to 1266 he appealed to the Hafsids of Tunis, to the Marinids of Fez and to Castile's Mudejars for a campaign against Alfonso X that failed. The author judges that he was no martyr in the strict sense but was committed to Islam, and, quoting L. P. Harvey, that he snatched from disaster a relatively secure refuge for Islam in the peninsula. He died in 671 AH/1273 CE, leaving a regime whose seat of government, the Alhambra, would grow in splendour. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1075}

## Consolidation: Muhammad II to Nasr, 1273 to 1314
Muhammad II al-Faqih, the Jurist, set out to block Castile's claim to hegemony and to subdue the Ashkilula, who held Malaga and Guadix. He took Antequera from them in 1273, agreed to pay Alfonso X an annual tribute of 300,000 maravedis, and then turned for help to the Marinid sultan Abu Yusuf Ya'qub, who crossed the Straits in 1275 and led a successful campaign. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1075} The relationship with the [[polity/marinid-dynasty|Marinids]] stayed uneasy. In 1279 Muhammad recovered Malaga, which gave Granada access to the Mediterranean, and a combined attack by Alfonso, Abu Yusuf and the Ashkilula from 1280 to 1281 was survived largely because Alfonso and his heir Sancho fell out. In 1288 the Ashkilula left for Ksar el-Kebir in Morocco and ceded Guadix to Granada. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1076} Muhammad then sought to gain a hold on the Straits of Gibraltar. After Marinid withdrawal from Spain in 1295, he gained Quesada in 1295 and Alcaudete in 1300, and he died on 8 Sha'ban 701 AH/8 April 1302 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1076}

His son Muhammad III accepted a three-year vassalage to Castile from 1303, and in 1308 Castile and Aragon spelled out in the treaty of Alcala de Henares how the kingdom should be divided between them. After he took [[place/ceuta|Ceuta]] in 1307, Marinids, Castilians and Aragonese united against him; he was forced to abdicate and his chief minister was assassinated in March 1309. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1076} His successor Nasr, aged twenty-two, ruled a kingdom at war on two fronts. In 1309 the Ceutans returned to Marinid rule, Castile took Gibraltar, and Granada made peace with Fez, ceding Algeciras and Ronda. Castile's siege of Algeciras ended in January 1310 when Ferdinand IV accepted Nasr as his tributary, and the Aragonese siege of [[place/almeria|Almeria]] was abandoned the same winter. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1076} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1077} The article judges that Nasr served Granada well, since the only major Christian gain was Gibraltar. He was dethroned by his kinsman Ismail in 713 AH/1314 CE and allowed to govern Guadix. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1077}

## Ismail I, Muhammad IV and Yusuf I, 1314 to 1354
Ismail I faced an attempt by Nasr to regain the throne with Castilian help, which ended after a hard-fought battle near Alicun in 1316, whose outcome the sources dispute; Nasr never again attempted such a venture. In 1319 a Castilian army reached the walls of Granada and was crushed in the [[event/battle-of-the-vega-1319|Battle of the Vega]], where both regent princes died on 26 June, and Castile accepted an eight-year peace. Ismail recovered Baza in 1324 and several places in 1325, and was assassinated on 27 Rajab 725 AH/6 July 1325 CE after a quarrel with his cousin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1077} His son Muhammad IV came to the throne as a minor under a powerful vizier. When Alfonso XI of Castile attacked in 1327, Marinid reinforcements were brought in; Castile took Teba in 1330, Gibraltar was regained, truces followed in 1333, and Muhammad IV was assassinated on 25 August 1333 CE by the sons of 'Uthman b. Abi al-'Ula, the Marinid commander of the Maghribi volunteers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1077}

Yusuf I, proclaimed by the loyal Ridwan, concluded a three-party peace with Castile and Fez in 1334. The Marinid sultan Abu al-Hasan and Alfonso XI both built fleets, and the Muslim ships struck first in April 1340. At the [[event/battle-of-rio-salado-1340|Battle of the Salado]], on 7 Jumada I 741 AH/30 October 1340 CE, Alfonso, with Portuguese support, won a decisive victory. Alfonso then besieged Algeciras from 1342, in one of the earliest major operations in which cannon were used effectively, the article says by the Muslims. Yusuf gave up Algeciras for a ten-year truce in March 1344, with tribute to Castile. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1077} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1078} A lunatic stabbed Yusuf to death in the Great Mosque of Granada on 1 Shawwal 755 AH/19 October 1354 CE. The article calls him an able ruler and diplomat, trusted by his subjects, who by refusing to risk the kingdom's heartlands preserved them, and under whom Granada moved toward literary and architectural brilliance. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1078}

## Muhammad V and the height of the kingdom, 1354 to 1391
Muhammad V began at sixteen under the tutelage of his father's hajib Ridwan, whose service included the young [[person/ibn-al-khatib|Ibn al-Khatib]]. In August 1359 he was ousted in a palace revolution by his half-brother Ismail II, in which Ridwan was killed, and took refuge at Guadix and then Fez. Ismail II reigned only a few months and was assassinated by Muhammad VI, called el Bermejo, the Red, who took the throne. Muhammad V returned from Fez and, allied with Pedro I of Castile, drove Muhammad VI out; Pedro had him put to death near Seville in 1362. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1078} Muhammad V's second reign began on 16 March 1362 CE and lasted almost thirty years, in which careful avoidance of war gave Granada the longest stretch of peace in its history. Amid the Castilian civil war he switched allegiance from Pedro I to Enrique II, took Algeciras in 1369 and concluded an eight-year truce in 1370, renewed in 1375. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1078} In 1372 he introduced direct Nasrid command of the Maghribi volunteers. Ibn al-Khatib, thought pro-Marinid, fled to Fez in 1371 or 1372 and was later tried for heresy by a Granadan tribunal and murdered in prison in 776 AH/1375 CE after intrigues by his former protege [[person/ibn-zamrak|Ibn Zamrak]]. Muhammad V died on 10 Safar 793 AH/16 January 1391 CE at the height of his power; the article says art and literature flourished under him and that much of what is admired in the Alhambra is owed to him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1080}

## Frontier warfare, 1391 to 1417
Yusuf II, Muhammad V's eldest son, was first dominated by his father's freedman minister Khalid and died in 794 AH/1392 CE. His younger brother Muhammad VII took the throne through a palace plot and opened an era of frontier raiding with a raid on Caravaca in 1392, a period that inspired the Castilian border ballads. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1080} A populist crusade in 1394 ended in disaster, a Castilian massacre at Collejares followed in October 1406, and a truce in April 1408 gave both sides a respite; Muhammad died that May. The article observes that Granada's military capacity was no longer underpinned by an organised Maghribi component, while Castile gained manpower and artillery. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1080} Yusuf III attacked Zahara in April 1410, and Ferdinand, regent of Castile, took [[place/antequera|Antequera]] on 16 September 1410 after a bitter [[event/siege-of-antequera-1410|siege]], earning the name el de Antequera. A truce signed on 10 November began a relatively long peace lasting until 1428. Yusuf III died on 9 November 1417 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1080}

## Division and fall, 1417 to 1492
The article gives only a skeleton of this tangled period. From 1419 Granada's internal history is bound up with the powerful Banu al-Sarraj, known in Europe as the Abencerrages. Muhammad IX alternated with rivals for decades, and Castile imposed a puppet, Yusuf IV, in 1431 after a show of force at La Higueruela. Castile's king Juan II sought to keep the Nasrids divided and to avoid long truces. The 1440s were militarily successful for Granada, but it was beaten at Alporchones in 1452. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1080} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1081} Sa'd ruled from 1453 to 1464 and was then overthrown by his son [[person/abu-al-hasan-nasrid|Abu al-Hasan Ali]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1081}

The article names 1479 as a turning point, when Ferdinand became king of Aragon and, with Isabel of Castile, the Catholic Monarchs turned to Granada. Alhama fell in February 1482. A palace plot then replaced Abu al-Hasan with his son Boabdil, and Abu al-Hasan withdrew with his brother [[person/al-zaghal|al-Zaghal]] to [[place/malaga|Malaga]], so dividing the kingdom between the Granada and Almeria area in the east and the [[place/ronda|Ronda]] and Malaga area in the west. Boabdil was captured at Lucena in 1483; in October 1483 most Granadan jurists denied by fatwa his right to the throne; and Abu al-Hasan, struck by illness in 1485, was replaced by al-Zaghal. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1081} Boabdil formally surrendered Granada on 2 January 1492. Town after town had already fallen, among them Malaga in 1487 and Baza and Almeria in 1489; al-Zaghal sold his lands in 1490 and sailed for Oran. Boabdil, with the rights of a Mudejar under the Capitulations, later left for the Maghrib, died in Fez in 949 AH/1533 to 1534 CE, and his descendants were found there in poverty in 1617 to 1618. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1082}

## Why it fell, as the article sees it
The article calls the last decade enigmatic and gives only a sketch of the causes, which are its author's judgement. The Catholic Monarchs united their subjects, whereas the Nasrids failed to end family quarrels and factional strife, and cultivators and jurists did not always agree. Most damaging was the split between Boabdil and al-Zaghal, which in 1486 made Granada itself a scene of bloody fighting; the author regards Boabdil as the more useful collaborator to Castile and al-Zaghal as the better soldier. Castile exploited every division, deployed artillery, and devastated cultivated land outside the walls. Heroism could not replace the reinforcements the Maghrib could no longer supply, and in 1487 Mamluk Egypt, to which an appeal was made, offered only diplomatic intervention and goodwill. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1082} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1083} For a different reading of the same decline, see the section on al-Suwaidan's account below.

## Art and architecture
A second part of the article, by a different author, divides Nasrid architecture into four periods and places its high point in the fourteenth century: inheritance of Almohad forms under Muhammad I to III and Nasr, monumental architecture under Ismail, Muhammad IV and Yusuf I, richest decoration under Muhammad V, and only a brief flowering under Muhammad VII and Yusuf III. Work on the Alhambra began in 1238. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1083} Surviving civic buildings include the Corral del Carbon, a funduk of Yusuf I, and part of the hospital built by Muhammad V in 1365 to 1367; Yusuf I also inaugurated the [[building/madrasa-of-granada|madrasa of Granada]]. The [[building/generalife|Generalife]] preserves the only Nasrid gardens. The chancery directed decoration, and four of its poet-viziers, Ibn al-Jayyab, Ibn al-Khatib, Ibn Zamrak and Ibn Furkun, composed the poems and texts carved on palace walls. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1083} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1084}

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "History Taught Me"
The kingdom of Granada is the centre of episodes 2 to 7 of [[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]]'s programme [[work/allamani-al-tarikh-program|"History Taught Me"]]; he says he chose to begin with it because he has found nothing in Muslim history so like the community's situation today as that of al-Andalus after the taifa statelets {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:05:00}.

### Extent and provinces
Al-Suwaidan relates that after the fall of [[place/cordoba|Córdoba]] and then of [[place/jaen|Jaén]] in 643 AH, 1245 CE, only two large provinces were left in al-Andalus: Granada in the south-east, and [[place/seville|Seville]], about ten per cent of the land {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:07:00}. He says that once Seville fell in 646 AH, 1248 CE, no Muslim province was left but Granada, which with its surroundings was called the kingdom of Granada, eighty-five per cent of al-Andalus having fallen {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:16:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:17:00}; he puts its extent at about fifteen per cent of al-Andalus {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep04 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:09:30}. It comprised, he says, three provinces, Granada, [[place/malaga|Málaga]] and [[place/almeria|Almería]], each with some self-rule but subject politically and militarily to Ibn al-Ahmar {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:17:30}. He points out that Islam remained in al-Andalus about 250 years after the fall of its great cities {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:07:00}.

### Two hundred years of treaties
Al-Suwaidan relates that from 709 AH, 1309 CE, Granada lived through successive treaties between its Nasrid kings and the king of Castile, paying tribute for many years, and yet did not fall {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:03:30}. The secret of its survival for more than two hundred years, in his view, was not its own strength but the long rivalry between the two Christian kingdoms of [[polity/castile|Castile]] and Aragon: for him the balance among great powers is what keeps small states alive, as it does today {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:16:00}.

### Civilisation in a time of weakness
Al-Suwaidan sees Granada as an exception showing that civilisation does not always follow politics: a bad political situation, and yet al-Andalus kept pace with civilisation as far as its means allowed, which for him shows the vitality of the community {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:06:30}. He mentions the many books written there and the schools and institutes of learning founded mainly from waqf (endowment) funds rather than state money {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:07:30}. Among inventions he names cannon firing incendiaries and the turning of gunpowder into a propellant; the Chinese came first, he says, but Muslims developed it, and the military museum in Madrid still keeps guns the Muslims used defending Granada {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:08:00}. He lists shipbuilding, textiles, paper and pottery, farming and herding, building of mosques, palaces, houses and bridges, and [[building/nasrid-palaces-alhambra|the Alhambra]] with its carvings, on which he thinks they spent so lavishly that they lost the state {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:08:30}. Granada became, he says, one of the most beautiful cities in the world, with a million inhabitants, and Europe learned from it in matters such as chivalry and its pageants {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:12:00}. He stresses that it was waqf, not politics, that made this civilisation, since schools and mosques were in the people's hands, and calls for reviving it {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:12:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:12:30}.

### The neglect of jihad
Al-Suwaidan says Granada cared for everything except its army and strength; he counts faith and piety, knowledge, the economy and jihad among the elements of a nation's rise, and holds that neglecting preparation and relying on the international balance prepared Granada's fall {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:10:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:10:30}.

### Division, and the union of Spain
Al-Suwaidan relates that in 871 AH, 1467 CE, about 26 years before the fall, power was held by a Nasrid he names Muhammad ibn Sa'd ibn Isma'il ibn al-Ahmar, titled al-Ghalib billah, and that his brother Abu Abdallah Muhammad, called [[person/al-zaghal|al-Zaghal]] ("the brave"), disputed his rule and sought the king of Castile's help against him {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:12:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:14:00}. The brothers' war ended, he says, in a peace dividing Granada: a northern part, the main province, under al-Ghalib billah, and a southern part around Málaga under al-Zaghal {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:14:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:15:00}. Three years after the division, in 874 AH, 1469 CE, he relates, Ferdinand, king of Aragon, married Isabella, heiress of Castile, ending a rivalry of more than two hundred years that had kept Granada alive; five years later, in 879 AH, 1474 CE, the two kingdoms were united in what was called the kingdom of Spain {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:16:00}. He says Ferdinand swore to hold his wedding nowhere but in the Alhambra {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:16:30}. The people, by contrast, had sunk into luxury, wine, singing and slave-girls, he says, and [[person/ziryab|Ziryab]], who had come from Abbasid Baghdad, had long before spread among them fashions in dress and the laying of tables; he counts luxury among the chief obstacles to victory {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep05 00:18:00}.

### The last years
Al-Suwaidan relates that after the struggle between [[person/boabdil-muhammad-xii|Abu Abdallah Muhammad al-Saghir]], his father and his uncle al-Zaghal, Granada was left in three parts: [[place/wadi-ash|Wadi Ash (Guadix)]] in the east under al-Zaghal, Granada under al-Saghir, and Málaga, now held by the king of Spain {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep06 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep06 00:14:00}. In 895 AH, 1490 CE, he says, the king of Spain moved from Málaga to Almería and took it, then to Wadi Ash and took it, al-Zaghal fled to Tunis, and the city of Granada was besieged on every side {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep06 00:16:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep06 00:16:30}. He tells its end in [[event/fall-of-granada-897|the surrender of Granada]] in 897 AH {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep07 00:17:30}.
