## Overview
The Encyclopaedia of Islam describes the Dulafids as an important tribe whose holdings in al-Jibal formed a fief of their own known as al-Igharayn, "the two fiefs", east of Nihawand between Hamadan and Isfahan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 650} Its founder's father, Isa b. Idris, laid the basis of the family's fortune, the article says, by highway robbery on such a scale that he was able to retire and build a stronghold at al-Karaj; his son and successor al-Qasim b. Isa, known as Abu Dulaf, used it as the foundation of the Dulafid dynasty. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 650}

## Abu Dulaf and the dynasty
The article calls Abu Dulaf a Shi'i, a highly educated man, a lauded poet and a great general; although he was a fervent pro-Alid and had led troops against al-Ma'mun, the caliph pardoned him and accepted him at court. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 650} He took part in subduing the revolt of Babak al-Khurrami in 222/836-7, and his descendants served the reigning caliphs as loyal supporters; Abu Dulaf and his grandson Ahmad distinguished themselves as generals under al-Mu'tasim and al-Mu'tadid respectively. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 650} The article describes the Dulafids as an almost completely independent dynasty that lasted some seventy years: their fief was granted in perpetuity, they paid the caliphs a fixed yearly tribute, no other taxes were levied, and they struck their own coinage. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 650}

## Al-Karaj and the succession
The Dulafid capital, al-Karaj, was a long town built on a height amid fertile lands that yielded about 3,100,000 dirhams a year; Abu Dulaf extended it to about two leagues, with well-built clay-brick houses. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 650} After Abu Dulaf's death in 225/839-40 the principality passed in turn to his descendants: his son Abd al-Aziz, who died in 260/873-4 and was also governor of al-Rayy, and then his grandsons, among them Ahmad, who died in 280/893-4, Umar, who died in 283/896-7, and al-Harith, known as Abu Layla, all loyal to the caliphate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 650} Al-Harith was accidentally killed in battle in 284/897-8; according to al-Mas'udi his horse was felled under him and the unsheathed sword on his shoulder plunged into him. With his death the power of the dynasty ended and its lands reverted to the central government. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 650}
