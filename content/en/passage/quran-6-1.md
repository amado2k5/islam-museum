## Summary
Verse 1 of [[passage/quran-6|Surat al-An'am]], a Meccan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 1 of 165 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

By "praise be to God" al-Tabari means complete praise to God alone, who has no partner, not to all the rivals and gods, nor to anything else that the unbelievers among his creatures worship, of idols and images. This is speech in the form of a report that carries the sense of a command: give praise and thanks purely to him who created you, people, and created the heavens and the earth, and join no one or anything with him in it, for he is the one who deserves your praise for his kindnesses and favours to you, not those you worship besides him and make partners to him among his creatures. He has explained earlier, with evidence, the difference between the meanings of praise and thanks. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 249}

The editor keeps "anyone or anything" because the sense requires it, and refers for "praise" to volume 1.

Then "and made the darknesses and the light": praise be to God who created the heavens and the earth, darkened the night and lit the day. Report 13040, from al-Suddi: the darknesses are the darkness of night and the light the light of day. Report 13041, from Qatada: he created the heavens before the earth, darkness before light, and Paradise before the Fire. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 250}

If one asks what then is the meaning of "ja'ala", the answer is that the Arabs use it as a frame for a report and an act, saying "ja'altu af'alu kadha" and "ja'altu aqumu wa-aq'udu", showing by "ja'altu" that the act continues, as one says "'alaqtu af'alu kadha", not that it is itself an act; this shows that there is no "making" there but the standing, and "ja'altu" only marks the act's continuity and persistence. An example is a poet's two lines addressed to one who claimed he would go his way alone, which urge him to make his oath a false one. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 250}

The editor refers to what he wrote at reports 8317 in volume 7 and 12834 in volume 11, which he calls "words of assistance", and says Abu Jafar expressed this meaning well; he says he did not know the poet or find the two lines in the books before him, and that the first line in the printed text is empty of sense.

The rest: in the second line "fa-ja'al tahallal" means swear by fits and starts, bit by bit, not that there is a "making" apart from the swearing; so every "ja'ala" in speech is only a sign of an act that continues, and has no share in the sense of the act. So "he made the darknesses and the light" means only: he darkened their night and lit their day. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 251}

The editor says he preferred to read the two lines as he gave them, as he thinks he recalls the sense, taking them to be the words of a poet to his brother or companion who wanted to go his way alone and swore he would, so he mocked him; "farid" is one alone, cut off from his friend, "muktani'" is near, close upon you, one says "kana'a al-mawt wa-ktana'a" when death drew near, "umm al-luhaym" is a name for death because it swallows everything; and he calls this his own effort to set the verse right until it is found elsewhere.

Then "but those who disbelieve hold others equal with their Lord": God, in wonder to his believing creatures at the unbelievers among his servants and arguing against them, says that the god whom you, people, must praise is the one who created the heavens and the earth, who made of them your livelihoods and food and the food of your cattle by which you live; from the heavens the rain comes down on you and in them the sun and moon run in succession and difference for your good; from the earth the grain springs by which you are fed and the fruits that are your delight. Those who deny God's favour to them, people, "hold equal with their Lord", who did this and brought it about, make a partner to him in worship of him, serving with him gods, rivals, images and idols, none of which shares with him in the creation of any of this or in his favours to them; he alone does all that, and they join others with him in serving him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 251}

Al-Tabari adds: glory to God, how eloquent an argument and concise an exhortation for whoever thinks on it with reason and takes it in with understanding! It has been said that it is the opening of the Torah. Report 13042, from Ka'b: the opening of the Torah is the opening of al-An'am; 13043, from him, the same, adding that the close of the Torah is the close of Hud. One says "adaltu hadha bi-hadha" of making one thing equal to another, and "adaltu fihi a'dilu adlan" of being fair in judgement. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 252}

The editor identifies the narrators of 13042: Abd al-Aziz ibn Abd al-Samad al-Ammi, Abu Imran al-Jawni (Abd al-Malik ibn Habib), Abdullah ibn Rabah al-Ansari, and Ka'b, the well-known Ka'b al-Ahbar of the Israelite reports; and for "justice" refers to volume 11 and earlier.

The interpreters said much the same: report 13044, from Mujahid: "ya'dilun" means they associate. They then differed on who is meant. Some said the People of the Book. In 13045 Ibn Abza is reported to have been approached by a Kharijite reading him this verse, who asked him: are not those who disbelieve in their Lord the ones who hold others equal? Yes, said Ibn Abza; the man left, and someone said, this man meant a different explanation, he is a Kharijite! Ibn Abza said bring him back, and asked: do you know about whom this verse was revealed? No. It was revealed about the People of the Book; go, and do not put it on anything but its proper limit. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 253}

The editor identifies Ya'qub al-Qummi, Ja'far ibn Abi al-Mughira and Ibn Abza (Sa'id ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Khuza'i), and says the Kharijite meant to use the verse to charge the people of the qibla with unbelief over Ali ibn Abi Talib's arbitration, which is the Kharijite view.

Others said it means the polytheist idol-worshippers. In 13046 Qatada is reported, in a form the editor could not decipher in the manuscript; in 13047 al-Suddi says they are the polytheists; in 13048 Ibn Zayd says the gods they served, which they held equal to God, while God has no equal or like, has no gods with him, and took neither wife nor child. Al-Tabari holds the soundest to be that God reported that those who disbelieve in their Lord hold others equal, a general term for all unbelievers without singling out any, so all are included: their Jews, Christians, Zoroastrians, idol worshippers and every other kind of disbelief. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 254}

On 13046 the editor says the printed text has a sentence without sense, and the manuscript has signs he could not resolve, which may turn up in another book.

Verse 1 closes here at p. 254, and al-Tabari goes on to [[passage/quran-6-2|6:2]].

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
