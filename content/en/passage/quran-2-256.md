## Summary
Verse 256 of [[passage/quran-2|Surat al-Baqara]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 256 of 286 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

Al-Tabari opens ([[passage/quran-2-256|2:256]]) on p. 407 by saying the interpreters differed on its meaning. Some said it came down about some of the Ansar, or one of them, whose children they had made Jews or Christians; when Islam came they wished to force them to it and God forbade it, so that the children themselves choose to enter. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 407} From Ibn Abbas (5812), via Sa'id ibn Jubayr: a woman whose children did not live would vow that if a child lived she would make him a Jew; when the Banu al-Nadir were expelled there were among them sons of the Ansar, who said we will not leave our sons, and the verse came down. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 407} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 408} From Sa'id ibn Jubayr (5813), to like effect, adding that whoever wished to stay stayed and whoever wished to go went; in the note the editor says Abu Dawud, al-Nasa'i, Ibn Hibban and Ibn Abi Hatim transmitted it and that this last sentence is Sa'id's, as in al-Bayhaqi's Sunan. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 408} From Amir al-Sha'bi (5814): a woman of the Ansar whose children did not live would vow that if hers lived she would place him with the People of the Book in their religion; when Islam came some Ansar sons were still in their religion and their families said they would force them, and the verse came down; what decided between those who chose Judaism and those who chose Islam was that whoever joined them chose Judaism and whoever stayed chose Islam. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 408} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 409} From him (5815, 5816) to like effect: the dividing event was the Prophet's expulsion of the Banu al-Nadir, those who were Jews and had not accepted Islam joined them and those who had accepted stayed; in one version they went to Khaybar. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 409}

From Ibn Abbas (5817): it came down about a man of the Ansar of the Banu Salim ibn Awf called al-Husayn, a Muslim with two Christian sons, who asked the Prophet whether he should force them since they refused anything but Christianity, and the verse was sent down; the editor refers to Ibn Hajar's inquiry into the name of this Companion. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 409} From Sa'id ibn Jubayr (5818), when Abu Bishr asked him: it came down about the Ansar specifically; a woman in the Jahiliyya would vow that if she bore a child she would place him among the Jews; when the Nadir were expelled they said, our sons and brothers are among them, and the Prophet was silent, then the verse came down, and the Prophet said, your companions have been given the choice: if they choose you they are yours and if they choose them they are theirs; so they were expelled with them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 409} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 410}

From al-Suddi (5819): it came down about a man of the Ansar called Abu al-Husayn with two sons; traders came from Syria to Medina carrying olive oil, and when they had sold and wished to return the two sons called them to Christianity, became Christian and went back to Syria with them; their father came to the Prophet and asked whether he should pursue them; the Prophet said the matter stood on the rule against compulsion in the religion, since fighting the People of the Book had not yet been commanded, and said, God keep them far, they are the first to disbelieve; Abu al-Husayn felt resentment against the Prophet for not sending after them, and the passage in ([[passage/quran-4-65|4:65]]) was sent down. Then, in al-Suddi's account, the ruling was abrogated and fighting the People of the Book was commanded in the chapter of Repentance. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 410} In the note the editor says Abu Ja'far did not mention this report in his commentary on the chapter of Women, and that this shows the commentary was abridged, as they transmitted from him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 410} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 411} This is al-Suddi's account, with the claim of abrogation reported as transmitted, and the museum does not rule on it.

From Mujahid (5820): the Jews of the Banu al-Nadir had suckled men of the Aws; when the Prophet ordered their expulsion, their foster-sons of the Aws said we will go with them and follow their religion, and their families prevented them and forced them to Islam, and the verse came down about them. From him (5821): some of the Ansar were put out to nurse among the Banu Qurayza and their families wished to force them to Islam, and the verse came down. From Ibn Jurayj from Mujahid (5822), like the report of 5820, with Abd al-Karim reporting from Mujahid that the sons of the Aws had taken the religion of the Nadir. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 411} In the notes the editor discusses readings of wording in the manuscript and the printed text. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 411} We stop before report 5823, which is cut at the page break.

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-256|2:256]]) from p. 411: from al-Sha'bi (5823): a woman of the Ansar would vow that if her child lived she would place him among the People of the Book; when Islam came the Ansar asked whether they should force their children who were in Judaism to Islam, since they had placed them there thinking Judaism the best of religions, and the verse came down. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 411} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 412} From him (5824) the same, adding that the dividing event between those who chose Judaism and those who chose Islam was the expulsion of the Banu al-Nadir: whoever left with them was theirs and whoever left them chose Islam. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 412} From Ibn Zayd (5825): this part of the verse through what follows is abrogated. From Mujahid and Wa'il from al-Hasan (5826): some of the Ansar were placed to nurse among the Banu al-Nadir, and when the Nadir were expelled their families wanted to attach them to their religion, so the verse came down. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 412}

Others said the sense is that the People of the Book are not forced into the religion once they pay the jizya but are left in their religion; the verse concerns a particular group of unbelievers and nothing of it is abrogated. From Qatada (5827): this Arab tribe was forced into it, being an unlettered people with no scripture they knew, so nothing but Islam was accepted from them, and the People of the Book are not forced if they acknowledge the jizya or tribute. From him (5828): from the Arabs only death or Islam was accepted, while the jizya was accepted from the People of the Book. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 412} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 413} From al-Dahhak (5829): the Prophet was commanded to fight the Arabian peninsula's idol-worshippers, accepting from them only the declaration of faith or the sword, then commanded to accept the jizya from others. From Qatada (5830): the Arabs had no religion and were forced by the sword, while Jews, Christians and Magians are not forced if they give the jizya. From Mujahid (5831): he used to say to a Christian slave of his, become Muslim, then said that this is how they used to be addressed. From Ibn Abbas (5832): that was when people entered Islam and the People of the Book gave the jizya. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 413} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 414}

Others said the verse is abrogated and came down before fighting was made obligatory; from Zayd ibn Aslam (5833): the Prophet stayed in Mecca ten years forcing no one in religion, then the polytheists insisted on fighting and he asked God's permission to fight them and was given it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 414}

Al-Tabari then says that the soundest of these views is that of those who said the verse came down about a particular group of people, meaning the People of the two Books, the Magians and everyone whose staying in his religion and payment of the jizya is allowed, and who denied that any of it is abrogated; this is his choice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 414} He reasons from what he had shown in his Kitab al-Latif on the foundations of rulings: that a ruling abrogates another only if it removes it so the two cannot stand together, and that what is general in form but particular in sense lies outside abrogation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 414} He argues that Muslims transmitted from their Prophet that he forced some on Islam and ruled they be killed if they refused, such as idol-worshippers among the Arab polytheists and the apostate and their like, and that he left others unforced by accepting the jizya and leaving them in their religion, such as the People of the two Books and their like; so the sense is that compulsion is ruled out for anyone from whom the jizya is permitted, and there is no meaning in the claim of abrogation by the permission to fight. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 414} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 415}

To what is reported from Ibn Abbas and others, that it came down about some of the Ansar who wished to force their children, he replies that this is not rejected, but a verse may come down on a particular matter and its rule then be general for whatever matches its sense: those about whom it came down had followed the religion of the people of the Torah before the pact of Islam was established for them, so God forbade their being forced, and the rule became general for anyone in a religion from whose people the jizya may be taken. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 415} He says the definite article on the word for the religion defines the one meant, Islam, and the compulsion denied is that no one is forced into Islam. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 415} All this is al-Tabari's choice and reasoning; the editor's notes correct manuscript readings and refer to his earlier discussion of abrogation, and are his. The reported views are presented as they are and the museum does not decide among them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 412} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 414} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 415}

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-256|2:256]]) from p. 416: al-Tabari mentions another possibility about the word for religion: that the definite article stands in for an implied pronoun, so that the sense is that God, the Exalted and Great, has ruled compulsion out of His religion; he says this view is closer to the verse's meaning in his opinion, and this is his preference. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 415} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 416} He explains that rushd is the verbal noun of attaining truth and rightness and ghayy of straying beyond the truth; he notes the readers read the verb in ([[passage/quran-53-2|53:2]]) with the open vowel, the more eloquent of the two forms; and the sense is that truth is now distinguished from falsehood and the seeker of truth sees the way he seeks, so do not force anyone of the People of the two Books, or those from whom you may take the jizya, to your religion, for whoever turns from right guidance after it is clear, his affair is with his Lord, who is the judge of his punishment in the Hereafter; this is his interpretation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 416} In the notes the editor explains two words and the addition the context requires, and these are his. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 416}

Al-Tabari then turns to the clause on the taghut, saying the interpreters differed on its meaning. Some said it is Satan: from Umar ibn al-Khattab (5834, 5835) via Hassan ibn Fa'id, and from Mujahid (5836), al-Sha'bi (5837), al-Dahhak (5838), Qatada (5839) and al-Suddi (5840). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 416} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 417} Others said it is the sorcerer: from Abu al-Aliya (5841), a report on which, al-Tabari says, Abd al-A'la was contradicted, and from Muhammad (5842). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 417} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 418} Others said it is the soothsayer: from Sa'id ibn Jubayr (5843), from Rufay', who the editor says is Abu al-Aliya al-Riyahi (5844), and from Ibn Jurayj (5845) that they are soothsayers on whom devils descend; and from Jabir ibn Abdullah, asked about the taghuts to which they used to take their disputes: there was one among Juhayna, one among Aslam, one in each tribe, soothsayers on whom Satan descends. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 418} In the notes the editor gives the biography of Hassan ibn Fa'id and corrects names in the chains. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 417} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 418} These reports are presented as transmitted, without a museum preference.

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-256|2:256]]) from p. 419: al-Tabari says the correct view in his opinion on the taghut is that it is everything that is arrogant against God and is worshipped besides Him, whether by coercion of the worshipper or by obedience from him, whether a human, a devil, an idol, a statue or anything else; this is his choice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 419} He holds the word originally to be taghawut from tagha, to exceed one's measure and limit, on the pattern of jabarut, with its letters then transposed as in jadhaba and jabadha; in the note the editor explains the word khalabut and cites a verse about kings, and these are his. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 419} The sense for him is: whoever rejects the lordship of everything worshipped besides God and affirms that God is his God and Lord has taken hold of the most trustworthy thing by which one seeking rescue from God's torment and punishment can hold. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 419}

From Abu al-Darda (5846): he visited a sick neighbour and found him in the throes of death, rattling, whom those present could not understand; he asked whether he wanted to speak, and they said yes, he wants to declare his faith and his rejection of the taghut; he asked how they knew and they said he kept repeating it until his tongue was broken; Abu al-Darda said, your companion has succeeded, and recited the verse. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 419} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 420} In the editor's notes: biographies of the chain, an explanation of the idiom and of the rattling and broken tongue, and the remark that a part of the old division of the manuscript ends here; these are his. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 420}

On the handhold, al-Tabari says the word here is a figure for the faith to which the believer clings, likening his attachment to that of one holding a thing's handle, and that God made faith the most trustworthy of handholds, the form being the feminine of the superlative from reliability. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 421} Mujahid (5847, 5848): faith; al-Suddi (5849): Islam; Sa'id ibn Jubayr (5850, 5851) and al-Dahhak (5852): the declaration of oneness. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 421} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 422} In the note, the biography of Abu al-Sawda' al-Nahdi. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 421}

On the clause that it does not break, al-Tabari says it means it cannot snap, the pronoun referring to the handhold; the sense is that whoever turns from the taghut to belief in God has taken hold of obedience to God in a way that is not to be feared to let him down when he needs it in the terrors of the Hereafter, like one holding a reliable handle that will not snap; the root meaning of the verb is to break, and he cites a verse of al-A'sha; in the note the editor gives the preceding lines of the poem and corrects a wording. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 422} The reports are shown as transmitted, and al-Tabari's choices in them are attributed to him.
