## Summary
Verse 81 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 81 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**Verse 81: the covenant with the prophets.** Al-Tabari says the sense is: remember, People of the Book, when God bound the prophets by a covenant, their covenant being what they bound themselves to of obedience to God in what He commanded and forbade them; the editor refers to the earlier account of the root sense of covenant. Al-Tabari notes that the reciters differed over the particle and the verb after it. Most reciters of the Hijaz and Iraq read the particle with an open vowel and then differed on the verb between the singular and the plural, some reading the singular and others the plural.

**The grammarians on the particle.** Al-Tabari reports that the people of Arabic differed when it is read with the open vowel. Some Basran grammarians said that the first particle is the particle of beginning, as in a speaker's 'surely Zayd is better than you', since 'ma' is a noun and what follows is its relative clause, and that the particle in the answer is the particle of the oath, so that he reinforced it at the start and at the end of the sentence, as one says 'by God, had you come to me, it would have been so', and it may be dispensed with, the predicate of 'ma' then being the answer. He said: if you wish, make the predicate of 'ma' the phrase 'of a book' with the 'of' redundant. Some Kufan grammarians faulted all of that, saying that the particle that enters at the start of a conditional is answered with the answers of oaths, so that when 'ma' and 'la' occur in the answer it is known that it is no reinforcement of the first, and that dropping the 'of' is a mistake, since the 'of' that enters and leaves does not take the place of nouns nor occur in the predicate but only in denial, question and condition. In the notes the editor records the differences between the printed text and the manuscript in these sentences and judges that in one place what the first editors did changed the wording entirely.

**Al-Tabari's choice on the reading with the open vowel.** Al-Tabari holds that, on the reading of those who open the particle, the better view is that 'lama' has the sense of 'whatever', that 'ma' is a conditional particle on which the particle was brought in, the verb with it being in the past form, then answered with what oaths are answered with, so that the first particle became an oath when it met the answer of an oath.

**The reading with the closed vowel.** He mentions another reading with a lowered vowel on the particle, the reading of a group of Kufans, whose readers differed on its sense. Some said 'ma' means 'that which', and the sense is: when God bound the prophets by a covenant on account of what He gave them of the book and of wisdom, then if a messenger comes to you, meaning that Muhammad is mentioned in the Torah, your faith in him shall follow, so that your belief in him is on account of what you have in the Torah of his mention. Others said that on the lowered reading God bound the prophets by a covenant on account of the books He gave them, and the answer is then the answer of the taking, as one says 'I took your covenant that you would do it', since taking a covenant is like administering an oath; so on this view the sense is: when God put the prophets on oath on account of what He gave them, that whenever a messenger attesting to what they hold comes to them they will have faith in him and aid him.

**Al-Tabari prefers the open vowel.** Al-Tabari chooses the reading with the open vowel, because God bound all the prophets by a covenant to confirm every messenger He sends to His creation in what He sent him with, whether they reach him or not, and it is not permissible to describe any of His prophets and messengers as one to whom denying any of His messengers was allowed. Since it is known that to some of them a book was sent down and to some not, the reading with the lowered vowel, meaning on account of the book I gave you, has no understandable face except through a far-fetched interpretation and a deep wrenching.

**Was the covenant taken from the prophets or from the People of the Book?** Al-Tabari then says that the interpreters differed over those from whom the covenant was taken to believe in the messengers of God who confirm what is with them. Some said that God took by it the covenant of the People of the Book and not of their prophets, citing the command to believe and to support, and arguing that the nations are the ones commanded to believe in God's messengers and support them against those who oppose them, whereas for the messengers there is no ground to be ordered to support anyone, since they are the ones in need of help against the unbelievers; and if there are only they and the unbelieving nations, who is there to support the prophet so that a covenant is taken from him to support him?

**Reports of Mujahid and al-Rabi (7323-7325).** Muhammad ibn Amr relays from Abu Asim, from Isa, from Ibn Abi Najih, from Mujahid that this wording is a scribe's error and that in the reading of Ibn Masud it is the covenant of the People of the Book; al-Muthanna relays the same from Abu Hudhayfa, from Shibl, from Ibn Abi Najih, from Mujahid (7324). Al-Muthanna relays from Ishaq, from Abd Allah ibn Abi Jafar, from his father, from al-Rabi that what is meant is the covenant of the People of the Book, that al-Rabi used to read it so and that Ubayy ibn Ka'b used to read it so, and al-Rabi said: do you not see that God speaks of a messenger coming who confirms what is with them, in whom they will believe and whom they will support, that is Muhammad, and that they are the People of the Book (7325). The editor records in a note the reading 'they are' for 'it is' and appends a long note on the report of Mujahid.

**The editor's note on the report of Mujahid.** The editor says that by such a report those among the orientalists and their followers who do so argue for error and corruption in God's book, that they were not the first to say it, since the extremists among the Rafida and similar deniers had said it before them, and that Muslim scholars did not fall short in refuting them; he names among the greatest he has read on this the book al-Intisar li-naql al-Quran by the judge al-Baqillani, a manuscript still unpublished, in the possession of his brother Ahmad Saqr, in which the judge devoted chapters to the reliance of those who say so on the irregular readings and the single-chain additions reported from the early generations, and exposed the weakness of their reliance. He gives the sense of a passage of al-Baqillani's, that though he deems trustworthy those predecessors mentioned, he does not hold that everything reported from them is true, since there is much falsehood in it which proof shows to be fabricated, and part of it may be true or false and is not established by single reports, and that, knowing they agreed to accept the copy of Uthman and to recite, teach and act by it and no other, one need not attend to any of these reports. The editor then says that by 'a scribe's error' Mujahid meant that the reading of Ibn Masud was the one in the final presentation and that the scribe wrote the reading before the final presentation, not that the scribe composed it himself, since the Quran is recited by transmission and inheritance from the Prophet and not by what is written in the leaves, and that the detail belongs elsewhere. (This is the editor's view, relayed by the museum as his, without a verdict.)

**The other party.** Others said that those from whom the covenant was taken were the prophets and not their nations; the account of those who said so follows on the next pages. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 554}
