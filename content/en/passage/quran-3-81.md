## Summary
Verse 81 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 81 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**Verse 81: the covenant with the prophets.** Al-Tabari says the sense is: remember, People of the Book, when God bound the prophets by a covenant, their covenant being what they bound themselves to of obedience to God in what He commanded and forbade them; the editor refers to the earlier account of the root sense of covenant. Al-Tabari notes that the reciters differed over the particle and the verb after it. Most reciters of the Hijaz and Iraq read the particle with an open vowel and then differed on the verb between the singular and the plural, some reading the singular and others the plural.

**The grammarians on the particle.** Al-Tabari reports that the people of Arabic differed when it is read with the open vowel. Some Basran grammarians said that the first particle is the particle of beginning, as in a speaker's 'surely Zayd is better than you', since 'ma' is a noun and what follows is its relative clause, and that the particle in the answer is the particle of the oath, so that he reinforced it at the start and at the end of the sentence, as one says 'by God, had you come to me, it would have been so', and it may be dispensed with, the predicate of 'ma' then being the answer. He said: if you wish, make the predicate of 'ma' the phrase 'of a book' with the 'of' redundant. Some Kufan grammarians faulted all of that, saying that the particle that enters at the start of a conditional is answered with the answers of oaths, so that when 'ma' and 'la' occur in the answer it is known that it is no reinforcement of the first, and that dropping the 'of' is a mistake, since the 'of' that enters and leaves does not take the place of nouns nor occur in the predicate but only in denial, question and condition. In the notes the editor records the differences between the printed text and the manuscript in these sentences and judges that in one place what the first editors did changed the wording entirely.

**Al-Tabari's choice on the reading with the open vowel.** Al-Tabari holds that, on the reading of those who open the particle, the better view is that 'lama' has the sense of 'whatever', that 'ma' is a conditional particle on which the particle was brought in, the verb with it being in the past form, then answered with what oaths are answered with, so that the first particle became an oath when it met the answer of an oath.

**The reading with the closed vowel.** He mentions another reading with a lowered vowel on the particle, the reading of a group of Kufans, whose readers differed on its sense. Some said 'ma' means 'that which', and the sense is: when God bound the prophets by a covenant on account of what He gave them of the book and of wisdom, then if a messenger comes to you, meaning that Muhammad is mentioned in the Torah, your faith in him shall follow, so that your belief in him is on account of what you have in the Torah of his mention. Others said that on the lowered reading God bound the prophets by a covenant on account of the books He gave them, and the answer is then the answer of the taking, as one says 'I took your covenant that you would do it', since taking a covenant is like administering an oath; so on this view the sense is: when God put the prophets on oath on account of what He gave them, that whenever a messenger attesting to what they hold comes to them they will have faith in him and aid him.

**Al-Tabari prefers the open vowel.** Al-Tabari chooses the reading with the open vowel, because God bound all the prophets by a covenant to confirm every messenger He sends to His creation in what He sent him with, whether they reach him or not, and it is not permissible to describe any of His prophets and messengers as one to whom denying any of His messengers was allowed. Since it is known that to some of them a book was sent down and to some not, the reading with the lowered vowel, meaning on account of the book I gave you, has no understandable face except through a far-fetched interpretation and a deep wrenching.

**Was the covenant taken from the prophets or from the People of the Book?** Al-Tabari then says that the interpreters differed over those from whom the covenant was taken to believe in the messengers of God who confirm what is with them. Some said that God took by it the covenant of the People of the Book and not of their prophets, citing the command to believe and to support, and arguing that the nations are the ones commanded to believe in God's messengers and support them against those who oppose them, whereas for the messengers there is no ground to be ordered to support anyone, since they are the ones in need of help against the unbelievers; and if there are only they and the unbelieving nations, who is there to support the prophet so that a covenant is taken from him to support him?

**Reports of Mujahid and al-Rabi (7323-7325).** Muhammad ibn Amr relays from Abu Asim, from Isa, from Ibn Abi Najih, from Mujahid that this wording is a scribe's error and that in the reading of Ibn Masud it is the covenant of the People of the Book; al-Muthanna relays the same from Abu Hudhayfa, from Shibl, from Ibn Abi Najih, from Mujahid (7324). Al-Muthanna relays from Ishaq, from Abd Allah ibn Abi Jafar, from his father, from al-Rabi that what is meant is the covenant of the People of the Book, that al-Rabi used to read it so and that Ubayy ibn Ka'b used to read it so, and al-Rabi said: do you not see that God speaks of a messenger coming who confirms what is with them, in whom they will believe and whom they will support, that is Muhammad, and that they are the People of the Book (7325). The editor records in a note the reading 'they are' for 'it is' and appends a long note on the report of Mujahid.

**The editor's note on the report of Mujahid.** The editor says that by such a report those among the orientalists and their followers who do so argue for error and corruption in God's book, that they were not the first to say it, since the extremists among the Rafida and similar deniers had said it before them, and that Muslim scholars did not fall short in refuting them; he names among the greatest he has read on this the book al-Intisar li-naql al-Quran by the judge al-Baqillani, a manuscript still unpublished, in the possession of his brother Ahmad Saqr, in which the judge devoted chapters to the reliance of those who say so on the irregular readings and the single-chain additions reported from the early generations, and exposed the weakness of their reliance. He gives the sense of a passage of al-Baqillani's, that though he deems trustworthy those predecessors mentioned, he does not hold that everything reported from them is true, since there is much falsehood in it which proof shows to be fabricated, and part of it may be true or false and is not established by single reports, and that, knowing they agreed to accept the copy of Uthman and to recite, teach and act by it and no other, one need not attend to any of these reports. The editor then says that by 'a scribe's error' Mujahid meant that the reading of Ibn Masud was the one in the final presentation and that the scribe wrote the reading before the final presentation, not that the scribe composed it himself, since the Quran is recited by transmission and inheritance from the Prophet and not by what is written in the leaves, and that the detail belongs elsewhere. (This is the editor's view, relayed by the museum as his, without a verdict.)

**The other party.** Others said that those from whom the covenant was taken were the prophets and not their nations; the account of those who said so follows on the next pages. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 554}

**Those who said the covenant was from the prophets (7326-7332).** Al-Muthanna and Ahmad ibn Hazim relay from Abu Nuaym, from Sufyan, from Habib, from Said ibn Jubayr, from Ibn Abbas that God took the covenant of the prophets only over their peoples (7326). Al-Hasan ibn Yahya relays from Abd al-Razzaq, from Mamar, from Ibn Tawus, from his father that the sense is that they confirm one another (7327), and al-Qasim relays from al-Husayn, from Hajjaj, from Ibn Jurayj, from Ibn Tawus, from his father that God took the covenant of the first of the prophets that they would confirm and believe in what the last of them brought (7328). Al-Muthanna relays from Ishaq, from Abd Allah ibn Hashim, from Sayf ibn Umar, from Abu Ruq, from Abu Ayyub, from Ali ibn Abi Talib that God sent no prophet from Adam onward without taking from him a covenant about Muhammad, that if he were sent while the prophet lived he would believe in him and support him, and commanded him to take the covenant from his people (7329). In a note the editor says the correct form of the name is that of the manuscript, Sayf ibn Umar al-Tamimi, author of the books on the apostasy wars and the conquests, whom al-Tabari often cites in his history; he quotes Ibn Adi that some of his reports are well known but most are rejected and not corroborated, Ibn Hibban that he relays fabrications from reliable authorities, adds that they said he used to fabricate reports and accused him of heresy, and quotes al-Hakim that he was accused of heresy and is rejected in transmission. Bishr relays from Yazid, from Said, from Qatada (7330) that this was a covenant God took from the prophets, that they confirm one another and convey God's book and messages; they conveyed them to their peoples and took from them that they would believe in Muhammad, confirm him and support him. Muhammad ibn al-Husayn relays from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal, from Asbat, from al-Suddi (7331) that God sent no prophet since Noah without taking his covenant to believe in Muhammad and support him if he came out in his lifetime, and otherwise taking from his people that they would believe in him and support him if he came out while they lived. Muhammad ibn Sinan relays from Abd al-Kabir ibn Abd al-Majid Abu Bakr al-Hanafi, from Abbad ibn Mansur (7332) that he asked al-Hasan about the verse and he said: God took the covenant of the prophets that the last of you would convey what the first did and that you would not differ.

**Those who said it was from the prophets and their nations (7333 and 7334).** Others said the sense is that God took the covenant from the prophets and their nations, the mention of the prophets sufficing in place of their nations, because mention of the covenant on the one followed implies it on the followers, nations being followers of prophets. This has the report of Ibn Humayd from Salama, from Ibn Ishaq, from Muhammad ibn Abi Muhammad, from Ikrima or Said ibn Jubayr, from Ibn Abbas that God then mentioned what He took from the People of the Book and their prophets of covenant to confirm Muhammad when he came to them and their acknowledging it against themselves (7333), and Abu Kurayb relays it from Yunus ibn Bukayr, from Ibn Ishaq, from Muhammad ibn Abi Muhammad the client of Zayd ibn Thabit, from Said ibn Jubayr or Ikrima, from Ibn Abbas the same (7334). The editor notes that both complete the two reports that closed the commentary on verse 79 in the Sira of Ibn Hisham, and corrects the printed text in a note by removing a pronoun it added without need.

**Al-Tabari's choice.** Al-Tabari says that the most correct of these views is that the verse reports God's taking the covenant from His prophets to confirm one another, and that the prophets took from their nations and followers a covenant like the one their Lord took from them, to confirm God's prophets and messengers in what they brought, since the prophets were sent to their nations for that. None of those who confirm the messengers claimed that a prophet was sent to a nation to deny any of God's prophets and proofs among His servants; rather all nations, even if some denied some of God's prophets by rejecting their prophethood, acknowledge that whoever's prophethood is established must be confirmed in religion, so this is a covenant to which all of them assent. He holds that the claim that the covenant was taken only from the nations and not the prophets has no meaning, since God reported that He took it from the prophets; saying that He did not take it from them is the same as saying that He did not order them to convey what they were sent with, and God has stated that He ordered them to convey it, for both are reports from God about them, and if doubt is allowed over one it is allowed over the other. As for what al-Rabi ibn Anas offered as proof that the People of the Book are meant, namely the mention of belief and support, al-Tabari says that it is no witness to what he said, since prophets were ordered to confirm one another, and their confirming one another is support of one another.

**Who is meant by the coming of the messenger? (7335 and 7336).** Al-Tabari then says that they differed over those meant by the coming of the confirming messenger. Some said it is the prophets, from whom pledges were taken that they confirm one another and support him, and he refers to the reports he has given. Others said it is the People of the Book, ordered to confirm Muhammad when God sent him and to support him, a pledge taken from them in their books. Others, of those who said that those from whom the covenant was taken were the prophets, said that the coming of the confirming messenger refers to the People of the Book. This has the report of al-Hasan ibn Yahya from Abd al-Razzaq, from Mamar, from Ibn Tawus, from his father (7335) that God took the covenant of the prophets to confirm one another, and then said that the part on the coming of the messenger is for the People of the Book, from whom God took the covenant to believe in Muhammad and confirm him. Al-Muthanna relays from Ishaq, from Ibn Abi Jafar, from his father that Qatada said (7336) that God took from the prophets their covenant that they confirm one another and convey God's book and message to His servants; they conveyed them to their peoples and took the pledges of the People of the Book in their book that they would believe in Muhammad, confirm him and support him. The editor refers to the earlier reports 7326-7332 and 7323-7325.

**Al-Tabari's choice on those meant, and the sense of the verse for him.** Al-Tabari says the most correct view in his opinion is that all of it is a report from God about His prophets, that He took their covenant to it and bound them to call their nations to it and to acknowledge it, because the start of the verse reports that He took their covenant, then it describes what the covenant was; and that the prophets in turn took the covenants of their nations, since they were sent to call God's servants to adhere to what they were commanded to adhere to in themselves, the confirmation of God's messengers. He sums up the sense of the verse as: remember, People of the Book, when God made a covenant with the prophets that whatever He gave them of book and wisdom, and then a messenger came to them from Him confirming what they have, they were to confirm and support him. The editor notes his handling of the pronouns, preferring the manuscript to the printed text.

**The report of al-Suddi and al-Tabari's reply (7337).** Muhammad ibn al-Husayn relays from Ahmad, from Asbat, from al-Suddi that God is saying to the Jews that He took the covenant of the prophets about Muhammad, who is mentioned in the book they have, so that the reading on his view is with the preposition 'bi' and not the particle 'li': when God took the covenant of the prophets on account of what He gave the Jews of book and wisdom. Al-Tabari says this interpretation would have had a face had the revealed wording been with 'bi', but the revealed wording is with 'li', and it is not permissible in the language of any Arab to say, in the sense of 'bi', what comes with 'li'. The editor corrects the manuscript and printed text in two notes, saying in the last that the copyist wrote 'it has no face' in place of 'it has a face'.

**The clause of acknowledgement and of taking.** Al-Tabari says the sense is: when God took the covenant of the prophets as described, He said to them: did you acknowledge the covenant you pledged to Me, that whenever a messenger comes to you from Me confirming what is with you, you would confirm him and support him, and did you take upon that My pledge and charge (which is the sense of the word isr), and accept it from Me and approve it? He explains that 'taking' here is acceptance and approval, as when one says the ruler took the pledge of allegiance from him, meaning that he swore allegiance to him and accepted and approved his rule. He says the meaning of 'isr' and the dispute over it has been explained earlier, and the editor refers to the place in this volume; in a note he supplies the interrogative particle that fell from the manuscript and the printed text and calls its omission a corruption. Al-Tabari says the conjunction was dropped from this sentence because it begins a new speech, as in similar cases. As to the prophets' reply, he says they said: we acknowledge what You bound us to of believing in Your messengers whom You send confirming what we have of Your books, and of supporting them.

**The witnessing and the report of Ali (7338).** Al-Tabari says God said to them: act as witnesses, prophets, to what I took your covenant on, of believing in the confirmation of my messengers who come to you confirming what you have of the book and wisdom and supporting them against yourselves and your followers among the nations when you take their covenant on that, and I am a witness over you and over them with you. Al-Muthanna relays from Ishaq, from Abd Allah ibn Hashim, from Sayf ibn Umar, from Abu Ruq, from Abu Ayyub, from Ali ibn Abi Talib that the meaning is: act as witnesses against your nations to that, and I am witness over you and over them (7338). The editor says that the printed text again has 'Sayf ibn Amr' and that he will correct it in many later places without remark. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 561}
