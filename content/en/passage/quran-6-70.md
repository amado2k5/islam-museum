## Summary
Verse 70 of [[passage/quran-6|Surat al-An'am]], a Meccan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 70 of 165 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

On "leave those who take their religion as play and amusement, and whom the life of this world has deluded; but remind by it, lest a soul be given up for what it earned, having no protector or intercessor apart from God" al-Tabari says to his prophet Muhammad: leave these who took God's religion and their obedience to him as play and amusement, making their share of obeying him playing with his signs and amusement and mockery of them when they hear them and they are recited to them; turn away from them, for I am lying in wait for them, and behind them lies vengeance on them and punishment for what they do and for their being deluded by the adornment of the life of this world and forgetting the return to God and going to him after death. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 441}

13401 and 13402, from Mujahid: "leave those who take their religion as play and amusement" is like God's words, [[passage/quran-74-11|74:11]], "leave me with him whom I created alone".

The editor refers for "leave" and "play" to earlier passages.

Al-Tabari says God abrogated this verse by his words, [[passage/quran-9-5|9:5]], "kill the polytheists wherever you find them", and a number of interpreters said so. 13403, from Qatada: "leave those who take their religion as play and amusement", then it was revealed in Surat Bara'a and he was told to fight them; 13404, from Qatada, the like, giving the verse. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 442}

As for "remind by it, lest a soul be given up for what it earned" it means: remind, Muhammad, by this Qur'an those who turn away from you and from it; "lest a soul be given up" means that a soul not be given up, as in [[passage/quran-4-176|4:176]], "God makes clear to you lest you go astray", meaning that you not go astray; the sense is: remind them by it that they may believe and follow the truth that came to them from God, so that their souls not be given up for the burdens they earned; but "not" is dropped because the speech shows it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 442}

The interpreters differed on "lest a soul be given up". Some said: lest it be handed over. 13405, from Ikrima: handed over; 13406 and 13407, from al-Hasan: handed over; 13408 and 13409, from Mujahid: handed over; 13410, from Mujahid on "those who were given up": handed over. Others said it means held fast: 13411 and 13412, from Qatada: taken and held; 13413, from Ibn Zayd: that a soul be taken for what it earned. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 443}

Others said it means disgraced, and 13414, from Ibn Abbas: disgraced. Others said: repaid, and 13415, from al-Kalbi: repaid. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 444}

The root of "ibsal" is forbidding; one says "I made the place bas'l" when you forbid it so that it is not approached. Al-Tabari cites a line of Damra ibn Damra al-Nahshali in which he tells his wife, who blamed him for his generosity, that her blame and reproach are forbidden to him. The editor tells the story of the verses: his wife reproached him for milking his camels and slaughtering them for his guest and household, made stinginess dear to him and forbade him to spend in the drought; he answered by the grief his cousins and guests would feel at his death, which the camels would not, mentions the Jahiliyya belief that a slain man's spirit becomes a bird like an owl crying at his grave, explains the words of the verses and points to their sources. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 444}

From it also comes "a fierce lion", meaning that nothing approaches him, as if he had forbidden himself, then it became a description of anything so strong that it is shunned; "give the charmer his due" means his fee; "a bitter drink" means one left alone; and the one given up for a crime is the one held in pledge for it, called "mubsal" because he is forbidden everything but what he is pledged for and handed over by. He cites a line of Awf ibn al-Ahwas al-Kilabi about handing over his sons as pledges without a crime or spilt blood, and a line of al-Shanfara saying he hopes for no life to please him through the nights, handed over for crimes. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 445}

The editor says the wording in print and manuscript is a plain error and he inferred the right one from the course of the explanation and al-Farra's Ma'ani al-Qur'an, adding the bracketed words; that "bisaylatihi" in print is an error whose right form is in the manuscript; he gives the sources of Awf's line and its story, explaining "mufariq" as the plural of a camel parted from her young and "hiqaq" as plural of "hiqqa", and that the poet says he was glad to give the property to spare blood and keep bonds; that al-Shanfara's line is also attributed to Ta'abbata Sharran and comes from well-known verses in which he asks his people not to bury him, and that "sameer al-layali" means the nights for ever.

Abu Jafar says: so the sense is: remind by the Qur'an those who plunge into our signs and others who follow their way among the polytheists, lest a soul be given up for its sins and its unbelief in its Lord, and be pledged and locked up for what it earned of crimes in God's punishment; "it has, apart from God" says: when it is handed over for its sins and pledged for what it earned of wrongs, none to help it and rescue it from God who repaid it for its sins its due, "nor intercessor" to intercede for it by a standing with him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 446}

The editor refers for "earn", "apart from" and "intercessor" to earlier passages.

On "even if it offers every ransom, it will not be accepted from it" he says: even if the soul given up for what it earned offers every ransom. One says "adala ya'dilu" when one ransoms, and from it comes [[passage/quran-5-95|5:95]], "or the equivalent of that in fasting", being what is equal of another kind. 13416, from Qatada: had it come with the earth full of gold it would not be accepted from it; 13417, from al-Suddi: had it come with the earth full of gold to ransom itself with, it would not have been accepted; 13418, from Ibn Zayd: even if it ransoms, having the world and what is in it to ransom with, no equivalent for itself is taken from him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 447}

Some scholars of Arabic took it as: even if it pays every just share it will not be accepted, and said it is repentance in life, which is the view of Abu Ubayda in Majaz al-Qur'an. Al-Tabari says there is no sense in what he said, since God accepts the repentance of every penitent in this world. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 448}

On "those are the ones given up for what they earned; for them is a drink of boiling water and a painful punishment because they used to disbelieve" he says: these who, if they ransom themselves from God's punishment on the day of resurrection with every ransom, would not have it taken, are "those given up for what they earned", that is, handed over to God's punishment and pledged by it as repayment for the sins and burdens they earned in this world, "for them is a drink of hamim". Hamim in Arab speech is the hot, being a "mahmum" turned into the form fa'il, and from it the bath is called hammam for heating the body; he cites a line of al-Murraqqish describing a pampered woman who has every evening a censer with aloes to perfume with, and hot water. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 448}

The editor refers for "given up" and "earn" to earlier passages, and says al-Murraqqish's line is from his poem about Ibnat Ajlan, the maid of Fatima bint al-Mundhir, who had a castle at Kazima; that "miqtara" is a censer and "kiba" a kind of aloeswood; and that the poet describes her luxury and care for her body.

We stop here at the foot of p. 448, where al-Tabari backs the sense of "hamim" with the verse of al-Murraqqish and before his citation of Abu Dhu'ayb al-Hudhali on a horse, and he continues on p. 449.

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
