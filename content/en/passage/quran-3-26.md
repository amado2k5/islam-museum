## Verse 26 in al-Tabari's commentary (Shakir ed., volume 6, pp. 295-301)

**The meaning of 'Allahumma'.** Al-Tabari ([[passage/quran-3-26|3:26]]) says the opening command means: say, Muhammad, 'O God.' He notes that the Arabic grammarians differed on the final doubled mim of this word, which is a vocative of a single noun that would normally take the nominative ending, and on the entry of the mim into it, since its origin is 'Allah' without a mim. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 295}

The first view is that the mim was added because this name is not called with the vocative particle as names without the definite article are, as in 'O Zayd'; so the mim was made a substitute for the particle, as they said fam, ibnam, hum, zurqum and sathum, words that lose a letter and take a mim in its place. The particle was dropped from the word and the mim set at the end of the name as its replacement. In a long note the editor says that in both sources a word before 'hum' has no sense he can accept; that it appears so in al-Farra's Ma'ani al-Qur'an; that the publishers of that book took it as the plural pronoun with a stated origin, and that an earlier publisher of al-Tabari thought it corrupted from two other words listed by al-Suyuti in al-Muzhir. The editor rejects both opinions and confesses that after all his searching he cannot identify the word, hoping others have the knowledge he was denied. He glosses zurqum as very blue-eyed and sathum as large in the rear, and cites a rajaz verse describing two women. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 296}

Others rejected this, saying they had heard the Arabs call with the particle as well as with the mim, which would not be so if the claim of substitution were right, since the substitute and the thing replaced would not be combined. They cite a verse heard from the Arabs in which a woman, after prayer or takbir, says the word with the particle and asks for the return of their old man safe, a verse with a variant reading. They add that the Arabs added such a mim only in shortened form to defective nouns like al-fam, ibnam and hum. They hold the word to be 'Allah' joined with 'umma', meaning 'O God, direct us to good', which became frequent in speech and fused, the vowel of the hamza of 'umma' passing to the letter before it; they see halumma likewise, 'hal' joined to 'umma' and left with its a-vowel. Some Arabs, they say, say 'O God, forgive me' with the connecting alif, and some pronounce the hamza of the alif of 'Allah' at times and connect it at others: one who connects it follows its origin as the same alif and lam as the article, while one who pronounces the hamza imagines it part of the root letters. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 297}

The editor's notes say the author of the cited verse is unknown, that it occurs in Ma'ani al-Qur'an, al-Zajjaji's al-Jumal, al-Insaf, al-Khizana and Lisan al-Arab with varying transmission, and was brought as evidence of an added 'ma' after the vocative word; Kufan grammarians added a further line. He keeps the manuscript reading in the phrase 'like al-fam, ibnam and hum', since it agrees with al-Farra's own text, and restores the faulty reading in both sources to its origin, blaming the copyist's haste. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 297}

Al-Tabari adds that those holding this view say the word became so frequent that its mim was lightened in some dialects, and they cite two verses for the pronounced hamza, one a blessing on God's name and the other by al-A'sha, about a false oath like that of Abu Riyah which the great God hears. The editor's notes tell the story behind the verse: Abu Riyah of the Banu Dubay'a killed a neighbour of a man of the Banu Sa'd ibn Tha'laba; they asked him to swear or pay the blood-price; he swore, and was killed after his oath, so the Arabs made him a proverb for an oath that avails nothing. The verses speak of the destruction of Iram and Ad, followed by Thamud and Qidar, the hamstringer of the she-camel; 'irar' means fighting; and whoever explains the verb 'ta'adaw' as 'cooperated and grew many' is mistaken, for it comes from putting on the instruments of war so that they destroyed one another. He corrects the name Abu Riyah to the spelling with a y, and reports that another recited the verse differently, from al-Farra' on the authority of al-Kisa'i, and that Abu Ubayda gave a different ending. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 298-299}

**Owner of dominion.** Al-Tabari says the sense is: O owner of dominion, you to whom belongs the dominion of this world and the next, to the exclusion of others. He reports (6789), from Ibn Humayd from Salama from Ibn Ishaq from Muhammad ibn Ja'far ibn al-Zubayr: Lord of the servants and of dominion, none decides among them but you. The editor says this is in Ibn Hisham's Sira (2: 227) in near wording and is the remainder of the reports ending at number 6776. Giving dominion means granting it to whom he wills, making him king and empowering him over whom he wills; taking it away means removing it from whom he wills to remove it from. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 299}

Al-Tabari says the clause 'that you take it from him' is left out because what precedes indicates it, as one says 'take what you wish and be in what you wish', meaning take what you wish to take, and as in [[passage/quran-82-8|82:8]], where the sense is that he composed you in whatever form he willed to compose you. The editor notes this abridges al-Farra' (1: 204-205), who did it justice, and that a phrase about removing dominion fell out of the print and he restored it from the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 299-300}

It was said that the verse came down in answer to the Prophet's request that God make the kingdoms of Persia and Rome belong to his community. Reports: (6790) from Bishr from Yazid from Sa'id from Qatada, 'it was mentioned to us' that the Prophet asked his Lord this and God sent the verse down; (6791) from al-Muthanna from Ishaq from Ibn Abi Ja'far from his father from Qatada, 'it was mentioned to us, and God knows best'. From Mujahid it is related that dominion here means prophethood (6792, from Muhammad ibn Amr from Abu Asim from Isa from Ibn Abi Najih; 6793, from al-Muthanna from Abu Hudhayfa from Shibl from Ibn Abi Najih, the same). The editor refers to the earlier gloss of 'dominion'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 300-301}

**Honour and humiliation.** Al-Tabari says honouring means giving dominion and authority and extending power, humbling means taking dominion away and setting the enemy over him; all good is in God's hand and no one else can do it, for he is able over all things, unlike the rest of his creatures and unlike those whom the polytheists of the People of the Book and of the unlettered Arabs took as gods and lords besides him, such as the Messiah and the rivals the unlettered took. He reports (6794), from Ibn Humayd from Salama from Ibn Ishaq from Muhammad ibn Ja'far ibn al-Zubayr, that this is in God's hand and not another's, and none but he has power for this by his authority and might. The editor notes that Ibn Hisham's wording is close and that this too is the remainder of earlier reports. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 301}
