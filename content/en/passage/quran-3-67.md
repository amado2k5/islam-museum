## Summary
Verse 67 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 67 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**Verse 67: Abraham neither Jew nor Christian.** Al-Tabari says this is God's denial of the claim of the Jews and Christians who argued about Abraham and his creed and said he followed theirs, an acquittal of him from them and a statement that they differ from his religion, and a judgement for the people of Islam and the community of Muhammad that they are the people of his religion, on his path and laws, to the exclusion of other communities. He says Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian nor one of those who worship idols and images or a creature in place of its Creator, but was a hanif, that is one who follows God's command and obedience, upright on the road of guidance he was told to keep, and a Muslim, that is humble before God in his heart, submissive in his limbs and compliant with what was made obligatory for him. He says he explained the disagreement of interpreters on the sense of hanif earlier and indicated the better-founded view. The editor notes that the print quoted the verse in its usual reading, that the manuscript copyist wrote a faint, unclear negation which the publisher could not read, and that he restored al-Tabari's wording. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 493} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 494}

**Reports 7211 and 7212.** In report 7211 (Ishaq ibn Shahin al-Wasiti, from Khalid ibn Abdallah, from Dawud, from Amir) the Jews said Abraham was on their religion and the Christians said he was on theirs, so God sent down the verse, giving them the lie and refuting their argument, meaning the Jews who claimed he died a Jew. Report 7212 (al-Muthanna, from Ishaq, from Ibn Abi Jafar, from his father, from al-Rabi) is the same. The editor says Abu Jafar narrated from Ishaq ibn Shahin in places in his history but that he found no biography of him, that Khalid ibn Abdallah ibn Abd al-Rahman Abu al-Haytham al-Muzani al-Wasiti is reliable, a memoriser with sound reports, that Dawud is Ibn Abi Hind and Amir is al-Shabi. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 494}

**Report 7213: Zayd ibn Amr ibn Nufayl.** Al-Tabari gives (Yunus ibn Abd al-Ala, from Ibn Wahb, from Yaqub ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Zuhri, from Musa ibn Uqba, from Salim ibn Abdallah, the transmitter saying he thinks he was narrating it from his father) that Zayd ibn Amr ibn Nufayl went out to Syria asking about the religion in order to follow it. He met a Jewish scholar and said he might adopt their religion and asked to be told of it; the Jew said he would not be of their religion until he took his share of God's wrath. Zayd said he fled only from God's wrath and would bear none of it as long as he could, and asked if the Jew could direct him to a religion without this. The Jew said he knew only that it would be the hanif way, and when asked what that was said: the religion of Abraham, who was neither a Jew nor a Christian and worshipped none but God. Zayd left and met a Christian scholar and asked the same, and was told he would not be of their religion until he took his share of God's curse; he answered as before and the Christian said much as the Jew had, that he knew only the hanif way. He left satisfied with what the two had agreed about Abraham, raised his hands to God and said: O God, I call You to witness that I am on the religion of Abraham. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 495}

**The editor's notes on report 7213.** He says the print added a negative before "I am able" which is not in the manuscript, a grave error that contradicts the version in al-Bukhari from which he will trace the report; that the print has a feminine form in two places where the masculine form is correct as in al-Bukhari; and that he doubts the wording "he kept raising his hands", thinking it an old corruption of al-Bukhari's text "when he went out he raised his hands and said", in which the three words were each altered, the context requiring al-Bukhari's wording. He then gives the transmitter Yaqub ibn Abd al-Rahman ibn Muhammad ibn Abdallah al-Zuhri, who lived in Alexandria, is reliable and is cited by al-Bukhari, Muslim, Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi and al-Nasai. His remaining notes continue on p. 496. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 495}

**Tracing report 7213 and the change of transmitter in the manuscript.** The editor says al-Bukhari narrated this report (Fath al-Bari 7: 109-110) through Fudayl ibn Sulayman from Musa ibn Uqba in wording like al-Tabari's with some differences. He says a part of the old division ends at this point, and that the manuscript has here a note pointing to the commentary on the next verse, followed by a prayer of thanks and blessing, then a new invocation and a new chain: Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Dawud ibn Sulayman informed us, Muhammad ibn Jarir al-Tabari narrated to us. The editor sees this as something new in this copy: all that came before was the close of the old division in the transmission of Abu Muhammad al-Farghani from al-Tabari, and now the commentary's transmission begins with another chain through a man other than al-Farghani, who is the one famous for transmitting the commentary; so he recorded the chain at the head of the commentary, and wonders whether Abu Bakr's transmission runs to the end of the book or covers only a part of it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 496}

**Biography of Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi.** The editor says the transmitter of the commentary from this point is Muhammad ibn Dawud ibn Sulayman al-Baghdadi, the jurist, Abu Bakr, who settled in Egypt and narrated there from al-Tabari and Uthman ibn Nasr al-Tai, and from whom Abu al-Fath Abd al-Wahid ibn Muhammad ibn Masrur al-Balkhi narrated; he was reliable. He quotes al-Khatib al-Baghdadi in his history, through a chain to Abu Said ibn Yunus, that he came to Egypt, held the judgeship at Tinnis, transmitted al-Tabari's books, was heard from by a group of Baghdadis, was clean in conduct and sensible, administered the endowments office in Egypt, and died on a Thursday with three nights left of Jumada II in the year 336 AH (a Hijri date as the editor quotes it, not converted). He says he found no other biography of him in the History of Baghdad, in al-Kindi's book on the judges of Egypt or in any other book at hand, and hopes to find elsewhere in the commentary something that clarifies his transmission. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 496} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 497}
