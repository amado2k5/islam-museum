## Summary
Verse 4 of [[passage/quran-1|Surat al-Fatiha]], a Meccan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 4 of 7 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

## In al-Tabari's commentary
Al-Tabari opens "the interpretation of the words 'Master of the Day of Judgement'" by noting that the readers differ in reciting it: some read "malik" (king), some "malik" with a long first vowel, "owner" (the editor's text spells both alike in transliteration), and some "malika" with a fatha on the last letter. He says he has gathered the reports of whoever is reported to have had a reading here in his book on the readings, and has given his choice and the ground for it, so he does not repeat that here, since his aim in this book is to explain the meaning of the verses and not their readings. He begins by saying that the people of knowledge of Arabic do not differ that "malik" (king) comes from "mulk" (kingship). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 148}
Al-Tabari goes on: for those who read "malik yawm al-din" (King), the sense is that God alone is King on the Day of Judgement, apart from all His creatures who were tyrant kings in the world and contested His kingship and greatness, and who, on meeting Him, will be the small and humbled, as [[passage/quran-40-16|40:16]] says of His sole rule on that day. For those who read "malik" (Owner) he gives Ibn Abbas's report through al-Dahhak that no one will share with Him a ruling on that day as they did in the world, along with [[passage/quran-78-38|78:38]], [[passage/quran-20-108|20:108]] and [[passage/quran-21-28|21:28]]. The editor says the chain is weak and that Ibn Kathir quotes it and says others of the Companions and Followers said the same. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 149}
Al-Tabari's own choice is that the sounder of the two readings and the better of the two interpretations is "malik" in the sense of kingship, because acknowledging His sole kingship makes it due and it has the excellence of a king over an owner, since no king is not an owner, whereas an owner may not be a king. He adds that in the preceding verse God had already told that He is the owner of all the worlds, their master, their reformer and merciful to them, so the fitting attribute to follow is one the earlier words did not contain, and repeating "lord" in the word "owner" would be repeating different words of one meaning with no benefit; the attribute not yet given is that He is King. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 149} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 150}
He answers those who think "Lord of the worlds" speaks only of His lordship in this world, so that it needed joining to "Master of the Day of Judgement" to show it holds in the next: if "Lord of the worlds" could be limited to this world without evidence from the wording of revelation, a report from the Prophet or a rational proof, then someone else could limit it to the world of the age in which it was sent down, when the world of each age is other than that of the age after it. He cites [[passage/quran-45-16|45:16]] on the Children of Israel being favoured over the worlds, and [[passage/quran-3-110|3:110]] on the community of Muhammad as the best, so the Children of Israel of the Prophet's time, who denied him, were not the best of the worlds. He asks the proponent what difference there is between his view and that of someone who limits it to Muhammad's time. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 150} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 151} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 152}
He also answers the view that "Master of the Day of Judgement" means the One who has power to bring the Day about: the same objection applies, since raising the dead is the return of the creatures He called His in "Lord of the worlds". As for the reading with fatha on the kaf, he says it is a vocative, "O Master of the Day of Judgement", like "Joseph, turn away from this" in [[passage/quran-12-29|12:29]], and gives lines by a pre-Islamic poet of Banu Asad and another in the vocative; the editor tells the story behind the first line. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 152} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 153}
Al-Tabari says what drove the reader to that reading was his puzzlement over "You alone we worship" after the genitive "Master of the Day of Judgement": thinking it could not be addressed after that, he put it in the accusative as a vocative. Had he known that "praise be to God, Lord of the worlds" is God's command to His servant to say it, as in Ibn Abbas's report that Gabriel told Muhammad "say this and say that", and that the Arabs address, then report of an absent person, and return to address, the matter would have been easy. He gives as examples a line by Abu Kabir al-Hudhali that returns to speaking to the dead man after speaking of him, a line attributed to Labid that returns to addressing his own soul (the editor notes Ibn Sallam's view that these two lines are forged), and [[passage/quran-10-22|10:22]], which addresses and then returns to speaking of the absent. So the reading with fatha is forbidden, he says, because the whole body of readers and scholars agreed to reject it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 153} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 154}
Under "the interpretation of 'the Day of Judgement'" he says "din" here means reckoning and requital for deeds, citing a line of Ka'b ibn Ju'ayl and another attributed to a Kilabi, addressed to a king that his power will pass and he will be repaid as he acts; the editor tells their story. He adds [[passage/quran-82-9|82:9-10]], where requital is called "din", and [[passage/quran-56-86|56:86]], meaning "not repaid for your deeds nor reckoned with". He says "din" has other senses in Arabic, to be mentioned where they occur. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 155}

We stop here at the foot of p. 155, at the account of "din" as reckoning and requital; the next batch begins at p. 156, with the reports of the early commentators on it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 155}
