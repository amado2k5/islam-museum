## Summary
Verse 153 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 153 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**The two readings of the first verb (pp. 299-300).** Abu Ja'far: the sense is that God pardoned you, believers, in that He did not wipe you out for your sins and flight, when you were going off and none of you turned back to another. The readers differed on the verb. Most readers of the Hijaz, Iraq and Syria, except al-Hasan al-Basri, read it with damma on the first letter and kasra on the third, and this is his reading, for the agreement of the authoritative readers and their rejection of whatever differs. It is reported of al-Hasan al-Basri that he read it with fatha on both (8047, through Ahmad ibn Yusuf from al-Qasim ibn Sallam from Hajjaj from Harun from Yunus ibn 'Ubayd from al-Hasan). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 299} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 300}

**The case of those who read it with damma (pp. 300-301).** They take it that when the men fled from their enemy they went along the valley, and they say Ubayy's reading adds mention of the valley (8048, through Ahmad ibn Yusuf from Abu 'Ubayd from Hajjaj from Harun). They say flight over level ground and valley bottoms and ravines is 'isi'dad', not 'su'ud', since 'su'ud' is climbing up mountains, ladders and steps; going along level ground or downhill is 'isi'dad', as people say 'we set out from Mecca' for beginning a journey from it, and 'we set out from Kufa to Khurasan' for leaving it to travel there. They add that most commentators interpreted it as the men taking to the valley floor when routed. Qatada (8049): that was Uhud day; they went along the valley in flight while the Prophet called to them from behind: to me, servants of God, to me, servants of God. The editor notes the manuscript has a blank here, that the printed text is right and agrees with al-Durr al-Manthur (2: 87), despite an evident slip in the Durr, and that the publisher of the printed edition added a word before the call, which he removed because the manuscript's form is a mis-copying of the repeated call. Earlier (p. 300) he also gives a correction to the phrase 'flight on level ground' and cites Abu 'Ubayda's Majaz al-Qur'an (1: 105) and al-Farra's Ma'ani al-Qur'an (1: 239). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 300} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 301}

**The case of those who said they climbed the mountain (pp. 301-302).** Abu Ja'far: he thinks al-Hasan, with fatha on both letters, took it that when the men were routed they climbed the mountain, and a number of commentators said this. Al-Suddi (8050): when the idolaters charged the Muslims at Uhud and routed them, some entered Medina and some went up the mountain to the rock and stood on it, while the Messenger of God called to the people: to me, servants of God, to me, servants of God; God mentions their climbing the mountain and then the Prophet's call to them. Mujahid (8051 and 8052): they took refuge with the Prophet and went climbing the mountain while the Messenger called to them from behind. Ibn 'Abbas through Ibn Jurayj (8053): they climbed at Uhud in flight. The editor notes that 8050 is part of the earlier report 7943 with additions, and is also in al-Tabari's history (3: 20) with an addition there. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 301} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 302}

**Al-Tabari's choice (p. 302).** Abu Ja'far holds that the sounder of the two readings is the one with damma and kasra, meaning going quickly and fleeing over level ground or low places, on the agreement of the authoritative readers; in that agreement he sees clear evidence that the sounder of the two interpretations is that of those who said they went along the valley and passed on in it, not that of those who said they climbed the mountain. He then explains that not turning means that none of them inclined to another or looked to another, in flight from their enemy as they went along the valley (the editor refers to the earlier commentary on that verb, 6: 536-537). He then begins to explain the Messenger's calling to them from their rear, that is behind them, and introduces a report with the word 'as', which finishes on the next page. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 302}

**The Messenger's call from behind them (8054-8058, p. 303).** Ibn 'Abbas through Ibn Jurayj (8054): he was calling to them: to me, servants of God, come back. Qatada (8055) and al-Suddi (8056): they heard the Prophet calling to them: to me, servants of God. Ibn Ishaq (8057): God rebuked them for fleeing from their Prophet while he called them, none turning to him at his call. Ibn Zayd (8058): this was Uhud day, when people broke away from him. The editor notes that Ibn Ishaq's report is in Ibn Hisham (3: 121) as the continuation of the reports ending at 8046 (the report number is garbled in the scanned text). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 303}

**The sense of being repaid with grief (pp. 303-305).** Abu Ja'far: God repaid you for fleeing from your prophet, failing against your enemy and disobeying your Lord with distress upon distress. He calls the punishment, the enemy being given power over them until it took what it took, a 'reward' because it was recompense for their deed that He disliked; this shows that every recompense for an act, good or bad, or what one man gives another, or a favour done earlier, deserves the name 'reward', whether honour or punishment. He cites a line of al-Farazdaq about Ziyad ibn Abi Sufyan, in which the poet fears the governor's gift will be black fetters or twisted whips, al-Tabari taking 'gift' to mean fetters. The editor lists the line's sources (al-Farazdaq's diwan, p. 227, the Naqa'id, Tabaqat Fuhul al-Shu'ara', al-Tabari's history and al-Farra's Ma'ani al-Qur'an, 1: 239), says al-Tabari's wording follows al-Farra and does not scan, whereas the transmitters agree on another wording, and explains the black fetters and the twisted whips with their colour. Al-Tabari adds the everyday example of saying to someone who wronged you: I will repay you for your act and give you your reward. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 303} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 304}

**The preposition in the phrase (pp. 304-305).** Abu Ja'far: it is said the preposition here means 'upon', that is grief upon grief, as in [[passage/quran-20-71|20:71]] on crucifying 'upon' palm trunks, meaning on top of them; this is allowed because 'God repaid you grief upon grief' means He repaid you grief following a grief that preceded it. So the sense is that God repaid them with grief in the wake of an earlier grief, like saying 'I stayed with the tribe of so-and-so' and 'on the tribe of so-and-so', or 'I struck him with the sword' and 'on the sword'. In the notes the editor says the manuscript has a blank for one word, which he supplied from al-Tabari's following words; that the printed edition replaced the manuscript's word for fetters with the word for gift, and he does not know why the first publisher changed it; that he forgot to cite where the earlier commentary on 'reward' is; and that the printed edition is certainly wrong in the word for 'preceded' in both places. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 304} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 305}

**The commentators differ on the two griefs (pp. 305-308).** First view: the first grief was what the men were saying, that their prophet had been killed, and the later one what struck them of killing and wounds. Qatada (8059): that day they said the Prophet had been hurt, and the later grief was the killing of their companions and their wounds; it was mentioned to him that seventy of the Prophet's companions were killed, sixty-six Ansar and four Muhajirun; not grieving over what escaped them means the enemy's spoils, and what struck them means the killing and wounds in themselves. Mujahid (8060, 8061): flight after flight, the first when they heard the cry that Muhammad was killed, the second when the unbelievers returned and struck them as they turned away, killing seventy of them, after which they took refuge with the Prophet and went up the mountain while he called to them from behind.

Second view: the first grief was the wounds and killing, and the second their hearing that the Prophet was killed. Qatada through Ma'mar (8062) and al-Rabi' (8063): the later grief made them forget what had struck them of wounds and killing and the spoils they had hoped for.

Third view: the first grief was losing the victory and spoils, and the second Abu Sufyan's looking down on them in the glen; this, as some biographers claim, was when Abu Sufyan, having done what he did to the Muslims, came and looked down on them, the Messenger among them in the glen of Uhud where they had gone, and they feared he and his men would wipe them out (the editor explains the verb on p. 306). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 305} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 306}

**Al-Suddi's report on the glen (8064, pp. 307-308).** The Messenger went calling to the people until he reached the men at the rock; when they saw him, one put an arrow to his bow to shoot, and he said: I am the Messenger of God. They rejoiced to find him alive, and he rejoiced that some of his companions held firm. When they gathered and their sorrow left them, they began to recall the victory they had missed and their dead; then Abu Sufyan appeared above them, and they forgot it and were troubled by him. The Messenger said they must not get above them and made a plea to God about this band, then ordered his men to pelt them with stones until they came down. That day Abu Sufyan called out a boast to Hubal, 'Hanzala for Hanzala' and 'a day for the day of Badr'; Hanzala ibn al-Rahib was killed that day, in a state of major impurity, and the angels washed him, as the report has it, and Hanzala ibn Abi Sufyan had been killed at Badr. Abu Sufyan said: we have al-'Uzza and you have none; the Prophet told 'Umar to answer: God is our protector and you have none. Abu Sufyan asked whether Muhammad was among them, and they said yes; he said there had been mutilation among their dead, which he neither ordered nor forbade, and which neither pleased nor displeased him. In al-Suddi's account God mentions Abu Sufyan's looking down: the first grief was what escaped them of spoils and victory, the second the enemy's looking down on them; and not to grieve over the spoils or over the dead when they remembered, Abu Sufyan having kept them busy. The editor notes the report is in al-Tabari's history (3: 20-21), partly in 8050 and 7943, that he had added a word from the history on p. 256 but the manuscript drops it again here so he left it as is though he does not approve it, and that the history has a variant verb with the same sense and the interrogative particle in the question. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 307} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 308}

**Ibn Ishaq's report from the scholars of the campaigns (8065, p. 308).** Ibn Ishaq from al-Zuhri, Muhammad ibn Yahya ibn Hibban, 'Asim ibn 'Umar ibn Qatada, al-Husayn ibn 'Abd al-Rahman and others: that day the Muslims were in thirds, a third killed, a third wounded and a third routed, the fighting having reached them until they did not know what to do, and the enemy reached the Messenger, who was struck with stones until he fell on his side, his tooth was broken, his face cut and his lip wounded; the one who struck him was 'Utba ibn Abi Waqqas. Mus'ab ibn 'Umayr fought before him with his banner until killed, by Ibn Qami'a al-Laythi, who thought him the Messenger and returned to Quraysh saying he had killed Muhammad. The editor says this report is stitched from Ibn Ishaq's biography at several places (Ibn Hisham 3: 77 and 84), the first part not found there, and glosses 'pelted' as repeated striking with stones or a staff, 'side' and 'wound'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 308}

**Ibn Ishaq's report on who recognised the Messenger and on Abu Sufyan (8066, pp. 308-310).** Ibn Ishaq from al-Zuhri: the first to recognise the Messenger of God after the rout and the saying that he was killed was Ka'b ibn Malik, who said he knew his eyes gleaming under the helmet and shouted loudly to the Muslims to rejoice, for here was the Messenger; he signalled him to be silent. When the Muslims knew him they took him up toward the glen, with 'Ali, Abu Bakr, 'Umar, Talha, al-Zubayr and al-Harith ibn al-Simma among a group of Muslims. While he was in the glen a party of Quraysh got above them on the mountain, and he said it was not fitting for them to be above them; 'Umar and a group of Muhajirun fought until they brought them down. The Messenger went to climb a rock, having grown heavy and wearing two coats of mail one over the other, and could not rise; Talha sat under him and he rose until he stood upon it. When Abu Sufyan wanted to leave he looked down from the mountain and shouted his idol-consulting phrase, that war is turns and a day for the day of Badr; the Messenger told 'Umar to answer: God is higher and greater, they are not equal, our dead are in paradise and yours in the fire. When 'Umar answered, Abu Sufyan called him over, and the Messenger told him to go and see; Abu Sufyan asked him by God whether they had killed Muhammad, and 'Umar said: God forbid, and he is listening to you now. He said: you are more truthful and righteous to me than Ibn Qami'a. Then he called out that there had been mutilation among the dead, which by God he neither approved nor disliked, neither forbade nor ordered. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 308} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 309} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 310}

**The editor's notes (pp. 309-310).** The printed edition has a wrong name for al-Harith ibn al-Simma, corrected from the manuscript and sources. On the verb for growing heavy, he says the commentators on the biography glossed it as having aged and Abu Dharr al-Khushani as having grown stout; he thinks both wrong here, taking it to mean that movement had become heavy for him, and says the active form would also be sound Arabic; 'wore two coats of mail' means one over the other. On Abu Sufyan's phrase beginning 'an'amta', he explains that people of the Jahiliyya wrote 'yes' on one arrow and 'no' on another and cast them before an idol, that Abu Sufyan consulted Hubal when setting out for Uhud and the 'yes' arrow came out, and that the next word means to withdraw from the idols without speaking ill of them; he relates an anecdote of Imru' al-Qays's divination at Dhu al-Khalasa, which Jarir ibn 'Abd Allah al-Bajali later demolished, and that Abu Sufyan was asked on the day of the conquest where his phrase had gone, replying that God had done good and the Jahiliyya was over. The printed edition is wrong in its wording, 'war is turns' means now for one side and now for the other, the command to be exalted is from the root meaning to rise, not another form, and 'more righteous' comes from the word for truthfulness and all good. On 8066 he adds that it is gathered from several places in the biography (Ibn Hisham 3: 88-91 and 99) and al-Tabari's history (3: 21). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 309} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 310}

**The rest of the reports on the two griefs (8067-8070, pp. 311-313).** Ibn Ishaq (8067): distress after distress: the killing of those of your brothers who were killed, your enemy's rising above you, and what arose in your souls from the saying that your prophet was killed, all following one another; and not to grieve over missing the victory over your enemy after seeing it with your own eyes, nor over your brothers' deaths, until God relieved the distress by turning back from them Satan's lie of their prophet's killing; when they saw the Messenger of God alive among them, what they had missed and the calamity in their brothers became light as God turned killing from their prophet. Mujahid through Ibn Jurayj (8068): sorrow and grief struck the people for their companions who were killed; when they went into the glen wounded, Abu Sufyan and his men halted at its mouth, and the believers thought they would fall on them and kill them too; this new sorrow made them forget their sorrow for their companions; Ibn Jurayj explained what was missed as the enemy's spoils and what struck them as what happened in themselves. 'Ubayd ibn 'Umayr (8069): Abu Sufyan stood by the glen and called: is Ibn Abi Kabsha among the people? They were silent, and he said: killed, by the Lord of the Ka'ba. He asked about Ibn Abi Quhafa and then about 'Umar, with the same words; then he said it was a day for the day of Badr and Hanzala for Hanzala, and they would find mutilation among their men that had not been by the counsel of their nobles though they did not dislike it when they saw it. The Prophet told 'Umar to call out: God is higher and greater; yes, this is the Messenger of God, this is Abu Bakr and here am I; and he mentioned the difference between the people of the fire and the people of paradise ([[passage/quran-59-20|59:20]]); our dead are in paradise and yours in the fire. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 311} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 312}

**Another view (8070, p. 313).** Ibn Sa'd from Ibn 'Abbas: they turned back saying: by God we shall go to them and kill them, for they have wounded us; the Messenger said: gently, what struck you struck because you disobeyed me. While they were so, the enemy came on them gathered with their swords drawn, so there was the grief of the rout and their grief when the enemy came, and what they had missed of killing and what struck them of wounds; that was Uhud day.

**The editor's notes (pp. 311-313).** On 8067 he says the manuscript and printed edition both omit a word he adds from Ibn Hisham (3: 121-122), and that the printed edition misread a word split between the end of one line and the start of the next, the right reading being Ibn Hisham's. On 8068 he says the printed wording is meaningless, the manuscript's is right, but its copyist had skipped a passage and then written it in the margin, so the first publisher could not read it. On 8069 'Ibn Abi Kabsha' is the Prophet, whom the idolaters called by this name; it is said Abu Kabsha was a man of Khuza'a who differed from Quraysh over idol worship and worshipped Sirius, so they likened him to him for differing from them; or that it was the by-name of Wahb ibn 'Abd Manaf, his maternal grandfather, whom he resembled; or of the husband of Halima al-Sa'diyya. 'Mutilation' is disfiguring a corpse by cutting off nose, ear and the like. On 8070 the printed wording is a different word, which al-Suyuti drops in al-Durr al-Manthur (2: 87); the manuscript is undotted and he reads 'wounded us', meaning they struck some of us with wounds and killing, which is old Arabic though lexicons do not record it; in another place he prefers a reading meaning they gathered and closed in. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 312} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 313}

**Al-Tabari's choice on the two griefs (pp. 313-314).** Abu Ja'far: the best of these views is that God repaid them for their disobeying their Lord and going against their prophet's order with being denied the idolaters' spoils, the victory and triumph over them and what struck them that day of killing and wounds, after He had shown them in all that what they love: the grief of thinking their prophet was killed, and the enemy's turning on them after their retreat. His proof is that God gave as the aim that they not grieve over what was missed and what struck them; what was missed is certainly what they hoped to reach, victory over the enemy or spoils to take, and what struck them is what befell their bodies or their brothers; so the second grief must be something other than these, the one God mentions after the first. The editor says the lexicons do not give a verbal noun for the intransitive three-letter verb that al-Tabari uses for 'retreat', but that the proverb 'whoever flees is humbled' supports his wording, and that Ibn Kathir (2: 270) quotes the text with another wording while the commentary's wording is right. Ibn Zayd (8071): what was missed is the spoils they hoped for and what struck them the rout. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 313} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 314}

**God's knowledge of what they do (pp. 314-315).** Abu Ja'far: God knows what the believers do, their going up the valley in flight from their enemy, their rout, their leaving their prophet calling from behind, and their sorrow over what was missed and what struck them; He counts it all against them until He repays the doer of good for his good and the evildoer for his evil, or pardons him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 314} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 315}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
