## Summary
Verse 95 of [[passage/quran-5|Surat al-Ma'ida]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 95 of 120 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

Al-Tabari begins with the opening of the verse. God addresses those who believe in Him and His messenger and forbids them to kill game, meaning land game and not sea game, while they are "hurum", that is in the sacred state for hajj or umra. "Hurum" is the plural of "haram", the same word for male and female (a man or a woman may be called haram; the usual forms are "muhrim" and "muhrima"), and "ihram" is entering that state, as one says a people entered ihram when they enter the sacred month or the sanctuary. The clause "whoever kills it among you deliberately" announces the ruling for a pilgrim who kills game on purpose. The people of interpretation then differ about what "deliberate" means, the deliberateness for which God made expiation and compensation due. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 7}

The first view: deliberate means intending to kill the game while forgetting one's ihram; if he kills it remembering his ihram and meaning to kill, no judgment is passed on him and his affair is with God, for that is too grave for a judgment or an expiation. Report 12544, from Mujahid (through Sufyan ibn Waki, Ibn Uyayna and Ibn Abi Najih): he who kills it forgetful of his ihram but intending the killing is the one who is judged; if he kills it remembering his ihram, intending to kill, no judgment is made. Report 12545, Mujahid: one who kills knowing he is in ihram is not judged and has no hajj; the "expiable deliberateness" is hitting it while forgetful of ihram though intending the kill, or hitting it while aiming at something else, and then he is judged once (the editor explains "once" as one time only; if he repeats, God takes retribution). Report 12546 is to the same effect, and 12547 adds only: intending the killing, forgetful of ihram. The editor's notes explain a reading of the word for ihram and correct a printed wording from the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 8}

Mujahid's view recurs in reports 12548 to 12552 (among them 12550 and 12551 through Shu'ba and al-Hakam, and 12552 through Ibn Jurayj): the expiable deliberateness is hitting game while aiming at something else, whereas one who hits it neither forgetful nor aiming elsewhere is not judged. Report 12553, from al-Hasan: "deliberately" means killing it forgetful of ihram, and "whoever transgresses after that" means one who kills it deliberately remembering his ihram. Report 12554: al-Hasan gave the opinion that one who kills game deliberately, remembering his ihram, is not judged; Hammad from Ibrahim says the same. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 9}

Report 12555 is a story told by Hammad ibn Salama: Ja'far ibn Abi Wahshiyya asked him to ask Amr ibn Dinar about the verse; Amr said that Ata used to say it is a choice: he may offer an animal, or feed, or fast. When Hammad told Ja'far, Ja'far laughed and then said that Sa'id ibn Jubayr used to say that an animal from the livestock is judged for him, reaching the Ka'ba, that food and fasting were made expiation only, and that if he cannot find the animal the food is valued and the fast runs from three to ten days. The editor notes that the printed text differs from the manuscript, that he kept some additions to make the sentence stand, and that he found the report nowhere else. Report 12556 repeats Mujahid. Report 12557, from Ibn Zayd: those who are judged are the ones who kill forgetful of ihram or ignorant that it is forbidden; one who kills deliberately after the prohibition, knowing he is in ihram, is left to God's retribution. Report 12558, Mujahid: intending the killing, forgetful of ihram. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 10}

The second view: deliberate means the pilgrim's intending to kill the game while remembering his ihram. Report 12559, from Ata: he is judged in deliberateness, error and forgetfulness. Reports 12560 and 12565, from Tawus: by God, God said only "whoever kills it among you deliberately". Report 12561, from al-Zuhri: the Qur'an came down with the deliberate case and the sunna ran on the case of error, meaning a pilgrim who hits game. Report 12562, from Ibn Abbas: if he kills it deliberately or forgetfully he is judged, and if he returns to it deliberately punishment is hastened unless God pardons. Reports 12563 and 12564, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr: expiation was set for the deliberate case, but it was made harsher for error so that they would be on guard. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 11}

Al-Tabari's choice: God forbade killing land game to every pilgrim for as long as he is in ihram, then stated the ruling for one who kills deliberately, without singling out the one who forgot his ihram or the one who erred while remembering it; the revelation covers every pilgrim who kills game deliberately. He holds it impermissible to turn the apparent sense of the revelation into a hidden interpretation with no support in a text of the Book, a report from the messenger, or the consensus of the community. So it is the same whether the killer meant to kill remembering his ihram, meant to kill forgetting it, or aimed at something else and killed it remembering: all owe what God stated, a like of the livestock judged by two just men, or the expiation of feeding the poor, or its equivalent in fasting. This is the view of Ata and al-Zuhri, not that of Mujahid. As for the ruling on error, he says he treated it in his book "Latif al-qawl fi ahkam al-shara'i" and this is not the place, since his aim here is the interpretation of the revelation, which says nothing of error. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 12}

Turning to "the like of what he killed of livestock", al-Tabari says it means an equivalent and a counterpart, that is, the compensation for the game killed. He notes that in the reading of Abd Allah (ibn Mas'ud) the word is "its compensation". The readers differ: most Medinan readers and some Basrans join "compensation" to "like" in a genitive construction; most Kufans read "compensation" with tanwin and "like" in the nominative, as "upon him is a compensation, the like of what he killed". Al-Tabari prefers the tanwin and nominative reading, because the compensation is itself the like and there is no ground for joining a thing to itself. He supposes those who read it as a genitive thought that the killer must compensate with a like of the game by a like from the livestock; he says it is not as they take it, for the duty is to compensate the animal killed with its counterpart from the livestock. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 13}

Al-Tabari adds to his reasoning that no reader, as far as he knows, reads "compensation" with tanwin and "like" in the accusative; had "like" been other than "compensation" the accusative would be allowed with tanwin, as "orphan" is accusative in Sura 90 because it is other than "feeding", and as "living" and "dead" are accusative with "kifat" in tanwin in Sura 77 because "kifat" is other than the living and the dead. Here it was not possible, since the "like" is the "compensation", so the sense is: he owes a compensation which is the like of what he killed of livestock. The editor says Ibn Jinni (al-Muhtasib) and Ibn Khalawayh (Shawadhdh al-qira'at) report an accusative reading attributed to others. The scholars then differ over the nature of the compensation and how the pilgrim pays the like of what he killed. Some say: look to what is nearest to it in likeness among the livestock, pay with that and send it to the Ka'ba. Report 12566, from al-Suddi: for an ostrich or a wild ass, a camel (badana); for a wild cow, a stag or a mountain goat, a cow; for a gazelle or a hare, a sheep; for a lizard, a chameleon or a jerboa, a kid or lamb that has eaten herbage and drunk milk. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 14}

Report 12567: Ata was asked whether one pays for small game as for large, and answered by quoting the verse (the editor could not identify "Ibn Mujahid" in the chain). Report 12568, Mujahid: he owes its like from the livestock. Report 12569, from Ibn Abbas through Miqsam: when a pilgrim hits game he owes its compensation from the livestock; if he finds it he slaughters it and gives it as charity; if he does not, the compensation is valued in dirhams, the dirhams in wheat, and he fasts a day for each half sa'; "food" was meant as fasting. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 15}

Reports 12570 and 12572 repeat this, and 12571 has Ibn Abbas say two days of fasting for each sa'. Reports 12573 to 12577 are the story of Qabisa ibn Jabir: he and a companion, both in ihram, chased a gazelle at al-Aqaba and he hit it; he went to Umar ibn al-Khattab, who consulted with a man beside him and said: slaughter a ram. (The editor gives notes on Abd al-Malik ibn Umayr and Qabisa, says al-Tabari tells the story through reports 12573 to 12577 and again from 12586 to 12588 in different wording, and that al-Bayhaqi relates it in his Sunan.) {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 16}

In 12575 Umar ordered Qabisa's companion to slaughter a sheep, give its meat as charity and ... its hide (the editor says the phrase is explained at 12588). In 12576, from Bakr al-Muzani, a bedouin killed a gazelle in ihram and Umar told him: offer a sheep (a mursal report, the editor notes). In 12577 Qabisa says: Umar sent for Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf; I said, "Commander of the believers, its matter is lighter than that", and he struck me with the whip until I ran ahead of him, saying: you killed game in ihram and then belittle the ruling? Abd al-Rahman came and the two judged a sheep. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 17}

Report 12578, from Ibn Abbas: when a pilgrim kills a gazelle or the like he owes a sheep slaughtered at Mecca; if he cannot find one, feeding six poor persons; if not, three days of fasting; for a stag or the like, a cow; for an ostrich or wild ass or the like, a camel. Report 12579: Ibn Jurayj asked Ata about game that was one-eyed, lame or defective, whether he pays a like; Ata said yes if you wish, the sound one is dearer to me; for the young of a gazelle a lamb, for the calf of a wild cow a domestic calf. Report 12580, from al-Dahhak ibn Muzahim (the chain opens "I was told from al-Husayn ibn al-Faraj"), gives a schedule: hornless land game such as the ass or ostrich, a like from the camels; horned game such as the mountain goat or stag, from the cattle; a gazelle, from the sheep; a hare, a young goat; a jerboa or the like, a small lamb; a locust, a handful of food; land birds are valued and the price given as charity, or a day's fast for each half sa'; a chick or an egg, the value in food or fasting; for ostrich eggs a stallion camel is put to young she-camels according to the number of eggs. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 18}

The schedule ends: what conceives is offered to the House and what spoils owes nothing. Report 12581, from Mujahid: one who killed forgetfully or aimed at something else and missed is in the expiable deliberate case and owes its like as an offering reaching the Ka'ba; if he cannot find it he buys food with its price, and if not he fasts a day for each mudd. From Ata: one who hit an ostrich, if well off, has latitude: a camel, or its equal in food, or in fasting, whichever he likes, because of "or", and whatever in the Qur'an is "or ... or" lets the person choose. Report 12582, from al-Hasan ibn Muslim: game that comes to a sheep or more is the "compensation, a like of what he killed of livestock"; "expiation by feeding poor persons" is for what does not reach an offering, such as a sparrow. Al-Tabari then gives another view: the game killed is valued in dirhams, and with that value the killer buys a counterpart from the livestock and sends it to the Ka'ba. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 19}

Reports 12583 and 12584, from Ibrahim: whatever the pilgrim takes is judged by its value, and for every game animal its price. Al-Tabari prefers what Umar and Ibn Abbas and those who agree said: the game killed is compensated by a like from the livestock, as the verse says. It cannot be that the like is dirhams when God said "of the livestock", and dirhams are not livestock at all. He takes up an objection: dirhams are not a like, but with them the like can be bought and offered, so the killer compensates with a like from the livestock. His reply is a question: if the animal killed was small or defective and only a large or sound animal can be had for its price, or the reverse, may he buy something unlike it in kind and offer that? If the objector says he may buy only its like, he abandons his own view. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 20}

Al-Tabari continues his reply: if the objector allows buying the like of the animal killed with its value, though the animal may have been small and defective, he allows in an offering what is not allowed in the festival sacrifices; and if he says only what is allowed in a sacrifice may be bought, he plainly contradicts the apparent sense of the revelation, since God made the like from the livestock obligatory on the killer when he can find it. He then asks: if another said there is no feeding and no fasting for one whose game is not worth enough to buy a livestock animal acceptable as a sacrifice, since God gave the killer a choice among three things and when one is out of reach the other two lapse, is there any difference in principle or analogy between him and you? Whatever he says on one, al-Tabari holds, binds him to the same on the other. The notes correct wording in both the printed text and the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 21}

He turns to "two men of justice among you shall judge it, an offering reaching the Ka'ba." The compensation that is the like of the game killed is judged by two just men among you, meaning two jurists learned in religion and of merit; "an offering" means it is sent and reaches the Ka'ba; and the pronoun in "judge it" returns to the compensation. As to the manner of the two judges, al-Tabari says they look at what was killed and ask the killer to describe it: if a young gazelle, they judge a young sheep like it in age and body; if a large one, a large sheep; if a wild ass, a cow, large or small according to it; a male by a male of the cattle and a female by a female; and so they look at what most resembles the animal killed among the livestock and judge by it. He says the people of interpretation spoke along these lines, with some difference among them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 22}

Report 12585, from Bakr ibn Abd Allah al-Muzani: two bedouins in ihram, one of whom drove a gazelle and the other killed it, came to Umar, with Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf beside him; Umar asked him: what do you think? A sheep, he said; Umar said: I think so too, go and offer a sheep. When they left, one said to the other: the commander of the believers did not know what to say until he asked his companion. Umar heard, called them back and asked whether they read Sura al-Ma'ida; they said no, so he recited "two men of justice among you shall judge it" and said: I sought the help of this companion of mine. Report 12586, from Qabisa ibn Jabir: he and a companion chased a gazelle at al-Aqaba and he hit it; he went to Umar, who consulted a man beside him and said: slaughter a ram (in Ya'qub's version, a sheep). He told his companion that the commander did not know what to say, and the companion said: sacrifice your she-camel. Umar heard it, turned on him with the whip and said: you kill game in ihram and belittle the ruling? God says in His Book "two men of justice among you shall judge it"; this is Ibn Awf and I am Umar. The editor explains a verb and corrects the name Umar in the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 23}

Report 12587, from al-Sha'bi, has Qabisa say much the same. Report 12588 gives Qabisa's longer account: we set out on pilgrimage, and after the dawn prayer we led our mounts on foot, talking; a gazelle crossed our path from one side, and one of us threw a stone and did not miss the bone behind its ear, so it fell dead; we felt the weight of it, and when we reached Mecca we went to Umar, with a man beside him whose face was like a silver bracelet, meaning Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf. Umar spoke to him and then asked the man: did you kill it deliberately or by mistake? He said: I meant to hit it and did not mean to kill it. Umar said: I see you have joined deliberateness and error; take a sheep, slaughter it, give its meat as charity and give its hide for a waterskin. The editor explains the words for the side the gazelle came from, the bone behind the ear, falling on one's blood, the silver bracelet, and quotes Ibn Sa'd's description of Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 24}

Qabisa continues: we got up from him and I said: man, honour the rites of God! The commander of the believers did not know what ruling to give you until he asked his companion; take your she-camel and sacrifice it, perhaps that will do (the editor says that Ibn Kathir adds "meaning that it will suffice for you", which is not in the manuscript). I did not remember the verse in al-Ma'ida. My words reached Umar, and suddenly he was on us with the whip; he struck my companion, saying: you killed in the sanctuary and belittled the judgment! Then he turned to me and I said: commander of the believers, I do not make lawful to you today anything that is forbidden to you from me. He said: Qabisa, I see you young, broad-chested and clear in speech; a young man has nine good traits and one bad, and the bad one spoils the good; beware the slips of youth. The editor notes a variant in al-Bayhaqi, and that Sufyan said that when Abd al-Malik related this report he would say: I have not left out an alif or a waw from it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 25}

Report 12589, from Tariq: Arbad let his mount tread on a lizard and killed it while in ihram; he came to Umar to be judged and Umar told him: judge with me. They judged a kid that had gathered water and trees, and Umar recited "two men of justice among you shall judge it" (the editor identifies Mukhariq, Tariq and Arbad, and cites al-Bayhaqi and al-Shafi'i). Report 12590, from Qatada: a man hit game and asked Ibn Umar, with Abd Allah ibn Safwan beside him; Ibn Umar said to Ibn Safwan: either I speak and you confirm, or you speak and I confirm; Ibn Safwan told him to speak, and Ibn Umar spoke and Ibn Safwan agreed. Report 12591, from Shurayh: had I found a just judge I would have judged a kid for a fox, and a kid is dearer to me than a fox. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 26}

Report 12592, from Abu Mijlaz, is to the effect of 12590. Report 12593, from Abu Jarir al-Bajali: I hit a gazelle while in ihram and told Umar, who said: go to two of your brothers and let them judge you; I went to Abd al-Rahman and Sa'd and they judged a white buck (al-Tabari glosses "a'far" as white). The editor says Abu Jarir is noticed only by Ibn Sa'd and that some versions add "and I was forgetful of my ihram". Report 12594, from Shu'ba through Mansur, is the same from Umar. Report 12595 begins at the foot of p. 27. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 27}

The rest of report 12595, from Ibn Sirin: a man on a she-camel in ihram saw a gazelle making for a mound and said: let me see whether I reach that mound before the gazelle; a female gazelle fell under his camel's feet and was killed; he went to Umar, and Umar and Ibn Awf judged a white she-goat. Report 12596, from Muhammad ibn Sirin: a man's mount trampled a gazelle while he was in ihram; he went to Umar, with Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf beside him; Umar spoke with him and told the man: offer a white she-goat. Report 12597, from Ibrahim: whatever a pilgrim takes on which no judgment has gone before is taken up afresh and two just men judge it. Report 12598: Amr ibn Hubshi heard a man ask Ibn Umar about one who had hit a young hare; Ibn Umar said, a young goat, as I see it, and asked: is that right? Amr said: you know better than I; and Ibn Umar recited the words "two men of justice among you shall judge it". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 27}

Report 12599, from Bakr: two pilgrims in ihram saw a gazelle and wagered, the stake of each going to whoever reached it first; one reached it first, struck it with his stick and killed it. They came to Umar at Mecca disputing, with Ibn Awf beside him; Umar said: this is gambling and I do not allow it! He then asked Abd al-Rahman, who said, a sheep, and Umar said, I think so too. When the two had gone, one said to the other: Umar did not know what to say until he asked the man! Umar called them back and said: God was not satisfied with Umar alone, for He said "two men of justice among you shall judge it", and I am Umar and this is Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 28}

Al-Tabari then gives the view of those who say the two judges look at the game killed, value it in dirhams, and tell the killer to buy an offering from the livestock with that; on this view the judges judge by value and are needed only to value the game at the place where it was hit. This is the opinion of Ibrahim al-Nakha'i and a group of Kufan jurists. He explains that "an offering" is a verbal noun in the state of the pronoun in "judge it", and that "reaching the Ka'ba" qualifies the offering; it may qualify it even though annexed to a definite noun because it is indefinite in sense, meaning "that reaches the Ka'ba", as in "this is a cloud bringing us rain". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 29}

He turns to "or an expiation, the food of poor persons": or he owes an expiation of feeding the poor, "expiation" being joined to "compensation". The readers differ: most Medinans read "expiation" annexed to "food", most Iraqis read "expiation" with tanwin and "food" in the nominative, and al-Tabari prefers the latter for the reason he gave over "compensation, a like of what he killed". The interpreters then differ on the meaning. Some say the pilgrim who kills deliberately cannot escape owing one of these three things, and that his ruling, if he can afford the like, is that the like of the animal killed is adjudged against him and nothing else suffices as long as he can find it; if he cannot, or the game has no like among the livestock, then his expiation is feeding the poor. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 30}

Among these, report 12600 from Ibn Abbas: if a pilgrim kills a gazelle or the like he owes a sheep slaughtered at Mecca, if he cannot, feeding six poor persons, if not, fasting three days; for a stag or the like a cow, if not, feeding twenty poor persons, if not, twenty days of fasting; for an ostrich or wild ass or the like a camel, if not, feeding thirty poor persons, if not, thirty days of fasting; the food is a mudd each, enough to satisfy them. Report 12601, from him: the expiation for killing less than a hare is food. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 31}

Report 12602, from Ibn Abbas, repeats valuation in dirhams, then wheat, and a day of fasting per sa'. Report 12603, from Ata, Mujahid and Amir: food is for one who cannot find the offering. Report 12604, from Ibrahim: valuation in dirhams, then food, then a day for each half sa'. Report 12605, from Hammad: when judged, if a remainder smaller than half a sa' is left he fasts a day for it; fasting falls only on one who has not the price of an offering and is judged food, and if he has no food to give as charity he is judged fasting, a day for each half sa'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 32}

Report 12606, from Mujahid: he owes its like from the livestock as an offering reaching the Ka'ba; who cannot buys food with its value and feeds each poor person two mudds, and if he cannot, fasts a day for every two mudds. Report 12607, from al-Suddi: if he kills game he owes its compensation; if he cannot, it is judged, the ransom is valued in dirhams, the price set in food for the poor, and he fasts a day for each poor person (the editor says the wording of this report is difficult in both printed text and manuscript and he could not trace it elsewhere). Report 12608 repeats al-Hasan ibn Muslim on the sheep and the sparrow, and Ata: whatever in the Qur'an is "or ... or", its person may choose. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 33}

Report 12609, from Ibn Abbas: if he finds no compensation, the compensation is valued in food and he fasts two days for each sa'. Then the other view: the deliberate killer in ihram is free to choose one of the three expiations, the like of it from the livestock, food, or fasting. Report 12610, from Ata: if a pilgrim hits an ostrich, even if well off, he may offer what he likes, a camel or its equivalent in food or in fasting; whatever in the Qur'an is "or ... or", its person chooses what he likes. Report 12611 repeats this. Report 12612, from Ikrima (finished on p. 35): whatever is "or ... or" is a choice, and whatever is "whoever does not find" is the next in order. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 34}

Report 12613 gives the same from al-Hasan, 12614 from Ata and Mujahid, 12615 from al-Dahhak, 12616 from al-Hasan and Ibrahim, and 12617 from Ibn Abbas: whatever in the Qur'an is "or ... or" is a choice, and whatever is "whoever does not find" goes first to the first and then to the next. Those who allow the choice then differ about what feeding and fasting require when he chooses one of them rather than the offering. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 35}

Some say he values the like from the livestock in food and fasts a day for each mudd; in 12618 Ibn Jurayj asked Ata about "or its equal in fasting", and he said: if he hit what equals a sheep, the sheep is valued in food and a day is set for each mudd. Others say the game killed is valued in food and that food given in charity if he chooses charity, or he fasts if he chooses fasting; they then differ over the fast: a day per mudd, per half sa', or per sa'. Report 12619, from Qatada: they judge by livestock; if he has not enough, they look at the price, value it in food, and he fasts two days for each sa'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 36}

Another group says feeding has no place as an expiation, since whoever can feed can pay the like from the livestock, and whoever can pay the like is not allowed anything else; God mentioned feeding only to show the manner of expiation by fasting, not because it is one of the expiations. Al-Tabari then gives his choice on the two clauses. On "compensation, the like of what he killed of livestock": it means that the killer owes the like of what he killed from the livestock and not its value, if he chooses to compensate by the like, since value is in dinars or dirhams, which are no like of game, and God made a like from the livestock obligatory. On "or an expiation of feeding poor persons or its equal in fasting": it is a choice, and the killer may atone by whichever of the three he likes, because God made the compensation and expiation a punishment for his act and an atonement for his sin in destroying what was lawful to him before ihram, as He made the fidya of fasting, charity or sacrifice for the pilgrim who shaves his hair. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 37}

He adds that all agree that the pilgrim who shaves for an ailment may choose among the three, so the killer of game likewise. To whoever refuses this he says: God judged against the killer of game a like, or the food of poor persons, or its equal in fasting, as He judged against the shaver a fidya of fasting, charity or sacrifice; you grant the choice to one and deny it to the other, so is there any difference in principle or analogy between you and one who reverses the matter? The editor has a long note on how the wording of this sentence stands between the printed text and the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 38}

They then differ on valuation when he atones by feeding. Some say the game is valued at the price of the place where it was hit, the view of Ibrahim al-Nakha'i, Hammad, Abu Hanifa, Abu Yusuf and Muhammad. Others say at the price of the land where he atones; in 12620 Amir says of a pilgrim who hit game in Khurasan that he atones at Mecca or Mina and values the food at the price of the land where he atones; in 12621 al-Sha'bi says he is judged at Mecca. Al-Tabari's choice: when the killer compensates with a like, he compensates with its counterpart in build and body, the nearest in resemblance; when he atones with food, he values it at the price where he hit it, since the duty arose there; and then he may feed there, in Mecca or elsewhere, because God made reaching the Ka'ba a condition only for the offering and not for the rest of the compensation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 39}

Report 12622, from Ibrahim: what is blood is at Mecca, and charity or fasting wherever he likes. Others differ and say the offering and the feeding count only at Mecca, while fasting may be done anywhere; report 12623, from Ata: blood and food at Mecca, fasting where he likes; 12624, from Ata: the expiation of hajj is at Mecca; 12625, Ata asked where charity of food is given: at Mecca, because it stands in the place of the offering, and since he hit the game in the sanctuary, its compensation is at the House. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 40}

Al-Tabari says an offering does not satisfy the expiation for game killed unless it reaches the Ka'ba, and it is slaughtered and given to the poor of the sanctuary, "Ka'ba" here meaning the whole sanctuary; one who sends his obligatory offering may slaughter it at any time he likes, before the day of sacrifice or after, and likewise one who atones by feeding or fasting may do so when and where he likes. Report 12626, from Ata: there is no time for the fast, whenever and wherever he likes, though hastening is dearer to me. Report 12627: Ibn Jurayj asked Ata about a man who hit game in hajj or umra and sent its compensation to the sanctuary in Muharram or another month, does that suffice? He said yes and recited "reaching the Ka'ba"; Hannad adds that Yahya said: we hold to it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 41}

Report 12628, from Ata: when you reach Mecca with the compensation for game, sacrifice it, for God says "reaching the Ka'ba", unless you arrive in the ten days (of Dhu al-Hijja), in which case delay it to the day of sacrifice. Report 12629, from him: one who hits game gives charity at Mecca. Al-Tabari then explains "or its equal in fasting": the killer of game in ihram owes the fasting equal to the game killed, that is, the game is valued alive, as if not killed, in food at the place where the pilgrim killed it, and he fasts a day for each mudd, because the Prophet equated a mudd of food with a day's fast in the expiation of one who has intercourse in Ramadan (the editor refers to al-Bayhaqi's Sunan). If someone asks why not a day per sa', by analogy with the Prophet's ruling on Ka'b ibn Ujra, whom he told to feed a faraq of food, which is three sa' among six poor persons, or fast three days. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 42}

Al-Tabari answers: analogy is only returning disputed branches to agreed principles, and there is no dispute among the authorities that one who atones for game by fasting does not satisfy it by equating a day with a sa' of food. So the rule of equating fasting to food in killing game differs from the rule in the expiation for shaving, since one principle cannot be returned to another by analogy, only a branch to a principle; and it is the same whether someone says "why not return the fasting in killing game to the shaving rule" or another says the reverse. He recalls that "adl" with fatha, in Arab usage, is the measure of a thing from another kind, and "idl" with kasra its measure from the same kind. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 43}

He notes that some students of Arabic say "adl" is a verbal noun from "I made this equal to that, a good equalising", and that they distinguish it from "idl" of goods by kasra for goods and fatha in "nor will any equivalent be accepted" and in "or its equal in fasting"; some say "adl" is fairness in right and "idl" with kasra is the like. The accusative of "fasting" is of specification, as one says "I have a skinful of ghee" or "a ratl's measure of honey". A group of the interpreters said as he does. The editor remarks that the eighth volume of the manuscript ends at this point, with the copyist recording its completion in Rabi' al-Akhir of the year 715, and that the ninth volume opens with the report that Ibn Bashshar relates from Ata on the meaning of "its equal in fasting". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 44}

Report 12630: Ibn Jurayj asked Ata what "its equal in fasting" means; he said the equal of the food in fasting, a day for every mudd, and he takes it by analogy with the Ramadan fast and the zihar expiation, saying that it is an opinion of his own that he did not hear from anyone and no sunna had run on it; later he asked again and Ata said, if he hit what equals a sheep, it is valued in food and a day is set for each mudd (Ibn Jurayj adds that he did not ask whether it was opinion or sunna). Report 12631, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr: he fasts three to ten days. Report 12632, from Hammad, repeats 12605, and 12633, from Ibn Abbas, repeats 12600. In 12634 Sa'id answers a question about a pilgrim who hits game and owes a sheep, cow or camel and cannot find it: what is the equal in fasting or charity? Its price; if he cannot find the price, it is valued in food given as charity, a mudd to each poor person, and he fasts a day for each mudd. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 45}

Next "that he may taste the evil consequence of his deed": God means that He imposed on the pilgrim who kills game the compensation and expiation so that he would taste the ill result of his deed and his punishment. By "his deed" is meant his sin and what he did in killing what God forbade him to kill in ihram, so He bound him to the expiation to make him taste the penalty of his sin, through a fine and bodily labour that tires and burdens him. The root of "wabal" is hardship in what is disliked, as in "so We seized him with a heavy seizure". Al-Tabari concludes that God showed by this that the expiations laid on property and on bodies are punishments from Him for His creatures, though also a cleansing of them and an atonement for their sins. Report 12635, from al-Suddi: "the evil consequence of his deed" is the punishment of his deed. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 46}

Then "God has pardoned what is past, and whoever returns, God will take retribution from him": God tells His believing servants that He has pardoned what passed from them in their pre-Islamic time, when they hit game in ihram and killed it, so He will not hold them to what happened before He forbade it, and no expiation in property or person is laid on them; but whoever returns to killing it in ihram after the prohibition, in the way that he killed it in unbelief by treating the killing as lawful, God will take retribution from him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 47}

It may also mean: whoever returns to killing it after it has been forbidden in Islam, God will take retribution from him in the next life, while in this world he owes the compensation and expiation already explained. The interpreters differ. Some say as al-Tabari does. Report 12636: I asked Ata what "God has pardoned what is past" is; he said what was in the pre-Islamic time; and "whoever returns"? He said whoever returns in Islam, God takes retribution from him, and he owes the expiation besides. Report 12637 adds that if he returns and kills he owes the expiation; asked whether there is a known hadd for returning, Ata said no; whether the imam should punish him, he said it is a sin between him and God, but he ransoms. Report 12638, from Ata: "in Islam", with the expiation besides, and no punishment from the imam. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 48}

Reports 12639 and 12641, from Ata, say the same. Report 12640, from him: he is judged in error, deliberateness and forgetfulness, and whenever he hits; the pardon is for the pre-Islamic time, "whoever returns" is with the expiation, and the ruler does not punish him. Report 12642, from Ata: he is judged whenever he returns. Report 12643, from Mujahid: whenever a pilgrim hits game forgetfully, he is judged. Report 12644, from Ibrahim: whenever a pilgrim hits game, he is judged. Report 12645, from Ata: one who killed game and returned is judged. Report 12646, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr: he is judged; is he to be cast off, to be left alone? (the editor explains this as an exclamation of surprise confirming the ruling each time, since otherwise he would be cast off and left to do as he pleased). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 49}

Report 12647, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, on one who hits game in ihram, is judged and returns: he is judged. Report 12648, from Ata: he is judged whenever he returns (the editor gives notes on Kathir ibn Hisham and al-Furat ibn Sulayman). Others say the meaning is that God pardoned what passed in the pre-Islamic time and whoever returns in Islam, God takes retribution from him by imposing the expiation; report 12649, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr and Ata, says "God takes retribution" means by the compensation, and the pardon is for the pre-Islamic time. Others say God pardoned what passed of killing game in ihram the first time, and whoever returns a second time, God is the avenger, with no expiation laid on him; report 12650, from Ibn Abbas: one who kills game by mistake in ihram is judged once, and if he returns it is said to him "God will take retribution from you". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 50}

Report 12651, from Ibn Abbas: when a pilgrim hits game he is judged; if he returns he is not judged and it is up to God, who punishes him if He wills or pardons him; then he recited the verse. Report 12652, from Amir: a man came to Shurayh saying I hit game in ihram; he asked, did you hit anything before? No. He said: had you said yes, I would have left you to God, who takes retribution from you; Dawud told Sa'id ibn Jubayr of this and he said: rather he is judged, is he to be cast off? Report 12653, from Ibrahim: when a man hits game in ihram he is asked whether he hit game before; if yes he is told go, God will take retribution; if no, he is judged. Report 12654, from Ibrahim: they used to say that one who returns is not judged and his matter is with God. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 51}

Reports 12655 and 12656, from al-Sha'bi through Shurayh: had you said yes, I would not have judged you. Report 12657, from Shurayh: he is judged, and if he returns God takes retribution. Report 12658, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr: he is judged for deliberate killing once; if he returns he is not judged and it is said to him go, God will take retribution; and for error he is always judged. Reports 12659 and 12660, from Sa'id: a concession was made for killing game once, and whoever returns, God does not leave him until He takes retribution. Report 12661, from Ibn Abbas, on one judged who then returns: he is not judged, God takes retribution. Report 12662, from Mujahid: "deliberately" means deliberately killing it, forgetful of ihram, and that one is judged; if he returns he is not judged, and it is said to him God will take retribution from you. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 52}

Report 12663, from Mujahid, is the same, and 12664, from al-Hasan, on one who hits game and is judged and then returns: he is not judged. Others say God pardoned what passed of killing game before He forbade it, and whoever returns to kill it after the prohibition, knowing it is forbidden, deliberately, remembering his ihram, God is the avenger, and no expiation or compensation is laid on him in this world. Report 12665, from Ibn Zayd: one who returns after God's prohibition, knowing it is forbidden and remembering his ihram, it is not right for anyone to judge him, and he is left to God's retribution, whereas one who kills deliberately while forgetful of his ihram, or ignorant that it is forbidden, is judged; al-Tabari finds this close to Mujahid's view in 12662. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 53}

Others say a particular person was meant. Report 12666, from Zayd Abu al-Mu'alla: a man hit game in ihram and it was overlooked for him; he returned, and God sent fire on him and burned him; that is "whoever returns, God will take retribution from him", in Islam. Al-Tabari chooses the view that whoever returns in Islam to killing it after God's prohibition, God takes retribution from him and he owes the expiation besides, because when God announced retribution, having laid on the deliberate killer of game the compensation or expiation, He did not tell us that He lifted the expiation on the second and third time; He gave the ruling on the one who kills and then announced retribution on the returner, and did not say "no expiation in this world". If anyone thinks that the expiation removes punishment, and that were the expiation due in this world the punishment in the next would lapse, he is mistaken: God may differ the punishments of disobedience as He pleases, increasing some and reducing others, as He differentiated between the virgin and the married adulterer, and between one who steals a quarter dinar and one who steals less. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 54}

So He differentiated between the punishment of a first deliberate killer of game and a returner: on the beginner He imposed the like from the livestock, or the expiation by feeding, or its equal in fasting, and made that the punishment of his offence by "that he may taste the evil consequence of his deed", and on the returner He laid an addition, the retribution He announced He would exact, as a harsher treatment of return after beginning; if His punishments were the same, no hadd could differ from another and no punishment in the next life could be heavier than another, which is against what the Furqan decisively states. Al-Tabari then notes the claim of some that the meaning is: whoever returns in Islam to killing it in the manner the people killed it in their pre-Islamic time, treating it as lawful, is pardoned, but whoever kills it as wrongdoing without treating it as lawful owes the compensation and expiation each time. He says he knows no interpreter who said this, that departing from the scholars' statements is error enough, and that the apparent sense of the revelation shows its falsity, since God made "whoever returns" general to every returner and did not single out one returner over another; whoever claims in the revelation what is not in its apparent sense is required to bring proof. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 55}

We stop here at the foot of p. 55, in the middle of the commentary on verse 95, in al-Tabari's reply on those who read "whoever returns" as returning after an earlier killing.
