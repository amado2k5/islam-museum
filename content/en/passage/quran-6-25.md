## Summary
Verse 25 of [[passage/quran-6|Surat al-An'am]], a Meccan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 25 of 165 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

On "among them are those who listen to you, and we have put coverings on their hearts lest they understand it, and deafness in their ears" al-Tabari says: among these who hold idols and images equal to their Lord from your people, Muhammad, are "those who listen to you", that is, those who listen to the Qur'an from you and to what you call to of your Lord's oneness, his command and prohibition, but do not understand what you say, their heart does not take it in or reflect on it, nor does their hearing attend to it so as to grasp it and understand God's arguments against them in the revelation he sent you; they hear only your voice, recitation and speech and do not take in what you say, because God has put "coverings" on their hearts, plural of "kinan", a cover, like "sinan" and "asinna". One says "akanantu al-shay' fi nafsi" with alif, and "kanantu al-shay'" when you covered it; from it comes [[passage/quran-37-49|37:49]], "hidden eggs", meaning covered, and the poet Umar ibn Abi Rabi'a, in a line that speaks of the shade of a figured mantle beneath a cloud. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 305}

The editor refers for "coverings" to volume 5, says it would be better to say "covered" and that the printed and manuscript wording seems distorted, though the ancients might use such an expression, so he left it; he notes that the line is not in Umar ibn Abi Rabi'a's Diwan but comes from a poem in it and is in the Aghani, Majaz al-Qur'an and Lisan al-Arab, and explains "eye" as cloud and "murahhal" as a garment with figures of saddles on it.

"And deafness in their ears" means he put heaviness and deafness in their ears against understanding what you recite and heeding what you call them to. The Arabs put fatha on the waw of "waqr" for heaviness in the ear, and kasra for a load, saying "it is the animal's wiqr". From a load one says "awqartu al-dabba, so it is muwqara", and from hearing "waqartu sam'ahu, so he is mawqur", from which comes a poet's line about a head whose hearing blows had made heavy... {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 306}

On p. 306 the editor notes that the page's text is mixed with notes in what was copied, that he did not find the poet though he recalls reading the verse somewhere, and refers to Abu Ubayda's Majaz al-Qur'an and to the readings of Abu al-Faraj in the Aghani and Ibn Barri of the preceding line, which correct the reading of Abu Ubayda and Abu Jafar.

The rest: God said "we have put coverings on their hearts lest they understand it" meaning that they not understand it, as in [[passage/quran-4-176|4:176]], "God makes clear to you lest you go astray", meaning that you not go astray, since the covering is put on the heart so that it not understand, not so that it understand. The interpreters said much the same: report 13152, from Qatada: they hear it with their ears and take in nothing of it, like the beast that hears the call and does not know what is said to it. 13153, from al-Suddi: "akinna" is the covering that covers their hearts so they do not understand the truth, and "deafness in their ears" is deafness. 13154 and 13155, from Mujahid: "among them are those who listen to you" is Quraysh. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 307}

The editor refers for the verse to volume 9 and for "understanding" to volume 8.

Then "if they see every sign they do not believe in it, until when they come to you disputing, those who disbelieve say: this is only the tales of the ancients": if these who hold idols and images equal to their Lord, on whose hearts I put coverings so that they not understand from you what they hear, see "every sign", that is, every proof and mark that shows people of reason and understanding God's oneness, the truth of your saying and the reality of your prophethood, "they do not believe in it", meaning they do not trust it or acknowledge that it points to what it points to; "until when they come to you disputing" means until they come to you after seeing the signs that show the truth of what you brought them, contending with you; "those who disbelieve say", those who rejected God's signs and denied their reality, say to God's prophet when they hear the arguments God makes against them and the explanation he gave them: "this is only the tales of the ancients", this is nothing but the tales of the ancients. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 308}

"Asatir" is the plural of "istara" and "usturah", like "ufkuha" and "uhduka", and it may be that the singular is "astar", like "abyat" and "abayit", "aqwal" and "aqawil", from [[passage/quran-52-2|52:2]], "and a book inscribed", from "satara yastur satran"; if it is from this, its sense is: this is only what the ancients wrote. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 308}

The editor refers for "sign" and "dispute" to the language indexes and volumes 9 and 4.

It is reported from Ibn Abbas and others that they interpreted it so, saying the sense is: this is only the stories of the ancients; this is in 13156 from Ibn Abbas. 13157, from al-Suddi: "tales of the ancients" are the rhymed sayings of the ancients. One scholar of Arab speech, Abu Ubayda Mamar ibn al-Muthanna, used to say "istara" is a dialect word and its figurative sense is that of idle talk. Al-Akhfash said that some say its singular is "usturah" and some "istara", and he thought it among the plurals with no singular, like "abadid", "madhakir" and "ababil"; some say the singular of "ababil" is "ibbil", some "ibbul" like "ijjul", but he found no singular known to the Arabs, it is like "abadid" with none; as for "shamatit", they claim its singular is "shamtat". He said all these have a singular that is not used or spoken, because this pattern is only plural, and he heard eloquent Arabs say "he sent his horses ababil", meaning groups, without using a singular. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 309}

The editor explains "asajii" as plural of "asju'a", what soothsayers speak in rhymed prose, restores from the manuscript Abu Ubayda's wording in Majaz al-Qur'an which the publisher had altered, and glosses "abadid" as scattered, "ababil" as groups from here and groups from there, "ijjul" as a calf, and "shamatit" as scattered pieces.

Their disputing with the Messenger of God that God mentions in this verse was, as reported, as in 13158, from Ibn Abbas: "until when they come to you disputing" are the polytheists disputing with the Muslims about the slaughtered animal, saying: what you slaughter and kill you eat, but what God kills you do not eat, while you follow God's command! {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 310}

On p. 310 the editor notes that a part of the old division from which his manuscript was copied ends here, with the closing: the saying on the interpretation of "they forbid it and keep away from it, and they destroy only themselves and do not perceive" follows; blessing upon Muhammad the prophet and his family, and praise be to God, Lord of the worlds; then it continues: in the name of God, the compassionate, the merciful, my Lord make it easy.

Verse 25 closes here at p. 310, and al-Tabari goes on to [[passage/quran-6-26|6:26]].

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
