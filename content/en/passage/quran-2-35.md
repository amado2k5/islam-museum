## In al-Tabari's commentary
On "And We said, O Adam, dwell, you and your wife, in the Garden" al-Tabari takes the verse as clear evidence that Iblis was expelled from the Garden after refusing to prostrate, that Adam was settled there before Iblis went down to earth, and that Iblis made them slip only after being cursed. He reports from al-Suddi that Iblis, once cursed and expelled and before descending to earth, swore by God's might to lead Adam, his descendants and his wife astray, except God's sincere servants. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 512} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 513}
He then records the difference over when Adam's wife was created. From Ibn Abbas through al-Suddi: Adam was settled in the Garden alone, slept, and woke to find a woman beside his head whom God had made from his rib; he named her Hawwa because she was made from a living thing, and the angels asked him her name to see how far his knowledge went. From Ibn Ishaq, relaying what he had heard from the People of the Book and others, she was created before his settling in the Garden, from a rib of his left side while he slept, and on waking he said she was his flesh, his blood and his wife and found rest in her. The report is given as what "they claim, and God knows best", and the editor glosses "qabilan" as face to face. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 513} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 514}
He notes that "zawja" with the feminine ending is more usual in Arabic for a man's wife than "zawj" without it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 514}
On "and eat from it freely" he explains "raghad" as wide, easy living that does not trouble its owner, citing Imru' al-Qays, and reports from al-Suddi "easy" and from Mujahid "no reckoning against them". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 515}

Al-Tabari explains "raghad" as ease of living, from Ibn Abbas, and gives the sense of the verse as: dwell in the Garden and eat its wide, pleasant provision wherever you wish. He reports from Qatada that the trial written for creation was written for Adam too: all in the Garden was made lawful to him except one tree, and the trial did not leave him until he did what he had been forbidden. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 515} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 516}
On "do not approach this tree" he says "shajar" in Arabic is whatever stands on a stem. He then records the differences over which tree it was. That it was the ear of grain (sunbula) is reported from Ibn Abbas, Abu Malik, Atiyya, Qatada, al-Hasan and Muharib ibn Dithar; the editor calls the chain from Ikrima weak because it includes al-Nadr, who is very weak. He gives a letter from Ibn Abbas to Abu al-Jald and the reply that it was the sunbula and the tree where Adam repented was the olive, the editor calling this chain weak because a man from Banu Tamim is unnamed. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 516} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 517} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 518}
Other reports say wheat: from Ibn Abbas, and from Wahb ibn Munabbih that it was wheat but that a grain of it in the Garden was like a cow's kidney, softer than butter and sweeter than honey, adding that the People of the Torah say wheat; from Yaqub ibn Utba that it was the tree the angels rub against to live forever; and from al-Hasan that it was the grain God made the provision of his children on earth. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 518} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 519}
He then gives the other view, that it was the grapevine, from Ibn Abbas, al-Suddi, Jada ibn Hubayra and al-Sha'bi, with al-Suddi's report adding that the Jews claim it was wheat. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 519}

Al-Tabari goes on to the reports of the vine and grapes, from Said ibn Jubayr, al-Suddi and Muhammad ibn Qays, and then a third view that it was the fig, from Ibn Jurayj on the authority of a Companion. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 520}
His own choice is that God reported that Adam and his wife ate from the tree He had forbidden them after pointing it out to them, but that neither the Qur'an nor sound Sunna gives a clear indication of its name, so he has no knowledge of which tree it was. It was said to be wheat, grapes or fig, and it could be any of them; this is knowledge that does not benefit the one who has it nor harm the one who lacks it. A note records the differences between the manuscript and the printed text for this sentence. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 520} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 521}
On "lest you be of the wrongdoers" he sets out the grammarians' disagreement: some Kufans take the verb as the answer to the prohibition, put in the subjunctive after "fa", meaning that if you approach it you will be of the wrongdoers; some Basrans say a silent "an" is understood, which he rejects since all agree that "it pleased me you stand" is wrong where "your standing" is meant. He gives two ways to read it: joined to "do not approach", so that it is in the jussive, or as the answer to the prohibition, in the subjunctive. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 521} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 522} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 523}
He explains "wrongdoers" as those who go beyond what was permitted, following the path of those who transgress God's limits and disobey His command, "zulm" in Arabic being placing a thing where it does not belong, as in a line of al-Nabigha. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 523}
