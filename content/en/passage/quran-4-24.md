## Summary
Verse 24 of [[passage/quran-4|Surat an-Nisa]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 24 of 176 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**The muhsanat: the first view, married women other than captives (pp. 151-152, reports 8961-8962).** Abu Jafar begins that the sense is: the muhsanat among women are forbidden to you except those who have come to you by ownership. He says the interpreters differed on who the muhsanat are here. Some said they are the women who have husbands, other than those taken captive; ownership means the captives whom capture separated from their husbands, so they became lawful to those who now own them without any divorce from the husband in the enemy land. Ibn Bashshar (8961, from Abd al-Rahman, Israil, Abu Husayn, Said ibn Jubayr) reports from Ibn Abbas that every woman with a husband, intercourse with her is adultery, except those you capture; Abu Kurayb (8962, from Ibn Atiyya, Israil) gives the same. The editor identifies Abd al-Rahman as Ibn Mahdi, Israil as Ibn Yunus ibn Abi Ishaq al-Sabii, Abu Husayn as Uthman ibn Asim al-Asadi, reliable, and Ibn Atiyya as al-Hasan ibn Atiyya al-Kufi, and notes that al-Hakim gave the report in the Mustadrak through Abd al-Wahhab ibn Ata, Shuba, Abu Husayn, calling it sound on the condition of the two shaykhs, with al-Dhahabi agreeing, and that al-Bayhaqi gave it in the Sunan al-Kubra. All of this is the editor's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 152}

**The first view continued, and the captives of Awtas (pp. 152-155, reports 8963-8971).** Al-Muthanna (8963, from Abdallah ibn Salih, Muawiya, Ali ibn Abi Talha) reports from Ibn Abbas that every woman with a husband is forbidden except a slave woman you own who has a husband in the land of war; she is lawful once you have ascertained that she is not pregnant. The editor notes a manuscript wording as if the glottal stop were softened. Al-Muthanna (8964, from Amr ibn Awn, Hushaym, Khalid) gives from Abu Qilaba that it means women taken captive: if a woman is captured whose husband is among her people, there is no harm in having intercourse with her. Yunus (8965, from Ibn Wahb) reports from Ibn Zayd that every married woman is forbidden except captives, even when married, and that his father used to say so. Al-Muthanna (8966, from Utba ibn Said al-Himsi, Said, Makhul) reports that they are the captives; the printed number is smudged and is taken from the editor's note, which says that Utba ibn Said ibn Hibban al-Sulami al-Himsi was called "Wajin", was mentioned by Ibn Hibban among the reliable, and that the Said narrating from Makhul seems to be Said ibn Abd al-Aziz al-Tanukhi. Abu Jafar then says reports support this view that the verse came down about those captured at Awtas. Bishr ibn Muadh (8967, from Yazid, Said, Qatada, Abu al-Khalil, Abu Alqama al-Hashimi) reports from Abu Said al-Khudri that on the day of Hunayn the Prophet sent an army to Awtas; they met an enemy and took captive women who had polytheist husbands, and the Muslims felt hesitant about approaching them, so the verse came down: they are lawful once their waiting periods have ended. Ibn Bashshar (8968, from Abd al-Ala, Said, Qatada, Salih Abu al-Khalil) gives it through Abu Alqama from Abu Said, of a detachment that took a tribe of the Arabs at Awtas, some Companions hesitating because of the husbands. Ali ibn Said al-Kindi (8969, from Abd al-Rahim ibn Sulayman, Ashath ibn Siwar, Uthman al-Batti, Abu al-Khalil) reports from Abu Said that they said: Messenger of God, how can we approach women whose lineage and husbands we know? and the verse came down. Al-Hasan ibn Yahya (8970, from Abd al-Razzaq, al-Thawri, Uthman al-Batti, with the editor's addition "from Abu al-Khalil", from Abu Said) reports that they took women of Awtas who had husbands and disliked approaching them, asked the Prophet, the verse came down, and they held their private parts lawful. Al-Hasan ibn Yahya (8971, from Abd al-Razzaq, Mamar, Qatada, Abu al-Khalil) gives from Abu Said that it came down on the day of Awtas about captives with polytheist husbands, and that the exception means what God had bestowed on them; he said they therefore held them lawful. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 155}

**The editor's long note on the Awtas chains (pp. 153-155).** The editor says these five chains (8967-8971) are one hadith; that Abu al-Khalil is Salih ibn Abi Maryam, whose reliability was established earlier; and that the disagreement over whether he narrates from Abu Said directly or through Abu Alqama al-Hashimi is really a disagreement about Qatada, not about Abu al-Khalil. Abu Alqama al-Hashimi is the Egyptian client of Banu Hashim, a reliable Tabii; Said in the first two chains is Ibn Abi Aruba; Uthman al-Batti is Uthman ibn Muslim al-Basri, reliable, authenticated by Ahmad, Ibn Main and Ibn Sad, "al-Batti" referring to a place called al-Batt. He says al-Mizzi in Tahdhib al-Kamal, followed by Ibn Hajar in Tahdhib al-Tahdhib, asserted without proof that Abu al-Khalil's narration from Abu Said is mursala, although Muslim narrated the hadith both ways, a sign that both were sound to him; he quotes al-Nawawi in his commentary on Muslim that both the inclusion and the omission of Abu Alqama may be right, Abu al-Khalil having heard it both ways, and says this is in his view the truth, an instance of addition within connected chains. He traces the hadith: in Ahmad's Musnad through Abd al-Razzaq from al-Thawri as in 8970; in al-Tirmidhi through Hushaym from Uthman al-Batti, who called it good; in Muslim through Shuba from Qatada from Abu al-Khalil from Abu Said directly, which agrees with the reports here that omit Abu Alqama; and with Abu Alqama in al-Tayalisi, Ahmad, Muslim, Abu Dawud, al-Nasai and al-Bayhaqi through Qatada, from which it appears that Shuba narrated it from Qatada both ways. He notes al-Tirmidhi's remark that he knew no one to mention Abu Alqama except Hammam from Qatada, and says others did know what al-Tirmidhi did not, since Said ibn Abi Aruba and Shuba agreed with Hammam, as Ibn Kathir pointed out in correcting al-Tirmidhi. The hadith is sound by either route, as Muslim gave it; al-Suyuti cited it without the dispute and added al-Firyabi, Ibn Abi Shayba, Abd ibn Humayd, Abu Yala, Ibn al-Mundhir, Ibn Abi Hatim, al-Tahawi and Ibn Hibban. He adds that he supplied "from Abu al-Khalil" in 8970 because it is correct and matches Ahmad's version through al-Thawri, its omission here being the copyists' error. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 155}

**The second view: the sale of a slave woman is her divorce (pp. 155-158, reports 8972-8990).** Abu Jafar says others of those who took the muhsanat to be women with husbands said rather that every married woman is forbidden to anyone but her husband unless she is a slave bought from her owner, in which case she is lawful to her buyer and her owner's sale voids the marriage between her and her husband. Abu al-Saib Salm ibn Junada (8972, from Abu Muawiya, al-Amash, Ibrahim) reports from Abdallah that every woman with a husband is forbidden to you unless you buy her. Al-Muthanna (8973, from Muhammad ibn Jafar, Shuba, Mughira) reports that Ibrahim was asked about a slave woman sold who has a husband and said Abdallah used to say: her sale is her divorce, reciting the verse. The editor notes that the printed text has "Ahmad ibn Jafar" and the correct reading is Muhammad ibn Jafar, known as Ghundar, Shuba's stepson, who sat with him some twenty years and narrated much from him. Ibn Humayd (8974, from Jarir, Mughira, Ibrahim) gives it from Abdallah; al-Hasan ibn Yahya (8975, from Abd al-Razzaq, Mamar, al-Zuhri) from Ibn al-Musayyab that they are the women with husbands, God having forbidden marrying them except by ownership, so her sale is her divorce, and Mamar said al-Hasan said the same. Ibn Bashshar gives it from al-Hasan (8976, from Abd al-Ala, Said, Qatada) and (8977, same chain) from Qatada that Ubayy ibn Kab, Jabir ibn Abdallah and Anas ibn Malik said her sale is her divorce; Ibn al-Muthanna (8978, same chain) has Ubayy, Jabir and Ibn Abbas. Abu Kurayb (8979, from Umar ibn Ubayd, Mughira) reports it from Ibrahim from Abdallah, and the editor notes that Umar ibn Ubayd al-Tanafisi is reliable. Further reports from Abdallah ibn Masud: Ibn Bashshar (8980, from Abd al-Rahman, Sufyan, Mansur, Mughira and al-Amash, Ibrahim), Ibn Bashshar (8981, from Muammal, Said, Hammad) and Ibn al-Muthanna (8982, from Muhammad ibn Jafar, Shuba, Hammad, Ibrahim). Yaqub ibn Ibrahim (8983, from Ibn Ulayya, Khalid, Ikrima) reports from Ibn Abbas that the divorce of a slave woman is six: her sale, her manumission, her gift, her bara'a, and her husband's divorce of her. The editor says all the sources read "six" but name only five, with a mark of doubt, and that he surmised the sixth to be her inheritance; he identifies Khalid as al-Hadhdha, Khalid ibn Mihran, and notes a corruption in Ibn Kathir. Ahmad ibn al-Mughira al-Himsi (8984, from Uthman ibn Said, Isa ibn Abi Ishaq, Ashath, al-Hasan) reports from Ubayy ibn Kab that the sale of a slave woman is her divorce; the editor's note says Uthman ibn Said al-Qurashi al-Himsi is reliable and was said to be one of the abdal, and that the name "Isa ibn Abi Ishaq" is garbled in the print and he read it as Isa ibn Yunus ibn Abi Ishaq al-Sabii, whom he did not find narrating from Ashath ibn Siwar but who, having seen his grandfather, was fit to. Ibn Bashshar (8985, from Abd al-Ala, Awf) reports from al-Hasan that her sale is her divorce, and "his sale is her divorce"; Humayd ibn Masada (8986, from Bishr ibn al-Mufaddal, Khalid) reports from Abu Qilaba that Abdallah said her buyer has more right to her, meaning a slave sold who has a husband. Muhammad ibn Abd al-Ala (8987, from al-Mutamir, his father) reports from al-Hasan that the divorce of a slave woman is her sale; Humayd (8988, from Sufyan ibn Habib, Yunus) from al-Hasan that Ubayy said so; Ahmad (8989, from Sufyan, Khalid, Abu Qilaba) from Ibn Masud that when she is sold with a husband her master has more right to her. Humayd (8990, from Yazid ibn Zurai, Said, Qatada, Abu Mashar) reports from Ibrahim that her sale is her divorce, and that when he was asked about the sale of the male slave he said this is something about which we say nothing. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 158}

**The third view: the chaste, and marriage up to four (pp. 158-160, reports 8991-8999).** Abu Jafar says others held that the muhsanat here are the chaste women, and that the verse then means that chaste women are also forbidden except those a man acquires by marriage, dower, a "sunna" and witnesses, from one to four. The editor's first note on p. 158 is a long comment on the word "sunna" here and in 8991, and on the word "bayyina" to come in 9002 and 9008: he says he is doubtful of both words, does not know what a "bayyina" in marriage is, and thinks "sunna" may mean the guardian, since for him the pillars of marriage are dower, guardian and witnesses, and the requirement of a guardian is not in the plain text of the Quran but in the sunna ("no marriage without a guardian"), on whose senses, and on whether it is a condition of validity or completion, a sunna or an obligation, the jurists differ at length in their books. He says this is only his surmise and invites anyone who finds another sense to teach him. Al-Qasim (8991, from al-Husayn, Hajjaj, Abu Jafar) reports from Abu al-Aliya that God mentioned marriage of two, three and four ([[passage/quran-4-3|4:3]]), then forbade what he forbade by lineage and marriage, then mentioned the muhsanat, returning to the start of the sura, to four, and said they too are forbidden except with dower, sunna and witnesses; the editor says al-Suyuti gave it, ascribing it to Ibn Jarir and Abd ibn Humayd. Al-Hasan ibn Yahya (8992, from Abd al-Razzaq, Mamar, Ayyub, Ibn Sirin) reports from Ubayda that God made four lawful at the opening of the sura and forbade marrying every muhsana beyond the four except by ownership, and Mamar adds that Ibn Tawus reported from his father that your wife is among what you own, God having forbidden adultery, so that no woman is lawful except by ownership. Ali ibn Said al-Kindi (8993, from Abd al-Rahim ibn Sulayman, Hisham ibn Hassan) reports that Ibn Sirin asked Ubayda about the verse and he said four, and (8994, from Ashath ibn Siwar, Ibn Sirin, Ubayda) from Umar ibn al-Khattab the same. Abu Kurayb (8995, from Ibn Yaman, Ashath, Jafar) reports from Said ibn Jubayr that it is the four and what is beyond them is forbidden. Al-Qasim (8996, from al-Husayn, Hajjaj) reports that Ibn Jurayj asked Ata, who said God forbade the kin and then forbade what is above four. Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (8997, from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal, Asbat) reports from al-Suddi that the fifth is forbidden like mothers and sisters. In a branch of this view the muhsanat are the chaste among Muslims and the People of the Book: Ishaq ibn Ibrahim ibn Habib ibn al-Shahid (8998, from Attab ibn Bashir, Khusayf, Mujahid) reports from Ibn Abbas that she is the chaste, sane woman, Muslim or of the People of the Book; Abu Kurayb (8999, from Ibn Idris, some of his companions) reports from Mujahid that they are the chaste. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 160}

**The fourth view: women with husbands, the forbidden thing being adultery with them (pp. 160-161, reports 9000-9004).** Abu Jafar says others held that the muhsanat here are women with husbands, but that what God forbade of them in this verse is adultery with them, and he made them lawful by the exception through marriage or ownership. Muhammad ibn Amr (9000, from Abu Asim, Isa, Ibn Abi Najih) reports from Mujahid; the line is damaged in the print and its sense is adultery. Al-Muthanna (9001, from Abu Hudhayfa, Shibl, Ibn Abi Najih) reports from Mujahid that it forbids adultery and a woman's being married to two husbands. Al-Muthanna (9002, from Abdallah ibn Salih, Muawiya ibn Salih, Ali ibn Abi Talha) reports from Ibn Abbas that every woman with a husband is forbidden to you except the four who are married by "bayyina" and dower. The editor says he did not know what Ibn Abbas meant by this, nor in 9008, and that he found in a hadith in Ahmad and al-Hakim that Rabia ibn Kab al-Aslami was sent by the Prophet to a clan of the Ansar to marry a woman from them, was honoured and married and not asked for the bayyina, returned sad and asked where the dower would come from; he adds that he does not know whether this is the same, and that al-Suyuti ascribes the report to Ibn Abi Hatim and al-Tabarani. Ahmad ibn Uthman (9003, from Wahb ibn Jarir, his father, al-Numan ibn Rashid, al-Zuhri) reports that Said ibn al-Musayyab was asked about the muhsanat and said they are the women with husbands. The editor says the first narrator's name is garbled in the print, that he is the one known as Ibn Abi al-Jawza, from whom Abu Jafar narrates in his History with the same chain, and that he is not Ahmad ibn Uthman ibn Hakim al-Awdi. Report 9004 (Ibn Bashshar from Abd al-Rahman, Sufyan, Hammad, Ibrahim, Abdallah) begins and is cut off at the end of the page. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 161}

**The fourth view concluded, and the reading limited to polytheists (pp. 161-162, reports 9004-9008).** Report 9004 (Ibn Bashshar from Abd al-Rahman, Sufyan, Hammad, Ibrahim, Abdallah) ends: the women with husbands, among Muslims and polytheists; and "Ali" is quoted as saying: the women with husbands among the polytheists, the text not saying who he is. Al-Muthanna (9005, from al-Himmani, Sharik, Salim, Said) reports from Ibn Abbas that every woman with a husband is forbidden to you; then (9006, from Sharik, Abd al-Karim) from Makhul the like, and (9007, from Sharik, al-Salt ibn Bahram) from Ibrahim the like; the editor notes that al-Salt ibn Bahram al-Taymi was mentioned earlier. Muhammad ibn Sad (9008, from his father, his uncle, his father, his grandfather) reports from Ibn Abbas that the reference is to women with husbands, whom it is not lawful to marry, with a warning against "turning" a woman against her husband or making her promises so that she rebels against him; and that every woman who is not married except by "bayyina" and dower is among the muhsanat whom God forbade, the exception being what God made lawful of women, namely free women, two, three and four. The editor says the printed text reads "la tukhlib" and the manuscript has an undotted word, as does al-Durr al-Manthur; he chose "tukhabbib", the word used for corrupting women against their husbands, and cites a line by al-Farazdaq about people he accused of corrupting his wife al-Nawar against him, which he describes as likening such a man to one walking into a lion's den. He refers to the notes on 9002, p. 158 and 8991 on the difficulty of "bayyina", and says al-Suyuti gave the report, ascribing it to Ibn Jarir and Ibn Abi Hatim. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 162}

**Other views: the People of the Book, free women, and the chaste with the married (pp. 163-164, reports 9009-9012).** Abu Jafar says others held that they are the women of the People of the Book, and Ibn Humayd (9009, from Yahya ibn Wadih, Isa ibn Ubayd, Ayyub ibn Abi al-Awja) reports this from Abu Mijlaz. The editor identifies Yahya ibn Wadih as Abu Tumayla al-Ansari; Isa ibn Ubayd al-Marwazi al-Kindi as narrating from Abu Mijlaz here through Ayyub ibn Abi al-Awja, mentioned by Ibn Hibban among the reliable; Ayyub the Qurashi of Marw as counted among the Khurasanis; he says the manuscript and printed text have "Ayyub from Abu al-Awja", an error, and that Abu Mijlaz is Lahiq ibn Humayd. Others said they are free women, and Ibn Bashshar (9010, from Hammad ibn Masada, Sulayman) reports this from Azra. The editor says Hammad ibn Masada al-Basri is reliable and one of Ahmad's teachers, Sulayman is al-Taymi, and Azra is the son of Abd al-Rahman ibn Zurara al-Khuzai; the printed text has "Sulayman ibn Arara", and he does not know where the printer got it, while the manuscript has "Sulayman ibn Azra", who is not among the narrators, so it seems to be "Sulayman from Azra". Others said they are both the chaste and the married, each kind forbidden except by marriage or ownership, and al-Muthanna (9011, from Abdallah ibn Salih, al-Layth, Uqayl) reports from Ibn Shihab al-Zuhri, asked about the verse, that he thinks God forbade in it the married women from being married while with their husbands, that the muhsanat are the chaste, and that they are lawful only by marriage or ownership; ihsan is of two kinds, ihsan of marriage and ihsan of chastity, in free and owned women, and all of it God forbade except by marriage or ownership. Others said it came down about women who emigrated to the Prophet with husbands, whom some Muslims then married, after which their husbands arrived as emigrants and the Muslims were forbidden to marry them; al-Qasim (9012, from al-Husayn, Hajjaj, Ibn Jurayj, Habib ibn Abi Thabit) reports from Abu Said al-Khudri that women used to come to them and then their husbands emigrated and they prevented them, meaning by the verse. The editor says Habib ibn Abi Thabit is Habib ibn Qays ibn Dinar, also called Habib ibn Hind, who narrated from Ibn Umar, Ibn Abbas, Anas, Zayd ibn Arqam, Mujahid, Ata and Tawus, was named by Abu Jafar al-Tabari in his generations of jurists, is reliable, though no narration of his from Abu Said is recorded; and that al-Suyuti ascribed the report to Ibn Jarir alone. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 164}

**Reports that Ibn Abbas and Mujahid found the meaning unclear (p. 165, reports 9013-9014).** Abu Jafar notes that Ibn Abbas and others were reported to find the interpretation unclear to them. Muhammad ibn al-Muthanna (9013, from Muhammad ibn Jafar, Shuba, Amr ibn Murra) reports that a man said to Said ibn Jubayr: did you not see Ibn Abbas when he was asked about this verse and said nothing? He said: he did not know it. Al-Qasim (9014, from al-Husayn, Hushaym, Abd al-Rahman ibn Yahya) reports from Mujahid that if he knew who could explain this verse to him he would ride camels hard to reach him, meaning the passage from its opening to its mention of enjoyment near its end. The editor says he does not know who Abd al-Rahman ibn Yahya is, and that al-Suyuti ascribed the report to Ibn Jarir alone. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 165}

**Al-Tabari on the language of ihsan (pp. 165-166).** Abu Jafar says muhsanat is the plural of muhsana, the woman whose private part is guarded by a husband; one says a man "ahsana" his wife, he "yuhsinuha" with ihsan, and she "hasunat", "tahsun" with hasana, when she is chaste, and she is "hasin", chaste, citing a line of al-Ajjaj. The editor says it is in his Diwan and the Lisan, that the neighbouring lines mention Abu al-Abbas al-Saffah and his caliphate, that he could not tell whom the poet meant by "hasin", perhaps the mother of Abu al-Abbas, that "muls" means freedom from every blameworthy fault like a smooth thing, quotes a line of al-Mutalammis that the free man dies free and accepts no wrong, and glosses "adha" as fault and "qiraf" as mixing. Abu Jafar adds that one also says, when she is chaste and has guarded her private part against depravity, "ahsanat farjaha", so she is "muhsina", as in the report of Maryam daughter of Imran in [[passage/quran-66-12|66:12]], meaning she guarded it from suspicion and kept it from depravity. The fortresses of cities and villages are called "husun" for protecting those who want to be in them and guarding what is behind them, and for the same reason a coat of mail is called "hasina". Since the root of ihsan is preventing and guarding, the muhsanat here are the women kept from you, forbidden except for the exception. He then says ihsan may be by freedom, as in [[passage/quran-5-5|5:5]], by Islam, as in [[passage/quran-4-25|4:25]], by chastity, as in [[passage/quran-24-4|24:4]], and by a husband; God did not single out one muhsana over another here, so every muhsana, whatever the sense of her ihsan, is forbidden to us whether by illicit union or by marriage except those who become ours by purchase, as the Book allowed, or by marriage as the revelation permitted. The editor's two notes trace the run of coordinated clauses in this long sentence and say the apodosis of "idh" is "so it is necessary". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 166}

**Al-Tabari's choice: what is lawful and forbidden, and the report of Barira (pp. 167-168).** Abu Jafar says what God made lawful of free women in marriage is four, apart from those forbidden by lineage and marriage; of slave women, those we capture from the enemy, apart from those whose case matches the forbidden free women by lineage and marriage, and apart from those captured from the two peoples of the Book who have husbands, whom capture makes lawful to their captor after ascertainment of pregnancy and after paying God's right due to the owners of the fifth. As for illicit union, God forbade it with all of them, free or slave, Muslim or polytheist. A slave woman with a husband is not lawful to her owner until her husband divorces her or dies and her waiting period ends; her owner's sale of her neither separates her from her husband nor makes her lawful to the buyer, because of the sound report from the Prophet that, when Aisha freed Barira, he gave her the choice between staying with the husband to whom her owners had married her in slavery and leaving him, and did not make Aisha's freeing of her a divorce; had freeing and the end of Aisha's ownership been a divorce, the choice would have had no meaning. Sale is therefore the counterpart of freeing in that separation follows from neither, although they differ in that a freed woman has the choice and a sold one does not. The editor says Barira's report is in Muslim and is given by al-Bukhari in several places; that the manuscript reads "and separation became obligatory by freeing", an evident error, the printed text being right; and in his note on p. 168 that the printed wording is corrupt and the manuscript wrong with an added "and divorce", so he put in brackets what he inferred from the arguments, and that Abu Jafar's sentence is so densely built that he marked it with dashes to separate the explanation from the main line. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 168}

**The objection on the fifth wife and beyond, and the answer on the captives of Awtas (pp. 168-169).** Abu Jafar says that if someone asks how the exception can cover women beyond four by marriage, since wives are not owned, the answer is that God did not restrict the exception to owned persons to the exclusion of those whose affair one holds by the marriage contract, but covered both senses, ownership of the person and ownership of enjoyment by marriage: one is an ownership of enjoyment, the other of service, enjoyment and disposal. Whoever claims that only some ownerships are meant is asked for proof from a principle or analogue and whatever he says here binds him to the same in the other. If someone objects with Abu Said al-Khudri's report that the verse came down about the captives of Awtas, he replies that they were not approached by ownership and capture without Islam, being polytheist idol-worshippers, and that proof is established that idolaters' women are not lawful by ownership without Islam, and that when they become Muslim, Islam separates them from their husbands, captive or emigrant, though captives are lawful after Islam once pregnancy is ruled out. So the report gives no argument that the muhsanat are only married captives, for even if it came down about them it did not come down to permit their enjoyment by capture alone, and a verse that comes down about one matter covers it and others, as he explained on general and particular in his book "Kitab al-Bayan an Usul al-Ahkam". Of the closing clause he says it means a prescription laid by God, the word for it being put as a verbal noun of other than its own wording; this is permissible because the opening statement of the prohibitions means that God prescribed the prohibition of what he forbade and the permission of what he allowed. The editor glosses "masdar" as the absolute object and refers to earlier places. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 169}

**The interpretation of the clause on God's prescription, and the rejection of the urging reading (pp. 170-171, reports 9015-9020).** Abu Jafar says the interpreters said what he said about its sense. Ibn Bashshar (9015, from Abu Ahmad, Sufyan, Mansur) reports from Ibrahim that it means what he forbade you; al-Qasim (9016, from al-Husayn, Hajjaj, Ibn Jurayj) from Ata that it is the one who prescribed the four for you so that you do not exceed it, the printed word being "tuzilu"; Yaqub ibn Ibrahim (9017, from Ibn Ulayya, Ibn Awn) from Ibn Sirin that he mentioned the verse to Ubayda and Ibn Awn pointed with his four fingers; then (9018, from Hushaym, Hisham) from Ibn Sirin that Ubayda said four; Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (9019, from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal, Asbat) from al-Suddi that it is the four; and Yunus (9020, from Ibn Wahb) from Ibn Zayd that this is God's command for you, meaning what he forbade and what he allowed, and that "the Book" is what he wrote and commanded. The editor refers to earlier explanations of "al-kitab" as obligation, ruling and decree. Abu Jafar then says some grammarians claimed that the word is in the accusative by urging, meaning "hold to what God wrote", and he rejects this as not common in Arab usage, since they hardly put the thing urged in the accusative when it is placed after the urging particle, though it is permissible; the better course is to take the Book on the known usage of the tongue it came down in, together with what the interpreters said. The editor says the two bracketed sentences are his additions, needed for the argument to hold without self-contradiction, the second inferred from al-Tabari's earlier discussion of urging, and refers to al-Farra's Maani al-Quran. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 171}

**What is beyond the forbidden: the views and al-Tabari's choice (pp. 171-173, reports 9021-9024).** Abu Jafar says the interpreters differed on the sense of God's making lawful what is beyond the foregoing. Some said it is what is below five, to be sought with property by marriage: Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (9021, from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal, Asbat) reports from al-Suddi that it is what is below four, and Ibn Waki (9022, from his father, Sufyan, Hisham, Ibn Sirin) from Ubayda al-Salmani the same. Others said it is what lies beyond the relatives named as forbidden: al-Qasim (9023, from al-Husayn, Hajjaj, Ibn Jurayj) reports from Ata that it is whatever lies beyond the kin women. Others said it is the number allowed of the muhsanat, free and slave: Ibn Bashshar (9024, from Abd al-Ala, Said) reports from Qatada that it is ownership. Abu Jafar chooses that God stated the lineage and marriage prohibitions, then those of the muhsanat, then said that he made lawful all beyond these prohibitions stated in the two verses, to be sought with property by marriage and ownership and not by illicit union. If asked which of the muhsanat are lawful, he says what is below five, from one to four of free women, as Ubayda and al-Suddi said, and apart from women with husbands an unlimited number by ownership; the reason is that the clause is general for every woman lawful to us, so directing it to some over others needs a proof that must be accepted, and there is none. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 173}

**The two readings, the meaning of "beyond", and the grammar (pp. 173-174).** Abu Jafar says the readers differed over the verb of making lawful: some read it with a fatha on the first letter, God being the subject, with God prescribing and then making lawful what is beyond, and others with a damma, on the model of the passive in the opening of verse 23. He says both are well known and widespread among the readers of Islam and do not differ in meaning, so whichever the reader uses is right. "Beyond" means apart from the women he forbade, and seeking with property means seeking by purchase or by marriage with a known dower; he cites [[passage/quran-2-91|2:91]] for "beyond" meaning other than a thing. The editor refers to earlier explanations of "ibtagha" and "wara" and to al-Farra. On the grammar, the clause of seeking is in the nominative as an explanation of "what" on the damma reading, and accusative on the fatha reading; the accusative is possible in both by assuming and then dropping a preposition, and the genitive is also possible in that sense. The editor says "tarjama" here means explanation, as al-Farra uses it, and that the printed text has a very corrupt "fa-hadha". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 174}

**Protecting oneself and not committing illicit union (pp. 174-175, reports 9025-9027).** Abu Jafar says the protecting here is chastity in seeking, with property, what lies beyond the forbidden women, and the negation of illicit union is not committing zina. Muhammad ibn Amr (9025, from Abu Asim, Isa, Ibn Abi Najih) reports from Mujahid that the first means marrying one another and the second not committing zina with every fornicatress; al-Muthanna (9026, from Abu Hudhayfa, Shibl, Ibn Abi Najih) gives the same from Mujahid, and that safah is zina; Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (9027, from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal, Asbat) reports from al-Suddi that it means protecting oneself without zina. The editor refers to the recent explanation of ihsan. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 175}

**The clause on enjoyment: the first view, marriage (pp. 175-176, reports 9028-9032).** Abu Jafar says the interpreters differed on the enjoyment mentioned. Some said the sense is: what you married of them and had intercourse with, give their dowers as a fixed, known duty. Al-Muthanna (9028, from Abdallah ibn Salih, Muawiya ibn Salih, Ali ibn Abi Talha) reports from Ibn Abbas that when a man marries a woman and has intercourse once, her whole dower is due, and that enjoyment is marriage, citing the giving of dowers in [[passage/quran-4-4|4:4]]. Al-Hasan ibn Yahya (9029, from Abd al-Razzaq, Mamar) reports from al-Hasan that it is marriage; al-Muthanna (9030, from Abu Hudhayfa, Shibl, Ibn Abi Najih) from Mujahid, marriage; al-Qasim (9031, from al-Husayn, Hajjaj, Ibn Jurayj) from Mujahid, that he meant marriage; and Yunus (9032, from Ibn Wahb) from Ibn Zayd that this is marriage and the Quran has only marriage: when you take her and enjoy her, give her her wage, the dower; if she remits some of it to you it is lawful for you; God made the waiting period binding on her and gave her inheritance, and enjoyment here is marriage once he has been with her. The editor refers to the language indexes of earlier volumes under "mata", "ata" and "farada". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 176}

**The second view: temporary marriage (pp. 176-177, reports 9033-9039).** Abu Jafar says others held that the sense is: what you enjoyed of them for a wage, the enjoyment of pleasure, not by unrestricted marriage in the manner that involves a guardian, witnesses and dower. Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (9033, from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal, Asbat) reports from al-Suddi, who gives the verse with an added clause fixing a term, that this is mutah: a man marries a woman on condition for a fixed term, with two witnesses and the permission of her guardian; when the term ends he has no claim on her and she is free of him, she must ascertain her womb, and there is no inheritance between them. The editor notes that the term clause here is from al-Suddi's wording of the verse, and refers to reports 9035-9043 and to al-Tabari's rejection of this reading at the end of the commentary on the verse. Muhammad ibn Amr (9034, from Abu Asim, Isa, Ibn Abi Najih) reports from Mujahid that it means mutah marriage. Abu Kurayb (9035, from Yahya ibn Isa, Nusayr ibn Abi al-Ashath) reports from a son of Habib ibn Abi Thabit, from his father, that Ibn Abbas gave him a codex and said this is on Ubayy's reading, and Yahya said he saw the codex with Nusayr with the reading that adds the term clause. The editor says the manuscript and printed text have "Abu Bakr" for "Abu Kurayb", a copyist's slip; that Yahya ibn Isa al-Ramli, Abu Kurayb's teacher, was treated earlier; that Nusayr ibn Abi al-Ashath al-Aradi al-Asadi narrated from Abu Ishaq al-Sabii; that he could not establish which son of Habib ibn Abi Thabit is meant, all being reliable; and that the father Habib narrated from Ibn Abbas. Humayd ibn Masada (9036, from Bishr ibn al-Mufaddal, Dawud) reports from Abu Nadra that he asked Ibn Abbas about the mutah of women and Ibn Abbas said: do you not read sura al-Nisa? I said yes; he said: and what do you read in it, with the term clause added? I said no, had I read it so I would not have asked you; he said: it is so. Ibn al-Muthanna (9037, from Abd al-Ala, Dawud) gives the like from Abu Nadra; Ibn al-Muthanna (9038, from Muhammad ibn Jafar, Shuba) reports that Abu Nadra read the verse to Ibn Abbas, who read it with the term clause, and when Abu Nadra said he did not read it so, said: by God, God sent it down so, three times, the narrator from Abu Nadra being printed as Abu Salama; and Ibn al-Muthanna (9039, from Abu Dawud, Shuba, Abu Ishaq) reports from Umayr that Ibn Abbas read it with the term clause. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 177}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict. We stop at p. 177 in the commentary on verse 24 of sura 4, in the view that the enjoyment means temporary marriage, after report 9039, and continue from p. 178. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 177}
