## Summary
Verse 260 of [[passage/quran-2|Surat al-Baqara]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 260 of 286 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

Al-Tabari's commentary on ([[passage/quran-2-260|2:260]]), whose first page in volume 5 is p. 485. He says the connection to the earlier passages ([[passage/quran-2-258|2:258]]) and ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]) is sound because seeing there is with the heart, not the eye, meaning have you not known and so recalled, and so it is joined sometimes by what matches its wording and sometimes by what matches its sense; this is al-Tabari's explanation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 485}

He then gives the interpreters' differing accounts of why Abraham asked his Lord to show him how He revives the dead. Some said he saw an animal carcass divided among beasts of prey and birds and asked to see how God would revive it despite its flesh being scattered in the bellies of birds and beasts, to see it plainly and add certainty by sight to what he knew by report. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 485} Qatada (5963): we were told that Abraham, the friend of God, came upon a carcass divided among animals and beasts, made his request, and his Lord asked whether he did not believe, and he answered yes but sought reassurance. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 485} Al-Dahhak (5964): Abraham passed a dead animal, decayed and divided among the winds and beasts, stood looking, though he already knew God is able to do it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 486} Ibn Jurayj (5965): it reached me that Abraham, walking on the road, came upon the carcass of a donkey with beasts and birds over it, its flesh torn apart and its bones left; when the beasts went and the birds flew to hills and mounds he stood amazed and said he knew God would gather it from the bellies of those beasts and birds, and asked to be shown; his Lord asked whether he did not believe and he said yes, but report is not like seeing. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 486} Ibn Zayd (5966): Abraham passed a fish half on land and half in the sea, the sea creatures eating the part in the sea and the beasts the part on land, and the wicked one, whom the editor's note says is Iblis, asked when God would gather this from the bellies of these; and he made his request. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 486} The editor's notes correct readings of the manuscript and explain a verb meaning they divided it up among themselves. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 486}

Others said the cause was the debate and dispute between him and Nimrod. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 486} Ibn Ishaq (5967): after what passed between Abraham and his people, which God relates in ([[passage/quran-21-51|21:51]]), Nimrod, as they report, asked Abraham about the God he worshipped and described the power of; Abraham answered that He gives life and death; Nimrod said he too gives life and death, and the dispute went as God relates; and then Abraham asked his Lord to show him the reviving of the dead, and his Lord asked whether he did not believe, and he said yes, with no doubt in God or in His power, but he wished to know it and his heart longed for it, so that it might be at rest in what it longed for once known. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 487} Al-Tabari says the two accounts are close in sense in that the request was to see plainly what he already knew by report; this is his assessment. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 487}

Others said the request came at the glad tidings that God had taken him as a friend, so he asked to be shown a sign at once so that his heart would be at rest that God had chosen him as friend; report 5968 from al-Suddi begins at the foot of p. 487 and we stop before it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 487} These reports are given as transmitted, without a verdict from the museum.

More on why Abraham asked in ([[passage/quran-2-260|2:260]]). Report 5968 from al-Suddi, for the view that the request came at the tidings of friendship: when God took Abraham as a friend the angel of death asked his Lord for leave to bring him the news, and was allowed; he came to Abraham, who was out, and entered his house; Abraham was the most jealous of men and locked the door when he went out; when he came and found a man in his house he rose to seize him and asked who permitted him to enter his house, and the angel said the Lord of this house; Abraham said you have spoken truly and knew it was the angel of death, who told him God had taken him as a friend, and he praised God. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 487} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 488} Abraham then asked to be shown the form in which he takes the souls of unbelievers; the angel said he could not bear it, but Abraham insisted, turned his face away and looked, and saw a black man whose head reached the sky, flame coming from his mouth and ears, and Abraham fainted, then recovered with the angel back in his first form. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 488} He said that if the unbeliever met at death only your form in affliction and grief it would suffice, and asked to see how he takes the souls of believers; he turned away, then looked and saw a young man of the fairest face and sweetest scent in white clothes, and said that if the believer had with his Lord only this form of yours as comfort and honour it would suffice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 488} The angel left and Abraham stood praying that his Lord show him how He revives the dead so that he would know he was His friend; his Lord asked whether he believed in His friendship, and he said yes, but so that he be at rest in that friendship. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 488} This report is given as transmitted from al-Suddi, without a verdict from the museum. Al-Tabari's choice. Abu Jafar says the best interpretation of the verse is what was authentically reported from the Messenger of God in this hadith, and that Abraham's request to be shown the reviving of the dead arose from a passing thought cast into his heart by Satan, like what we reported from Ibn Zayd: Abraham saw the fish half on land and half in the sea, shared by land beasts, sea creatures and birds, and Satan put it in his mind when God would gather this from the bellies of these, so he asked his Lord to show him plainly, that Satan could not afterwards cast the like into his heart. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 491} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 492} His Lord asked whether he did not trust that He is able, and he said yes, but I asked so that my heart be at rest and Satan not be able to cast into it what he did when I saw this fish; this choice and argument are attributed to al-Tabari, and he gives it from Ibn Zayd in report 5975. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 492} The reassurance sought means that the heart settles and is calm with the certainty it attains, which is the interpretation of those who took the sense to be that his faith might increase or that he might be certain. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 492} The editor notes that the manuscript has a garbled word in three places which he corrected from al-Qurtubi's commentary. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 492}

Those who glossed the reassurance as certainty or increase of certainty: Said ibn Jubayr (5976), that he may be certain, and (5977, 5982, 5983, 5985) that my certainty or faith may increase; al-Dahhak (5978), that certainty may increase; Qatada (5979, 5980), certainty added to his certainty; al-Rabi' (5981), he wanted his certainty to increase; Mujahid and Ibrahim (5984), that I may add faith to my faith. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 492} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 493} Al-Tabari notes he has already given the view that the sense is that he is God's friend, reports 5968 and 5969. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 493} Others said the reassurance means to know that God answers him when he calls and gives him when he asks; their reports begin at the foot of the page and we stop there. These reports are given as transmitted, al-Tabari's choice is attributed to him, and the museum gives no verdict.

More on ([[passage/quran-2-260|2:260]]). Report 5986 from Ibn Abbas on the reassurance sought: to know that You answer me when I call You and give me when I ask. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 493} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 494} Al-Tabari then explains God's question about his belief: it means did you not trust, and the editor refers to the language indexes on belief in the sense of trusting. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 494} Al-Suddi (5987) and Said ibn Jubayr (5988): did you not hold certain that I am your friend; Ibn Zayd (5989): did you not hold certain. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 494}

Al-Tabari then explains God's command to him to take four birds, in ([[passage/quran-2-260|2:260]]). The four birds are reported to be the rooster, the peacock, the raven and the dove. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 494} Ibn Ishaq (5990), from some people of knowledge: the earlier People of the Book mention that he took a peacock, a rooster, a raven and a dove; Mujahid (5991): the rooster, peacock, raven and dove; Ibn Jurayj (5992): they claim a rooster, a raven, a peacock and a dove; Ibn Zayd (5993): he took a peacock, a dove, a raven and a rooster, differing in kind and colour. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 494} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 495} What is transmitted here from the People of the Book is given as transmitted, with no verdict from the museum.

Al-Tabari then says the reciters differed on the reading of the verb meaning draw them to you. Most reciters of Medina, the Hijaz and Basra read it with a damma on the first letter, from the saying I inclined to this matter, as in I long for you, meaning yearning and inclined, and he cites a line of unknown authorship recited by al-Farra' about a lover's looking back toward his neighbours on the day of parting, giving the plural of the adjective for inclined. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 495} In notes the editor gives sources for the line, a variant wording in the printed text against the manuscript, and the line that follows it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 495} He also cites a line of al-Tirimmah about chaste women whom passion inclines, explaining that the verb there means to incline them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 496} In a note the editor cites al-Tirimmah's Diwan, verses before it and explanations of their words. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 496} On this reading al-Tabari gives the sense: draw them to you and turn them toward you, as one says turn your face to me, meaning face me; and for those who take it so, something is omitted in the speech, left out because the plain wording shows it, so it means take four birds, draw them to you, then cut them up, then distribute parts of them over the mountains; this construction is al-Tabari's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 496} He adds that on the damma reading it could also mean cut them up, citing a line of Tawba ibn al-Humayyir about pulling a rope; we stop here, before the rest of the citation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 496} These reports and readings are given as transmitted, al-Tabari's construction is attributed to him, and the museum gives no verdict.

More on the verb for drawing the birds in ([[passage/quran-2-260|2:260]]). Al-Tabari explains that Tawba ibn al-Humayyir's line means he cuts them, and that if this is the sense of the command, the speech has a transposition, so that the meaning is take four birds to you and cut them up, with the word for to you belonging to the taking; this is al-Tabari's construction. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 497} In a note the editor gives two lines from a poem of Tawba, thinks their order differs in the transmission of his verse, calls al-Tabari's version of it not good, and explains words in the lines, among them the ropes, the girth straps and the knots. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 497}

A group of Kufan reciters read the verb with a kasra on its first letter, meaning cut them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 497} A group of Kufan grammarians claimed that they know of no such use meaning to cut in Arab speech and that they know the kasra and damma of the first letter only as one meaning, inclining, and that these are two dialects, the kasra being a dialect of Hudhayl and Sulaym; they recited two lines of a man of Sulaym about thick black hair over the neck and the swaying of the camel litter, and the editor says he does not know who said them and takes the word meanings from al-Farra' and Lisan al-Arab. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 497} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 498} These say that speakers of this dialect say he inclined it, inclining it, and turn your face to me, meaning incline it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 498}

Some Kufan grammarians claimed that no sense of cutting can be found for this verb with a damma or kasra unless, on the kasra reading, it is a reversed form, in which the final root letter is put in place of the middle and the middle in place of the final, so that it comes from a verb meaning to draw water and then cut off and draw again; the Arabs say he spent the night drawing at his trough when he drew and stopped and drew. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 498} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 499} They cite a line, whose author the editor does not know, about a glance that cut, and another about Syria and its people and fathers who became desert Arabs, asking why death did not cut them off. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 498} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 499} The editor says this is a good explanation not found in the dictionaries and gives sources for the lines, the explanation of their words and a better wording in Lisan al-Arab. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 498} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 499} The grammarians explain that its y, the final root letter, was moved to become the middle and the middle moved to be the final, hence it was said to turn, as a verb meaning he raided gave a verb of the other shape. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 499}

The Basran grammarians said that the verb has the same meaning read with damma or kasra, namely cutting in this place, and that they are two dialects, one saying it with the damma form, the other with the kasra form; they cited the line of Tawba ibn al-Humayyir given before and a line of al-Mu'alla ibn Jamal al-Abdi about choice black goats scattered by a spotted, tufted buck. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 499} The editor's notes correct the poet's name, add that the line is also ascribed to Aws ibn Hajar, give sources, a better wording and glosses; these are the editor's, not al-Tabari's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 499} We stop here, as the discussion runs onto the next page. These positions of the Kufan and Basran grammarians are given as attributed to them, with no verdict from the museum.

More of the Basran grammarians' evidence for the verb meaning to cut up in ([[passage/quran-2-260|2:260]]). They say al-Mu'alla ibn Jamal's line means the buck scatters and cuts up the goats, and cite a line of al-Khansa in which the lofty ones are mountains that split and scatter, and a line of Abu Dhu'ayb about a wild bull alarmed by dogs. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 500} They said the verb has two senses, I inclined it and I cut it, and reported hearing that they decided a judgment with it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 500} In notes the editor explains words in al-Mu'alla's line, refers to Abu Ubayda's Majaz al-Qur'an, Abu Dhu'ayb's Diwan, the Mufaddaliyyat and the books on words with opposite senses, and says that the reading of the verb in Abu Dhu'ayb's line, as Abu Ubayda, al-Asma'i, Ibn al-Sikkit and al-Tabari relate it, is strange and that he rejects its meaning as damaging the poem's account of the bull; this view is the editor's, not al-Tabari's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 500}

Al-Tabari's choice. Abu Jafar says the Basran view, that the damma and kasra of the first letter are one meaning, two dialects whose sense here is cutting up, and that the word for to you is put first because it belongs to the taking, is sounder than the view of the Kufan grammarians who denied that cutting has an intelligible sense except through the reversal; his argument is the consensus of interpreters that the verb's sense does not go beyond one of two, either cut them up or draw them to you, whether read with kasra or damma, with no regard to the vowel and no distinction between the two readings, which is the clearest proof that the Basrans are right and the Kufans wrong. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 501} He says that if the Kufans had taken it as cutting by reversal, they would have distinguished the sense of kasra from that of damma, since one who reverses may not read it with the damma; yet despite their different readings they interpreted it one way, which shows the error of those who said the kasra meaning cutting is a reversal, and the ignorance of whoever claimed that the verb in the sense of cut is not known in Arab speech; this choice and argument are attributed to al-Tabari. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 501} The editor explains in a note the construction of the sentence and the predicate of the phrase on being sounder. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 501}

Al-Tabari then gives reports he had at hand that the verb means cut them up: Ibn Abbas (5994), that it is a Nabataean word, so split them, with the editor's notes on transmitters in the chain (Sulayman ibn Abd al-Jabbar, Muhammad ibn al-Salt and Abu Kudayna); Ibn Abbas (5995), that it is only a parable: cut them up, then put them in the quarters of the world, a quarter here and a quarter there, then call them and they come to you hastening, with the editor's note on Abu Jamra and the correction of his name in the printed text and manuscript; Ibn Abbas (5996), cut them; Abu Malik (5997, 5998), cut them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 502} Report 5999 begins at the foot of the page and we stop before it. These reports are given as transmitted, al-Tabari's choice is attributed to him, the notes and corrections are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.

More reports taking the verb as cutting up in ([[passage/quran-2-260|2:260]]). Report 5999 from Said ibn Jubayr: the wing of this one by the head of that, and the head of that by the wing of this. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 502} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 503} Ikrima (6000): in Nabataean, cut them; Mujahid (6001): cut them, and (6002, 6003) pluck them with feathers and flesh, tearing them, then mix their flesh with their feathers; in a note the editor says the printed text and the manuscript differ in pointing the word, and prefers another reading close in sense. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 503} Qatada (6004): the Prophet of God was told to take four birds and slaughter them, then mix their flesh, feathers and blood; and (6005) tear them, and he was told to mix blood with blood and feather with feather, then distribute parts of them over the mountains. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 503} Al-Dahhak (6006): split them, and the Nabataean word for it is splitting; al-Suddi (6007): cut them; al-Rabi' (6008): cut them and tear them to pieces; Ibn Ishaq (6009): cut them, and in Arab speech it is the same verb. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 503} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 504}

Al-Tabari then says that in these reports there is clear evidence for what he has said and against those who differ, so whether the reader reads the first letter with damma or with kasra makes no difference, as they are two well-known dialects of one meaning, but the form he prefers to read is the damma one, because it is the higher and better known of the two and the more widespread among the Arab tribes; this is his preference. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 504} He adds that the view taking the verb as draw them to you, turn them toward you and gather them was held by a small number of interpreters, and the editor notes in a note that he supplied this sentence from the course of the commentary, because its place in the printed text and manuscript is disordered, which he takes as another sign of the copyist's carelessness. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 504} Ibn Abbas (6010): bind them; Ata (6011): draw them to you; Ibn Zayd (6012): gather them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 505}

Al-Tabari then turns to the next part of ([[passage/quran-2-260|2:260]]): the interpreters differed on what it means to distribute parts of them over the mountains. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 505} Some said it means on each quarter of the world a part of them; Ibn Abbas (6013): put them in the quarters of the world, a quarter here and a quarter there, then call them and they come to you hastening, and the editor refers to his note on report 5990 about the name of the transmitter. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 505} We stop here. These reports are given as transmitted, al-Tabari's preference is attributed to him, the corrections are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.

More accounts of where the bird parts were placed in ([[passage/quran-2-260|2:260]]). Ibn Abbas (6014): when he had bound them he slaughtered them, then distribute parts of them over the mountains. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 506} Qatada (6015): the Prophet of God was told to take four birds and slaughter them, then mix their flesh, feathers and blood, then divide them among four mountains; we were told that he held their heads in his hand, and bone went to bone, feather to feather and piece of flesh to piece of flesh, before Abraham's eyes; then he called them and they came to him hastening on their feet, each bird casting its head toward him; this is a parable God gave Abraham, saying that as He raised these birds from these four mountains so will God raise people on the Day of Resurrection from the quarters of the earth and its regions. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 506} The editor says in a note that he does not understand a phrase at the start of the report, suspects a corruption, and has not found the report elsewhere. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 506} Al-Rabi' (6016): he slaughtered them, then cut them up, mixed their flesh with their feathers, divided them into four parts and put a part on each mountain; bone went to bone, feather to feather and flesh to flesh before Abraham's eyes; then he called them and they came hastening, meaning running on their feet; this is a parable God showed Abraham. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 506}

Ibn Ishaq (6017), from some people of knowledge: the People of the Book mention that he took the four birds, cut each into four parts and put a quarter of each bird on each of four mountains, so on each mountain was a quarter of the peacock, of the rooster, of the raven and of the dove; then he called them by God's leave and each quarter leapt to its fellow until they gathered, each bird as it was before being cut, and they came to him hastening, and he was told that this is how God gathers His servants and revives the dead for the resurrection from the east and west of the earth, its north and its south, and so he understood; the editor explains in a note the reference in the report to what Nimrod said. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 506} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 507} This report from the People of the Book is given as transmitted, with no verdict from the museum. Ibn Zayd (6018): he took a peacock, a dove, a raven and a rooster, cut them up and scattered them in quarters on the mountains, then called them and they all came; God said that as he had called them and they came, and as He had revived these and gathered them after this, so He would gather these too, meaning the dead. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 507}

Others said the sense is: put a part of them on each of the mountains where the birds and beasts were that ate of the carcass Abraham saw dead, and they said these were seven mountains. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 507} Ibn Jurayj (6019): when Abraham said what he said on seeing the animal from which the birds and beasts had dispersed, he slaughtered the birds, mixed their blood, feathers and flesh, and put a part on each mountain where he had seen the birds and beasts go; he made seven parts, kept their heads with him, then called them by God's leave, and he saw every drop of blood fly to the other, every feather and every bone fly one to another from the mountain tops until each body met its fellow in the sky, and then they came running until the heads joined. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 507} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 508} Al-Suddi (6020): he put the birds on seven mountains, cut them into limbs, not putting a limb of a bird with its own fellow, then put the head of this with the leg of that and the breast of this with the wing of that, divided them on seven mountains, then called them and each limb flew to its own and they all came to him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 508} In a note the editor explains the word for breast and corrects a phrase in the printed text and the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 507}

Others said God told him to put it on every mountain; Mujahid (6021): scatter them on every mountain, and (6022) put them as parts on every mountain, and this is a parable God drew for Abraham. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 508} These accounts and reports are given as transmitted and attributed to their holders, with no verdict from the museum; the corrections and references are the editor's.

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-260|2:260]]). Mujahid (6023): he scattered them in parts over every mountain, then called them by God's leave and they came, and so God revives the dead, a parable God drew for Abraham. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 509} Al-Dahhak (6024, 6025): he was told to mix up their legs, heads and wings and then distribute them over the mountains, and Abraham mixed their legs and wings. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 509}

Al-Tabari's choice. Abu Jafar says the best interpretation of the verse is Mujahid's: that God told Abraham to scatter the limbs of the four birds, after cutting them up, over all the mountains he could reach at the time of the command, since God said every mountain, and the word every indicates inclusion of all it is attached to, single in form and plural in sense; this is his choice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 509} He argues that the mountains meant can only be some or all: if some, only those to which Abraham had access to scatter the limbs; if all, the same; and God reports that He told him to place it on every mountain, either every mountain Abraham knew by name or the mountains on the earth. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 509} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 510} As for the saying that they were four mountains and the saying that they were seven, he finds no indication of the truth of either that would let him adopt it; God told Abraham to place the parts of the four birds on every mountain so that he would see His power to gather their parts while scattered in various places until He joined them one to another and they returned as before, living birds that fly, and Abraham's heart would be at rest and he would know that God would gather the limbs of the dead for the resurrection and join their parts after decay. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 510} This construction and argument are al-Tabari's. The editor's notes give references, corrections of the manuscript's readings and two words he supplied from context, and judge al-Tabari's distinction between a part and a share a good one seldom found in the dictionaries. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 509} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 510}

Al-Tabari says a part of anything is some of it, whether or not the whole divides into it exactly, unlike a share, which is the portion into which the whole divides exactly, which is why people use share and not part when they speak of inheritance portions. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 510} As for the command to call them, it means what is reported from Mujahid, that he was told to say to the parts of the birds, after scattering, come by God's leave. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 511} He raises the question whether he was told to call them while they were torn and dead or after they were revived: if the first, what sense is there in commanding one without life to come; if the second, why call them when he had seen them being raised on the mountain tops. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 511} He answers that the command to call them while they were scattered parts is a command of coming into being, like God's command to those He turned into apes in ([[passage/quran-2-65|2:65]]), not a command of worship, which would be impossible before the one commanded exists; this answer is al-Tabari's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 511}

Al-Tabari then explains the close of ([[passage/quran-2-260|2:260]]), on the two attributes of God that it mentions: know, Abraham, that the One who revived these birds after you tore them up and scattered their parts on the mountains, gathering them and returning the spirit to them until they were as before, is mighty in His seizing when He seizes the tyrants and arrogant who disobeyed His command, rejected His messengers and worshipped another, and in His vengeance until He takes it, and wise in His command. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 511} Ibn Ishaq (6026): mighty in His seizing, wise in His command. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 511} Here p. 511 and its commentary on ([[passage/quran-2-260|2:260]]) end. These reports are given as transmitted, al-Tabari's choice, argument and answer are attributed to him, and the corrections and references are the editor's, with no verdict from the museum.
