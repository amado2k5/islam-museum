## In al-Tabari's commentary
Al-Tabari explains "and give good news" as "tell them"; glad tidings are news of what pleases. It is God's command to His Prophet to announce to the believers who confirm their faith with good works that they have gardens with rivers flowing beneath them, and to them alone, not to those who denied him or who professed belief in words, denied it inwardly and never made it real in deeds; for these there is the Fire. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 383} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 384}
He says "jannat" is the plural of "janna", a garden, and that what is meant is its trees, fruit and plantings, not its ground, hence "rivers flowing beneath them", since water running under the ground would not be seen without removing a covering. He reports from Masruq that the date palms of Paradise are heaped with fruit from root to branch, the fruit like large jars, and when one is picked another takes its place, while its water flows without a channel; al-Tabari holds this a better description than rivers beneath the earth. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 384} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 385}
He reads the verse as God encouraging His servants to believe, as the previous verse warned them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 385}
On "whenever they are provided with fruit from it they say: this is what we were provided with before" he says the pronoun returns to the gardens, meaning their trees. He gives two readings. The first, "what we were given before in the world", comes from Ibn Abbas, Ibn Mas'ud and others of the Companions, Qatada and Ibn Zayd, with Mujahid's "how like it is". The second is "what we were given from Paradise's fruits before this", because of how closely they resemble one another in colour and taste, reasoning that when anything is picked the like of it returns in its place, reported from Abu Ubayda through Masruq. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 385} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 386}

Al-Tabari sets out the second reading more fully: the fruits are alike because the one that returns matches the one picked in all respects. A third view is that they say it because it matches the earlier fruit in colour though not in taste, and he reports from Yahya ibn Abi Kathir that one of them is brought a dish and eats, then another is brought and he says "this is what we were brought before", and the angel says "eat, the colour is one and the taste differs". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 387}
He chooses "what we were given before in the world", holding that the plain wording rules out the other readings, because God says "whenever they are provided", which includes the very first provision they receive in Paradise, before which they had none of its fruit; they could not then say "before" of Paradise's fruit. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 387} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 388}
To the objection that what they were given before has been used up by eating, he answers that the meaning is "this is of the kind we were given before, of its type in marks and colours", as a man told that someone prepared certain dishes for him says "this is my food in my house", meaning the kind, not the very items. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 388} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 389}
On "and they are given it in likeness" he says the pronoun refers to the provision, and gives views on the likeness: that all of it is the best, with none inferior, reported from al-Hasan al-Basri, Qatada and Ibn Jurayj; and that it is alike in colour and look but differs in taste, reported from Ibn Abbas, Ibn Mas'ud and others of the Companions, and from Mujahid and al-Rabi' ibn Anas. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 389} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 390}

Al-Tabari goes on through the views on "alike": alike in both colour and taste (from Mujahid and Yahya ibn Sa'id), and resembling this world's fruit but better (from Qatada and Ikrima). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 391}
He reports from Ibn Abbas that nothing in Paradise resembles anything in this world except in name, and from Ibn Zayd that they know its names as in the world, apple as apple and pomegranate as pomegranate, though it is not the same in taste. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 392}
He chooses "alike in colour and appearance, different in taste", for the reason given on "this is what we were given before". Against those who deny that anything in Paradise has a counterpart in this world he argues that the names are agreed to match, so colours may match too, with greater splendour and beauty in Paradise, and whatever is said of the one holds for the other. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 392} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 393}
He reports from Abu Musa al-Ash'ari that when God took Adam out of Paradise He provisioned him with its fruit and taught him the craft of everything, so "these fruits of yours are from Paradise's fruits, except that these change and those do not". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 393}
He mentions a view of some Arabic grammarians that the likeness is in excellence and rejects it, since it departs from all the interpreters. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 394}
