## In al-Tabari's commentary

On "when We took a covenant from the Children of Israel: you shall worship none but God", al-Tabari says "mithaq" comes from making firm by oath or the like, so the sense is: remember when We took your covenant. Readers differ on "la ta'budun" between a "ta" and a "ya", with one meaning, because taking a covenant is administering an oath: you say "I made your brother swear he would stand" in either person. He holds the verb is in the indicative because "an" is dropped, and notes the Basran grammarians' view that it is a quoted speech, which is close to his. He reports from Abu al-'Aliya and al-Rabi' that they were bound to be sincere to God and worship no other, and from Ibn Jurayj that it was the covenant taken from them in al-Ma'ida. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 288} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 289} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 290}
On "and to be good to parents", it is joined to the place of the dropped "an", so the sense is: that you worship none but God and show kindness to parents. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 290}

Al-Tabari explains the grammar of "and to parents, kindness": it is joined to the place of the dropped "an", and "ihsan" is accusative through an implied verb; he rejects the view that the "ba" belongs to "ihsan" moved before it, and the view that it makes two clauses, since the sentence runs as one. The kindness meant, he says, is like what God made binding on our community toward parents: good dealing, gentle words, lowering the wing of humility, compassion and prayer for their good. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 290} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 291} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 292}
On "and to kinsfolk, orphans and the poor", he says joining his kin and knowing his right, showing orphans tenderness, and giving the poor their due, which God made binding on their wealth; the "miskin" is one humbled and abased through need. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 292} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 293}
On "and speak good to people", he answers why a command follows a statement: taking a covenant is itself speech, so a command or prohibition suits the place of the statement, and he notes that in the reading of Ubayy ibn Ka'b it is "do not worship". Readers differ on "husnan": with fatha on both letters, with a damma and a sukun, and a reading "husna". In his own choice al-Tabari prefers the fatha reading, as it describes a kind of speech, and he calls it the known one; he judges "husna" mistaken because it departs from the reading of the Muslims and from familiar Arabic usage. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 293} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 294} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 295}
