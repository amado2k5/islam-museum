## Summary
Verse 14 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 14 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

## Verse 14 in al-Tabari's commentary (Shakir ed., volume 6, pp. 243-244)

For [[passage/quran-3-14|3:14]] al-Tabari says the sense is that the love of what people desire, of women, sons and the rest the verse lists, has been made attractive to them, and that he meant by it to rebuke the Jews who preferred this world and the love of leadership in it to following Muhammad, after knowing that he spoke truly. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 243} Al-Hasan used to say: part of its adornment is that no one censures it more than its Creator. In 6694 (Ahmad ibn Hazim, through Abu Nu'aym and Abu al-Ash'ath, from al-Hasan). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 243} The editor says al-Qurtubi (4: 28) gives it in the form of a question, that he did not find al-Hasan's report but recalls having read it long ago, and that al-Hasan mocks the matter of this world in it, saying that part of its beauty is that the one who censures it and calls it ugly is the one who created it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 243}

In 6695, Umar (through Ibn Humayd, Jarir, Ata and Abu Bakr ibn Hafs ibn Umar ibn Sad): when this verse came down, I said: now, Lord, when You have made it attractive to us! Then came the next verse ([[passage/quran-3-15|3:15]]), which speaks of what is better than that for those who are mindful of God with their Lord. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 244}

Al-Tabari ([[passage/quran-3-14|3:14]]) then says that the word for the plural is the plural of qintar, and records the interpreters' difference over the amount of a qintar in several views.

The first is that it is twelve hundred uqiya. In 6696, Mu'adh ibn Jabal (through Ibn Bashshar, Abd al-Rahman, Sufyan, Abu Husayn and Salim ibn Abi al-Jad). In 6697, Mu'adh to the same effect (Abu Kurayb, Abu Bakr ibn Ayyash, Abu Husayn). In 6698, Ibn Umar (Yunus, Ibn Wahb, Hafs ibn Maysara, Abu Marwan, Abu Tayba). In 6699, Asim ibn Abi al-Najud (Yaqub ibn Ibrahim, al-Qasim ibn Malik al-Muzani, al-Ala ibn al-Musayyab). In 6700, Abu Hurayra to the same effect (Ibn Bashshar, Abd al-Rahman ibn Mahdi, Hammad ibn Zayd, Asim ibn Bahdala, Abu Salih). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 244} The editor says Ibn Kathir (2: 109-110) gives this report and points to Ahmad's version, by the chain of Abd al-Samad, Hammad, Asim and Abu Salih from Abu Hurayra, in a raised wording that a qintar is twelve thousand uqiya, each better than what lies between heaven and earth; that he mentions the versions of Ibn Majah and Waki; and that he judges this report to be a Companion's saying, as Ibn Jarir and Waki narrated it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 244} In 6701, Ubayy ibn Ka'b, from the Prophet (through Zakariyya ibn Yahya al-Darir, Shababa, Mukhlad ibn Abd al-Wahid, Ali ibn Zayd, Ata ibn Abi Maymuna and Zirr ibn Hubaysh): a qintar is a thousand and two hundred uqiya. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 245} The editor gives notes on the narrators of this chain: Zakariyya ibn Yahya ibn Ayyub Abu Yahya al-Darir al-Mada'ini (Tarikh Baghdad 8: 457), whose name both copies wrote wrongly as 'al-Siddiq', the right form being from Ibn Kathir; Shababa ibn Sawwar al-Fazari, of whom Ahmad said he left him and did not write from him because of Irja, since he was a caller to it, and al-Saji said he was truthful but called to Irja, while Ibn Ma'in and Ibn Sad held him reliable despite his Irja; Mukhlad ibn Abd al-Wahid Abu al-Hudhayl al-Basri, whom Ibn Hibban called very unreliable in hadith and Abu Hatim weak in hadith; and Ata ibn Abi Maymuna, whom Abu Zur'a and al-Nasa'i held reliable, while Abu Hatim said his hadith is not to be used as proof and that he was a Qadari, and Ibn Adi said some of his hadith are objected to. He adds that Ibn Kathir called this hadith also unreliable, and that the likeliest is that it is a saying of Ubayy himself, as it is of Abu Hurayra, Mu'adh and Ibn Umar. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 245}

The second is that it is twelve hundred dinars. In 6702, al-Hasan (through Imran ibn Musa, Abd al-Warith ibn Said and Yunus), who attributes the saying to the Messenger. In 6703, al-Hasan (Bishr, Yazid, Yunus): twelve hundred dinars. In 6704, Ibn Abbas (Muhammad ibn Sad, his father, his uncle, his father and his grandfather): twelve hundred dinars, and of silver twelve hundred mithqals. In 6705, al-Dahhak (reported to al-Tabari through al-Husayn, Abu Mu'adh and Ubayd ibn Sulayman): it means much wealth in gold and silver, and the qintar is twelve hundred dinars and of silver twelve hundred mithqals. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 246}

The third is that it is twelve thousand dirhams or a thousand dinars. In 6706, Ibn Abbas (Ali ibn Dawud, Abu Salih, Mu'awiya, Ali). In 6707, al-Dahhak (al-Muthanna, Amr ibn Awn, Hushaym, Juwaybir): a thousand dinars, and of silver twelve thousand dirhams. In 6708, al-Hasan (Bishr, Yazid, Said, Qatada): twelve thousand. In 6709, al-Hasan (Bishr, Yazid, Awf): twelve thousand. In 6710, al-Hasan (Ibn Bashshar, Awf). In 6711, al-Hasan (Ibn Bashshar, Abd al-A'la, Said, Qatada). In 6712, al-Hasan (al-Muthanna, Amr ibn Awn, Hushaym, Awf): a qintar is a thousand dinars, the blood-money of one of you. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 247} The editor says the chain of 6710 is without doubt incomplete, because the scribe ended the page with 'Ibn Bashshar said, he told us' and went to the next page, beginning it with 'he said, Awf told us', a slip, so that he put dots in place of what is missing and compares a chain of the same kind at 2570. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 247}

The fourth is that it is eighty thousand dirhams or a hundred ratl of gold. In 6713, Said ibn al-Musayyab (Muhammad ibn Bashshar and Muhammad ibn al-Muthanna, Yahya ibn Said, Sulayman al-Taymi, Qatada): eighty thousand. In 6714, Ibn al-Musayyab (al-Muthanna, Amr ibn Awn, Hushaym, Ali ibn Zayd): eighty thousand. In 6715, Qatada (Bishr, Yazid, Said): we were told that the qintar is a hundred ratl of gold or eighty thousand of silver. In 6716, Qatada (al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, Mamar): the same. In 6717, Abu Salih (Ahmad ibn Hazim, Abu Nu'aym, Sufyan, Isma'il): a hundred ratl. In 6718, al-Suddi (Musa, Amr, Asbat): a hundred ratl, which is eight thousand mithqals. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 247} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 248}

The fifth is that it is seventy thousand. In 6719, Mujahid (Muhammad ibn Amr, Abu Asim, Isa, Ibn Abi Najih): seventy thousand dinars. In 6720, Mujahid (al-Muthanna, Abu Hudhayfa, Shibl): the same. In 6721, Ata al-Khurasani (al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, Umar ibn Hawshab): Ibn Umar was asked about the qintar and said seventy thousand. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 248} The editor says Umar ibn Hawshab al-San'ani narrated from Isma'il ibn Umayya and Abd al-Razzaq from him, that Ibn Hibban listed him among the reliable, and that Ibn al-Qattan said his condition is not known. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 248}

The sixth is that it is a bull's skin filled with gold. In 6722, Abu Nadra (Ibn Bashshar, Salim ibn Nuh, Said al-Jurayri). In 6723, Abu Nadra (Ahmad ibn Hazim, Abu Nu'aym, Abu al-Ash'ath): a bull's skin filled with gold. The editor glosses the word for skin as the flayed hide in which a bull or other animal lies. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 248}

The seventh is that it is much wealth. In 6724, al-Rabi ibn Anas (al-Muthanna, Ishaq, Abd Allah ibn Abi Jafar from his father): much wealth, one part on another. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 249}

Al-Tabari then notes that one of the people who know the speech of the Arabs said the Arabs do not fix the qintar at a known weight but say 'it is the measure of a weight'; the editor refers to Abu Ubayda Mamar ibn al-Muthanna in Majaz al-Qur'an (1: 88) and says the print added a letter to his wording. Al-Tabari says it should be so, since if its amount had been fixed among the Arabs there would not have been all this difference among the earlier interpreters. Then, as his own choice, he says the right thing is to say that it is much wealth, as al-Rabi ibn Anas said, and that the amount of its weight is not to be fixed by a limit through forced reasoning, whatever was said of what he reports. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 249} The editor notes that the print has a different word from the undotted one in the manuscript, which he judges the better reading. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 249}

Al-Tabari explains the doubled form of the adjective as 'multiplied', as if the plural of qintar were three and the doubled form nine, and as al-Rabi ibn Anas said, much wealth one on another. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 249} The editor shows that this is from al-Farra in Ma'ani al-Qur'an (1: 195), with some change. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 249} In 6725, Qatada (Bishr, Yazid, Said): much wealth, one on another. In 6726, al-Dahhak (reported through al-Husayn, Abu Mu'adh and Ubayd ibn Sulayman): much wealth of gold and silver. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 250}

Others said the sense of the doubled adjective is struck into dirhams or dinars. In 6727, al-Suddi (Musa, Amr, Asbat): struck until it became dinars or dirhams. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 250}

Al-Tabari then mentions a report from the Prophet on the verse of al-Nisa that speaks of a qintar in a bride's dowry ([[passage/quran-4-20|4:20]]), and says that if its chain were sound he would go to nothing else. In 6728, Anas ibn Malik (through Ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Barqi, Amr ibn Abi Salama, Zuhayr ibn Muhammad, and Aban ibn Abi Ayyash and Humayd al-Tawil), from the Prophet on that verse: twelve hundred, meaning two thousand. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 250} The editor spends long on the chain. He says 'Ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Barqi' stands so in both copies and he did not know him; that Ibn Kathir took it from Ibn Abi Hatim's commentary through Ahmad ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Raqqi from Amr ibn Abi Salama; that he found no one so named; that among al-Tabari's teachers is Ahmad ibn Abd Allah ibn Abd al-Rahim al-Barqi the hafiz, who might be the man, with 'Ibn Abd al-Rahman' a scribes' error, but that agreement of the two books on the name makes that unlikely, so it may be another teacher whose entry has not reached us. He says Zuhayr ibn Muhammad al-Tamimi al-Khurasani al-Marwazi is reliable, as Ahmad and others held; that Aban ibn Abi Ayyash, whose father's name was Fayruz, was a Successor who narrated from Anas but was weak, Ahmad calling him unreliable in hadith, Ibn Ma'in saying his hadith is nothing, Abu Hatim calling him abandoned in hadith although a pious man afflicted with a poor memory, and al-Bukhari saying that Shu'ba had a bad opinion of him; and he then says Aban's weakness does not, for him, affect the soundness of this hadith because Zuhayr heard it from him and also from Humayd al-Tawil, who is reliable. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 250} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 251}

The editor also gives references. Al-Hakim narrated it in al-Mustadrak (2: 178) from al-Asamm, from Ahmad ibn Isa ibn Zayd al-Lakhmi, from Amr ibn Abi Salama, from Zuhayr ibn Muhammad, from Humayd al-Tawil and another man, from Anas, that the Prophet was asked about this word and said a qintar is two thousand uqiya; al-Hakim said it is sound on the condition of the two Shaykhs though they did not record it, and al-Dhahabi agreed, a printing error in the printed abridgement being correct in the manuscript he holds. Ibn Kathir took it from Ibn Abi Hatim, where the other man is named as Yazid al-Raqashi and the sum is a thousand dinars. So the unnamed man in al-Hakim's version may be Aban, as in al-Tabari's, or Yazid al-Raqashi, as in Ibn Abi Hatim's, who is also weak, as was said in the notes on report 6654. He adds that al-Suyuti gave al-Hakim's version at this verse (2: 10) and al-Tabari's at the verse of al-Nisa (al-Durr al-Manthur 2: 133), that the copies vary in the wording of the hadith, as in the print and in some other copies and in al-Suyuti's citation of al-Tabari ('twelve hundred, meaning two thousand'), and that he thinks all of this corrupt and the right wording that of al-Hakim's version. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 251}

Al-Tabari ([[passage/quran-3-14|3:14]]) then records the interpreters' difference over the meaning of the word that describes the horses, in four views.

The first is that it means grazing, that is, feeding at pasture. In 6729, Said ibn Jubayr (through Ibn Waki, his father, Sufyan and Habib ibn Abi Thabit). In 6730 and 6731, Said to the same effect (Ibn Bashshar, Abd al-Rahman, Sufyan; al-Muthanna, Abu Nu'aym, Sufyan). In 6732, Said (al-Husayn ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, Sufyan): grazing, meaning those let loose to feed. In 6733, Abdullah ibn Abd al-Rahman ibn Abza (Ibn Waki, his father, Talha al-Qannad). In 6734, Ibn Abbas (Muhammad ibn Sa'd and his line back through his father, uncle and grandfather). In 6735, al-Hasan (Bishr, Yazid, Said, Qatada): those sent out to pasture. In 6736, al-Rabi (a report passed on through Ammar ibn al-Hasan, Ibn Abi Ja'far, his father). In 6737, Mujahid (through Ammar, Ibn Abi Ja'far, his father, Layth).

The second view is that it means beautiful. In 6738, Mujahid (Ibn Bashshar, Abd al-Rahman, Sufyan, Habib): well-formed. In 6739, Mujahid (al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, al-Thawri, Habib): well-formed and handsome. In 6740, Mujahid (Muhammad ibn Amr, Abu Asim, Isa, Ibn Abi Najih): well-formed in beauty. In 6741 and 6742, Mujahid to the same effect. In 6743, Ikrima (Ibn Humayd, Abu Abd al-Rahman al-Muqri, Said ibn Abi Ayyub, Bashir ibn Abi Amr al-Khawlani): the marking of the horses is their beauty. In 6744, Ikrima to the same effect (Yunus, Ibn Wahb, Said ibn Abi Ayyub, Bashir). In 6745, al-Suddi (Musa ibn Harun, Amr ibn Hammad, Asbat): splendid, a word that al-Tabari says another transmitter than Musa gave from Amr ibn Hammad as 'grazing'.

The third view is that it means marked. In 6746, Ibn Abbas (Ali ibn Dawud, Abu Salih, Mu'awiya, Ali). In 6747, Qatada (Bishr, Yazid, Said): its mark is its blaze or patch of colour. In 6748, Qatada (al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, Ma'mar): the blazes of the horses are in their faces.

The fourth view is Ibn Zayd's, that it means those made ready for jihad. In 6749 (Yunus, Ibn Wahb, Ibn Zayd).

On report 6743 the editor notes that Abu Abd al-Rahman al-Muqri is Abdullah ibn Yazid al-Adawi, a client of the family of Umar, and that Bashir ibn Abi Amr al-Khawlani was an Egyptian who narrated from Ikrima and al-Walid ibn Qays al-Tujibi and from whom Said ibn Abi Ayyub, al-Layth and Ibn Lahi'a narrated; he calls him trustworthy, listed in al-Tahdhib and by Ibn Abi Hatim, and says that the printed text and the manuscript both give the name wrongly as 'Bishr', which he corrects again at 6744. He explains the word for a blaze as anything on a horse's body, or another animal's, that differs from its main colour, its plural coming from the verb for embellishing and figuring a cloth. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 253-254}

Al-Tabari's own choice is that the sounder of these views is that the word means horses marked with their blazes and patches, beautiful and splendid to whoever sees them, since in the speech of the Arabs the word means making a sign. The beautiful horses are marked by God's marking them with the beauty of their colours, markings and builds, and they are also the well-formed. He cites a line of al-Nabigha al-Dhubyani describing lean horses, marked, with men on them who look like jinn, and a line of Labid about a morning in his people's days when the horses came in bands with the marks showing among them. He holds that the senses 'well-formed', 'marked' and 'splendid' come to one. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 254-255}

On al-Nabigha's line the editor notes that it is in his Diwan (p. 86), from a poem composed when the Banu Abs killed Nadla al-Asadi and the Banu Asad killed two of them, and Uyayna ibn Hisn wished to help the Banu Abs and take the Banu Asad out of their alliance with the Dhubyan; that he corrected a word in the manuscript and the print which meant brown spears into the word for lean horses, since brownness belongs to spears; and that he explains the plural of the word for lean and the word for the shaped arrows to which lean horses are compared. On Labid's line he notes that it is in his Diwan (poem 16, line 41), among verses boasting of the glory of his people; that Huwayy, al-Dhahab, Ruqat Rahrahan and Qa' al-Qurnatayn are places where his people won battles; that the pronoun in the verb 'they came' refers to the horses with their riders and the other pronoun to his enemies; that the word for bands is the plural of a word for a group of men and horses; that the Diwan has 'in succession' for the reading in al-Tabari; and that the last word means 'amid them'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 254-255}

As for those who took it to mean grazing, al-Tabari says they went by the saying 'I pastured the herd', of feeding it on herbage, and that the verb with this sense appears in the verse of al-Nahl ([[passage/quran-16-10|16:10]]), glossed 'you pasture'. He cites a line of al-Akhtal that disparages two men, one as the son of a woman who sends her camels out to pasture. When it is meant that the herd itself grazes, one says it grazed; hence the grazing camel. But it is not common in their speech to say 'I made the herd graze' with the doubled form of the verb, for which one says the other form. So he holds the better reading to be 'marked' in the senses he has set out. As for Ibn Zayd's saying that it means those made ready in God's way, al-Tabari says that this is a meaning far from the sense of the word. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 255-257}

On al-Akhtal's line the editor notes that it is in his Diwan (p. 159), al-Aghani (8: 319) and Tabaqat fuhul al-shu'ara (p. 418), from a poem in praise of Ikrima ibn Rabi al-Fayyad, secretary of Bishr ibn Marwan. He relates that al-Akhtal went to Hawshab ibn Ruwaym al-Shaybani and said he had taken on two obligations to spare the blood of his people, and was rebuffed; then went to Shaddad (Shaddad ibn al-Mundhir al-Dhuhli, brother of al-Hudayn ibn al-Mundhir, who carried Ali's standard at Siffin), who excused himself; then to Ikrima al-Fayyad, who said he would neither rebuff him nor excuse himself but would give him one of the two in coin and the other in kind. Al-Akhtal praised him and satirised the other two. The editor discusses how the name of Shaddad's mother is vowelled in the Tabaqat and in al-Tabari's history, and repeats a report that when Shaddad was named among witnesses before Ziyad ibn Abi Sufyan, Ziyad asked what father he could be named for and said to drop him, then, hearing he was al-Hudayn's brother, said to name him for his father; Shaddad, on hearing of it, answered harshly about Ziyad's own descent through his mother Sumayya (Tarikh al-Tabari 6: 151). He adds that the manuscript has the line damaged. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 256}

Al-Tabari then turns to the rest of the verse ([[passage/quran-3-14|3:14]]). The word for cattle is the plural of the word for livestock, the eight pairs God names in His Book, sheep, goats, cattle and camels (the editor refers to al-An'am, [[passage/quran-6-142|6:142]] to [[passage/quran-6-144|6:144]]), and the word for crops means what is sown. The sense, he says, is that the love of the desires, for women, sons and the rest, and for the cattle and the crops, has been made beautiful in people's eyes. The demonstrative that follows, he says, refers to everything the verse lists, and one word stands for the whole, which shows that it can cover many things of different kinds. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 257-258}

Of God's describing it as the provision of this world, al-Tabari says it is a report from God that all this is what the living enjoy in this world and make do with, a link in their livelihood and a cause of satisfying the desires whose love was made beautiful to them in their present life, rather than a store for their return or a means of nearness to their Lord, except what is spent in His way and on what He commanded. The editor notes that the manuscript writes a word for 'carrying' in place of 'love', which he calls a slip of the copyist, and refers to the earlier commentary on the word for provision. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 258}

For the verse's last clause al-Tabari says it means that with God is the good return. In 6750, al-Suddi (Musa, Amr, Asbat): the good place of return, which is paradise. Al-Tabari adds that the word for return is a verbal noun on the pattern of the place-noun from the verb 'the man came back to us', with its vowel letter changed to an alif, like the words for saying, returning and moving about. The editor notes that the manuscript and print give one of these with the wrong letter and that he put the right one, and notes the variant vowelling of one of the nouns. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 258-259}

Al-Tabari raises a question: how can it be said that with God is the good return when you know what is also with Him of painful punishment? His answer is that this is meant for a particular group of people, that is, that with Him is the good return for those who fear their Lord, as the next verse ([[passage/quran-3-15|3:15]]) states. And what is the good return? It is what God described, a return to the Gardens in which they stay, with the purified companions and God's good pleasure. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 259}
