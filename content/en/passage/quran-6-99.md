## Summary
Verse 99 of [[passage/quran-6|Surat al-An'am]], a Meccan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 99 of 165 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

On "it is he who sent down water from heaven; with it we brought out the growth of everything, and from it we brought out green, from which we bring out grain piled one on another" al-Tabari says: God, to whom worship belongs pure, with no partner in it for anything else, is the God who sent down water from heaven, "with it we brought out the growth of everything", we brought out by the water we sent down from heaven the food of cattle, beasts, birds and wild animals and the provision and sustenance of the children of Adam, what they feed on and eat, grow on and flourish. The sense of "with it we brought out the growth of everything" is: we brought out with it what everything grows by, flourishes on and is made sound. Were it said that the sense is we brought out with it the growth of all kinds of plant, so that "everything" is the kinds of plants, it would be an allowable view, though the sound way is the first. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 573}

The editor says the printed text has "no sharing in it for anything else", differing from the manuscript through bad judgement, and refers to al-Farra's Ma'ani al-Qur'an.

"We brought out from it green" says: we brought out from it, meaning from the water we sent down from heaven, "khadir", fresh of crops. "Khadir" is "green", as the Arabs say "show her to me as a streak, I'll show her to you as rain", and one says "the earth turned green", "khadaran" and "khadara", and "khadir" is the fresh of herbs; one says "a green date-palm" when its unripe dates are seen green before they ripen, and "a man was cut down green" when he dies young and sound, and "it is yours, khadiran madiran", meaning pleasant and wholesome. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 574}

The editor says "show her to me as a streak, I'll show her to you as rain" is a proverb that the author of Lisan ascribes in "ghamar" to Abu Dhu'ayb al-Hudhali and does not ascribe in "khadir", and which al-Maydani in al-Amthal and Abu Hilal in Jamharat al-Amthal give without ascribing it to him; that "namra" is a cloud with black and white, said as a proverb for the soundness of a sign of a thing and the soundness of the indication of it; that "khadara" is a verbal noun like "ghadara" not given in the dictionaries under its root; and that "midr" is the soft, bare.

"We bring out from it grain piled one on another" says: we bring out from the green grain, meaning what is in the ear, the ear of wheat, barley, rice and the like, ears whose grains pile on one another. 13661, from al-Suddi: "from it green from which we bring out grain piled one on another", this is the ear. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 574}

The editor refers for "grain" to p. 550.

On "and from the palm, from its spathe, clusters within reach" he says: and from the palm, from its spathe, clusters of it within reach, and that is why "clusters" is in the nominative. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 574}

The editor says print and manuscript have "and from the palm, from its spathe, clusters within reach", which is the verse text, and that this explanation does not stand; the right form is as he set, inferred from al-Farra's Ma'ani al-Qur'an.

"Qinwan" is the plural of "qinw", as "sinwan" is of "sinw", being the bunch; the singular is said "qinw", "qunw" and "qina", the dual "qinwan" and the plural "qinwan" and "qunwan", and for the small number they say "three aqna'". "Qinwan" is Hijazi speech and "qunwan" Qaysi. Al-Tabari cites a line of Imru' al-Qays describing the tops of palms heavy with fruit bending with clusters of red unripe dates, and another line whose speaker is unknown describing a she-camel's tail like a bunch; Tamim say "qunyan" with ya. By "within reach" he means near and hanging low. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 575}

The editor says "midhq" is the bunch of the palm and its stalks; that Imru' al-Qays's line is in his diwan and Lisan, from his admired poem near its beginning describing the departing women of the tribe and likening them to palms, with earlier lines on the caravan leaving at the side of al-Aflaj near Tayma and comparing them to gardens of doum-palm or pitched ships; that this is another transmission than the one Abu Jafar and others related; that "athutha a'alihi" means became great and bent from the weight of their load, and "adat" bent and leaned; that the second line is related by Abu Zayd in his Nawadir as a single line, explaining "tashadhdhur" as the she-camel, once with young, knotting her tail and raising it on her rump, "madhal" as not moving her tail, that he did not know the sense of "asham" in the line, Abu Zayd's "wa-asmah" being, he thinks, the right, and that "takhtar" is a verbal noun from the stallion's swishing his tail, not given in the dictionaries.

13662, from Ibn Abbas: "clusters within reach" means by the near clusters short palms whose bunches cling to the earth; 13663 and 13664, from Qatada: hanging bunches; 13665 and 13666, from al-Bara: near; 13667, from Ibn Abbas: near through the bunches hanging from the spathe; 13668, from al-Dahhak: meaning the short palms clinging to the ground, "qinwan" being its spathe. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 576}

On "and gardens of vines, and olives and pomegranates, alike and unlike" he says: and we brought out also gardens of vines, meaning orchards of vines. The readers differed in its reading. Most readers read "jannatin" in the accusative, though the ta has a kasra since it is the ta of the feminine plural, which takes the genitive form in the place of the accusative. 13669, from al-A'mash that he read "jannatun min a'nab" in the nominative, making "gardens" follow "clusters" in case though not of its kind, as the poet said in a line about a husband girded with a sword and a spear. Abu Jafar said: the reading I do not allow other than is the accusative, "jannatin min a'nab", for the consensus of the authority of the readers on its correctness and reading it, their rejecting anything else, and the distance of that sense from the right if read nominative. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 577}

The editor refers for "gardens" to an earlier passage and says the printed text dropped the "fa" from the speech by oversight, and that the line has often been traced before.

"And olives and pomegranates" joins olives to the gardens, meaning we brought out olives and pomegranates "alike and unlike". Qatada used to say (13670): alike in leaf, unlike in fruit. It may also be meant alike in form and unlike in taste. Abu Jafar said: the sense is the tree of olives and pomegranates, contenting himself with mention of the fruit for mention of the tree, as it was said [[passage/quran-12-82|12:82]], "ask the town", contenting himself with the town for its people since those addressed know its sense. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 578}

The editor refers for "alike" to earlier passages.

On "look at its fruit when it bears and its ripening" he says: the readers differed in reading it. Most readers of Medina and some of Basra read "thamarihi" with fatha on the tha and mim, and some readers of Mecca and most of Kufa "thumurihi" with damma on the tha and mim. It is as though whoever opened the tha and mim took the sense as look at the fruit of these trees we named, palm, vines, olive and pomegranate, when it bears, "thamar" being the plural of "thamara" as "qasab" is of "qasaba" and "khashab" of "khashaba"; and whoever put damma took it to be the plural of "thimar", as "humur" is of "himar" and "jurub" of "jirab". 13671, from Yahya ibn Wathab that he read "thumurihi", saying it is the kinds of wealth; 13672, from Mujahid: "thumur" is wealth and "thamar" the fruit of the palm. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 578}

The better of the two readings in my view is that of those who read "thumurihi" with damma on the tha and mim, because God, glorified be his praise, described kinds of wealth as Yahya ibn Wathab said, such as the piled grain of crops, the clusters of palms within reach and the gardens of vines, olives and pomegranates, which were kinds of fruit; the fruit was pluralised "thamar", then "thamar" pluralised "thimar", then that was pluralised, so it was said "look at its thumur", that being the plural of "thimar" and "thimar" the plural of "thamar", and its "bearing" is the setting of fruit. As for "its ripening" it is its ripeness and its reaching maturity when it reaches it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 579}

The editor says a clearer report from Mujahid is that he said it is gold and silver, as al-Farisi reported from him.

Some Basran scholars of Arab speech said of "yan'ih", when the ya is opened, that it is the plural of "yani'" as "tajr" is of "tajir" and "sahb" of "sahib"; some Kufans denied that and held it a verbal noun from "the fruit ripened, it ripens, yan'an", with three forms reported of the Arabs for its verbal noun, "yan'", "yun'" and "yana'", and likewise "nadj" as "nadj", "nudj" and "nadaj". As for the reading of "wa-yani'ihi" it means its ripe and mature part, and for its verbal noun "yunu'an" is possible, and "the fruit ripened, it ripens, ina'an" is heard from the Arabs; of the speech of those who said "yana'" is the line of a poet about domes by a palace around which the olive has ripened. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 580}

The editor says the first speaker is Abu Ubayda in Majaz al-Qur'an, also ascribed to Ibn Kaysan; that Abu Jafar gave for "yan'" and "nadj" a third verbal noun other than that of the lexicographers, which should be added to the dictionaries, he inferring its vowelling with fatha on ya and nun in "yana'" and on nun and dad in "nadaj"; and that the line is disputed, ascribed to Yazid ibn Mu'awiya, to al-Ahwas by al-Mubarrad, to Abu Dahbal by al-Jahiz, and wrongly to al-Akhtal, found in al-Hayawan, al-Kamil, Ansab al-Ashraf, Majaz al-Qur'an, Ibn Kathir's history, al-Khizana, Lisan and others, from a poem said to be Yazid's about a Christian woman who became a nun in a ruined monastery near al-Matrun, a place in Syria, with verses on worry, sleep, the ants' food, the entering of spring, Jilliq a village of Damascus, "bi'a" being a Jewish or Christian church and "daskara" a palace-like building the foreigners used for drinking and entertainment.

13673, from Ibn Abbas: "its ripening" means when it ripens, its ripening is its maturity. Reports 13674 and 13675, from Ibn Abbas and Qatada, follow on "look at its fruit when it bears and its ripening", but page 581 in the available source is damaged in its scan, its margins running into the text, so only report 13673 can be read clearly there, and the rest will be revisited when an image of the page is available. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 581}

We stop here at the foot of p. 581, after report 13673 on "its ripening" in verse 99, and al-Tabari continues on p. 582.
