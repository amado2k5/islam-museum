## Summary
Verse 114 of [[passage/quran-2|Surat al-Baqara]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## In al-Tabari's commentary

On "who is more unjust than one who prevents the mosques of God from having His name mentioned in them and strives to ruin them", al-Tabari refers to what came earlier on injustice being putting a thing in the wrong place, and says the sense is: what person is more transgressing and bold against God than one who prevents God's mosques from being worshipped in. "Masajid" is the plural of "masjid", the place where one prostrates to God, like "majlis" and "manzil". On "that His name be mentioned in them" there are two ways: that the sense is preventing God's mosques from His name being mentioned in them ("an" being accusative through loss of the preposition), or preventing God's name being mentioned in His mosques ("an" being accusative as a substitute for "mosques"); "and strove" is coordinated with "prevented". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 519} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 520}

The interpreters differed over who is meant. Some said the Christians, and the mosque is Jerusalem, from Ibn Abbas and Mujahid (that they threw filth in Jerusalem and prevented people from praying in it). Others said Nebuchadnezzar and his troops and those of the Christians who helped them, from Qatada (that hatred of the Jews led them to help Nebuchadnezzar the Babylonian ruin Jerusalem) and by another route from him, and al-Suddi (that the Romans backed Nebuchadnezzar in ruining it and he ordered carcasses to be thrown in it, because the Children of Israel had killed Yahya ibn Zakariyya). Others said God meant the polytheists of Quraysh, who barred the Messenger of God from the Sacred Mosque, from Ibn Zayd (at al-Hudaybiya, when they stood between him and entering Mecca until he slaughtered his offering at Dhu Tuwa and made a truce, saying whoever killed our fathers at Badr will not enter among us; and on "strove to ruin them" that they cut off those who would tend it by remembering Him and who would come for pilgrimage and umra). Al-Tabari then says that the best of these readings, in his view, is that of those who said the Christians are meant, because they were those who strove for the ruin of Jerusalem and helped Nebuchadnezzar. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 520} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 521}

Al-Tabari explains his choice of the Christians as those meant: that those who strove to ruin Jerusalem helped Nebuchadnezzar do so and kept the believers of the Children of Israel from praying in it after he returned to his land; that there is no view of the verse except one of the three, the mosque being either Jerusalem or the Sacred Mosque; that Quraysh never strove to ruin the Sacred Mosque, though at times they prevented the Prophet and his Companions from praying in it, and they built it in the pre-Islamic period and were proud of tending it; and that the verses before and after speak of the Jews and Christians and blame them, with no mention of Quraysh or the Sacred Mosque before, so the better course is to take the verse as like the account of the two verses around it unless proof requires otherwise. He adds that the generality of the verse shows that anyone who bars a worshipper from a mosque of God, whether the prayer is obligatory or voluntary, and anyone who strives to ruin it, is among the transgressors. In a note the editor gives Ibn Kathir's objection to al-Tabari's choice (preferring the general view, as Ibn Zayd said, and arguing that Quraysh drove the Prophet and his Companions out and took the Ka'ba for their idols, so what ruin could be greater), and holds that it does not stand against al-Tabari's argument from the context of the verses. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 521} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 522} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 523} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 524}

On "it was not for them to enter them except fearing" al-Tabari says it reports that God forbade those who barred His mosques to enter the mosques they strove to ruin, while they remain in hostile stance, except in fear of punishment; reported from Qatada (that the Christians today enter Jerusalem only by stealth, and are punished if caught), al-Suddi (that there is no Roman on earth who enters it but fears his neck will be struck or has been frightened into paying the jizya, which he pays), and Ibn Zayd (that the Prophet proclaimed that after the year no polytheist should make pilgrimage nor circle the House naked). The report is of the group because "man" is singular in form and plural in sense. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 524} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 525}

On "for them is disgrace in this world and a great punishment in the Hereafter" he says "khizy" is shame, evil and abasement, either killing and capture or abasement and subjection by paying the jizya, from Qatada; and from al-Suddi that their disgrace in this world is that when the Mahdi arises and Constantinople is opened, he will kill them, and the great punishment is the punishment of Hell, which is not lightened for its people nor are they finished off so as to die. He explains the verse: for them in this world is abasement, humiliation, killing and capture for barring God's mosques from His name being mentioned in them and striving to ruin them, and in the Hereafter, for their disobedience and disbelief in their Lord, the punishment of Hell. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 525} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 526}
