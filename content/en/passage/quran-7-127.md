**Commentary on verse 127 (pp. 36-42)**

Al-Tabari says: a body of men of Pharaoh's people said to him, will you leave Moses and his people of the Children of Israel "to spread corruption in the land", meaning that they corrupt your servants and slaves against you in your land of Egypt, "and leave you and your gods", that is, abandon serving you and worshipping you and your gods. The editor refers to the earlier explanations of "chiefs", "leave" and "corruption in the land". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 36}

"And leave you and your gods" has two interpretations. First: will you leave Moses and his people to corrupt the land, when they have already left you and left worship of you and of your gods? On this reading the accusative of "and leave you" is the accusative of "diversion" and does not depend on "to spread corruption". Second: will you leave Moses and his people to spread corruption and to leave you and your gods, as a rebuke to Pharaoh for letting Moses do these two things; on this reading the accusative is a coordination with "to spread corruption". Al-Tabari holds that the first is the sounder, because the interpreters' explanation came with it; moreover the reading of Ubayy ibn Ka'b is clear evidence that the accusative is the accusative of diversion, as in report 14961, from Harun through Ahmad ibn Yusuf, al-Qasim and Hajjaj: in Ubayy ibn Ka'b's text it is "they have left you, so that they worship you and your gods". The editor refers to the explanation of "diversion" and to al-Farra. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 37}

It is reported that al-Hasan al-Basri read "and he leaves you" in the nominative, coordinating it with "will you leave Moses", as though he took the sense to be: will you leave Moses and his people, and he leaves you and your gods, to corrupt the land? His reading could also mean: will you leave Moses and his people to spread corruption while he is leaving you and your gods, so that "leaves you" is nominative as the beginning of a clause, free of governing particles. The editor notes that the printed text and manuscript differ in this wording and that he adopted the better. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 37}

On "your gods" he says the readers of the great cities read it with fatha on the alif and lengthening, meaning Moses has left worship of you and of the gods you worship; it is related from Ibn Abbas that Pharaoh had a cow he worshipped. It is related from Ibn Abbas and Mujahid that they read it with kasra on the alif, "and your godhead", meaning your worship. Al-Tabari says the reading he sees no reason to depart from is that of the readers of the great cities, because the authoritative readers are agreed on it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 38}

The reports of those who said Pharaoh worshipped gods: report 14962, from al-Suddi through Musa ibn Harun, Amr and Asbat: his gods, as Ibn Abbas claimed, were cattle; when they saw a fine cow he ordered them to worship it, which is why he brought out the calf for them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 38}

Report 14963, from al-Hasan through al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Abu Sufyan and Amr: Pharaoh had a pearl hung at his throat which he worshipped and prostrated to. Report 14964, from al-Hasan through Muhammad ibn Bashshar, Abd al-Rahman ibn Mahdi and Aban ibn Khalid: it has reached me that Pharaoh worshipped a god in secret, and he recited the phrase. Report 14965, from al-Hasan through Muhammad ibn Sinan, Abu Asim and Abu Bakr: Pharaoh had a god he worshipped in secret. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 39}

The reports of those who said the sense is "and leaves you and your worship", on the reading "your godhead": report 14966, from Ibn Abbas through Ibn Waki', Ibn Uyayna, Amr ibn Dinar and Muhammad ibn Amr ibn al-Hasan: Pharaoh was worshipped and did not worship. Report 14967, from Ibn Abbas, that he read it so and said it means your worship, and said Pharaoh was worshipped and did not worship. The editor corrects the names of transmitters in these two chains. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 39}

Report 14968, from Ibn Abbas through al-Muthanna, Abd Allah ibn Salih, Mu'awiya and Ali ibn Abi Talha: he leaves your worship. Report 14969, from Ibn Abbas through al-Muthanna, Abu Hudhayfa, Shibl and Amr ibn Dinar: he read it so, meaning your worship. Report 14970, from Mujahid through Muhammad ibn Amr, Abu Asim, Isa and Ibn Abi Najih: your worship. Report 14971, from Ibn Abbas through Sa'id ibn al-Rabi' al-Razi, Sufyan, Amr ibn Dinar and Muhammad ibn Amr ibn Hasan: he read it so and said Pharaoh was worshipped and did not worship. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 40}

Al-Tabari notes that some claimed those who read "your godhead" intend about the same as "your gods", but put it in the feminine while meaning one god. Some Basrans related that a Bedouin asked about "godhead" said it is a sign, meaning a landmark, making the word feminine, as something set up for worship; he cites a line by the daughter of Utayba ibn al-Harith al-Yarbu'i lamenting her father, in which the word means the sun, and the editor's footnote gives the variants in ascription between manuscript and printed text, its sources and context. This interpreter took the added feminine ending like the ending in some other words and in a line of rajaz by an unknown poet. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 41}

Al-Tabari answers that Ibn Abbas and Mujahid have themselves made clear the sense they intended in their reading, so this speaker's view has no ground against their own explanation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 41}

On "he said, we shall kill their sons": Pharaoh said we shall kill their male children of the Children of Israel, "and spare their women", that is keep their females alive, "and we are above them, subduing", that is, prevailing over them through the force of kingship and authority. He has shown that for anything that prevails by force over something, the Arabs say "he is above it". The editor refers to the earlier explanations of "sparing", "force" and "above". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 41}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
