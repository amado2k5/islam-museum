**Commentary on verse 13: the broken covenant, the curse and the hardened hearts (pp. 125-131)**

On 'so for their breaking of their covenant We cursed them', al-Tabari addresses the Prophet: do not wonder at these Jews who planned to stretch out their hands to you and your companions and broke the pact by treachery, for this is their habit and their forebears' habit. God had taken their ancestors' covenant in the time of Moses to obey Him, had sent twelve chiefs chosen from among them to scout the giants, and had promised them victory and their land, after showing them the lessons and signs of Pharaoh's drowning and the parting of the sea. They broke the covenant and God cursed them. If this was the deed of their best, despite His favours to them, similar conduct from their lesser people should not surprise anyone. He holds that something is left unsaid in the sentence, which the plain wording indicates: they broke the covenant, so I cursed them; and that 'fa-bima naqdihim' means 'for their breaking', as Qatada said. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 125}

Report 11584 gives this meaning from Qatada, and report 11585, from Ibn Jurayj from Ibn Abbas, says it is a covenant God took from the people of the Torah and they broke it. Al-Tabari has explained the meaning of cursing elsewhere, and the pronoun in 'their breaking' refers back to the Children of Israel. The editor notes an error in the chain of report 11584 in the printed text and the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 126}

Next he takes 'and We made their hearts hard' and the variant readings. Most reciters of Medina, some of Mecca, Basra and Kufa read 'qasiya', from the hardness of the heart, meaning it became thick, firm, dry and stiff; he cites a verse by a rajaz poet for this usage. On that reading the sense is: We cursed those of the Children of Israel who broke His covenant and made their hearts thick and dry toward belief and success in obedience, stripped of tenderness and mercy. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 126}

Most Kufan reciters read 'qasiyya', and they differ over it. Some say it carries the same meaning of hardness, because the form qasiyya is stronger in blame than the participle qasiya. Others say it is different: hearts whose belief was not sincere in God but mixed with disbelief, like 'qasiyya' coins whose silver is mixed with an alloy of copper or lead. He cites a verse of Abu Zubayd al-Ta'i that likens the ring of digging tools to the sound of such coins in moneychangers' hands. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 127}

In a long note the editor explains that the poem is an elegy for Uthman ibn Affan, glosses its rare words, and notes that the digging tools cut his grave in the black stony ground of Medina; the museum does not reproduce the verses. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 128}

Al-Tabari prefers the reading 'qasiyya', because it is stronger in blaming the people than 'qasiya'. Of the two interpretations he judges the sounder to be that it is the qasiyya form derived from hardness, as one says a pure soul in either form or a woman as witness in either form: God described them by breaking the covenant and disbelief, not by any belief, so their hearts cannot be described as having belief mixed with disbelief like adulterated coins. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 128}

Then 'they alter words from their places': God made the hearts of those of the Children of Israel who broke His covenants hard, stripped of good and divine success, so they neither believe nor are guided. They alter the words of their Lord sent down on Moses, the Torah, replacing it and writing with their hands what God did not send down, then telling the ignorant that this is God's word. He explains that this describes generations after Moses, some of whom reached the Prophet's time; God counts them with those first spoken of because they are their descendants and follow their way of lying about God and breaking covenants. Report 11586, from Ibn Abbas, says it means God's limits in the Torah, as they would say: if Muhammad commands what you already follow, accept it; if he differs from you, beware. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 129}

On 'they forgot a portion of what they were reminded of', he says it means they left a share, as in the sura of al-Tawba, 'they forgot God and He forgot them', meaning they left His command and He left them; he has shown this with evidence elsewhere. Report 11587, from al-Suddi, says they left a share, and report 11588, from al-Hasan, says they left the bonds of their religion and God's duties without which deeds are not accepted. The editor defines the word for duties and notes a gap the scribe left in the manuscript for the commentary on this clause. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 130}

Then 'you will not cease to discover treachery from them, except a few of them': you, Muhammad, will keep finding such betrayal and treachery from those Jews whose breaking of the covenant I told you about, except a few who did not betray. The word for treachery here is a noun used in place of the verbal noun, as with two other such forms he names; the exception is from the pronoun in 'from them'. Report 11589, from Qatada, says it is treachery, lying and wickedness, and report 11590, from Mujahid, says they are the Jews, like what they intended against the Prophet the day he entered their orchard. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 131}

Report 11591 gives much the same from Mujahid, and report 11592, from Ibn Jurayj, has Mujahid and Ikrima say it concerns the Jews, like what they intended against the Prophet the day he came to them. Al-Tabari then mentions some who read 'on a treacherous one' in place of 'on treachery', since the Arabs add the feminine ending to masculine forms, as in 'a transmitter of poetry' or 'a very learned man', and he cites a poetic line for this. The editor remarks on how sharply al-Tabari rejects the opinions of Abu Ubayda Ma'mar ibn al-Muthanna, to the point of citing him without naming him, gives Abu Ubayda's own words from 'Majaz al-Qur'an', and tells the story behind the line, of a poet of the Kilab tribe, his protector and a killing between two brothers, glossing the rare words; the museum describes this and does not reproduce the verses. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 132}

Al-Tabari then judges the sound view to be what was reported from the commentators, because God meant by this verse the Jews of Banu al-Nadir who planned to kill God's Messenger and his companions when he came to ask their help with the blood-money of the two Amiri men, and God informed him of their plan. He had told him of their forebears' conduct and their ancestors' way, and that the last of them follow the first in treachery, so that their deed would not weigh heavily on him. It does not mean he would keep finding one treacherous man among them, since the report began with their whole group, in the verse where a people planned to stretch out their hands to the believers, so closing with the group is more fitting. The editor gives the rest of the story behind the lines and their vocabulary. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 133}

Then 'so pardon them and overlook; God loves the doers of good': this is God's command to His Prophet to pardon those Jews who planned to stretch out their hands against him and his companions to kill, and to overlook their offence by not pursuing what they dislike, for God loves one who shows good pardon and forbearance to one who wronged him. Qatada said this was abrogated by the verse of Bara'a commanding the fighting of those who do not believe in God or the last day, which is 9:29 in al-Tawba. Report 11593 gives the abrogation from Qatada, and report 11594 from him says that he was not yet commanded to fight them, so God told him to pardon and overlook, then this was abrogated in Bara'a. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 134}

In that same report Qatada adds that these are the People of the Book and God commanded His Prophet to fight them until they submit or accept the poll tax; report 11595 gives something similar from Qatada. Al-Tabari answers that what Qatada said is not impossible, but a sure abrogating text is one that negates every sense of the earlier command; where it does not negate it entirely, only a report from God or His Messenger shows it abrogates. The verse on fighting does not indicate the end of pardon and overlooking toward the Jews, and it is permissible, even with their acceptance of humbled status and payment of the tax after fighting, to command pardon of a treachery they planned, so long as they do not wage war or refuse the rulings binding on them. So it is not required to rule that the verse of fighting abrogates 'so pardon them and overlook'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 135}

We stop here at the foot of p. 135, at the end of the commentary on verse 13; verse 14 begins on the same page.

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
