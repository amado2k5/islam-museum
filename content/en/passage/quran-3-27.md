## Summary
Verse 27 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 27 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

## Verse 27 in al-Tabari's commentary (Shakir ed., volume 6, pp. 302-312)

**The sense of 'making enter'.** Al-Tabari ([[passage/quran-3-27|3:27]]) says the verb means to cause to enter; one says that a man 'entered' his house, and 'I made it enter' when one brought it in. The sense, he says, is that God enters what He has taken from the hours of the night into the hours of the day, so that one grows by what the other loses, and likewise the reverse. In a note the editor, Ahmad Shakir, observes that the verbal noun 'walj' is not recorded in the lexica, and that 'lijja' is of the pattern of 'idda'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 302}

**Reports on the succession of night and day.** Musa, from Amr, from Asbat, from al-Suddi (6795) says the night comes to be fifteen hours and the day nine, and then the reverse. Al-Muthanna, from Ikrima, from Ibn Abbas (6796) says that what is taken from the one is put into the other; the editor notes that Hafs ibn Umar al-Madani is entered in al-Bukhari's al-Tarikh al-Kabir and in Ibn Abi Hatim, that this chain occurs under nos. 1406 and 533 and again at 6814, and that the manuscript and printed text wrongly have 'Hafs from Umar'. From Mujahid (6797-6798) comes the statement that what is taken from one goes to the other as the two follow each other in the hours, with Abu Asim unsure between two close verbs; the editor says the printed text added a word absent from the manuscript, and that the manuscript form is undotted and corrupt. Bishr, Abd al-Razzaq and Ammar (6799-6801), from Qatada, some through al-Hasan, say that the night's loss is the day's gain and the day's loss the night's gain. From al-Dahhak (6802) the night is sometimes longer and the day sometimes longer; the editor corrects the printed 'Sulayman' to 'Ubayd ibn Sulayman' and calls the chain a common one in the commentary. Ibn Zayd, via Yunus and Ibn Wahb (6803), says one is long and the other short, one being taken and made to enter the other. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 304}

**Bringing the living out of the dead: three views.** Al-Tabari reports that the commentators differed. The first: the living creature comes out of the lifeless seed, and the lifeless seed comes out of the living. Abu al-Sa'ib, from Abu Muawiya, from al-A'mash, from Ibrahim, from Abdullah, that is Ibn Mas'ud (6804), says it is the seed that leaves a man while it is lifeless and he is alive, and a man comes from it alive while it is lifeless. Mujahid (6805-6806) says living people come from lifeless seed and the seed from living people and cattle. The same is given from al-Dahhak (6807), al-Suddi (6808), Ismail ibn Abi Khalid (6809), Qatada (6810), and Mujahid and Said ibn Jubayr by way of Ibn Jurayj (6811). For 6809 the editor says Muhammad ibn Umar ibn Ali ibn Ata al-Muqaddami was trustworthy and recorded in al-Tahdhib, and that both the manuscript and printed text garbled his name. For 6811 he says he could find no narrator of the name Yazid ibn Uwaymir and fears a corruption in the name. Ibn Zayd (6812) adds that grain is lifeless and a living plant comes out of it, and the reverse. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 306}

The second view: the palm from the date stone and the stone from the palm, the ear from the grain and the grain from the ear, the egg from the hen and the hen from the egg. Ibn Humayd, from Ikrima (6813), says the egg leaves the living hen lifeless and the living chick comes from it, and al-Muthanna from Ikrima (6814) names the palm, the stone, the grain and the ear. The third view: the believer from the unbeliever and the unbeliever from the believer. Bishr, from Qatada, from al-Hasan (6815), says the believer is a servant with a living heart and the unbeliever a servant with a dead heart; Abd al-Razzaq, from Ma'mar, from al-Hasan (6816) gives the same sense. The editor notes that numbers 6817 and 6818 are missing from the numbering. Imran ibn Musa, from Abd al-Warith, from Sa'id ibn Amr, from al-Hasan (6819) gives the same; the editor could not find a biography of Sa'id ibn Amr and suspects something fell from the chain, the correct form being 'Abd al-Warith ibn Sa'id'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 307}

**The report on Adam's clay.** Humayd ibn Mas'ada, from Bishr ibn al-Mufaddal, from Sulayman al-Taymi, from Abu Uthman, from Salman or from Ibn Mas'ud (6820), with the narrator saying his stronger belief is that it was Salman, says God left Adam's clay to ferment for forty nights or days, then moved His hand in it, and everything good came out in His right and everything bad in the other; He mixed the two and created Adam from them, 'and from this the living comes out of the dead and the dead out of the living, the believer out of the unbeliever and the reverse'. In his notes the editor describes the narrators: Bishr ibn al-Mufaddal was among the teachers of Ahmad and Ishaq, and Ahmad said the farthest point of firm verification at Basra was reached with him; Sulayman al-Taymi, the son of Tarkhan, was trustworthy and narrated from Anas ibn Malik and Tawus; Abu Uthman is Shurahil ibn Marthad al-San'ani, who narrated from Salman, Abu al-Darda and others, and of whom Ibn Hibban says in al-Thiqat that he was the 'man of the conquests' who narrates mursal reports. He adds that al-Suyuti gives the report in al-Durr al-Manthur and attributes it to Sa'id ibn Mansur, Ibn al-Mundhir, Ibn Abi Hatim, al-Bayhaqi and Abu al-Shaykh, and attributes a similar one to Ibn Mardawayh as raised to the Prophet; he says the manuscript had a gap that the printed text completed from al-Durr. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 307}

**Al-Zuhri's mursal report.** Al-Hasan ibn Yahya, from Abd al-Razzaq, from Ma'mar, from al-Zuhri (6821), says the Prophet entered the room of one of his wives and found a woman of fine appearance; he asked who she was and was told she was one of his maternal aunts, Khalida daughter of al-Aswad ibn Abd Yaghuth. He then exclaimed in praise of God, marvelling at His bringing the living from the dead, for she was a righteous woman and her father an unbeliever. The editor cites Ibn Sa'd's Tabaqat and Ibn Hajar's al-Isaba, entry 'Khalida bint al-Aswad', notes that 'fine appearance' is the wording of Ibn Sa'd and al-Isaba, and says he fears that the name written 'Khalda' in both texts is 'Khalida' as in other books; he traces her descent through Zuhra, her link to Amina daughter of Wahb, and notes that al-Aswad ibn Yaghuth was among the mockers. Al-Hasan, via Muhammad ibn Sinan, Abu Bakr al-Hanafi and Abbad ibn Mansur (6822), asks whether they know that an unbeliever begets a believer and a believer an unbeliever, and says it is so. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 308}

**Al-Tabari's choice.** Al-Tabari says the soundest of the interpretations he mentioned is that God brings the living human, the living cattle and beasts out of lifeless seed, which is bringing the living from the dead, and brings the lifeless seed out of the living human, cattle and beasts, which is bringing the dead from the living. His reason is that every living thing from which something of its body separates, what separates is dead; so the seed is dead by leaving the body it came from, and God then forms from it a living human and living cattle. He likens it to the verse on death and life, [[passage/quran-2-28|2:28]]. As for those who explained it as the grain and the ear, the egg and the hen, the believer and the unbeliever, he says it has an intelligible aspect but is not the commonly apparent usage among people, and that carrying the meanings of God's Book to the common usage is better than carrying them to the rare. This is al-Tabari's choice, given as his. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 309}

**The two readings of the word for 'dead'.** Al-Tabari says the reciters differed. One group reads it with the doubled letter, so that it means bringing the living out of what died and what has not yet died; another reads it lightened, meaning only what has already died. He explains that in Arab usage the doubled form covers one who has not died but will, and one who has died, while the lightened form is the one who has died, and that when they intend the attribute they say 'you are dying tomorrow, they are dying'; he sets this beside pairs such as 'jad'/'jawwad' and 'ta'ib'/'tayyib'. He prefers the doubled reading, because God brings the living from the seed that has left the man and become lifeless, and brings the lifeless from the living the seed that becomes dead on leaving though it was living before; the doubled form is in his view stronger in praise and fuller in extolling. The editor remarks that this account of the word is the best to be found in the lexica. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 310}

**Provision without reckoning, and the sense of the whole verse.** Al-Tabari says the closing phrase means that God gives to whom He wills of His creatures and is generous to them without counting what He gave, because He does not fear a loss in His treasuries or an end to what is in His hand. Al-Muthanna, from Ishaq, from Ibn Abi Ja'far, from his father, from al-Rabi (6823) says the provision comes from Him without reckoning and He does not fear that what He has will diminish. Al-Tabari then gives the sense of the verse together with [[passage/quran-3-26|3:26]]: O God, Owner of dominion, You give dominion to whom You will, take it from whom You will, exalt and humble; in Your hand is the power by which You do these things, as against those whom the deniers claimed to be gods, lords, partners or sons; and You make the night enter the day and the reverse, bring the living from the dead and provide, none being able to do this but You. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 311}

Ibn Humayd, from Salama, from Ibn Ishaq, from Muhammad ibn Jafar ibn al-Zubayr (6824), continuing the reports that ended at 6794, says the power by which God gives dominion and takes it away is the power that does these things. If He gave Jesus power over the things by which they claim he is a god, raising the dead, healing the sick, shaping a bird's likeness from clay, and telling of the unseen, to make him a sign to people and a confirmation of his prophecy, then He kept for His own authority what He did not give him: making kings, the matter of prophecy and where He places it, the entering of night into day, the bringing of the living from the dead, and providing for the righteous and the wicked alike. Since Jesus was given none of this, there was a lesson for them, for if he were a god all of it would be his, yet by their own knowledge he fled from kings and moved from land to land. In a note the editor compares the wording of Ibn Hisham, corrects readings in the printed text from the manuscript, and notes that this report is the remainder of the reports ending at 6794. These statements are given as transmitted, with no verdict from the museum. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 312}
