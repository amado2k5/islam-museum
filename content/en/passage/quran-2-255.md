## Summary
Verse 255 of [[passage/quran-2|Surat al-Baqara]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 255 of 286 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

Al-Tabari opens ([[passage/quran-2-255|2:255]]) on p. 386: for the meaning of the name Allah he refers to his earlier treatment, and he explains the declaration of oneness as a prohibition on worshipping anything other than God, the One characterised by life and self-subsistence, exempt from slumber, and who is as He described Himself in this verse. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 386} He holds that this verse is God's clarification to the believers of what the clear proofs brought to those who differed after the messengers, who differed over it and fought, some in disbelief and some in faith; in the note the editor sets out the sentence's structure and corrects the printed text. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 386} Al-Tabari explains the name al-Hayy as the One with everlasting life and a permanence with no beginning in a limit and no end in a term, since everything else, though alive, has a bounded beginning and an end that stops when its term stops; this is his interpretation; the editor explains in the note that the word for extended derives from 'term' and rejects the printed text's alterations, which is his judgement. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 386} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 387} From al-Rabi' (5763, 5764): living, never dying. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 387} Al-Tabari then notes that 'the people of inquiry', whom the editor takes to mean the theologians, differed: some said God named Himself living because He directs affairs and decrees things, so He is living by governance and not by life; others said He is living by a life that is an attribute of His; others said it is one of the names He gave Himself, and we say it in submission to His command. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 387}

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-255|2:255]]) from p. 388: al-Tabari explains the name al-Qayyum as a form on the pattern fay'ul from 'standing', originally qayyuum with the waw merged into the ya, meaning the one who stands over the provision and preservation of what He created, and cites verses by Umayya ibn Abi al-Salt that the Self-Subsisting, the Overseer, ordained the heaven, the stars, the sun and moon, the bridge, Paradise and Hell for a momentous matter. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 388} In the notes the editor corrects the readings of the verses between the printed text and manuscript and explains that 'the bridge' is the Sirat; this is his judgement. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 388} Similar glosses: Mujahid (5765), the one who stands over everything; al-Rabi' (5766), the keeper of everything who watches over, provides for and preserves it; al-Suddi (5767), the one who stands; al-Dahhak (5768), the standing and enduring. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 388} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 389}

Al-Tabari then explains the clause denying drowsiness and sleep to God: no dozing that makes Him doze and no sleep that makes Him heavy with sleep; he takes wasan as the thickening of sleep and cites verses by Adi ibn al-Riqa' and al-A'sha Maymun ibn Qays; the editor's notes explain the word for thickening, rare words and variant versions of the verses, which are his. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 389} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 390} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 391} From Ibn Abbas (5769, 5770): the first word is dozing and sleep is sleep; from Qatada and al-Hasan (5771): a nod; from al-Dahhak (5772, 5773, 5774): it is the lighter drowsiness, below sleep, and sleep is heaviness; from al-Suddi (5775): the breath of sleep that takes the face so a man nods; from al-Rabi' (5776): the drowsy one, between sleeper and waker; from Yahya ibn Rafi' (5777): dozing; from Ibn Zayd (5778): the drowsy one who rises from sleep not thinking, so that he may even take up a sword against his family. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 391} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 392} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 393} The editor's notes give biographies of some transmitters of these chains and explain a word for force. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 392}

Al-Tabari then says the meaning is that afflictions do not befall Him nor defects reach Him, since these two states overwhelm the understanding of those who have it and remove them from their prior condition. He sums up: God, the Living who does not die, the Self-Subsisting over all below Him by providing, guarding, ordering and turning them from state to state, is not altered by what alters others; had He slept He would be overpowered, and had He dozed the heavens and earth would be levelled, since they stand by His ordering and power; this is his interpretation and argument. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 393}

He then reports from Ikrima, freedman of Ibn Abbas (5779): Moses asked the angels whether God sleeps, and God told the angels to keep him awake three nights, then gave him two bottles, he dozed and broke them; Ma'mar said it is a parable God gave of the heavens and earth in His hands. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 393} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 394} He also reports (5780) from Abu Hurayra a saying of the Prophet, told from the pulpit, that it occurred to Moses whether God sleeps, so God sent him an angel who kept him awake three nights, then gave him two bottles, and he slept and his hands knocked together and they broke; God thereby gave him a parable. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 394} In the notes the editor cites the transmitters' biographies and the remarks of Ibn Kathir that this is among the reports of the Children of Israel and that Moses would not be unaware of such a matter, and of Ibn Hajar in Lisan al-Mizan that stopping the chain at Ikrima is closer and that it could not have occurred to Moses, rather the Israelites are said to have asked it; these are the judgements of Ibn Kathir and Ibn Hajar as the editor reports them, and the museum does not rule on them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 393} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 394}

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-255|2:255]]) from p. 395: on the clause of God's ownership al-Tabari explains that He owns all that is in the heavens and earth without partner or equal and created all of it, apart from any god or object of worship, the point being that worship is fitting for nothing else, since the owned is at the hand of its owner and a servant should not worship other than his owner; this is his interpretation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 395} On the question about intercession he explains it as who could intercede for His possessions if He wished to punish them, unless He lets him and permits it; he says it was said because the polytheists claimed they worshipped their idols only to bring them near to God, with reference to the meaning of ([[passage/quran-39-3|39:3]]); so God told them not to worship the idols they claim bring them near, which do not benefit them, and none intercedes with Him unless He releases the intercessor, from among His messengers, friends and the obedient; this is his interpretation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 395} In the note the editor relays that Ibn Kathir cited the earlier reports and then said that stranger than all is the report Ibn Jarir transmitted, which is gharib, and that it is more probably Israelite material than a saying traceable to the Prophet, and that Ibn Hajar's remark is decisive on it; these are the words of Ibn Kathir and the editor. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 395}

Al-Tabari then explains the clause on His knowing what lies before and behind them as His encompassing all that was and is to be, in knowledge from which nothing is hidden. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 396} Al-Hakam (5781): before them is this world and behind them the Hereafter; Mujahid (5782): what passed of this world and of the Hereafter; Ibn Jurayj (5783): what passed before them of this world and what comes after them of this world and the Hereafter; al-Suddi (5784): this world and the Hereafter. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 396} On the clause that they grasp nothing of His knowledge beyond what He grants, he says it means that He alone is the encompassing, all-knowing One, and no one else knows anything beyond what He chooses to teach him, the point being that worship is unfitting for one ignorant of things, so how can an idol or statue, which understands nothing, be worshipped; this is his interpretation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 396} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 397} Al-Suddi (5786): they know nothing of His knowledge beyond what He chooses to teach them. The editor notes in the margin that the numbering omits 5785. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 397}

Al-Tabari then says the interpreters differed on the meaning of the Kursi that God says reaches over the heavens and the earth; some said it is God's knowledge, and Ibn Abbas (5787) is reported as saying that His Kursi is His knowledge. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 397} We stop at the last report given in full; the next begins on the following page.

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-255|2:255]]) from p. 397, on the meaning of the Kursi: from Ibn Abbas (5788) the same, that His Kursi is His knowledge, adding: do you not see what follows, that the guarding does not weigh on Him? {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 397} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 398}

Others said the Kursi is the place of the two feet: from Abu Musa (5789), that it is the place of the feet and has a creak like the creak of a saddle; in the note the editor gives biographies of some transmitters and judges the report broken in its chain, since Umara ibn Umayr did not meet Abu Musa, and explains the word for creaking; this is his grading. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 398} From al-Suddi (5790): the heavens and earth are within the Kursi, and the Kursi is before the Throne and is the place of His feet. From al-Dahhak (5791): the stool placed beneath the Throne on which kings set their feet. From Muslim al-Batin (5792): the Kursi is the place of the feet. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 398} In the editor's note: Ibn Kathir reports it by Muslim al-Batin from Sa'id ibn Jubayr from Ibn Abbas; al-Hakim transmitted it stopping at Ibn Abbas and said it is sound by the standard of the two Shaykhs, with al-Dhahabi agreeing; Ibn Kathir mentions another version from Abu Hurayra traced to the Prophet through a transmitter abandoned by the scholars and says it is not sound; these are the statements of al-Hakim, al-Dhahabi and Ibn Kathir as the editor relays them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 398} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 399}

From al-Rabi' (5793): when the verse on the Kursi's expanse came down, the Companions asked how vast then the Throne must be, and God sent down the passage in ([[passage/quran-39-67|39:67]]) on the greatness of His measure. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 399} From Ibn Zayd (5794), via his father, a saying of the Prophet that the seven heavens in the Kursi are like seven dirhams cast into a shield, and from Abu Dharr, a saying of the Prophet that the Kursi in the Throne is like an iron ring cast in a wide wilderness; the editor gives sources for Abu Dharr's report in al-Suyuti and Ibn Kathir. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 399}

Others said the Kursi is the Throne itself; from al-Dahhak (5795), al-Hasan used to say so. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 399}

Al-Tabari then says each of these views has a basis and a school, but the most fitting reading of the verse in his view is what came in the report from the Prophet; this is his choice. He transmits (5796) from Abdullah ibn Khalifa that a woman came to the Prophet asking him to pray that she enter Paradise; he magnified the Lord, then said His Kursi reaches over the heavens and the earth, He sits on it and no more than four fingers' breadth is left over, and it has a creak like a new saddle when ridden, from its weight; then (5797, 5798) to like effect from Abdullah ibn Khalifa through Umar, and from Abdullah ibn Khalifa. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 399} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 400} In the editor's note: the transmitters' biographies; that al-Tabari relates it here stopping at a Companion; that Ibn Kathir cites it by Israil from Abu Ishaq from Abdullah ibn Khalifa from Umar, lists the hadith scholars who transmitted it, says Ibn Khalifa's hearing from Umar is open to question, and that some transmit it stopping at Umar, some as a mursal, and some add a strange addition to the text; these are the statements of Ibn Kathir as the editor relays them, and the museum does not rule on them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 400} The reports are given as transmitted, without a museum ruling or a description of how they are to be understood.

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-255|2:255]]) from p. 401: after relaying the Kursi reports, al-Tabari says that what indicates the soundness of reading the Kursi as knowledge is the plain sense of the Qur'an, namely Ibn Abbas's statement, via Ja'far ibn Abi al-Mughira from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, that it is His knowledge, because of the following clause about guarding not burdening Him: it tells that guarding what He knows and encompasses does not weigh on Him; he cites the angels' words in ([[passage/quran-40-7|40:7]]) on the breadth of mercy and knowledge, and says that since His knowledge encompasses everything, so does the Kursi; this is his argument. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 401} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 402} He says the origin of the word Kursi is knowledge, from which a written sheet is called kurrasa; he cites a rajaz verse about a hunter and a verse describing scholars as the Kursis of events; he says the Arabs call the origin of anything its kars, so one is of noble kars, meaning noble origin, citing a line of al-Ajjaj; this is his view. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 402} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 403}

In the notes the editor, Mahmud Muhammad Shakir, adds comments: he is surprised that al-Tabari's statement here differs from his earlier one that the most fitting reading is what came in the report from the Prophet on the Kursi, since the two meanings differ in attribute and substance and cannot combine in one interpretation; he says the report of Muslim al-Batin from Sa'id ibn Jubayr from Ibn Abbas has a sound chain by the standard of the two Shaykhs according to al-Hakim and Majma' al-Zawa'id; he quotes Abu Mansur al-Azhari that the sound report from Ibn Abbas is the one through Ammar al-Duhni from Muslim al-Batin, and that whoever relates from him that it is knowledge has erred; he considers al-Tabari's use of the other verse very weak, since by ([[passage/quran-7-156|7:156]]) the Kursi would then be mercy, and says most of the supports al-Tabari offers rest on nothing, pointing to ([[passage/quran-38-34|38:34]]) for the linguistic sense; he also disputes the derivation, holding that the root comes from piling up and compaction, that naming scholars so is purely figurative, and that he found neither the rajaz nor the verse in reliable language sources. These are the editor's views alone; al-Tabari says what is stated above and the museum does not decide between them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 401} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 402} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 403}

Al-Tabari then explains the clause about guarding the heavens and earth as meaning it does not weigh on Him or burden Him, noting that the Arabic says an affair burdened someone; in the note the editor says he did not find the verbal noun al-Tabari gives in the language books. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 403}

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-255|2:255]]) from p. 403: on the clause about guarding the heavens and earth not burdening Him: Ibn Abbas (5799, 5800, 5803): it does not weigh on Him; Qatada (5801): it does not weigh on Him or exhaust Him; al-Hasan and Qatada (5802) and al-Dahhak (5804, 5805): it does not weigh on Him; Abu Abd al-Rahman al-Madini (5806): it is not too great for Him; Mujahid (5807): it does not distress Him; al-Suddi (5808) and al-Rabi' (5809): it does not weigh on Him; Ibn Zayd (5810): it is not hard for Him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 403} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 404} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 405} In the notes the editor corrects a manuscript reading and explains a verb for something overwhelming one. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 404} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 405} Al-Tabari says the pronouns in the phrase on guarding them refer to the heavens and the earth; this is his statement. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 405}

Al-Tabari explains the name al-Ali as the one of height and elevation over His creation by His power, and al-Azim as the possessor of greatness beneath which everything is, so that nothing is greater; Ibn Abbas (5811): the one perfect in His greatness. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 405}

He then says the people of inquiry differed on the meaning of al-Ali. Some said it means high above any peer or likeness, and denied that it means height of place, because no place can be empty of Him and because that would describe Him as in one place rather than another. Others said it means high above His creation by the elevation of His place over their places, since He is above all creation and they beneath Him, as He described Himself as on the Throne. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 406} They also differed on al-Azim. Some said it means 'the magnified', the passive form turned into the adjective form, that is the one whom His creation magnify and revere, arguing that greatness of size and weight cannot be meant. Others said He has a greatness that is an attribute of His, which we do not describe by a how but ascribe to Him as affirmed, denying that it resembles the greatness of servants; they rejected the first view because it would mean He was not great before creating creation nor after its passing. Others said it is Himself describing Himself as great, everything below Him being small beside His greatness. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 406} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 407} In the editor's note, 'affirmation' means affirming the attributes of God as He described Himself without interpretive reading, as against the Mu'tazila and others. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 407} In the pages read al-Tabari gives no explicit preference among these; he reports the positions of the people of inquiry as they are, and the museum does not decide among them.
