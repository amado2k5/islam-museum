## Summary
Verse 103 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 103 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**Clinging to the rope (pp. 70-71).** Al-Tabari says the sense is: cling together to God's means, that is, hold to the religion God commanded and the covenant He made with them in His book, of harmony, gathering on the word of truth and surrender to His command; he refers to his earlier explanation of holding fast (pp. 62-63). The rope is the means by which one reaches an aim or need, and so security is also called a rope, since by it fear is removed and panic escaped. He cites a line of al-A'sha from his poem on Qays ibn Ma'dikarib (the editor traces it to the Diwan, Mushkil al-Qur'an, al-Ma'ani al-Kabir and Lisan al-Arab, notes it concerns his camel, quotes the lines before it, and explains the earlier line on pledges: tribes give a traveller a pledge and protection because they fear Qays, so whoever heads to him finds security). He also mentions the two ropes at [[passage/quran-3-112|3:112]] as the same usage. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 70} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 71}

**First view: the community (7562-7563, p. 71).** From Ya'qub ibn Ibrahim, Hushaym, al-Awwam, al-Sha'bi, Abdullah ibn Mas'ud: the community (7562); from al-Muthanna, Amr ibn Awn, Hushaym (7563): the divine rope is the community. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 71}

**Second view: the Qur'an and the covenant (7564-7572, pp. 71-72).** Others said it is the Qur'an and the covenant made in it. From Qatada (7564 via Bishr, Yazid, Sa'id): the firm rope God commanded them to hold, this Qur'an; and (7565 via al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, Ma'mar): God's covenant and command. From Ibn Mas'ud (7566 via Ibn Humayd, Jarir, Mansur, Shaqiq): the path is attended by devils who call, servant of God, come this way, to turn people from God's way, so hold the rope, for it is God's book; the editor says it is in Majma' al-Zawa'id in similar wording, attributed to al-Tabarani with his teacher called weak, and that al-Tabari's chain is sound. From al-Suddi (7567): God's book. From Mujahid (7568 via Muhammad ibn Amr, Abu Asim, Isa, Ibn Abi Najih): God's covenant. From Ata (7569 via al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hajjaj, Ibn Jurayj): the covenant. From Ibn Mas'ud (7570 via Abu Kurayb, Waki', al-A'mash, Abu Wa'il): the Qur'an. From al-Dahhak (7571 via al-Muthanna, Ishaq, Abu Zuhayr, Juwaybir): the Qur'an. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 71} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 72}

From Abu Sa'id al-Khudri (7572 via Sa'id ibn Yahya, Asbat ibn Muhammad, Abd al-Malik ibn Abi Sulayman al-Arzami, Atiyya) a report from the Prophet that God's book is the rope stretched from heaven to earth. The editor notes that Abd al-Malik is an imam whose reliability was shown earlier; that Atiyya is Ibn Sa'd al-Awfi, shown to be weak; that the words "from Atiyya" fell out of the manuscript and printed text and he restored them from Ibn Kathir. He traces the routes of the hadith, all turning on Atiyya al-Awfi, in Ahmad's Musnad and al-Tirmidhi's collection, where it is called good and strange; says the hadith of Zayd ibn Arqam is sound and part of a long story reported by Ahmad, Muslim and Ibn Hibban in which God's book is the divine rope; that al-Haythami gives it in Majma' al-Zawa'id from al-Tabarani's al-Awsat with men in its chain disputed; and that al-Suyuti attributes it to Ibn Abi Shayba, Ibn Jarir, Ibn Sa'd, Ahmad and al-Tabarani. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 72} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 73}

**Third view: sincere devotion (7573-7574, p. 73).** Others said it is sincere devotion of oneness to God. From Abu al-Aliya (7573 via al-Muthanna, Ishaq, Abdullah ibn Abi Ja'far, his father, al-Rabi): cling by sincerity to God alone; from Ibn Zayd (7574 via Yunus, Ibn Wahb): the rope is Islam. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 73}

**The prohibition of division (p. 74).** Al-Tabari says the sense is: do not split away from God's religion and the covenant He made in His book, of harmony and gathering on obeying Him and His messenger and ceasing at His command. From Qatada (7575 via Bishr, Yazid, Sa'id): God dislikes division for you, warned beforehand against it and forbade it, and is pleased for you with hearing, obeying, harmony and community, so be pleased for yourselves with what God is pleased with for you if you can. From Abu al-Aliya (7576): do not be hostile on sincerity to God, be brothers upon it; the editor prefers the wording of the printed text and al-Durr al-Manthur. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 74}

**The report on the division of the community (7577-7578, pp. 74-75).** From al-Muthanna, Abdullah ibn Salih, Mu'awiya ibn Salih, who says al-Awza'i related that Yazid al-Raqashi related that he heard Anas ibn Malik say that the Prophet said the Children of Israel divided into seventy-one groups and his community will divide into seventy-two, all in the fire except one; asked what that one was, he closed his hand and said the community, reciting the verse (7577). The like is related via Abd al-Karim ibn Abi Umayr, al-Walid ibn Muslim, al-Awza'i, Yazid al-Raqashi (7578). The editor says Yazid al-Raqashi is Yazid ibn Aban Abu Amr al-Basri the storyteller, who is weak; that al-Bukhari recorded that Shu'ba spoke against him, al-Nasa'i called him abandoned, and Ibn Sa'd said he was weak and a Qadarite; that Ibn Majah records it via al-Walid ibn Muslim from al-Awza'i from Qatada from Anas ending "all in the fire except one, the community", of which al-Busiri said the chain is sound with reliable men, and the editor agrees, concluding al-Awza'i took it from two shaykhs, one weak and one reliable, and the weak one added the recitation of the verse. On 7578 he cites al-Dhahabi that Abd al-Karim ibn Abi Umayr is unknown. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 74} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 75}

**Ibn Mas'ud on obedience and community (7579-7580, p. 75).** From Abu Kurayb, al-Muharibi, Ibn Abi Khalid, al-Sha'bi, Thabit ibn Qutba al-Madani, Abdullah ibn Mas'ud: people, keep to obedience and the community, for that is the rope God commanded, and what you dislike in the community and obedience is better than what you like in division (7579); likewise via Abd al-Hamid ibn Bayyan al-Sukkari, Muhammad ibn Yazid, Isma'il ibn Abi Khalid, al-Sha'bi, Thabit ibn Qutba, who heard Ibn Mas'ud preaching (7580). The editor corrects the name and attribution of Thabit ibn Qutba (al-Madani, al-Thaqafi) with his entry from al-Bukhari and Ibn Abi Hatim, and corrects the name al-Sukkari. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 75}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 75}

**End of Ibn Mas'ud's report (7581, p. 76).** From Isma'il ibn Hafs al-Ubulli, Abdullah ibn Numayr Abu Hisham, Mujalid ibn Sa'id, Amir, Thabit ibn Qutba al-Madani: Abdullah said keep to obedience and the community, for that is the rope God commanded, and then gave the like. The editor gives the entry for Isma'il ibn Hafs ibn Amr al-Ubulli, Abu Bakr al-Awdi al-Basri, named for al-Ubulla: al-Nasa'i, Ibn Majah and Ibn Khuzayma related from him and Abu Hatim heard from him; Ibn Abi Hatim reports his father saying he wrote from him and from his father, that the father lied and the son was unlike him, and that asked whether there was no harm in him, he said he could not say so; Ibn Hibban lists him among the reliable. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 76}

**Reminding of the favour (p. 76).** Al-Tabari says the sense is: remember what God favoured you with, harmony and gathering on Islam. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 76}

**The grammarians on the connected clause (pp. 76-77).** Al-Tabari notes that the scholars of Arabic differed over the clause that gives the time of enmity. Some Basran grammarians said the speech is cut off at the first sentence and then explained by the joining of hearts, reporting what they were in before the joining, as one says "he held the wall lest it lean". Some Kufan grammarians said it follows what precedes and is not cut off. Al-Tabari chooses that it is connected and not cut off, the sense in his view being: remember, believers, God's favour to you when you were enemies in your polytheism, killing one another out of partisanship that was neither obedience to God nor to His messenger; God joined your hearts by Islam and made you brothers to one another after enmity, linking up by the harmony of Islam and the unity of your word on it. The editor discusses a reading of a phrase in his note between the manuscript and the printed text. This is al-Tabari's choice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 76} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 77}

**Qatada and al-Rabi (7582-7583, p. 77).** From Bishr, Yazid, Sa'id, Qatada: in the pre-Islamic period they slaughtered one another and the strong ate the weak until God brought Islam, made brothers of them and joined them; he swore by God that harmony is mercy and division a punishment (7582). From al-Muthanna, Ishaq, Ibn Abi Ja'far, his father, al-Rabi, the like (7583). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 77}

**The enmity of the Aws and Khazraj (pp. 77-78).** Al-Tabari says the favour God told the Ansar to remember is the harmony of Islam and their unity on it, and that the enmity meant is the enmity of the wars between the two clans of the Aws and Khazraj before Islam, which scholars of the days of the Arabs say lasted one hundred and twenty years. From Ibn Humayd, Salama, Ibn Ishaq (7584): the war between the Aws and Khazraj lasted one hundred and twenty years until Islam came while they were still in it; they were brothers of one father and mother, and no people were heard of with such enmity and war between them; then God put it out through Islam and joined them through His messenger Muhammad. The editor says he could not find where this stands in Ibn Hisham's Sira. Al-Tabari adds that God reminded them, in admonishing them, of the greatness of the hardship and misery in which they lived in the pre-Islamic period, by hostility, killing and fear, and what they came to by Islam of harmony, security and brotherhood. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 77} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 78}

**The report of Suwayd ibn al-Samit (7585, pp. 78-79).** From Ibn Humayd, Salama, Ibn Ishaq, Asim ibn Umar ibn Qatada, elders of his people: Suwayd ibn al-Samit, of the Banu Amr ibn Awf, came to Mecca on pilgrimage or the lesser pilgrimage; his people called him "the perfect" for his toughness, poetry, lineage and nobility. The Prophet went to him on hearing of him and called him to God and Islam. Suwayd said perhaps what you have is like what I have, meaning the scroll of Luqman, that is Luqman's wisdom. The Prophet asked to hear it, and said it was fine speech but what he had was better, a Qur'an God had sent down as guidance and light; he recited it and called him to Islam. Suwayd did not hold back from it and said it was fine speech, then left and came to Medina, and was soon killed by the Khazraj; his people used to say he was killed as a Muslim. His killing was before the day of Bu'ath. The editor notes the report is in Ibn Hisham's Sira (2:67-69) and corrects a wording from the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 78} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 79}

**Abu al-Haysar and Iyas ibn Mu'adh (7586, pp. 79-80).** From Ibn Humayd, Salama, Ibn Ishaq, al-Husayn ibn Abd al-Rahman, Mahmud ibn Labid of the Banu Abd al-Ashhal: when Abu al-Haysar Anas ibn Rafi' came to Mecca with young men of the Banu Abd al-Ashhal, among them Iyas ibn Mu'adh, seeking an alliance from Quraysh against their Khazraj kin, the Prophet heard of them, came and sat with them and asked if they wanted something better than what they had come for. He said he was God's messenger, sent to the servants to call them to worship God without associating anything with Him, and that the Book was sent down to him; he spoke of Islam and recited the Qur'an. Iyas ibn Mu'adh, a talkative young lad, said this was better than what they had come for. Abu al-Haysar took a handful of pebbles and struck Iyas in the face, saying leave us, we came for something else. Iyas fell silent, the Prophet rose, they went back to Medina, and the battle of Bu'ath between the Aws and Khazraj took place; Iyas died soon after. The editor corrects names from Ibn Hisham (al-Husayn; Mahmud ibn Labid, a Successor whose Companionship is disputed; Abu al-Haysar, not Abu al-Jaysh). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 79} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 80}

**The six Ansar at al-Aqaba (pp. 80-81).** The report continues: when God wished to make His religion manifest, honour His Prophet and fulfil His promise, the Prophet went out in the season offering himself to the Arab tribes as he used to, and at al-Aqaba met a group of the Khazraj whom God intended good for. He asked who they were; a group of the Khazraj; whether allies of the Jews (the editor glosses the word as allies); they said yes, and sat with him, and he called them to God, offered Islam and recited the Qur'an. Part of what God did for them was that the Jews lived among them, people of scripture and knowledge, while the Ansar were polytheists with idols and had been raided by the Jews, who said a prophet was about to be sent whose time had come, whom they would follow and kill the Ansar with, as 'Ad and Iram were killed. When the Prophet spoke to them they told one another that this was the prophet the Jews threatened them with and that the Jews must not beat them to him. They believed him and accepted Islam and said they had left their people with no people having such enmity and evil between them, and that perhaps God would unite them through him; they would call them, and if God united them on it no man would be mightier than he. They were six, as was recalled. Back in Medina they told their people of the Prophet and called them to Islam until it spread, and no house of the Ansar lacked mention of him. The next year twelve men came at the season and met him at al-Aqaba, the First Aqaba, and pledged him the pledge of women, before fighting was made obligatory. The editor explains that the pledge of women is that of [[passage/quran-60-12|60:12]] and quotes its wording from Ubada ibn al-Samit via Ibn Ishaq: not to associate anything with God, steal, commit adultery, kill their children, bring slander or disobey in what is right; for the faithful, paradise; for one who commits something and is punished by its penalty in the world, it is an expiation; for one on whom it is covered until the day of resurrection, the matter is with God, to punish or forgive. It is so called because it mentions no fighting. The report is in Ibn Hisham (2:69-73), continuing 7585. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 80} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 81}

**Ikrima's report (7587, pp. 81-82).** From al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, Ma'mar, Ayyub, Ikrima: six of the Ansar met the Prophet, believed in him and wished him to come with them, but they said there was war among their people and they feared that if he came as he was, what he wanted would not be ready. They promised him for the next year, saying God might mend the war; it was mended though they had thought it beyond mending, that is Bu'ath. The next year seventy men who had believed met him, and he took twelve leaders from them; that was when the verse came. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 81} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 82}

**Al-Suddi and Ikrima's second report (7588-7589, pp. 82-83).** From al-Suddi (7588): the time of enmity is the war of Ibn Sumayr, and the joining was by Islam. From al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Abu Sufyan, Ma'mar, Ayyub, Ikrima (7589), the like with an addition: when the affair of A'isha happened, the two clans stirred and said to one another that their meeting-place was the Harra, and went out to it; the verse was revealed, the Prophet came and kept reciting it to them until they embraced and wept with stifled sobs. The editor explains that this refers to the affair of the slander, quotes the account of Usayd ibn Hudayr of the Aws and Sa'd ibn Ubada of the Khazraj and the near-clash between the two clans (Tarikh al-Tabari), and says he found this report in no book and found in the works on occasions of revelation no statement that the verse was revealed about A'isha but only about what passed between the Aws and Khazraj, and that al-Tabari does not say so here or in his comment on Surat al-Nur. He also explains the stifled sobbing and prints the word with its dotted letter from the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 82} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 83}

**Sumayr ibn Zayd (p. 83).** Al-Tabari says the Sumayr whose war al-Suddi means is Sumayr ibn Zayd ibn Malik of the Banu Amr ibn Awf, mentioned by Malik ibn al-Ajlan in his verse. The editor traces the lines to Jamharat Ash'ar al-Arab, al-Aghani and Lisan, corrects the reading of two words in them, and says Sumayr was the one who killed a Tha'labi man, the neighbour under Malik ibn al-Ajlan's protection; Malik demanded that the Banu Amr ibn Awf hand him over to be killed for his neighbour or give the full blood-price; they refused, Malik called out the Khazraj tribes and the Banu al-Harith ibn al-Khazraj declined to help, so he spoke these lines urging the Banu al-Najjar to support him. The editor also corrects the name of Sumayr's father in the sources. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 83}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 83}

**The origin of the Aws–Khazraj enmity (p. 84).** Al-Tabari says the scholars of the Ansar held that the beginning of the enmity that kindled the wars between the Aws and the Khazraj came from the killing of a client of Malik ibn al-Ajlan of the Khazraj, called al-Hurr ibn Sumayr of Muzayna, who was Malik's ally; the enmity then continued until God extinguished it through His Prophet Muhammad, which is what al-Suddi means by the war of Ibn Sumayr. The editor says he is not sure of this name and found it nowhere else, and that elsewhere the man is called Ka'b ibn al-Ajlan, with other names given; he also completes in the page notes his explanation of Malik's verses, glossing a verb in them as meaning to submit to an injury and giving parallels from the Hamasa. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 83} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 84}

**Becoming brothers (7590, pp. 84-85).** Al-Tabari says the sense is: you became, through God's joining you by Islam, the word of truth, mutual help for the believers and support against those of disbelief who opposed you, friendly brothers with no grudges or envy among you. From Bishr, Yazid, Sa'id, Qatada (7590), who adds that it was mentioned to them that a man asked Ibn Mas'ud how they had risen that morning, and he said they had risen by God's favour as brothers. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 84} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 85}

**The edge of the pit (pp. 85-87).** Al-Tabari says the meaning is: you, believers of the Aws and Khazraj, were at the lip of a fiery hollow, which is a figure for the disbelief they were in before God guided them to Islam: on the edge of hell by your disbelief, with nothing between you and falling in but dying in it and being among its abiding dwellers, and God rescued you from it by the belief to which He guided you. The edge of a pit is its rim, like the rim of a well, and he cites a line of rajaz about the well Sajla (the editor says he found this line in this form only in this commentary, that Sajla is a well whose digging is variously ascribed to Adi ibn Nawfal, Hashim ibn Abd Manaf or Qusayy, that it appears in Ibn Hisham, al-Azraqi's history of Mecca, al-Baladhuri's Futuh al-Buldan and other works, that Abu al-Faraj ascribes a verse about it to Qays ibn al-Hadadiyya, and that another rajaz is ascribed to Qusayy or Khulda daughter of Hashim). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 85} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 86}

**The pronoun that returns to the pit (pp. 86-87).** Al-Tabari explains that God said "rescued you from it", meaning from the pit, returning the statement to the pit though it began with the edge, because the edge belongs to the pit; this is permissible when what is said of the edge is, in the way described, said of the pit. He cites a line of Jarir ibn Atiyya in which he speaks of the passing of years taking from him as the last night of the month takes from the crescent and then turns to speak of the years (the editor gives the places in the Diwan, Majaz al-Qur'an and al-Kamil, says it is from a poem satirising al-Farazdaq, and explains the last night of the month), and a line of al-Ajjaj of the same kind (the editor notes the varying transmission and attribution). Al-Tabari says he explained the reason for this earlier. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 86} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 87}

**Qatada, al-Rabi, al-Suddi and al-Hasan ibn Hayy (7591-7594, pp. 87-89).** From Bishr, Yazid, Sa'id, Qatada (7591): this tribe of the Arabs was the most abased of people, the most wretched in living, the clearest in error, the barest in skin and hungriest in belly, muzzled on the top of a rock between the two lions Persia and Rome, with nothing in their land to be envied; whoever lived lived wretched, whoever died was thrown into the fire, they were eaten and did not eat, until God brought Islam, through which He made them heirs to the Book, made lawful for them the land of striving, spread provision for them, and made them rulers over people, so let them give thanks. The editor corrects the word for muzzled from the manuscript and explains the muzzling of a camel's mouth so it does not bite, then figuratively being kept from speaking; he says he is not sure the two phrases on the land of striving and provision are correct in the manuscript, noting that what Islam made lawful for fighters is known to be war booty, and fears a corruption. From al-Muthanna, Ishaq, Abdullah ibn Abi Ja'far, his father, al-Rabi ibn Anas (7592): they were in disbelief in God and He rescued them from that and guided them to Islam. From Muhammad ibn al-Husayn, Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal, Asbat, al-Suddi (7593): they were on the edge of the fire, whoever died was destroyed in it, and God sent Muhammad and rescued them through him from that pit. From Muhammad ibn al-Husayn, Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal, al-Hasan ibn Hayy (7594): partisanship. The editor says al-Hasan ibn Hayy is al-Hasan ibn Salih ibn Salih ibn Hayy al-Hamdani. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 87} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 88} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 89}

**The closing clause: making clear and being guided (p. 89).** Al-Tabari says the sense is: as your Lord made clear to you in these verses, believers of the Aws and Khazraj, the rancour the Jews conceal, their deceit, what He commanded and forbade, and the state you were in before Islam and came to in it, making known in all of that the places of His favour and kindnesses, so He makes clear His other proofs in His revelation and on His messenger's tongue, that you may be guided to the way of right direction and keep to it. The editor corrects a reading from the manuscript, glosses rancour as deep-seated spite, and sets out the sentence's structure and the accusative of state. At this point the editor records that the fifth part of the manuscript ends, with the copyist's note that it was finished at the start of Muharram in the year 715 AH, in Cairo, in the quarter called Harat al-Utufa, by a Shafi'i named Ali ibn Muhammad (the rest of his lineage is unclear in the reading), who prays for the owner of the book and its readers; the sixth part begins with the next verse. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 89} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 90}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 89}
