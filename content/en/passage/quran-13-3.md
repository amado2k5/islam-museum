**Commentary on verse 3 (pp. 328-330)**

On "it is He who spread out the earth and set in it firm mountains and rivers, and of all fruits made in it two pairs; He covers the night with the day; in that are signs for people who reflect", al-Tabari says: God is He who spread out the earth, stretching it in length and breadth. On "and set in it firm mountains" he says, mighty be His praise: He set in the earth fixed mountains. "Rawasi" is the plural of "rasiya", fixed; one says "arsaytu al-watad fi al-ard" when one fixes the peg, as the poet said in a verse mentioning the long-lasting hearth stones, the heaped ash and a peg a slave-girl drove in with a hand-stone, meaning she fixed it. The editor says the verse is al-Ahwas's, found in Majaz al-Qur'an and Lisan al-'Arab, that "khalidat" are the hearth stones, so called because they last long after the abodes are effaced, "ma yarimna" means they do not leave their place, "hamid" is clotted ash, "ash'ath" the peg, since its head is beaten and frays, "walida" the girl and "fihr" a hand-sized stone for pounding. On "and rivers" he says: and He made rivers of water in the earth. On "and of all fruits He made in it two pairs", the "min" in {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 328}

"and of all fruits He made in it two pairs" belongs with the second "made", not the first. The sense of the speech is: and He made in it two pairs from all fruits; by "two pairs" he means from each, two males and from each, two females, which is four, two males and two females, according to some. We have shown earlier that the Arabs call two "zawjayn" and one of the males "zawj" to his female, and likewise one female a "zawj" and a "zawja" to her male, which makes repetition here unnecessary. This is made clearer by God's words at [[passage/quran-53-45|53:45]], where He calls the two, the male and the female, "zawjayn". By "two pairs" he means two kinds and two types. The editor notes that the copyist added "from every" before "two pairs", which belongs to another verse, and that he removed it. On "He covers the night with the day" he says: the night covers the day with its darkness and the day the night with its light. Report 20066, from Qatada, through Bishr, Yazid and Sa'id: "He covers the night with the day", that is, the night clothes the day. On "in that are signs for people who reflect", mighty be His praise: {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 329}

in what I have described and mentioned of the wonders of God's creation and the greatness of His power by which He created these things, there are indications, proofs and admonitions for people who reflect on them, infer from them and take a lesson, so that they know that worship is fitting and allowed only for the One who created and arranged them, not for other gods and idols that can neither harm nor benefit, nor for anything else, but only for the One who brought it into being from nothing, blessed and exalted; and that the power by which He originated that is the power to which it is not hard to revive those of His creatures who perished, restore what has passed away and originate what He wills to originate. The editor makes the sentence structure clear. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 330}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
