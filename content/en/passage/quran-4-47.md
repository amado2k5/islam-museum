## Summary
Verse 47 of [[passage/quran-4|Surat an-Nisa]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 47 of 176 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**Those addressed and the dispute over the effacing (p. 440).** Al-Tabari says the address is to the Jews of the children of Israel around the Prophet's place of emigration, whom God calls to believe in what He sent down on Muhammad of the Furqan, bearing out what they hold of the Torah He sent down to Moses son of Imran, before what He threatened happens to their faces. The interpreters differed. Some said the effacing is the erasing of the features of the faces until they become like napes; others said the sight is effaced so they become blind, the word "face" being used to mean sight, and turning them back means putting the sight on the side of the nape. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 440}

**The view that the faces are turned to the napes (pp. 440-441, reports 9713-9716, note 1).** Muhammad b. Sa'd related from his father, from his uncle, from his father, from his father, from Ibn Abbas that its effacing is that it goes blind, and the turning back is that He puts their faces on the side of their napes so they walk backwards, one having two eyes in the back of his neck (9713); Abu al-Aliya Isma'il b. al-Haytham al-Abdi from Abu Qutayba, from Fudayl b. Marzuq, from Atiyya al-Awfi that He puts them at the napes so they walk back on their heels (9714); Muhammad b. Umara al-Asadi from Ubaydallah b. Musa, from Fudayl b. Marzuq, from Atiyya similarly, except that he said the effacing is turning them back on their napes (9715); al-Hasan b. Yahya from Abd al-Razzaq, from Ma'mar, from Qatada that it is the turning of their faces to the side of their backs (9716). The editor says he did not find this Abu al-Aliya, and refers to earlier entries for Abu Qutayba, who is Salm b. Qutayba (the reference numbers are unclear in the scan). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 441}

**The view that the sense is leading astray from the truth (pp. 441-442, reports 9717-9722, notes 2-3).** Others said the sense is: before We blind a people to the truth and so turn them back into error and disbelief. Muhammad b. Amr related from Abu Asim, from Isa, from Ibn Abi Najih, from Mujahid that He turned it away from the path and the truth, and the turning to the backs is into error (9717); al-Muthanna from Abu Hudhayfa, from Shibl, from Ibn Abi Najih, from Mujahid, the same (9718); al-Muthanna from Suwayd, from Ibn al-Mubarak by reading, from Ibn Jurayj, from Mujahid, the same (9719); al-Hasan b. Yahya from Abd al-Razzaq, from Ma'mar, from al-Hasan that the effacing is from the truth and the turning back is onto their error (9720); Muhammad b. al-Husayn from Ahmad b. Mufaddal, from Asbat, from al-Suddi that the verse came down about Malik b. al-Sayf and Rifa'a b. Zayd b. al-Tabut of Banu Qaynuqa, and that the effacing is blinding them to the truth and returning them as unbelievers (9721); "I was told from" al-Husayn b. al-Faraj, from Abu Mu'adh, from Ubayd b. Sulayman, from al-Dahhak that the sense is turning them back from guidance and insight, and He has turned them back on their heels, so they disbelieved in Muhammad and what he brought (9722). The editor says the print dropped a word in the sentence of 9717 and a preposition in another place. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 442}

**The view that they are returned to Syria (p. 442, report 9723, note 1).** Others said the sense is: before We wipe out their traces from the district where they are and send them back where they first came from, Syria. Yunus related from Ibn Wahb, from Ibn Zayd, who said his father used to say: to Syria (9723). The editor says the print and the manuscript differ in one word and both are right, and that "faces" here means districts. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 442}

**The view that the faces become places where hair grows (pp. 442-443, note 1).** Others said the sense is erasing the features of the faces and levelling them by making them grow hair like the faces of monkeys, since among human beings hair is on the back of the head, so if it grows on the faces they have been turned back. The editor notes that this view is given by al-Farra in his Ma'ani al-Qur'an. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 443}

**Al-Tabari's choice and argument (pp. 443-444, notes 1-3).** Abu Ja'far says the best view in his opinion is: before the sight is effaced and the features erased so they become like napes, then turn them back, putting the faces on the backs of the faces, that is turning the faces into napes and the napes into faces so that they walk backwards, as Ibn Abbas, Atiyya and those who said the same said. His argument: God addressed the Jews He had described in the first verse and warned them with His might, power and swift punishment if they did not believe in what they were commanded to believe, and they were then unbelievers; so the view that the sense is blinding them to the truth and returning them to error is plainly wrong, for what sense is there in returning someone who is already in error, since one is returned into a thing only if one is outside it? If it is sound that God threatened them with turning their faces to their backs, the view that He threatens them with their return into error is false. The view that the faces become places for hair is against the interpreters, and its departure from the Companions, Followers and those after them is enough to show its error. The view that He would send them back to Syria from their dwellings in the Hijaz and Najd, though it has a case, is far from the apparent text, for what is known of "faces" in Arabic is what is opposite to the napes, and God's book is taken in the commoner sense of those in whose tongue it came down unless proof points to another. The editor says the print and the manuscript are wrong in two sentences and he chose the right wording, notes a transposition in the structure of one sentence, and refers to an earlier place for the line of Ka'b b. Zuhayr. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 444}

**The sense of effacing in the language (pp. 444-445, notes 1-3).** Al-Tabari says effacing is wearing away and vanishing while level; it is said that the signposts of the road were effaced when they wore away, were buried and lay level with the ground. He cites a line of Ka'b b. Zuhayr about a she-camel whose neck sweats, describing her road as one whose markers have vanished (described, not reproduced). From this a blind man whose cleft between the eyelids has gone is called effaced, and so at [[passage/quran-36-66|36:66]]; the "gharr" is the cleft between the eyelids. The editor says the print dropped a word it could not read and that the manuscript is undotted; he explains "gharr" as a cleft in the ground, a crease in cloth or wrinkles in skin, says the word appears misread in another place in the commentary, cites Ibn Ishaq's explanation in the Sira (the effaced eye is one without a cleft between the lids), and rebukes the first publisher sharply for confusion over it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 445}

**Did the threat come to pass? (p. 445, notes 3-5).** Someone asked whether what He threatened them with happened. Al-Tabari answers that it did not, because a group of them believed, among them Abdullah b. Salam, Tha'laba b. Sa'ya, Asad b. Sa'ya, Asad b. Ubayd, Mukhayriq and others, and it was averted from them by their belief. The editor says "was" here is a full verb meaning "occurred", that Ibn Ishaq gives the name as Usayd b. Sa'ya and the names of these people vary a great deal, and that he found "Mukhayriq" only here and "Mukhayriq" in the other books, and left it as in the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 445}

**Reports on the Jewish leaders and the Islam of Ka'b (pp. 445-446, reports 9724-9725, notes 1-3).** Showing that the verse came down about the Jews described, Abu Kurayb from Yunus b. Bukayr and Ibn Humayd from Salama, together, from Ibn Ishaq, from Muhammad b. Abi Muhammad, client of Zayd b. Thabit, from Sa'id b. Jubayr or Ikrima, from Ibn Abbas related that the Messenger spoke to chiefs among the rabbis of the Jews, among them Abdullah b. Suriya and Ka'b b. Asad, saying: Jews, fear God and submit, for by God you know that what I have brought you is true. They said: we do not know that, Muhammad, and they denied what they had distorted and persisted in disbelief, and God sent down the verse about them (9724). Abu Kurayb from Jabir b. Nuh, from Isa b. al-Mughira said: we were discussing at Ibrahim's the Islam of Ka'b, and he said Ka'b accepted Islam in Umar's time; he was coming on his way to Jerusalem and passed Medina; Umar went out to him and said: Ka'b, accept Islam. He said: do you not read in your book the parable told of those charged with the Torah who then did not bear it ([[passage/quran-62-5|62:5]]), and I have carried the Torah? Umar left him. He went on to Homs and heard a man of its people, sad, reciting this verse; Ka'b said: Lord, I believe, Lord, I submit, for fear the verse would strike him; then he went back to his family in Yemen and came with them as Muslims (9725). The editor says that in 9724 the manuscript has "which I judged with" and in the margin an old hand noted "the right reading is: I brought", that the copyist erred and the print is right as it is the text of Ibn Hisham's Sira, that the report in the Sira follows the two reports 9689-9690, and that the Ka'b meant is Ka'b al-Ahbar. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 446}

**The second threat: cursing (pp. 447-448, reports 9726-9729, notes 1-2 on p. 447 and note 1 on p. 448).** Al-Tabari says the sense of this threat is: or We curse you, disgrace you and turn you into apes, as God disgraced those of your forebears who broke the limit set for the day of rest. The wording is in the form of address because it follows the earlier call to believe in what was sent down in agreement with what they already hold, and he likens this to a shift from address to third person elsewhere ([[passage/quran-10-22|10:22]]). He also allows another possibility: that the pronoun returns to the owners of the effaced faces, since the passage points to them. He then reports: Bishr b. Mu'adh (9726, from Yazid, from Sa'id) from Qatada that the sense is turning them into apes; al-Hasan b. Yahya (9727, from Abd al-Razzaq, from Ma'mar) from al-Hasan the same; Muhammad b. al-Husayn (9728, from Ahmad b. Mufaddal, from Asbat) from al-Suddi; and Yunus (9729, from Ibn Wahb) from Ibn Zayd that the Jews as a whole are meant, these being cursed as those of that earlier group who were cursed. In the editor's notes: a cross-reference to the earlier discussion of cursing (p. 439), a reference to the story of that group in vol. 2, and the remark that a verb's "falling upon" an object means its taking that object. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 448}

**The sense of what God ordains being carried out (p. 448).** Al-Tabari says the meaning is that all that God commands to be comes to be, created and existing, nothing He wills to create being beyond Him; "command" here means the thing commanded, called God's command because it comes from His command and by it. So the sense is that what God commanded is done. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 448}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 448}
