**Commentary on verse 84 (pp. 214-219)**

On "and he turned away from them and said, O my sorrow for Joseph, and his eyes turned white with grief, for he was suppressing it", al-Tabari says: "he turned away from them" means Ya'qub turned from them, "and said, O my sorrow for Joseph", meaning, O grief for him. The editor refers to the earlier discussion of "tawalli". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 214}

"Asaf" is said to be the most intense grief and regret; one says "asiftu 'ala kadha, a'safu 'alayhi asafan". "His eyes turned white with grief, for he was suppressing it" means he was held in grief, that is, filled with it, keeping it in and not showing it; the passive form is turned into "fa'il", as in God's word "those who restrain their anger" in Sura Al Imran, whose meaning he has explained earlier. The interpreters said the same: report 19646, from Ibn Ishaq, through Ibn Humayd and Salama: "he turned away from them", his grief was complete and reached its limit when his brother joined Joseph, and it stirred up his grief for Joseph, so he said "O my sorrow for Joseph". Report 19647, from Ibn Abbas, through Muhammad ibn Sa'd, his father, his uncle and his father from his father: "ya asafta" means O my grief for Joseph. Reports 19648 and 19648m, from Mujahid: O grief; O distress. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 215}

Reports 19649 and 19650, from Mujahid: O distress, grief. Reports 19651 to 19653, from Qatada: O grief for Joseph. Report 19654, from Ibn Jurayj, from Ibn Abbas, where the editor says the text of Ibn Abbas's saying on the verse was lost by the copyists. Report 19655, from al-Dahhak, through Abu Kurayb, Waki', Ibn Waki' and Abu Hujayra: O grief for Joseph. Report 19656, from al-Dahhak, through Ibn Waki', Amr, Abu Marzuq and Juwaybir: O grief. The editor says he could not identify Amr or find Abu Marzuq, that there is something doubtful in this chain, and that he fears some of its narrators have dropped out. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 216}

Report 19657, from al-Dahhak, through al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hajjaj, Hushaym and Juwaybir: O grief for Joseph. Report 19658, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, through al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, al-Thawri and Sufyan al-Usfuri: no one but this community was given the "istirja'"; do you not hear Ya'qub's words "O my sorrow for Joseph"? Report 19659, through al-Muthanna, Abu Nu'aym and Sufyan, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, is similar. The editor explains that "istirja'" is what Muslims say when a misfortune strikes them, that we belong to God and to Him we return. Al-Tabari then gives what was said on "for he was suppressing it": reports 19660 to 19663, from Mujahid: suppressing grief, through Muhammad ibn Amr, Abu Asim and Isa; al-Hasan ibn Muhammad, Shababa and Waraqa; Ibn Waki' and Ibn Numayr; and al-Muthanna, Ishaq and Abd Allah. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 217}

Report 19664, from Mujahid, through al-Muthanna, Abu Hudhayfa, Shibl and Ibn Abi Najih: "makmud" (worn out), and the editor corrects the manuscript's "maknud". Report 19665, from Mujahid, through al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hajjaj and Ibn Jurayj: suppressing his grief. Reports 19666 to 19668, from al-Dahhak, through al-Muthanna, Amr ibn Awn, Hushaym and Juwaybir; Ibn Waki' and al-Muharibi; and al-Qasim, al-Husayn and Hushaym: the "kazim" is the grief-stricken. Report 19669, from Qatada, through Bishr, Yazid and Sa'id: his grief went back and forth inside him and he spoke nothing evil. Report 19670, from Qatada: suppressing his grief, so he said nothing harsh. Report 19671, from Qatada, through al-Hasan ibn Muhammad, al-Husayn ibn al-Hasan, Ibn al-Mubarak and Ma'mar: suppressing his grief, so he said only good. Report 19672, from Ata' al-Khurasani, through Ibn Waki', Yahya ibn Yaman and Yazid ibn Zurayi': distressed. Report 19673, from al-Suddi: from anger. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 218}

Report 19674, from Ibn Zayd, through Yunus and Ibn Wahb: the "kazim" is the one who does not speak; grief reached him until he did not speak to them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 219}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
