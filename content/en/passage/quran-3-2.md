## Summary
Verse 2 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 2 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

([[passage/quran-3-2|3:2]]), pp. 149 to 156. Al-Tabari explains the clause denying divinity to any but God as God's report to His servants that divinity is His alone, apart from all other gods and rivals; that worship is fitting and permitted only to Him because He alone is Lord and sole God; and that all else is His property and creation, with no partner in His authority; it is His argument against them that worshipping anyone else is not allowed, since every worshipped thing besides Him is His property and every venerated thing His creature, and the owned owes obedience to its owner alone; and it makes known to any of His creatures, from the day He sent this down to His prophet Muhammad, who persists in worshipping an idol, statue, sun, moon, human, angel or anything else that the children of Adam worshipped, that he persists in error, has turned aside from the proof and is on a path that is not straight. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 149} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 150} The editor's notes on p. 150 are on the grammar and are his: the participle 'making known' is in the accusative because it is joined to the clause 'He told His servants' and not to 'as an argument', though manuscript and print have it in the nominative; the word for deification means worship of a god; the print's word for 'taking it' does not work and it is joined to 'persisting in the worship of'; the print has a wrong word for 'turned aside' where the manuscript is undotted and its dal looks like a ra, the verb meaning to deviate from the road; and 'thirty-odd verses' in print and manuscript is a pure error since the revelation shows the number and the following report says 'some eighty verses'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 150}

Al-Tabari mentions that it is said God began this sura with the denial of divinity to any but Himself and the description of Himself in the way He did as an argument against a group of Christians who came to the Messenger from Najran and disputed with him about Jesus and blasphemed against God; God sent down about them and Jesus some eighty verses from the start of the sura as an argument against them and those of like saying; they insisted on persisting in their error; he invited them to mutual imprecation and they refused, asked that the jizya be accepted from them, he accepted it, and they went back to their land. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 150} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 151} He adds that although this is so, and they were the target of the argument, anyone else among the creatures who shares their sense in unbelief in God and in taking something besides Him as lord and worshipped god is included in the proof by which God argued against those about whom the verses came down, and is refuted in the criterion by which He distinguished for His Messenger between himself and them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 151}

In 6543, Muhammad ibn Jafar ibn al-Zubayr (through Muhammad ibn Humayd, Salama ibn al-Fadl and Muhammad ibn Ishaq): the delegation of Najran came to the Messenger, sixty riders including fourteen of their nobles, among whom three were those their affairs returned to: al-Aqib, the people's emir, their man of opinion and counsel, from whose opinion alone they proceeded, named Abd al-Masih; al-Sayyid, their support and keeper of their caravan and gathering, named al-Ayham; and Abu Haritha ibn Alqama, brother of Bakr ibn Wail, their bishop, scholar and imam and keeper of their study house, who had risen in rank among them and studied their books until his knowledge of their religion was good, and whom the Christian kings of the Byzantines honoured, financed and served, building churches for him and extending honours for his knowledge and effort in their religion. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 151} The editor notes that Ibn Hisham has 'the delegation of the Christians of Najran', that the word for al-Sayyid's role means the mainstay and rescuer of a people who sees to their affairs, that the study house is where they study their books, so the man is their scholar who has studied the books, gives rulings and argues for their religion, that the print has 'his religion' where the manuscript and Ibn Hisham have 'their religion', and that al-Tabari left out of his narration from Ibn Ishaq what Ibn Hisham has in the Sira; this is his. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 151}

The rest of 6543 (pp. 152-153), from Ibn Ishaq from Muhammad ibn Jafar ibn al-Zubayr: they came to the Messenger in Medina and entered on him in his mosque when he had prayed the afternoon prayer, wearing striped garments, robes and cloaks, in the manner of the men of Balharith ibn Kab; one of the Companions who saw them that day said: we have not seen after them a delegation like theirs; their prayer time came and they stood to pray in the Messenger's mosque, and he said: leave them; so they prayed toward the east. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 152} The fourteen were named: al-Aqib, that is Abd al-Masih; al-Sayyid, that is al-Ayham; Abu Haritha ibn Alqama, brother of Bakr ibn Wail; Aws, al-Harith, Zayd, Qays, Yazid, Nubayh, Khuwaylid, Amr, Khalid, Abd Allah and Yuhannis, among sixty riders. Those who spoke to the Messenger were Abu Haritha, al-Aqib Abd al-Masih and al-Sayyid al-Ayham; they were of the Christianity of the king's religion with differences among them, saying: he is God; he is the son of God; he is the third of three, as the Christians say. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 152} For their saying he is God they argued that he revived the dead, healed the sick, told of the unseen and made from clay the likeness of a bird, then blew into it and it became a bird, all by God's leave, to make him a sign for people; for their saying he is God's son they argued that he had no known father and spoke in the cradle, which no child of Adam before him had done; for their saying he is the third of three they argued from God's saying 'We did, We commanded, We created, We decreed', and said that if He were one he would say only I did, I commanded, I decreed, I created, but He is He, Jesus and Mary. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 152} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 153} In all this of their saying the Qur'an came down and God mentioned their saying to His Prophet. When the two scholars spoke to him the Messenger said to them: submit; they said: we have submitted; he said: you have not submitted, so submit; they said: indeed we submitted before you; he said: you lie, three things keep you from Islam, your claim that God has a son, your worship of the cross and your eating of pork; they said: then who is his father, Muhammad? and the Messenger was silent and did not answer them; so God sent down about their saying and the differences in all their affair the opening of Al Imran, to some eighty verses of it, beginning the sura with His declaring Himself far above what they said and affirming His oneness in creation and command, with no partner in it, as a rebuttal of the unbelief they invented and the rivals they set beside Him, and an argument against them from what they said of their companion, so as to make them know their error. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 153} This is the narration as it came from Ibn Ishaq, reported as sayings ascribed to the Christians and to those who related of them, with no museum verdict. The editor's notes on pp. 152-153 are his: the striped garments are a kind of embroidered Yemeni cloth of fine quality; the words 'in the manner of men' are a needed addition from Ibn Hisham; 'Khuwaylid ibn Amr' in print and manuscript is a mistake corrected from Ibn Hisham, as is the phrase on their Christianity; Ibn Hisham has 'to make him a sign for people' as the verse's own wording; a phrase in the print is nonsense and the manuscript's is right; print and manuscript omit the separate letters, which he added from Ibn Hisham; the manuscript has 'by clearing Himself' where Ibn Hisham has 'by declaring Himself above'; and the report is in Ibn Hisham's Sira at greater length, its rest coming in the following reports. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 152} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 153}

In 6544, al-Rabi' (through al-Muthanna, Ishaq, Ibn Abi Jafar and his father): the Christians came to the Prophet and disputed with him about Jesus son of Mary, asking who his father was, and said falsehood and slander about God; the Prophet said to them: do you not know that there is no child that does not resemble its father? They said: yes. Do you not know that our Lord is living and does not die, and that extinction comes to Jesus? Yes. Do you not know that our Lord is the sustainer of everything, guarding, keeping and providing for it? Yes. Does Jesus own any of that? No. Do you not know that nothing on earth or in heaven is hidden from God? Yes. Does Jesus know any of that except what he was taught? No. Our Lord formed Jesus in the womb as He wished; do you know that? Yes. Do you not know that our Lord does not eat food, drink or relieve himself? Yes. Do you not know that Jesus was carried by his mother as a woman carries, delivered as a woman delivers her child, nourished as a child is nourished, and then ate food, drank and relieved himself? Yes. How then can it be as you claim? They recognised it, then refused but to deny, and God sent down the opening of the sura. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 154} The editor notes that the manuscript and al-Durr al-Manthur have a slightly different wording at the place of one sentence, that al-Baghawi abridged it, that he left the print as it is lest it came from another copy, and that 'his mother carried him' is right from al-Durr and al-Baghawi in place of 'a woman carried him'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 154}

Then al-Tabari gives the readers' difference over the word for the self-subsisting: the readers of the regions read it in the form qayyum, and it is reported that Umar ibn al-Khattab and Ibn Masud read it in the form qayyam, and of Alqama ibn Qays that he read it in the form qayyim. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 155} In 6545, Alqama (through Abu Kurayb, Ithaam ibn Ali, al-Amash, Ibrahim and Abu Mamar): I heard him read qayyim; I said: you heard him? He said: I do not know. In 6546 the same (through Abu Hisham al-Rifai and Waki). In 6547, Alqama (through Abu Hisham, Abd Allah, Shayban and al-Amash): that he read qayyam. Abu Jafar says that the only reading allowed in his view is what the Muslim readers brought by widespread transmission, without collusion, by inheritance and as fixed in their copies of the Qur'an, and that is the reading qayyum; this is his choice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 155} The editor refers back to p. 127. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 155}

Then he begins the explanation of God's description as the living. The interpreters differed over its meaning. Some said He described Himself with permanence and denied death, which is possible for others of His creatures. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 156} In 6548, Muhammad ibn Jafar ibn al-Zubayr (through Ibn Humayd, Salama ibn al-Fadl and Ibn Ishaq): the one who does not die, whereas Jesus died and was crucified in their saying, that is, the saying of the scholars who disputed with the Messenger from the Christians of Najran. In 6549, al-Rabi' (through al-Muthanna and Ishaq): living, never dying. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 156} Others said the sense of 'the living' is that to Him is made easy the management of whatever He wishes and wills, nothing He wants being withheld from Him, and that He is not like the gods and rivals who have no management. Others said it means that to Him belongs an everlasting life that has always been His attribute and will always be, and that He described Himself with life because He has life. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 156} The editor refers to the earlier commentary on the word. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 156}

We stop at the end of p. 156, in the account of the meanings of the word for the living. These are reports as transmitted, al-Tabari's choices and arguments are his, and the editor's notes and references are his, with no museum verdict.
