## In al-Tabari's commentary
On "and He taught Adam" al-Tabari reports from Ibn Abbas and Said ibn Jubayr that God sent the Angel of Death (in another place Iblis, as the editor notes) who took from the surface of the earth, sweet and salty, and Adam was made from it, hence the name Adam, from the "adim" of the earth. He reports from Ali that Adam was made from the surface of the earth, with the good, the sound and the bad in it, and all that is seen in his children, the editor noting the chain is weak. He gives, through al-Suddi, that Adam's children come in different kinds because the earth taken was red, white and black. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 480} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 481}
He reports from the Prophet, by a chain the editor calls sound, from Abu Musa al-Ash'ari: God made Adam from a handful taken from all the earth, so Adam's children came according to the earth, red, black, white and between, the soft and the rough, the bad and the good. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 481} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 482}
Al-Tabari says that on this reading "Adam" is originally a verb-form name given to the father of mankind, like "Ahmad" and "As'ad", which is why it is not declined, meaning he reached the "adama" of the earth, its visible surface. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 482}
On "all the names" he records differences over which names Adam was taught. He reports from Ibn Abbas that they are the names by which people know things: human, animal, earth, plain, sea, mountain, donkey and the like; from Mujahid that He taught him the name of everything; and from Said ibn Jubayr that this included camel, cow and sheep. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 482} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 483}

Al-Tabari reports from Qatada that He taught him the name of everything, this a mountain, this a sea, and then showed those things to the angels; from al-Hasan and Qatada that these included horses, mules, camels, jinn and wild animals; and from Ibn Abbas, by several chains (the editor notes that one is broken), that it reached even small and trivial things. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 484} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 485}
A second view, from al-Rabi' ibn Anas, is that the names were those of the angels, and a third, from Ibn Zayd, is that they were the names of all his descendants. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 485}
Al-Tabari's own choice is that the names were those of Adam's descendants and of the angels, not of every kind of creature. His reason is that the verse says "then He showed them" in the pronoun form the Arabs use almost only for human beings and angels, while for animals and other creatures they use the feminine singular or plural forms. He allows that Ibn Abbas's view is possible, by analogy with a verse in surah al-Nur where a mixed group is referred to with the human pronoun, and notes that the pronoun is "hunna" in Ibn Mas'ud's reading and "ha" in Ubayy's, so Ibn Abbas may have followed Ubayy's reading. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 485} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 486}
On "then He showed them to the angels" he repeats that the sense is that He showed the owners of the names, and lists the reports: from al-Dahhak through Ibn Abbas that He showed the names, from al-Suddi that He showed creation, from Ibn Zayd that He brought his descendants out of his back and showed them, and from Qatada that He showed those names. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 486} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 487}

On "tell me the names of these" al-Tabari glosses "tell me" as "inform me", cites a verse of al-Nabigha al-Dhubyani, and reports from Mujahid that the names meant are those Adam had been told, with another report from him naming the dove and the raven among those shown. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 488} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 489}
On "if you are truthful" he lists three views: from Ibn Abbas, if you know that I will not make a vicegerent on earth; from al-Suddi, by his familiar chain, if the children of Adam will cause corruption and shed blood; and from al-Hasan and Qatada, that He created nothing but they knew more of it than He had. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 490}
Al-Tabari's own choice is Ibn Abbas's reading: tell me the names of those I have shown you, if you are truthful in your claim that if I made my vicegerent from others than you his descendants would disobey me, while if I made you so you would obey. If they were ignorant of the names of existing creatures they could see, they were all the more likely not to know what did not yet exist or was hidden, so they should not ask what they had no knowledge of. He compares this reproof with the one given to Noah in surah Hud, and reads "I know what you do not know" as meaning that one among them was the first and last of disobedience, namely Iblis. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 490} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 491}

Al-Tabari answers a view of some Basran grammarians that "if you are truthful" does not imply that the angels had claimed anything, but only shows their ignorance of the unseen, as a man says to another "tell me this if you know" while knowing he does not. He argues that truthfulness in Arabic concerns a report, not knowledge, and that this view departs from all the commentators. He also rejects reading "in" as "idh" (since), because the readers agree on the kasra of the hamza. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 492} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 493}
