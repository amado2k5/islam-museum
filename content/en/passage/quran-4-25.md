## Summary
Verse 25 of [[passage/quran-4|Surat an-Nisa]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 25 of 176 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**The start of verse 25: the meaning of 'tawl', the view that it is wealth and ampleness (pp. 182-183, reports 9049-9056).** Abu Jafar says the interpreters differed over the meaning of the word for means in this verse. Some said it is bounty, wealth and ampleness. Muhammad ibn Amr (9049, from Abu Asim from Isa from Ibn Abi Najih) reports from Mujahid that it is wealth; al-Muthanna (9050, from Abu Hudhayfa from Shibl from Ibn Abi Najih) reports the same from Mujahid; al-Muthanna (9051, from Abdallah ibn Salih from Mu'awiya ibn Salih from Ali ibn Abi Talha) reports from Ibn Abbas that it is whoever has no ampleness; Bishr ibn Mu'adh (9052, from Yazid from Sa'id) reports from Qatada that it is ampleness; al-Qasim (9053, from al-Husayn from Hushaym from Abu Bishr) reports from Sa'id ibn Jubayr that it is wealth; al-Muthanna (9054, from Hibban ibn Musa from Ibn al-Mubarak from Hushaym from Abu Bishr) reports from Sa'id ibn Jubayr that it is ampleness, and the editor notes that the printed text added "ibn" in al-Muthanna's chain, which the manuscript lacks and which is the right reading, with references to earlier and later places; Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (9055, from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal from Asbat) reports from al-Suddi that it is ampleness of wealth; Yunus (9056, from Ibn Wahb) reports from Ibn Zayd that it is not finding what to marry a free woman with. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 183}

**The view that it means desire, and reports on a free man marrying a slave woman (pp. 183-184, reports 9057-9061).** Others said that here it means desire. Yunus (9057, from Ibn Wahb from Abd al-Jabbar ibn Umar) reports from Rabi'a that a man may marry a slave woman if his desire is for her; the editor corrects the narrator's name to Abd al-Jabbar ibn Umar al-Ayli and refers to his biography. Yunus (9058, from Ibn Wahb) reports from Ibn Zayd that Rabi'a softened this somewhat, holding that a man may marry a slave woman if he fears for himself because of loving her, even if he could marry another. Al-Muthanna (9059, from Hibban ibn Musa from Ibn al-Mubarak from Hammad ibn Salama from Abu al-Zubayr) reports from Jabir that he was asked about a free man marrying a slave woman and said that if he has means, no; asked what if love for her falls into his heart, he said that if he fears distress let him marry her. Ibn Humayd (9060, from Jarir from Mansur from Ubayda) reports from al-Shabi that a free man should not marry a slave woman unless he finds none, while Ibrahim used to say there is no harm in it. Al-Muthanna (9061, from Hibban ibn Musa from Ibn al-Mubarak from Ibn Jurayj) reports from Ata that they did not object to a man of means marrying a slave woman today if he feared being made miserable by her; the editor notes that the printed text reads the last word differently from his reading of the manuscript and ties it to the mention of hardship in the earlier reports. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 184}

**Al-Tabari's choice on the meaning of 'tawl' (pp. 184-185).** Abu Jafar holds that the sounder of the two views is that the word here means ampleness and wealth, because all agree that God did not forbid anything and then make it lawful because desire overcame a person or for the sake of pleasure; rather He made the forbidden lawful in necessity, when the person in need fears for his life, as with carrion for the desperate. If that is agreed for everything except the marriage of slave women by one who can afford a free woman, then the marriage of slave women by a man able to marry a free woman is the same: it does not become lawful because desire overpowers him, since that is satisfaction of pleasure and not a case of necessity that lifts the concession. He sees in the agreement that a man overcome by desire for a woman, free or slave, may have her only by marriage or purchase as God allowed, evidence against those who explained the word as desire and allowed a man able to marry a free woman to marry slave women. The meaning of the verse for him is therefore: whoever among you lacks the means for marrying free women, let him marry from the slave women he owns. The root sense of the word is bestowal of favour; the verb is used in that sense with one vowelling, and with another for length as the opposite of shortness. In footnotes the editor says the first publisher found the two sentences after the agreement hard to follow, explains that they mean God did not make the forbidden lawful because of desire but because of necessity, notes where he preferred his reading over the printed text and the manuscript, and notes that one phrase is the object of "God allowed His servants". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 185}

**The explanation of the clause on marrying believing free women (p. 185).** Abu Jafar begins to explain the next clause: whoever of you, people, lack, meaning of the free, the means to marry the muhsanat, who are the free women, the believing ones, meaning those who have, and the sentence breaks off at the end of the page. The editor refers in a footnote to the earlier explanation of the word muhsanat. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 185}

**The rest of the explanation of 'believing free women', with reports from Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, al-Suddi and others (pp. 186-187, reports 9062-9068).** Abu Jafar completes his sentence: the believing women are those who affirmed God's oneness and the truth the messenger brought, and the interpreters spoke of the muhsanat much as he did. Al-Muthanna (9062, from Abdallah ibn Salih from Mu'awiya ibn Salih from Ali ibn Abi Talha) reports from Ibn Abbas that the word means free women, so whoever cannot marry them should marry from the believers' slave women; Muhammad ibn Amr (9063, from Abu Asim from Isa from Ibn Abi Najih) reports from Mujahid that the muhsanat are free women and that one should marry a believing slave woman; al-Muthanna (9064, from Abu Hudhayfa from Shibl) reports the same from Mujahid; Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (9065, from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal from Asbat) reports from al-Suddi that the young women here are your slave women; al-Qasim (9066, from al-Husayn from Hushaym from Abu Bishr) reports from Sa'id ibn Jubayr that whoever cannot find the means to marry a free woman marries a slave woman, and the editor says the printed text has the verb in another form and that he kept the manuscript; Yunus (9067, from Ibn Wahb) reports from Ibn Zayd that such a man marries the slave woman and keeps chaste with her, and her owners spare him her upkeep, that God made this lawful to no one unless he lacks the means to marry and support a free woman, and that it is not lawful for him until he fears distress, the editor preferring the manuscript over the printed text in two places; al-Muthanna (9068, from Hibban ibn Musa from Ibn al-Mubarak from Sufyan from Hisham al-Dastuwa'i from Amir al-Ahwal) reports from al-Hasan that the Messenger of God forbade marrying a slave woman on top of a free wife and a free woman on top of a slave wife, and said that whoever has the means for a free woman should not marry a slave woman. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 187}

**The readers' differences over the vowel of 'muhsanat', and al-Tabari's choice (pp. 187-188).** Abu Jafar says the readers differed over this word. A group of the readers of Kufa and Mecca read it with a kasra on the letter sad here and in its parallels in the Qur'an, except the first place in [[passage/quran-4-24|4:24]], which they read with a fatha, taking it to mean women protected by their husbands; elsewhere they took the kasra to mean women who protected themselves by chastity. Most readers of Medina and Iraq read all of them with a fatha, meaning that some were protected by their husbands and some by their freedom or their Islam. Some early readers read all of them with a kasra, meaning they were chaste and guarded themselves; this reading is reported from Alqama, with differing transmission from him. The editor observes that Abu Jafar did not mention this reading or this difference in his commentary on verse 24 earlier, which he counts as evidence of how much he abridged that commentary. Abu Jafar's choice is that both readings are widespread among the readers of the regions and agree in meaning, so whichever a reader uses is right, except the first place in [[passage/quran-4-24|4:24]], where he does not allow the kasra because the regions agree on the fatha; had the kasra been as widespread there as the fatha, it too would be right, given the varied senses of the word for protection he explained, and the meaning would then be the chaste among women. The editor adds that none of this was mentioned in the first commentary and that the meaning of protection came a little earlier. Abu Jafar then says the word for young women is the plural of a word for a girl, meaning young women, that every slave woman of age or youth is called by it, and the male slave by the masculine form. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 188}

**The dispute over marrying slave women who are not believers (pp. 188-189, reports 9069-9072).** Abu Jafar says the scholars differed over marrying young women who are not believers, and whether God's description of them as believing means a prohibition of the others or is guidance for the believers. Some said it indicates that marrying the slave women of polytheists and the People of the Book is forbidden. Ibn Bashshar (9069, from Abd al-Rahman from Sufyan from Ibn Abi Najih) reports from Mujahid that it is not fitting to marry a Christian slave woman; Ibn Waki (9070, from his father from Sufyan from Ibn Abi Najih) reports from Mujahid that a free Muslim should not marry a slave woman of the People of the Book; Ali ibn Sahl (9071, from al-Walid ibn Muslim) says he heard Abu Amr (al-Awza'i), Sa'id ibn Abd al-Aziz, Malik ibn Anas and Abu Bakr ibn Abdallah ibn Abi Maryam say that a Christian slave woman is not lawful to a free Muslim or a Muslim slave, because the verse carries the description of belief, meaning for marriage. Others said it is guidance and recommendation, not prohibition, and among them are a group of the people of Iraq; (9072, from Ibn Humayd from Jarir from Mansur from Mughira) Abu Maysara said that the People of the Book are in the position of free women. Among them are Abu Hanifa and his companions, who argued from the verse of al-Ma'ida, [[passage/quran-5-5|5:5]], which made the chaste women of the People of the Book lawful, saying no one may single out a slave woman or a free woman among them, and that the description of belief here means only women who are not idol worshippers. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 189}

**The editor's notes on report 9071 (p. 189).** The editor identifies al-Walid ibn Muslim al-Dimashqi and refers to his earlier biography, says Abu Amr is al-Awza'i, and that the name stood in both printed text and manuscript as "Abu Amr Sa'id", as if one man, which he corrected to two men; for Sa'id ibn Abd al-Aziz al-Tanukhi, Abu Muhammad, he refers to the earlier biography. Abu Bakr ibn Abdallah ibn Abi Maryam al-Ghassani was, he says, one of the diligent worshippers, with many hadith but weak; he quotes Abu Hatim that he was weak in hadith, that thieves broke in and took his belongings and he became confused, that he died in 156, and that the Tahdhib al-Tahdhib errs in the year of his death, referring to Ibn Sa'd's Tabaqat. He adds that printed text and manuscript read "and Malik ibn Abdallah ibn Abi Maryam", that no narrator has that name, and that he corrected it to Abu Bakr ibn Abi Maryam, from whom al-Walid ibn Muslim transmitted as from the others named before. In another note he explains that "among them Abu Hanifa and his companions" is joined to the earlier mention of a group of the people of Iraq. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 189}

**Al-Tabari's choice in the dispute (p. 190).** Abu Jafar holds that the sounder view is that the description indicates a prohibition of marrying the slave women of the People of the Book, who are lawful only by ownership, because God made marriage to slave women lawful under conditions, and where the conditions are not all met a Muslim may not marry them. To the objection that the verse of al-Ma'ida shows they are permitted by marriage, he replies that its ruling has been shown to concern a particular class of their chaste women, their free women and not their slaves, by the description of belief here; neither verse sets aside the other, and one would set aside the other only if their rulings could not both stand rightly; since both can, neither is judged to override the other without a proof from a report or an analogy, and there is none; the verse allows his reading, that the chaste women meant are the free women of the People of the Book and not their slaves. In footnotes the editor says he preferred the manuscript's reading over the printed one in three places, including the dual form in place of the singular for the word for ruling, and that the first publisher misread one spot in the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 190}

**The clause on God's knowledge of people's faith, a transposed clause (p. 191).** Abu Jafar says this is a case of postposing whose sense is preposing, and the meaning is: whoever among you cannot afford the dowers of believing free women, let one of you marry from another, meaning let this man marry this man's girl. He explains that the word for "some" is in the nominative by the sense of the sentence, since the earlier clause means "let him marry from those his hands own", and "some of you" is then joined to that sense. He explains that God knows best the faith of those who believed in Him, in His messenger and in what the messenger brought, and affirmed all of it; so the poor man who cannot afford a free woman should marry from the wealthy man his believing girl who has shown and displayed her faith, and they leave their inner states to God, whose knowledge it is, not yours; He knows your inner states and theirs. The editor notes that the manuscript completes the verse here with an extra clause and that the first publisher did well to drop it, since the commentary treats the clause on God's knowledge of faith as coming before the other. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 191}

**The clause on marrying with the families' permission and giving the wages (p. 192, report 9073).** Abu Jafar says the command to marry them means to wed them; with their families' permission means with the permission of their owners, their command to you to marry them and their consent; and giving them their wages means giving them their dowers. Yunus (9073, from Ibn Wahb) reports from Ibn Zayd that the wages are the dower. The word for fairness he explains as in what you agree on, of what God has made lawful and allowed to be made a dower for them. The editor refers in footnotes to the earlier explanations of the words for marrying, permission, giving, wages and fairness. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 192}

**The description of chaste women who are not open fornicators or takers of lovers (p. 193, reports 9074-9075).** Abu Jafar says the muhsanat here are the chaste, the musafihat are the fornicating women, and the takers of lovers are women who take friends for illicit union. He says it was put so because the fornicating women among the Arabs in the Jahiliyya were those who announced their fornication, and the takers of lovers were those who confined themselves to one companion and friend for debauchery in secret and not openly. Al-Muthanna (9074, from Abdallah ibn Salih from Mu'awiya ibn Salih from Ali ibn Abi Talha) reports from Ibn Abbas that the sense is: marry them chaste, not fornicators in secret or in public, and that the akhdan are close friends. Muhammad ibn Sa'd (9075, from his father from his uncle from his father from his grandfather) reports from Ibn Abbas that the musafihat are the women who announce their fornication, that the taker of lovers is the one with a single companion, and that the people of the Jahiliyya forbade open fornication and allowed what was hidden, saying that what showed was baseness and what was hidden did no harm, and God then sent down a verse forbidding indecencies, open and hidden, which is [[passage/quran-6-151|6:151]] (the printed number is smudged). The editor refers to the earlier explanations of muhsanat and sifah. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 193}

**The rest of the reports on the description of chaste women who are not open fornicators (pp. 194-195, reports 9076-9083).** Muhammad ibn Abd al-A'la (9076, from Mu'tamir from Dawud) reports from Amir al-Shabi that fornication is of two kinds: a woman who fornicates with a lover and with no one else, and a woman who is on offer to whoever offers (the word is unpointed in the manuscript). The editor explains in a footnote that the printed text reads it in a way that makes no sense, that the word means an offer, as in offering goods for sale, and ties it to the description in 9080 of the harlot who hires herself out to whoever offers; he adds that the word is not explained in the lexica, so he vowelled it. Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (9077, from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal from Asbat) reports from al-Suddi that the chaste women are the virtuous, and a slave woman is to be married with her family's leave, chaste, not announcing fornication and not taking a friend; Muhammad ibn Amr (9078, from Abu Asim from Isa from Ibn Abi Najih) reports from Mujahid that lovers means a man taking a female companion and a woman taking a male companion; al-Muthanna (9079, from Abu Hudhayfa from Shibl) the same from him; Bishr ibn Mu'adh (9080, from Yazid from Sa'id) reports from Qatada that the fornicating woman is the harlot who hires herself out to whoever offers, that the one with a lover is the woman with a single companion, and that God forbade marrying either; a narrator reports from al-Husayn ibn al-Faraj (9081, from Abu Mu'adh from Ubayd ibn Sulayman) from al-Dahhak ibn Muzahim that the muhsanat are free women, so one marries a free woman, that the open fornicators are those who announce it without a dower, and that the takers of lovers are women with a single companion whom they keep secret, and God forbade that; the editor records in a footnote the reading he preferred between printed text and manuscript and glosses the word for secret as hidden; al-Qasim (9082, from al-Husayn from Hushaym from Isma'il ibn Salim) reports from al-Shabi that fornication has two ugly forms, one worse than the other, the worse being the woman who fornicates with whoever comes to her and the other the woman with a single lover; Yunus (9083, from Ibn Wahb) reports from Ibn Zayd that the open fornicator is the man who meets a woman, fornicates with her, and they part, while the one who takes a lover is the man who stays with her in disobedience to God and she with him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 195}

**The readers' dispute over the verb for protection in the clause on the penalty (pp. 195-196).** Abu Jafar says the readers differed over the form of the verb that opens the clause on the penalty. Some read it with a fatha on the first letter, meaning once they have become Muslim and so have their private parts guarded from the forbidden by Islam; others read it with a damma, meaning once they have married and so are guarded by their husbands. Abu Jafar's choice is that both readings are known and widespread in the regions of Islam, so whichever a reader uses is right. If someone thinks this impermissible because the meanings differ and holds that two readings are allowed only when the meanings agree, he has been heedless, because the two meanings do not cancel each other: God made the penalty obligatory on a slave woman, whether Muslim or not, on the lips of His messenger. He cites (9084) the Prophet as saying that when a slave woman of one of you fornicates he should flog her according to the Qur'an and not reproach her, that if she does it again he should flog her likewise, and that on the fourth occasion he should flog her and sell her even for a rope of hair. The editor says the hadith is sound, that al-Tabari gives it without a chain, that it seems to come from Abu Hurayra's Musnad, and that al-Bukhari, Muslim, Ahmad (no. 7389) and al-Bayhaqi have it in differing wording; he explains the phrase about not reproaching as not taunting her with the fornication and not blaming her harshly, takes this as a discipline the Prophet taught his community, not to taunt a wrongdoer for what he did but to be gentle with him, and then adds a remark on the habits of the people and the press of his own day who are quick to attack anyone who has committed a crime. This is relayed as the editor's comment, and the museum gives no verdict. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 196}

**The penalty on slave women, and the objection from the reports of Abu Hurayra and Zayd ibn Khalid (pp. 197-198, reports 9085-9087).** Abu Jafar cites (9085) the Prophet's saying to carry out the penalties on your slaves, and says he did not single out women with a husband or without one, so the penalties are obligatory on the masters of slave women when they fornicate, by the Qur'an and the Messenger's command. He then raises an objection: what do you say about what Ibn Bashshar (9086, from Abd al-Rahman from Malik ibn Anas from al-Zuhri from Ubaydallah ibn Abdallah) related from Abu Hurayra and Zayd ibn Khalid, that the Prophet was asked about a slave woman who fornicates and has not yet been protected, and said to flog her, and if she does it again flog her, and if she does it a third or fourth time to sell her even for a braid, the braid being hair; and Abu Kurayb (9087, from Ibn Uyayna from al-Zuhri) relates something similar. The objector says this shows that the penalty the Prophet's Sunna made obligatory on slave women is the one before their protection, and what the Qur'an made obligatory is the one after. Abu Jafar replies that he has shown that one meaning of protection is Islam and another marriage, that it is a word covering many meanings, and that nothing in the report shows that the woman the Prophet was asked about was one who fornicated before marriage, so it cannot serve as proof for someone who holds that protection means Islam alone or marriage alone. Since nothing is shown, the sound view for him is that every slave woman who fornicates, married or not, her master must apply the penalty on her, by the apparent sense of the Qur'an and the established Sunna, except anyone whom something that must be accepted has excluded; and this shows him the soundness of his choice of reading. In footnotes the editor gives for 9085 the references to Ahmad, al-Bayhaqi and Ibn Kathir, and for 9086-9087 the Muwatta', al-Bukhari and Muslim with slight differences in wording, noting that Malik explained the braid as a rope. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 198}

**Al-Tabari's reply to those who take the mention of belief as showing that the meaning is marriage, and his choice on the reading (pp. 198-199).** Abu Jafar says that anyone who thinks that, because this clause follows the description of the young women as believing, it must mean marriage has thought wrongly, because it is not impossible in speech that the sense is: whoever cannot afford to marry free women should marry from his believing slave women, and once they have believed, if they commit a shameful act the penalty described falls on them, so that the statement begins afresh after the explanation of whom we may not marry among them and whom we may. It is therefore not allowed to turn the meaning to marriage rather than Islam on account of the prior description of belief. The editor says the printed text altered the manuscript, and that the words for predicate and subject here are not used in their grammatical sense but mean God's statement as a new beginning. Abu Jafar then says that for one who reads the word for protected women with a fatha on the sad he chooses the verb with a damma, and for one who reads it with a kasra the verb with a fatha, so that the reader's reading holds together in one meaning and one context; the reverse would not be a solecism. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 199}

**The view that protection here means Islam (pp. 199-201, reports 9088-9099).** Abu Jafar says the interpreters differed on this as the readers did; some said it means once they have become Muslim. Muhammad ibn Abdallah ibn Bazi (9088, from Bishr ibn al-Mufaddal from Sa'id from Abu Ma'shar) reports from Ibrahim that Ibn Mas'ud said her Islam is her protection; the editor identifies Sa'id as Ibn Abi Aruba and Abu Ma'shar as Ziyad ibn Kulayb and corrects the name as printed. Yunus (9089, from Ibn Wahb from Jarir ibn Hazim from Sulayman ibn Mihran from Ibrahim ibn Yazid from Hammam ibn al-Harith) reports that al-Nu'man ibn Abdallah ibn Muqarrin asked Ibn Mas'ud about a slave woman of his who had fornicated; he said to flog her fifty lashes; the man said she had not been protected, and Ibn Mas'ud said her protection is her Islam. Ibn Bashshar (9090, from Abd al-Rahman from Sufyan from Hammad) reports from Ibrahim that al-Nu'man ibn Muqarrin asked Ibn Mas'ud about a slave woman who fornicated and had no husband, and he said her Islam is her protection; Ibn al-Muthanna (9091, from Muhammad ibn Jafar from Shu'ba from Hammad) reports from Ibrahim something close; Ibn Humayd (9092, from Jarir from Mughira from Ibrahim) reports from Alqama that Abdallah used to say her protection is her Islam; Abu Kurayb (9093, from Hushaym from Isma'il ibn Salim) reports that al-Shabi recited the clause and said it means once they become Muslim; Abu Hisham al-Rifa'i (9094, from Yahya ibn Abi Za'ida from Ash'ath) reports from al-Shabi, from Abdallah, that a slave woman's protection is her Islam; Ya'qub ibn Ibrahim (9095, from Hushaym from Mughira) reports that Ibrahim used to say once they become Muslim; Abu Hisham (9096, from Yahya from Ash'ath) reports from al-Shabi that protection is Islam; Ya'qub ibn Ibrahim (9097, from Ibn Ulayya from Burd ibn Sinan) reports from al-Zuhri that Umar flogged virgin slave girls of the emirate for fornication, and the editor identifies Burd ibn Sinan al-Shami, client of Quraysh and companion of Makhul, as truthful in hadith, notes that the word "emirate" is written in the manuscript's margin after the text was struck through, and suggests it may mean girls taken captive, which is his guess; Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (9098, from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal from Asbat) reports from al-Suddi once they become Muslim; Ibn Waki (9099, from his father from Isra'il from Jabir) reports from Salim and al-Qasim that her protection is her Islam and her chastity. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 201}

**The editor's notes on reports 9089-9090 (p. 200).** The editor says Ibrahim ibn Yazid is Ibrahim al-Nakha'i, and that Hammam ibn al-Harith al-Nakha'i was trustworthy, one of the devout who slept only sitting, and narrated from Ibn Mas'ud. For the name "al-Nu'man ibn Abdallah ibn Muqarrin" as it stands in printed text and manuscript he found no mention in the books; the next report has "al-Nu'man ibn Muqarrin", and there is disagreement on that name, some saying al-Nu'man ibn Amr ibn Muqarrin and some that they were two men, but none of them lists al-Nu'man ibn Abdallah ibn Muqarrin. He adds that al-Bayhaqi gives another version through Ibrahim al-Nakha'i, Hammam ibn al-Harith and Amr ibn Shurahbil, in which Ma'qil ibn Muqarrin came to Abdallah, and says he could not settle anything in this confusion. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 200}

**The view that protection here means marriage (p. 201, reports 9100-9101).** Others said the meaning is once they marry. Al-Muthanna (9100, from Abdallah ibn Salih from Mu'awiya from Ali ibn Abi Talha) reports from Ibn Abbas that it means once they have married a free man. Report 9101 (al-Qasim from al-Husayn from Hushaym) then begins and is cut off at the end of the page. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 201}

**The rest of the view that protection means marriage (p. 202, reports 9101-9107).** Report 9101 (from al-Qasim from al-Husayn from Hushaym from Husayn) relates from Ikrima that Ibn Abbas used to read the verb with a damma on its first letter and said it means once they marry; Ibn Waki (9102, from Jarir from Mughira) relates from Ikrima that Ibn Abbas used to read it with a fatha and said they married; Abu Kurayb (9103, from Ibn Idris from Layth) relates from Mujahid that a slave woman's protection is a free man marrying her and a male slave's is marrying a free woman; Ibn al-Muthanna (9104, from Muhammad ibn Jafar from Shu'ba from Amr ibn Murra) relates that he heard Sa'id ibn Jubayr say a slave woman is not to be flogged if she fornicates as long as she has not married; Ibn Bashshar (9105, from Abd al-A'la from Sa'id) relates from Qatada from al-Hasan that the husbands protected them; Bishr ibn Mu'adh (9106, from Yazid from Sa'id) relates the same from Qatada; Yunus (9107, from Ibn Wahb from Iyad ibn Abdallah from Abu al-Zinad) relates from al-Shabi that Ibn Abbas told him that he had relations with a slave girl of his who had fornicated and said "I have protected her", and the editor notes that the manuscript has another form of the verb. Abu Jafar says this interpretation goes with the reading of those who read the verb with a damma and with the sense of those who read it with a fatha, and that he has shown the sound view on reading and meaning earlier (pp. 195-196 and 199). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 202}

**The clause on half the penalty (pp. 203-204, reports 9108-9109).** Abu Jafar says the meaning is: if your young women, who are your slave women, commit the indecency, which is fornication, after being protected by Islam or by marriage, they owe a half share of the free women's penalty when they fornicate before being protected by husbands. The penalty here is the hadd, and the half God made for slave women who are protected is fifty lashes and exile of six months, because on a free woman who commits the indecency before being protected by a husband the hundred lashes and a year's exile are due. Al-Muthanna (9108, from Abdallah ibn Salih from Mu'awiya ibn Salih from Ali ibn Abi Talha) relates the clause itself from Ibn Abbas, and the editor says the report is cut off in manuscript and printed text, so he put dots to mark the break and found it nowhere else. Bishr (9109, from Yazid from Sa'id) relates from Qatada that it is fifty lashes, with no exile and no stoning. Abu Jafar then raises a question: how can it be said that flogging is "upon" them, and can flogging be upon anyone? He answers that the meaning is that it is binding on their bodies to be flogged half of what binds the bodies of protected women, as one says "upon me is a day's prayer" meaning it is binding on me to pray a day's prayer, and "upon me are the pilgrimage and the fast"; likewise "upon him is the hadd" means it is binding on him to submit himself to the penalty so that it be carried out. The editor notes that a bracketed insertion in the question is necessary though it is in neither manuscript nor printed text, and that in one place he preferred the printed text over the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 204}

**The meaning of 'anat' in the clause permitting this for one who fears it (pp. 204-206, reports 9110-9120).** Abu Jafar says the pointer refers to what God permitted people, marrying believing young women, for one who cannot afford to marry free believing women; He permitted it to those among them who dread hardship, not others who do not. The interpreters differed, and some said it is fornication. Abu Kurayb (9110, from Ibn Idris from Layth) relates from Mujahid that it is fornication; Ya'qub ibn Ibrahim (9111, from Hushaym from al-Awwam from one who told him) relates that Ibn Abbas said the husband of a slave woman moves away from fornication only a little, and the editor notes that the Lisan al-Arab gives this under two roots and that the full report there closes by quoting the next clause of the verse; al-Muthanna (9112, from Abdallah ibn Salih from Mu'awiya ibn Salih from Ali ibn Abi Talha) relates from Ibn Abbas that hardship is fornication; al-Muthanna (9113, from Ishaq from Ubayd ibn Yahya from Sharik from Ata ibn al-Sa'ib from Sa'id ibn Jubayr) relates the same from Ibn Abbas; Ya'qub (9114, from Hushaym from Abu Bishr) relates the saying about the husband of a slave woman from Sa'id ibn Jubayr; Abu Salama (9115, from Muhammad ibn Jafar from Shu'ba from Abu Bishr) relates something similar from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, and the editor says he could not identify who Abu Salama is among Abu Jafar's teachers; al-Muthanna (9116, from Hibban ibn Musa from Ibn al-Mubarak from Fudayl ibn Marzuq) relates from Atiyya that it is fornication; al-Muthanna (9117, from Ishaq from Ibn Abi Hammad from Fudayl) relates the same from Atiyya al-Awfi; al-Muthanna (9118, from Ishaq from Abu Zuhayr from Juwaybir) relates from al-Dahhak that it is fornication; al-Qasim (9119, whose number is printed damaged, from al-Husayn from Hushaym from Ubayda) relates from al-Shabi, and Juwaybir from al-Dahhak, that hardship is fornication; Ahmad ibn Hazim (9120, from Abu Nu'aym from Fudayl ibn Marzuq) relates from Atiyya that hardship is fornication. Others said the meaning is the punishment that distresses him, which is the hadd. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 206}

**Al-Tabari's choice on the meaning of 'anat' (pp. 206-207).** Abu Jafar holds that the sound view is that it is for whoever among you fears harm in his religion and his body, because the word means what harms a man; one says he suffered hardship when he did what harms him in religion or this world, and he notes a parallel use in [[passage/quran-3-118|3:118]]; it has also been said that the word means destruction. He then sets out each group's reasoning: those who took it as fornication said it is harm to religion; those who took it as sin said all sins are harm to religion; those who took it as punishment said the hadd is a harm to the body of the one punished in this world. He holds that God covered all the senses of the word, and that fornication gathers them, because it brings a punishment in this world and earns sin and harm in religion and the world; the interpreters who are authorities agreed that this is its meaning. Though in itself it is pleasure and fulfilment of desire, it is ascribed to hardship and described by it since it is its cause. The editor notes that the printed text has a reading which is correct but that he kept the manuscript's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 207}

**The clause on refraining from marrying slave women, forgiveness and mercy (pp. 207-208, reports 9121-9128).** Abu Jafar says the meaning is that refraining, people, from marrying slave women is better for you, and that God is forgiving of your marrying slave women when you marry them as He made lawful and permitted, and of what has gone before in that matter, if you set your own affairs right between you and God, and merciful to you in permitting their marriage at poverty and lack of means for a free woman. Ya'qub ibn Ibrahim (9121, from Hushaym from Abu Bishr) relates from Sa'id ibn Jubayr that it means refraining from marrying a slave woman; Abu Kurayb (9122, from Ibn Idris from Layth) relates from Mujahid that it is from marrying slave women; Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (9123, from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal from Asbat) relates from al-Suddi that to refrain and not marry a slave woman, so that your children are not slaves, is better for you; Muhammad ibn Amr (9124, from Abu Asim from Isa from Ibn Abi Najih) relates from Mujahid that refraining from marrying slave women is better, although it is lawful; Bishr ibn Mu'adh (9125, from Yazid from Sa'id) relates the same from Qatada; al-Muthanna (9126, from Hibban ibn Musa from Ibn al-Mubarak from Fudayl ibn Marzuq) relates the same from Atiyya; al-Muthanna (9127, from Hibban from Ibn al-Mubarak from Ibn Jurayj from Ibn Tawus) relates the same from his father; Ali ibn Dawud (9128, from Abdallah ibn Salih from Mu'awiya ibn Salih from Ali ibn Abi Talha) relates the same from Ibn Abbas. Abu Jafar adds that the particle there is in the nominative position, so the meaning is that refraining from marrying slave women is better for you.
These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 208}
