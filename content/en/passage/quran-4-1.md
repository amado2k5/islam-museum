## Summary
Verse 1 of [[passage/quran-4|Surat an-Nisa]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 1 of 176 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**The start of the commentary on the sura of al-Nisa (pp. 511-513).** Page 511 carries the title "Commentary on the sura of al-Nisa" and p. 512 has no text; p. 513 begins with the heading "The commentary on the sura in which women are mentioned", the basmala and the prayer for ease with which the copyist opens.

**Verse 1: one origin of creation (pp. 513-514).** Al-Tabari says the address to the people means: beware your Lord, lest you disobey Him in what He commanded and forbade, so that there falls on you from His punishment what you cannot withstand. God then described Himself as the One alone in creating all humankind from a single person, making known to His servants how the beginning of their coming into being from that one origin was, and alerting them that all of them are children of one man and one mother, that they are of one another, and that the right of each over the other is binding as a brother's right over his brother, because they meet in descent from one father and one mother; and that what binds them to respect one another's right, though the meeting in descent from their common father is distant, is like what binds them in the nearer kinship; and inclining them towards one another, so that they deal fairly and do not wrong each other, and the strong gives the weak his due with kindness as God obliged him to. In the editor's note the printed edition and the manuscript read "and He made known to His servants", and he concluded from al-Tabari's manner of exposition, and from his later "and alerting them" and then "and inclining", that the right reading is "making known", as the flow of the sentence requires, and that "inclining" is joined to "making known" and what follows. Al-Tabari then says the creation from the one origin is from Adam.

**Reports 8400-8402 (p. 514).** Al-Suddi (8400), through Muhammad ibn al-Husayn from Ahmad ibn Mufaddal from Asbat: as for the creation from the one origin, it is from Adam. Qatada (8401), through Bishr ibn Mu'adh from Yazid ibn Zuray' from Sa'id: he means Adam. Mujahid (8402), through Sufyan ibn Waki' from his father from Sufyan from a man: Adam. In two notes the editor says the printed edition added a formula of blessing on the Prophet after Adam's name in the first two reports and he set what the manuscript has.

**The feminine form of the word for "soul" (p. 514).** Al-Tabari says a parallel, where a man is meant, is the saying of the poet, in a line addressed to a praised man saying that his father was a caliph borne by another woman (in the feminine) while he means the man, using the feminine because of the word "caliph"; God used the feminine description here because the word for "soul" is feminine, the sense being "from one man"; had it been said in the masculine, giving the wording the masculine for the sense, it would have been right; the line is described and not reproduced. The note refers to the line and its tracing in volume 6, and says this is al-Farra's remark in Ma'ani al-Qur'an (1: 252).

**The creation of the mate (p. 515, reports 8403-8406).** Al-Tabari says the mate is the second of the pair for the one origin, and according to the interpreters she is his wife Eve. In the editor's note, for "mate" he refers to the earlier explanation and says that by "second" al-Tabari meant that "zawj" is the single one that has a partner, so that each of the two companions is called a "zawj", and then the wife of a man and the man who is the woman's partner are each called "zawj". Mujahid (8403), through Muhammad ibn 'Amr from Abu 'Asim from 'Isa from Ibn Abi Najih: Eve, from the short rib of Adam while he slept; he woke and said in Nabatean "atha", that is, a woman; and the editor says the short rib is the lowest of the ribs, or the rib next to the flank between the side and the belly. Mujahid (8404), through al-Muthanna from Abu Hudhayfa from Shibl from Ibn Abi Najih, the same. Qatada (8405), through Bishr ibn Mu'adh from Yazid from Sa'id: Eve, created from Adam, from one of his ribs. Al-Suddi (8406), through Musa ibn Harun from 'Amr ibn Hammad from Asbat: God settled Adam in paradise and he walked in it alone (that is, by himself, as the note says), having no mate to find rest in; he slept a sleep and woke to find a woman sitting by his head, whom He created ... and p. 515 breaks off here, with the text of the report continuing on p. 516. These statements are given as transmitted from the interpreters, and the museum gives no verdict.

**The creation of the mate, continued (p. 516, reports 8406-8409).** The close of al-Suddi's report 8406: God created her from his rib and asked her what she was; she said a woman; He asked why she was created; she said, so that he would find rest in her. The editor notes that this report appeared at length at no. 710 and that the printed edition read it with a lam, which he corrected to the manuscript and the earlier text. Ibn Ishaq (8407), through Ibn Humayd from Salama, in what reached them from the People of the Book, among the people of the Torah and other scholars, from 'Abd Allah ibn 'Abbas and others: God cast sleep on Adam, then took a rib from his left side and closed up its place, while Adam slept and did not stir until God had created his wife Eve from that rib and formed her as a woman for him to find rest in; when the sleep was lifted he woke, saw her beside him and said, as they claim and God knows, my flesh and my blood and my wife, and found rest in her. The notes explain the verb for closing up as mending the place until the cleft was gone, and say the earlier report no. 711 has "and closed its place with flesh" and that this report passed there with its tracing. Al-Suddi (8408), through Muhammad ibn al-Husayn from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal from Asbat: He made Eve from Adam.

**The scattering of many men and women (pp. 516-517, report 8409).** Al-Tabari says the scattering means that God spread out from Adam and Eve many men and women, whom people have seen, and he ties this to [[passage/quran-101-4|101:4]]; he gives the idiom that one says God scattered the creation. Two notes: the first refers to al-Farra's Ma'ani al-Qur'an (1: 252), the second to the earlier explanation of this root (3: 275). Al-Suddi (8409), through Muhammad ibn al-Husayn from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal from Asbat: scattering means creating.

**The reading and sense of the verb of mutual asking (pp. 517-518, reports 8410-8413).** Al-Tabari says the reciters differed: most Medinan and Basran reciters read it with a doubled sibilant, the original being a form with two ta letters of which one was assimilated into the sin; some Kufan reciters read it lightened, on the pattern of a different verbal form. He calls them two known readings and two eloquent dialects, and says whichever the reciter reads is right, since the sense does not differ. His interpretation: beware the God by whom, when one of you asks another, he asks, the asker saying to the one asked, I ask you by God, I adjure you by God, I charge you by God, and the like; as you honour your Lord with your tongues, so that you hold one who gave you his pledge and then betrayed it to have done something grave, so honour Him by obeying what He commanded and avoiding what He forbade, and beware His punishment. The note defines the verb for breaking a pledge. The reports: al-Dahhak (8410), through al-Muthanna from Ishaq from Abu Zuhayr from Juwaybir: the God by whom you make compacts and covenants. Al-Rabi' (8411), through al-Muthanna from Ishaq from Ibn Abi Ja'far from his father: the same sense. Al-Rabi' ibn Anas (8412), through al-Qasim from al-Husayn from Hajjaj from Ibn Abi Ja'far from his father: like it. Ibn 'Abbas (8413), through al-Qasim from al-Husayn from Hajjaj from Ibn Jurayj: the God by whom you show one another tenderness.

**The wombs, first view: asking by kinship (pp. 518-519, reports 8414-8420).** Al-Tabari says the interpreters differed over the word for the wombs. Some said the sense is: beware the God by whom you ask one another, the asker saying to the one asked, I ask you by Him and by kinship. Those who said it: Ibrahim al-Nakha'i (8414), through Ibn Humayd from Hukkam from 'Amr from Mansur, of a man asking by God and by kinship; Ibrahim (8415), through Ya'qub ibn Ibrahim from Hushaym from Mughira, similarly; Ibrahim (8416), through Muhammad ibn Bashshar from 'Abd al-Rahman from Sufyan from Mansur; Ibrahim (8417), through Abu Kurayb from Hushaym from Mughira; Mujahid (8418), through Ibn Bashshar from 'Abd al-Rahman from Sufyan from Ibn Abi Najih; Ibrahim (8419), through al-Muthanna from al-Himmani from Sharik from Mansur or Mughira; and al-Hasan (8420), through al-Muthanna from Suwayd from Ibn al-Mubarak from Ma'mar: it is a man's saying, I adjure you by God and by kinship.

**Al-Tabari's grammatical comment on the genitive reading (pp. 519-520).** Al-Tabari, writing this time "Muhammad said", says that on this interpretation is the reading of some who read the word for the wombs in the genitive, joined to the pronoun in the phrase "by Him", as if it meant by Him and by the wombs; this he calls not eloquent, since the Arabs do not join an overt noun to a pronoun in the genitive except of poetic necessity, because of the narrowness of verse, whereas in speech nothing forces a speaker to choose what is disliked and poor in inflection. He cites a line of poetry about swords hung like pillars and the space between them and the ankle, in which an overt noun is joined to a genitive pronoun; the line is described, not reproduced. In the editor's notes, "Muhammad said" means Muhammad ibn Jarir al-Tabari, and it is the first time he, his students or his copyists write it without the kunya "Abu Ja'far" (he refers to p. 569 below); the note glosses the word for joining and the word for the pronoun; says this is al-Farra's statement (1: 252-253), who gave the reading with its chain to Ibrahim al-Nakha'i and noted that it is Hamza's reading and others'; names the poet as Miskin al-Darimi and traces the line in al-Farra, al-Hayawan (6: 493-494), al-Insaf, al-Khizana (2: 338) and al-'Ayni, noting a variant in al-Hayawan and that both variants are right; glosses "pillars", "low ground" and "space between two things"; and describes the other lines of the poem, in which the poet boasts of his people and his descent from Adam and Eve, described here, not reproduced.

**Second view: beware the wombs lest you cut them (pp. 520-522, reports 8421-8434).** Others said the sense is: beware the God by whom you ask, and beware the wombs, that you cut them. Those who said it: al-Suddi (8421), through Muhammad ibn al-Husayn from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal from Asbat; Qatada (8422), through Bishr ibn Mu'adh from Yazid from Sa'id, who said it was mentioned to them that the Prophet used to say: beware God and keep your kinship ties, for it lasts longer for you in this world and is better for you in the next; Ibn 'Abbas (8423), through 'Ali ibn Dawud from 'Abd Allah ibn Salih from Mu'awiya ibn Salih from 'Ali ibn Abi Talha: and beware God in the matter of kinship, so join it; al-Hasan (8424), through Abu Kurayb from Hushaym from Mansur; 'Ikrima (8425), through Sufyan from his father from Sufyan from Khusayf: lest you cut them; al-Hasan (8426), through al-Hasan ibn Yahya from 'Abd al-Razzaq from Ma'mar: it is a man's saying, I adjure you by God and kinship (which agrees with the first view); Qatada (8427), through al-Hasan ibn Yahya from 'Abd al-Razzaq from Ma'mar, that the Prophet said: beware God and join kinship; Mujahid (8428), through al-Muthanna from Abu Hudhayfa from Shibl from Ibn Abi Najih; al-Dahhak (8429), through al-Muthanna from Ishaq from Abu Zuhayr from Juwaybir; al-Rabi' (8430), through al-Muthanna from Ishaq from Ibn Abi Ja'far from his father; al-Dahhak (8431), through al-Muthanna from Ishaq from 'Abd al-Rahman ibn Abi Hammad, and Abu Ja'far al-Khazzaz told us from Juwaybir, that Ibn 'Abbas used to read the word and said: do not cut them; Ibn 'Abbas (8432), through al-Qasim from al-Husayn from Hajjaj from Ibn Jurayj; al-Rabi' (8433), through al-Qasim from al-Husayn from Hajjaj from Ibn Abi Ja'far from his father: that you cut them; and Ibn Zayd (8434), through Yunus from Ibn Wahb, who recited [[passage/quran-13-21|13:21]].

**Al-Tabari's choice (pp. 522-523).** Abu Ja'far says that on this interpretation those who read the word in the accusative read it, meaning beware the wombs lest you cut them, joined to the name of God the Exalted; and that the reading he does not allow any reciter to depart from is the accusative, because the Arabs join an overt noun to a genitive pronoun only of poetic necessity. In the note the manuscript and printed edition read the reciter with the definite article, which the editor corrects, and he refers to pp. 519-520 above. This is al-Tabari's choice and argument.

**The one who keeps watch (pp. 523-524, reports 8435 and 8435m).** Al-Tabari says the sense is that God has always been watching over you, the addressees being the people he addressed at the start of the verse; and that when one addressed and one absent are joined in a statement, the Arabs put the speech in the form of address, saying, of one man or a group who did something with others absent, you did such and such. The watcher is the guardian who counts up your deeds and looks after your keeping of the sanctity of your kinship, your joining it, your cutting it and your neglect of its sanctity. Mujahid (8435), through al-Muthanna from Abu Hudhayfa from Shibl from Ibn Abi Najih: a guardian; Ibn Zayd (8435m, numbered with an m because it is an addition after 8435), through Yunus from Ibn Wahb: over your deeds, knowing and recognising them. Al-Tabari then cites a line by Abu Du'ad al-Iyadi, completed on p. 524, which likens the projecting dewclaws of a white bull to the watchers sitting by the gamblers while the players stretch out their hands; the line is described, not reproduced. The editor traces it in Tahdhib al-Alfaz (475), al-Ma'ani al-Kabir (1148), Majaz al-Qur'an (1: 113) and al-Maysir wa-l-Qidah (133), says the lines describe the white bull, explains the dewclaws as what projects behind the bull's hoof, the watchers as those who guard the casting of the arrows, and the players as those who cast them, and says Ibn Qutayba claimed the hands were the players' and erred, in the editor's view, since the watchers' hands are meant. These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 524}
