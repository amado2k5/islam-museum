## Summary
Verse 107 of [[passage/quran-5|Surat al-Ma'ida]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 107 of 120 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

Al-Tabari explains "if it is found" as: if something is learned of them or appears. The root meaning of "athar" is to come upon a thing or fall on it; so one says that someone's finger stumbled on something when it struck it and landed on it. He cites a line of al-A'sha Maymun ibn Qays, who describes his she-camel as strong and lively and denies she could stumble in her speed; if she did, a prayer that she fall would suit her better than a prayer that she recover. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 179}

The editor gives the line's place in al-A'sha's Diwan, p. 83, in his poem to Hawdha ibn Ali al-Hanafi, adds the two preceding lines on a land that is fearful at night, and explains the words: "lawth" is strength, "ifranat" a strong she-camel whose energy seems like madness, "tas" is stumbling or falling, and "la'an" a word said to one who has stumbled, that he may rise. He corrects the printed "in both" to "from them".

Al-Tabari adds that "she stumbled" means the tip of her foot struck a stone or something else, and the word is then used for any coming upon something that was hidden, as in the proverb about a woman who came upon the spinning late and left no scrap of wool in Najd unspun, meaning she came upon it. He then explains "that they had deserved sin": if it is found, of the two executors whose case God stated, after they swore by God that they would not sell their oaths for a price, even for a relative, nor conceal God's testimony, that they had incurred sin by their oaths, namely it is found that they lied in swearing that they had not betrayed, replaced or changed; if it appears that they took something from the dead man's property or changed his will or replaced it and so sinned in their oath by their Lord, then "two others stand in their place", that is, from the dead man's heirs, the two nearer in right. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 180}

On p. 180 the editor explains the proverb of the woman who neglects the spinning and then gleans scraps of wool, refers to Abu Ubayda's Majaz al-Qur'an and the proverbs of al-Maydani and Abu Hilal al-Askari, and says the version in Lisan al-Arab with a different verb offers no support. He glosses "minsam" as the tip of a camel's foot, corrects a printed phrase, and refers for "sinning in one's Lord" to his earlier notes in vols. 4 and 6.

Al-Tabari says the interpreters spoke along these lines. Report 12959, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr: if a man is in a land of polytheism and leaves his will to two of the People of the Book, they swear after the afternoon prayer; if after their oath it is found that they betrayed something, the dead man's guardians swear that it was such and such, and then take it. Report 12960, from Ibrahim, is the same. Report 12961, from Ibn Abbas: "two others from other than you" are non-Muslims; "you detain them after the prayer", and if their testimony is doubted they swear after the prayer by God that they did not sell their testimony for a small price. If the guardians find that the two unbelievers lied, two of the guardians stand up and swear by God that the unbelievers' testimony is false and that they themselves have not transgressed; the unbelievers' testimony is then set aside and the guardians' accepted. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 181}

Report 12962, from Qatada: "if it is found" means it is learned of them that they betrayed, lied or concealed. Al-Tabari then begins to set out how the interpreters differed on the reason for which God ruled against the two witnesses. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 181}

Al-Tabari sets out the interpreters' differences over why the two witnesses were put under oath and why the oath then passed to others once it was found that they had earned sin. Some said: the oath was laid on them if their testimony about the dead man's will was doubted, because he had willed something not allowed under the rule of Islam, as when they testify that he willed his whole estate or willed that one child be favoured with part of it. Report 12963, from Ibn Abbas: "just men from among you" are of the people of Islam and "two others from other than you" are not; they swear by God after the prayer; if they swear to something contrary to the shares God sent down, meaning the two who are not Muslims, then two others from the dead man's guardians swear by God that our companion would never have willed this, or that the two are liars and our testimony is truer than theirs. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 182}

The editor notes that the manuscript and the printed text differ over a word in this passage and chooses what fits the context, corrects the reading "for other than what is allowed", and explains "the shares" as the inheritance shares.

Report 12964, from al-Suddi: the two men are stood after their prayer in their own religion and swear by God that they will not sell it for a price nor conceal God's testimony, and that this is the will and estate of your companion. When they have testified and the imam has allowed their testimony, he tells the dead man's guardians to go and ask about them: if you find betrayal in them or anyone who criticises them, we will reject their testimony. The guardians go and ask; if they find someone who criticises them, or they are not approved among them, or it is learned that they betrayed some of the money found with them, the guardians come and testify before the imam and swear by God that their testimony that the two are traitors, suspect in their religion, is truer than the two's testimony, and that they have not transgressed. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 183}

The editor keeps "the guardians came and testified" because the context requires it and notes a variant in the manuscript in one phrase.

Others said: the oath was laid on the two witnesses because they claimed that he had willed them part of the property, and it passes to the others for that reason when the heirs doubt their claim. Report 12965, from Yahya ibn Ya'mar, on "you detain them after the prayer and they swear by God": they claimed he willed them such and such; "they have earned sin" means by their claim for themselves; "two others stand in their place" and swear that our companion made no bequest to you of what you say. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 183}

Al-Tabari then gives what he holds to be correct: the witnesses were put under oath because the dead man's heirs suspected them regarding the property he had handed to them and claimed a betrayal of a sum of known amount; the oath then passes to the heirs when the suspicion the heirs had of them appears and is found sound through a witness's testimony against both or one of them, so the heir swears alongside the witness; or when the witnesses admit part or all of what the heir claimed against them and then assert, about what they admitted, something that cannot be accepted without proof, and they have no proof, so the oath passes to the dead man's guardians. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 184}

His reasoning is that he knows of no ruling of Islam by which an oath falls on witnesses, doubted or not, that this testimony could be likened to; nor is it sound from a report from the Messenger or by consensus of the community. A statement that is neither a principle nor like a principle in what the community disputes is plainly wrong. Worse still is the view that they were put under oath because they claimed a bequest from the dead man's property, since scholars do not differ that if someone claims a bequest from a dead man's property, the heirs' word with their oaths prevails over the claimant's with his oath when he has no proof, whereas God placed the oath in this verse on the witnesses when they are doubted. He holds that what he says is the interpretation that reports from some Companions bring, that the Messenger of God ruled by it when this verse was revealed, between those about whom it was revealed. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 184}

On p. 184 the editor clarifies the construction of the clause "nor, if we do not find it so", which is a parenthesis, notes that the printed text altered the manuscript, and explains that "worse" means worse than the previous view.

Report 12966, from Ibn Abbas: a man of Banu Sahm set out with Tamim al-Dari and Adi ibn Badda'. The Sahmi died in a land where there was no Muslim. When the two brought his estate, they were found to lack a silver bowl overlaid with gold; the Messenger of God made them swear. The bowl was then found in Mecca, and they said they had bought it from Tamim and Adi. Two of the Sahmi's guardians stood and swore that their testimony was truer than the two's and that the bowl belonged to their companion; the verse was revealed about them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 185}

The editor explains "jam" as a silver vessel and "mukhawwas" as plated with gold in the shape of palm leaves. He gives the biography of Muhammad ibn Abi al-Qasim al-Tawil of Kufa, the authentication by Ibn Ma'in and Abu Hatim and al-Bukhari's remark on him, and of Abd al-Malik ibn Sa'id ibn Jubayr; he identifies Tamim al-Dari as Tamim ibn Aws al-Lakhmi, named for an ancestor al-Dar, and Adi ibn Badda' as a Christian who is said to have become Muslim but whom Ibn Hajar in al-Isaba concludes died a Christian. He traces the report to al-Bukhari's Sahih, Abu Dawud, al-Bayhaqi, al-Nahhas, al-Jassas, al-Tirmidhi, Ibn Kathir and al-Suyuti.

Report 12967, from Tamim al-Dari: the people are clear of this verse except me and Adi ibn Badda'. They were Christians who went to and fro to Syria before Islam, and they came there to trade. A freedman of Banu Sahm called Budayl ibn Abi Maryam arrived with merchandise and a silver bowl intended for the king, the greater part of his trade; he fell ill and left his will with them, telling them to deliver what he left to his family. Tamim said: when he died we took the bowl, sold it for a thousand dirhams and shared it between us. When we reached his family we handed over what we had, they missed the bowl and asked about it, and we said he left nothing else and gave us nothing else. Tamim added that after he became Muslim, once the Messenger of God had come to Medina, he felt the sin of it; he went to the family, told them what had happened, paid them five hundred dirhams and told them his companion had the same. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 186}

They leapt on Adi and brought him to the Messenger of God, who asked them for proof and they had none; he told them to make him swear by what is held great among his religion, and he swore. Then God sent down the verses from [[passage/quran-5-106|5:106]] to "oaths be refuted after their oaths". Amr ibn al-As and another of them stood and swore, and the five hundred were taken from Adi ibn Badda'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 187}

In the notes on pp. 185 to 187 the editor observes that report 12967 comes through Abu al-Nadr, who is Muhammad ibn al-Sa'ib al-Kalbi, judged very weak, and Badhan, freedman of Umm Hani, whom the copies wrongly give as Zadhan; that al-Tirmidhi called it a strange report whose chain is not sound; that the name of the Sahmi freedman is variously given as Budayl, Buzayl and Burayl; he corrects the name of al-Hasan ibn Ahmad al-Harrani, restores the missing sentence in brackets from al-Nahhas's al-Nasikh wa-l-Mansukh, and glosses "he felt the sin" as he was troubled by it.

Report 12968, from Qatada, Ibn Sirin and Ikrima, whose accounts run into one another: Adi and Tamim al-Dari, both of Lakhm and Christians, traded to Mecca in the pre-Islamic period; when the Messenger of God emigrated they moved their trade to Medina. Ibn Abi Maria, a freedman of Amr ibn al-As, came to Medina intending to trade in Syria, and they all set out together. Part way he fell ill, wrote his will in his own hand, slipped it among his goods and left his affairs with the two. When he died they opened his goods and took what they wished, then came to his family and handed over what they chose. The family opened the goods, found his writing, his covenant and what he had set out with, and missed something, and asked them about it; they said, this is what we took in charge for him and what was given to us. The family asked: did he sell anything or buy anything? No. Did he use up any of his goods? No. Did he trade... {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 188}

We stop here at the foot of p. 188, in the middle of report 12968 from Qatada, Ibn Sirin and Ikrima on the story of Tamim and Adi, at the family's question "did he trade", which continues on p. 189.

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
