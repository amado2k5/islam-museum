## Summary
Verse 107 of [[passage/quran-5|Surat al-Ma'ida]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 107 of 120 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

Al-Tabari explains "if it is found" as: if something is learned of them or appears. The root meaning of "athar" is to come upon a thing or fall on it; so one says that someone's finger stumbled on something when it struck it and landed on it. He cites a line of al-A'sha Maymun ibn Qays, who describes his she-camel as strong and lively and denies she could stumble in her speed; if she did, a prayer that she fall would suit her better than a prayer that she recover. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 179}

The editor gives the line's place in al-A'sha's Diwan, p. 83, in his poem to Hawdha ibn Ali al-Hanafi, adds the two preceding lines on a land that is fearful at night, and explains the words: "lawth" is strength, "ifranat" a strong she-camel whose energy seems like madness, "tas" is stumbling or falling, and "la'an" a word said to one who has stumbled, that he may rise. He corrects the printed "in both" to "from them".

Al-Tabari adds that "she stumbled" means the tip of her foot struck a stone or something else, and the word is then used for any coming upon something that was hidden, as in the proverb about a woman who came upon the spinning late and left no scrap of wool in Najd unspun, meaning she came upon it. He then explains "that they had deserved sin": if it is found, of the two executors whose case God stated, after they swore by God that they would not sell their oaths for a price, even for a relative, nor conceal God's testimony, that they had incurred sin by their oaths, namely it is found that they lied in swearing that they had not betrayed, replaced or changed; if it appears that they took something from the dead man's property or changed his will or replaced it and so sinned in their oath by their Lord, then "two others stand in their place", that is, from the dead man's heirs, the two nearer in right. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 180}

On p. 180 the editor explains the proverb of the woman who neglects the spinning and then gleans scraps of wool, refers to Abu Ubayda's Majaz al-Qur'an and the proverbs of al-Maydani and Abu Hilal al-Askari, and says the version in Lisan al-Arab with a different verb offers no support. He glosses "minsam" as the tip of a camel's foot, corrects a printed phrase, and refers for "sinning in one's Lord" to his earlier notes in vols. 4 and 6.

Al-Tabari says the interpreters spoke along these lines. Report 12959, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr: if a man is in a land of polytheism and leaves his will to two of the People of the Book, they swear after the afternoon prayer; if after their oath it is found that they betrayed something, the dead man's guardians swear that it was such and such, and then take it. Report 12960, from Ibrahim, is the same. Report 12961, from Ibn Abbas: "two others from other than you" are non-Muslims; "you detain them after the prayer", and if their testimony is doubted they swear after the prayer by God that they did not sell their testimony for a small price. If the guardians find that the two unbelievers lied, two of the guardians stand up and swear by God that the unbelievers' testimony is false and that they themselves have not transgressed; the unbelievers' testimony is then set aside and the guardians' accepted. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 181}

Report 12962, from Qatada: "if it is found" means it is learned of them that they betrayed, lied or concealed. Al-Tabari then begins to set out how the interpreters differed on the reason for which God ruled against the two witnesses. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 181}

We stop here at the foot of p. 181, at the start of the account of how the interpreters differed on the reason for God's ruling against the two witnesses, which continues on p. 182.

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
