## Summary
Verse 133 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 133 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**The meaning (pp. 207-208).** Abu Ja'far says the sense of hastening is to hurry and compete, and the editor refers to its earlier explanation (7: 130); the call to forgiveness means to what covers the sins from His mercy and veils them by His pardon from punishment; and to a garden whose breadth is like that of the heavens and the earth. It was said the sense is like the breadth of the seven heavens and seven earths when put together. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 207}
- From Muhammad ibn al-Husayn, Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal, Asbat, al-Suddi, who said Ibn Abbas said (7830): the seven heavens and seven earths are joined as garments are joined to one another, and that is the breadth of Paradise. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 207}

**The breadth described by comparison (pp. 207-208).** Abu Ja'far says its breadth is described by the heavens and the earth while the meaning is that it is likened to their breadth in vastness and greatness, as elsewhere it is said that the raising of creation is like the raising of one soul, meaning like the raising of a single soul, with the first noun left out. He gives two lines of poetry for this idiom. The editor names the poet of the first as Shuqayq ibn Juz' al-Bahili (also wrongly ascribed to A'sha Bahila and to al-Nabigha), where the beaten men are compared to ostriches crying in a barren land, meaning the state of ostriches; in a long note he narrates Shuqayq's raid on Banu Dabba and explains the words and places in his lines. The second is by Dhu al-Khirq al-Tuhawi (traced and explained earlier, 3: 103), who says he took the cry of his she-camel for the cry of a young goat when it was not, meaning the cry of a young goat. Abu Ja'far counts this as an omitted first noun. Then he says that the Messenger was asked, and the report continues on p. 209. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 208}

**The end of the report on the question about Paradise and the Fire (p. 209).** The report begun on the previous page ends with the question: if this Paradise is as wide as the heavens and the earth, where is the Fire? The Prophet answers: when day comes, where is the night? {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 209}

**Reports from the Messenger and others on this question (pp. 209-212).**
- From Yunus, Ibn Wahb, Muslim ibn Khalid, Ibn Khuthaym, Sa'id ibn Abi Rashid, Ya'la ibn Murra (7831): I met al-Tanukhi, Heraclius's envoy to the Prophet at Homs, a very old man. He said he came to the Prophet with Heraclius's letter and the Prophet handed the sheet to a man on his left, who he was told was Muawiya. In the letter of the envoy's master was the line asking why the Prophet invited him to a garden as wide as the heavens and the earth, and where then the Fire is. The Prophet said: Glory to God, and where is the night when day comes? {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 209}
- From Ibn Bashshar, Abd al-Rahman ibn Mahdi, Sufyan, Qays ibn Muslim, Tariq ibn Shihab (7832): some Jews asked Umar ibn al-Khattab where the Fire is, given such a wide Paradise. He said: if night comes, can day be there? They said: by God, you have drawn something like it out of the Torah. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 211}
- From Ibn al-Muthanna, Muhammad ibn Ja'far, Shu'ba, Qays ibn Muslim, Tariq ibn Shihab (7833): three men of Najran came to Umar while his companions were with him and asked this; the people held back, and Umar asked where day goes when night comes and night when day comes. They said: you have drawn its like from the Torah. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 211}
- From Ibn al-Muthanna, Muhammad ibn Ja'far, Shu'ba, Ibrahim ibn Muhajir, Tariq ibn Shihab, from Umar, to the same effect about the three men who came and questioned him, like the report of Qays ibn Muslim (7834). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 211}
- From Mujahid ibn Musa, Ja'far ibn Awn, al-A'mash, Qays ibn Muslim, Tariq ibn Shihab (7835): a Jew came to Umar asking where the Fire would be; Umar answered with the night and the day. The man said it is the same in the Torah; his companion asked why he had told him, and the man said: leave him, he is certain of everything. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 212}
- From Ahmad ibn Hazim, Abu Nu'aym, Ja'far ibn Burqan, Yazid ibn al-Asamm (7836): a man of the People of the Book came to Ibn Abbas with the same question, and Ibn Abbas answered by asking where day is when night comes and where night is when day comes. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 212}

**The editor's notes on these reports (pp. 209-212).** Shakir records that the printed text read "qad uq'ida" and Ibn Kathir "qad fasada" for the description of al-Tanukhi; he judges the right reading to be "fundina", meaning attributed to dotage (fanad, loss of mind from old age), though he says the intent is only extreme old age, and he gives the Musnad wording that he had reached dotage or come near it. He says the printed edition tried to correct the manuscript's phrase about the letter, and that the reading of Ibn Kathir and Ahmad is the right one. He weakens the chain of 7831 because of Muslim ibn Khalid al-Zanji al-Makki, the teacher of al-Shafi'i, whom he calls truthful but often mistaken in what he narrates, and whom al-Bukhari called "munkar al-hadith". He then spends a long note on Sa'id ibn Abi Rashid: from Tahdhib al-Tahdhib he quotes that he narrated from Ya'la ibn Murra al-Thaqafi and from al-Tanukhi, the Christian envoy of Caesar, and that Ibn Hibban listed him among the trustworthy; he sets out a marginal note printed in that work saying the man accepted Islam late and that another narrator, Abu Muhammad al-Mazini the fish-seller, is among the weak, argues that these are two separate notes, refers to the entries on the fish-seller in al-Bukhari's al-Kabir, Ibn Abi Hatim, al-Dhahabi's Mizan, the Lisan al-Mizan and Ibn Hibban's al-Majruhin, and concludes that the Sa'id of this chain is the Companion who narrated from al-Tanukhi. He holds that the mention of Ya'la ibn Murra in this chain was wrongly inserted. Of al-Tanukhi he says he found no biography; if it is right that he became Muslim after meeting the Prophet, he is not counted a Companion, since he was not Muslim when he saw him, but his report is acceptable because he was Muslim when he transmitted it (he refers to Tadrib al-Rawi). He says the report is part of a long story in Ahmad's Musnad (15719) through Ishaq ibn Isa, Yahya ibn Salim al-Ta'ifi and Ibn Khuthaym, in which Sa'id ibn Abi Rashid himself met al-Tanukhi, who was his neighbour; Ibn Kathir reproduced it in his history and called it gharib with a chain that is not bad; copies of the Musnad read Yahya ibn Sulayman by a copyists' slip. Because Ahmad testified that Yahya ibn Salim had mastered the reports of Ibn Khuthaym, Shakir judges the addition "from Ya'la ibn Murra" in al-Tabari's chain to be a mistake, most likely Muslim ibn Khalid's. He adds that Ibn Kathir and al-Suyuti cite al-Tabari's report. On 7832 he notes that the printed text read "mithlihi", the manuscript another word, and that "naza'a" means to draw out and deduce. On 7836 he vouches for Ja'far ibn Burqan al-Kilabi al-Jazari and for Yazid ibn al-Asamm, a trustworthy Successor and a son of Ibn Abbas's maternal aunt, corrects the printed form of his name, and judges the chain to Ibn Abbas sound as his own statement. He also traces Yazid's report from Abu Hurayra raised to the Prophet: a man asked where the Fire is, and the Prophet asked him where this night has gone, now that it has been and nothing is left of it; the man said God knows, and the Prophet said God does what He wills. He cites it from Ibn Hibban's Sahih, al-Hakim's Mustadrak (who called it sound on the condition of the two sheikhs, with al-Dhahabi agreeing) and al-Bazzar (of whom al-Haythami said his men are those of the Sahih), and notes that Ibn Kathir took the report stopping at Ibn Abbas from this place in al-Tabari and al-Suyuti attributed it to him and to Abd ibn Humayd. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 210}

**The preparing for the God-fearing (p. 213).** Abu Ja'far says he means that the Garden, as wide as the seven heavens and seven earths, God prepared for those who feared Him and obeyed what He commanded and forbade, who did not overstep His limits and did not fall short of the duty owed to Him so as to waste it.
- From Ibn Humayd, Salama, Ibn Ishaq (7837): that is, an abode for whoever obeyed Me and My Messenger. The editor traces it to the Sira of Ibn Hisham (3: 115) and says the printed text read "that" where he preferred Ibn Hisham's wording, since "abode" and "that" are close in script. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 213}
