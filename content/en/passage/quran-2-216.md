## In al-Tabari's commentary

On "fighting has been prescribed for you, though it is hateful to you": it means fighting the polytheists has been made obligatory for you. The scholars differed over who was meant by the prescription; some said the Companions of the Messenger of God specifically; from Ibn Jurayj: I asked 'Ata' whether campaigning is obligatory on people because of it, and he said no, it was prescribed for those at that time (4072). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 295} From Ibn 'Abbas: "they said, we hear and obey" (2:285) abrogated it (4073); al-Tabari rejects this as meaningless, because abrogation of rulings comes from God, not from servants, and "we hear and obey" is a report of what His believing servants said, not an abrogation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 296} From Abu Ishaq al-Fazari: I asked al-Awza'i whether campaigning is obligatory on all people, and he said, I do not know that, but it is not fitting for imams and the public to abandon it, though for an individual in himself it is not (4074). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 296} Others said it is binding on every individual until enough of them undertake it and the duty falls from the rest of the Muslims, like prayer over the dead, washing and burying them, and this is the view of most Muslim scholars; al-Tabari says that is correct in his view because of the agreement of authority on it and because God said "God has preferred those who strive with their wealth and persons over those who sit, by a degree, and to each God promised the best" (4:95); had those sitting been neglecting a duty, theirs would be the worst, not the best. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 296} Others said it is a binding obligation on Muslims until the Hour; from Dawud ibn Abi 'Asim: I said to Sa'id ibn al-Musayyab, I know that campaigning is obligatory on people; he was silent, and I know that had he disagreed with what I said he would have explained it to me (4075). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 297}

On "and it is hateful to you": it means it is a thing of hatefulness to you, "dhu" being omitted because "hateful" shows it, as in "ask the town" (12:82); from 'Ata': it was made hateful to you then (4076). "Kurh" with damma is what a man takes on himself without compulsion, and "karh" with fatha what another imposes on him, which is the view of Mu'adh ibn Muslim: "kurh" is hardship and "karh" is compulsion (4077). Some grammarians said they are two dialectal forms of one meaning, like "ghusl/ghasl", "du'f/da'f", while others said the damma form is the noun and the fatha form the verbal noun. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 297} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 298}

On "it may be that you hate a thing and it is good for you, and it may be that you love a thing and it is bad for you": it means do not hate fighting, for you may hate it and it be good for you, and do not love abandoning jihad, for you may love it and it be bad for you; from al-Suddi: the Muslims disliked fighting, so He said it may be that you hate a thing that is good for you, for in fighting there is booty, victory and martyrdom, whereas in sitting there is no victory over the polytheists, no martyrdom and no gain (4078). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 298} Al-Tabari also reports (4079) from Ibn 'Abbas a raised report about contentment with what God decreed, saying it is set down in this verse; the editor says its chain is obscure and the text rejected, that he found no biography of two narrators in it, and that he found the wording nowhere else. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 298} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 299} On "God knows and you do not know": it means God knows what is good for you from what is bad, so do not hate what I prescribed for you of fighting your enemy, for fighting them is better for you in this life and the next and abandoning it is worse, and you do not know of that what I know, urging them thereby to fight His enemies. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 299}
