**Commentary on verse 69 (pp. 381-386)**

On [[passage/quran-11-69|11:69]] al-Tabari says: 'Our messengers came', of the angels, who were, it is reported, Gabriel and two other angels, and it is said the two others were Michael and Israfil with him, 'to Abraham', meaning Abraham the friend of God, 'with the glad tidings'. They differed on those glad tidings: some said it was the tidings of Isaac, others that it was the news of the destruction of Lot's people. 'They said, peace' means they greeted him with a greeting, 'salaman' being accusative through 'they said' governing it, as if it were 'they said a saying and greeted'; 'he said, peace' means Abraham said peace to them, in the nominative meaning 'peace be upon you' or 'peace from you'. It is reported that the Arabs say 'silm' for peace, as they say 'hill' and 'halal' and 'hirm' and 'haram', and al-Farra' cited a line from some Arab in this; the commentary on the verse continues on the next page. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 382}

...in the sense of 'peace'; 'as it was clouded' is also reported. Some claimed that if read so the sense is 'we are at peace with you', from peaceful dealing, which is the opposite of fighting, and that is the reading of most Kufan readers. Most readers of the Hijaz and Basra read 'they said, peace; he said, peace', as Abraham's reply to them in the like of their greeting, 'peace be upon you'. Al-Tabari says the right view for him is that they are two readings close in meaning, since 'silm' may mean 'salam' and 'salam' may mean 'silm', for greeting hardly occurs except between those at peace and not between enemies, and each is read by people of authority among the readers, so whichever the reader reads he is right. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 383}

His words 'he soon brought a roasted calf' — 'hanidh' is originally 'mahnudh', turned from the passive participle to the 'fa'il' form. The people of Arabic differed on its meaning. A Basran, Abu Ubayda, said 'mahnudh' is roasted, and one says 'I hanadhtu my horse' meaning I warmed it and made it sweat, citing a rajaz of al-Ajjaj on wild asses; another said 'hanadha his horse' means he made it lean, and they said 'hanadhahu hanadhan' means made it sweat; some Kufans said anything roasted in the earth, when you dig a pit for it and bury and cover it, is the 'hanidh' and 'mahnudh', and horses are 'hanadh' when blankets are put one on another to make them sweat. As to the interpreters, report 18297, from Ibn Abbas: 'hanidh' is well-cooked; report 18298, from Mujahid: 'a calf', a young of the cow, and 'hanidh' is roasted, well-cooked; report 18299, from him: well-cooked, hot, cooked with stones. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 384}

Report 18300, from Qatada: 'hanidh' is well-cooked; report 18301, from him: cooked, and al-Kalbi said 'hanidh' is what is baked in the earth; report 18302, from Shamir: 'hanidh' is what drips water, having been roasted, and Hafs said it is like the 'hinadh' of horses; report 18303, from al-Suddi: he slaughtered it and roasted it on heated stones, and it is the 'hanidh' when he roasted it; report 18304, from Shamir b. Atiyya: the roasted that drips; report 18305, likewise; report 18306, from al-Dahhak: well-cooked; report 18307, from him: cooked with stones; report 18308, from Sufyan: roasted; report 18309, from Wahb b. Munabbih: roasted; report 18310, from Ibn Ishaq: 'hinadh' is cooking. Al-Tabari says these sayings of the people of Arabic and the interpreters are close in meaning one to another, and the place of 'an' in 'an ja'a bi-'ijlin hanidh' is accusative, governed by 'he soon' (fa-ma labitha). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 386}
