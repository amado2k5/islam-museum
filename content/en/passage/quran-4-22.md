## Summary
Verse 22 of [[passage/quran-4|Surat an-Nisa]], a Medinan surah. Scholars connect its revelation to [[concept/jahili-marriage-forms|the four forms of marriage in the Jahiliyya]], and [[concept/social-condition-jahiliyya|the social condition of the Arabs before Islam]]. {cite:tanzil-quran} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 22 of 176 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**Occasion of the verse (pp. 132-135, reports 8938-8942).** Abu Jafar says it was reported that this verse came down about people who used to succeed to their fathers' wives; Islam came while they were so, and God forbade them to stay with them and pardoned what had passed in their Jahiliyya and idolatry if they feared God in Islam. Muhammad ibn Abdallah al-Makhrumi (8938, from Qurad, Ibn Uyayna and Amr) reports from Ikrima, from Ibn Abbas, that the people of the Jahiliyya held forbidden what is forbidden except a father's wife and taking two sisters together as wives, and God sent down the prohibition of both. Ibn Bashshar (8939, from Abd al-Ala, Said) reports something similar from Qatada. Al-Qasim (8940, from al-Husayn, Hajjaj, Ibn Jurayj) reports from Ikrima that it came down about Abu Qays ibn al-Aslat, who succeeded Umm Ubayd bint Sakhr, who had been his father's wife; about al-Aswad ibn Khalaf, who succeeded a woman who had been his father Khalaf's; about Fakhita bint al-Aswad ibn al-Muttalib, who had been the wife of Umayya ibn Khalaf and was succeeded by Safwan ibn Umayya; and about Manzur ibn Zabban, who succeeded Mulayka bint Kharija, who had been his father Zabban ibn Sayyar's wife. Al-Qasim (8941, same chain) reports that Ibn Jurayj asked Ata ibn Abi Rabah about a man who marries a woman and does not see her until he divorces her, whether she is lawful for his son; Ata said she is "mursala" and recited the prohibition, and explained the exception as the sons marrying their fathers' wives in the Jahiliyya. Al-Muthanna (8942, from Abdallah ibn Salih, Muawiya ibn Salih, Ali ibn Abi Talha) reports from Ibn Abbas that every woman your father or son married, whether he consummated or not, is forbidden to you. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 132} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 133} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 135} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 136}

**The editor's notes on these reports (pp. 133-135).** At 8938 he refers to al-Makhrumi's entry at 3730 and says Qurad is a nickname of Abd al-Rahman ibn Ghazwan. At 8940 he says the manuscript and printed text read "bint Damra" and that the right form from the sources is "bint Sakhr", that the printed text garbled "Zabban" which the manuscript leaves undotted, and that the second father's wife's name is Hunayna bint Abi Talha, a diminutive of Hamna. He then goes at length into the report. Ibn al-Athir gave it in the entry on Umm Ubayd bint Sakhr from Abu Musa al-Isfahani's supplement to Ibn Manda, and Ibn Hajar pointed to it in al-Isaba; report 8873 had Abu Qays ibn al-Aslat succeed Kubaysha bint Ma'n, his father's wife, so he fears the two reports together make him succeed two of his father's wives. He notes that al-Wahidi in Asbab al-Nuzul (p. 109) and al-Thalabi say it came down about Husn ibn Abi Qays, who married his father's wife Kubaysha; that Ibn Hajar (al-Isaba 5:252) gives it from al-Firyabi and Ibn Abi Hatim by Adi ibn Thabit and adds that its chain has Qays ibn al-Rabi from Ashath ibn Sawwar, both weak, and that it is broken; that Ibn al-Kalbi names the woman Kubaysha bint Ma'n while Muqatil gives the story to Qays; and that Abu al-Faraj al-Isfahani hints Qays was killed in the Jahiliyya. The editor says this needs long study and he only points to it. He explains his choice of "Sakhr" because her brother is named Jarwal ibn Malik ibn Amr ibn Aziz (Jamharat al-Ansab p. 315), jarwal being a stone, which fits Arab naming habit, and because he found no Ansari woman named Damra; he notes that Ibn Kathir (2:388) gives another name but that he does not trust Ibn Kathir's copying here, and that Ibn Hajar quoted Sayf's commentary, then gave a separate entry for this "Damra" and said al-Tabari named her, which the editor calls a muddle since al-Tabari does not, and takes as one sign of Ibn Hajar's haste in writing al-Isaba and of the saying that it remained a draft. He says al-Aswad ibn Khalaf is al-Aswad ibn Khalaf ibn Asad ibn Amir ibn Bayada al-Khuzai, not the al-Aswad ibn Khalaf ibn Abd Yaghuth of Ibn Hajar and Ibn Sad, the brother of Abdallah ibn Khalaf, father of Talha al-Talhat, and that Ibn Hajar and Ibn al-Athir record the report in the woman's entry but not the man's, and garble the grandfather's name. On Manzur ibn Zabban he reports a dispute that Ibn Hajar settled in his own entry and in Mulayka's: the story took place in Umar's time, Umar separated them, Manzur loved her and was pained and composed a verse (the editor quotes a line protesting against the one who parts them by force; it is not reproduced here), and the story is in al-Aghani (16:194). This note ends on p. 135. On 8941 he says on p. 135 that the word is "mursala" in the manuscript, the printed text, 8957 and al-Durr al-Manthur; that the dictionaries give "a woman who is mirsal" with close senses (one her husband parted from in any way, or whose husband died or who senses he will divorce her and adorns herself for another, or who has been divorced several times, or who sends messages to suitors; the philologists say one who has aged but kept some youth); that he combines them as a woman past youth whose husband died or divorced her, more in need of adornment and of messaging suitors; that a report has a man of the Ansar marrying a previously married woman and the Prophet saying "why not a virgin?"; and that here, if "mursala" is right, it means one her husband sent away, a divorced virgin who counts in law as previously married, and if the right form is "mirsal" its meaning should be extended to the divorced virgin. He refers to 8957. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 133} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 134} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 135}

**Meaning of the exception that closes the prohibition (p. 136).** Abu Jafar says the interpreters differed on the exception. Some said: but what has passed, leave it, a disconnected exception. Others said: do not marry as your fathers married, on corrupt grounds that are not permitted in Islam, for their marriages were shameful, hated and an evil road, except what passed in the Jahiliyya of the kind whose beginning is not allowed in Islam, which is pardoned; they said the prohibition is like saying "do not do what I did", meaning "do not do as I did". Others said: do not marry women your fathers validly married, except what happened between your fathers and women by what they counted as fornication, those women being lawful for you because they were never lawful wives to your fathers and what happened between them was shameful. In the note on p. 136 the editor says the printed text drops a conjunction in a phrase and that he printed the manuscript's reading. The reports for the third view come on the next page. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 136}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict. We stop at p. 136 in the commentary on verse 22 of sura 4, at the end of the page in the third view on the exception that closes the prohibition, just before report 8943, and continue from p. 137. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 136}
