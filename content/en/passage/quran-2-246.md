## Summary
Verse 246 of [[passage/quran-2|Surat al-Baqara]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 246 of 286 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

([[passage/quran-2-246|2:246]]) begins on p. 291. Al-Tabari explains the opening question as a seeing of the heart, the notables as the leading and noble men of the Children of Israel, after Moses had died, when they asked a prophet of theirs to send them a king so that they might fight in God's way. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 291} He says the prophet was Shamwil son of Bali son of 'Alqama, giving his lineage up to Levi son of Jacob son of Isaac son of Abraham, from Ibn Ishaq from Wahb b. Munabbih (5626), while in another report Wahb named him Shamwil without a lineage (5627). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 291} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 292} In the margin the editor compares the names in this lineage with the Book of Chronicles in the People of the Book's scripture and corrects the manuscript's readings; this is his estimate. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 291} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 292}

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-246|2:246]]): al-Tabari reports that al-Suddi said the prophet's name was Sham'un, so named because his mother prayed to God for a boy and He answered, so she called him by the name meaning that God heard her prayer (5628); Mujahid said Sham'ul (5629). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 292} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 293} Others said he was Joshua son of Nun son of Ephraim son of Joseph; Qatada said (5630) he was their prophet after Moses and one of the two men on whom God had bestowed favour, in ([[passage/quran-5-23|5:23]]). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 293} The editor comments on the variant spellings of the names and corrects the printed text by the manuscript and al-Durr al-Manthur. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 293}

Al-Tabari then gives the interpreters' differences over why the notables asked their prophet to send them a king. One group, from Ibn Ishaq from Wahb b. Munabbih (5631), said that Joshua succeeded Moses and kept the Torah among them, then Caleb son of Jephunneh, then Ezekiel son of Buzi, then God took him and troubles grew and they forgot God's covenant until they set up idols; God sent Elijah as a prophet; he was with a king called Ahab who listened to him, but the king said he saw what Elijah called to as false, seeing kings who worshipped idols living in ease; Elijah said the formula of return, left him, and the king worshipped idols. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 293} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 294} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 295} Elisha succeeded him, and they kept the Ark, inherited from elder to elder, with the sakina and a remnant of what the house of Moses and of Aaron left; no enemy met them but God defeated him when they brought the Ark forward. A king called Eli followed, and their land was blessed; when their misdeeds grew and they left God's covenant an enemy came, they went out with the Ark and it was seized from them; their king died of grief; their affairs fell into disorder and their sons and women were taken; among them was a prophet they would not heed, called Shamwil. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 295} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 296} The editor refers to the equivalents of these names in the People of the Book's scriptures and to al-Tabari's account in his History; this is his estimate. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 294} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 295} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 296}

He goes on that when the affliction came on them they spoke to their prophet Shamwil to send a king to fight with them; the order of the Children of Israel stood on gathering around kings and the kings' obedience to their prophets. He told them they had no loyalty, truthfulness or desire for fighting, and they said they had shrunk from fighting because they were protected in their land, but now that this had come, fighting was necessary. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 296} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 297} Al-Rabi' (5632): when Moses was dying he appointed Joshua son of Nun as successor, who followed the Torah and Moses' practice, and a series of successors followed, who departed from some of it and then all of it; then the Children of Israel, hurt in persons and property, came to a prophet of theirs and asked him to ask his Lord to prescribe fighting for them, and he asked whether they would hold back if it were prescribed. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 297} Ibn 'Abbas (5633) and al-Dahhak (5634): this was when the Torah was taken up and the believers were driven out, the tyrants having expelled them from their homes. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 297} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 298} These reports come from Successors and the People of the Book, and the museum does not rule on their soundness.

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-246|2:246]]): al-Tabari relates from al-Suddi (5635) that the Children of Israel fought the Amalekites, whose king was Goliath; they overcame them, imposed tribute and took their Torah. The Children of Israel kept asking God to send a prophet to fight with them; the tribe of prophecy had perished save one pregnant woman, whom they shut in a house fearing she might bear a girl and exchange her for a boy; she prayed to God for a boy, bore one and named him Sham'un, and sent him to learn the Torah in Jerusalem with an elder of their scholars who adopted him. When God was to send him as a prophet Gabriel came to him as he slept beside the elder and called him in the elder's voice; he rose in alarm to the elder three times, and the third time Gabriel appeared and told him to convey his Lord's message to his people. They called him a liar and said he had hurried to prophecy, and asked a sign of him, to send them a king; Sham'un said, it may be that if fighting were prescribed for you, you would not fight. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 298} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 299} The editor explains in the margin how he read the manuscript and the differences between the printed text, the History and al-Durr al-Manthur, and says Sham'un and Shamwil seem to be two forms of one name; this is his estimate. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 298} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 299}

On the reading, al-Tabari says that the verb read with the first-person plural can only be jussive, in the sense of an answer to a command; that if someone thinks the raised form is allowed, meaning the one with whom we fight, that is not allowed because the Arabs do not omit two particles; but had it been read with the third person, the raised form would be allowed as a description of the king, and he cites the verse of al-Baqara on Abraham's prayer. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 299} The editor refers to al-Farra''s Ma'ani al-Qur'an. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 299}

On what the prophet said, al-Tabari explains that he said, would you, if fighting is prescribed, fail to keep what you promise God of fighting in His way, for you are people of treachery, betrayal and little faithfulness; and that the notables answered, what prevents us from fighting our enemy and God's enemy when we have been driven from our homes and sons by force and conquest. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 300}

Al-Tabari takes up the grammar question of the particle that appears here and is omitted in another place in the Qur'an. He says both are eloquent Arab usages: it is omitted with the phrase "what ails you" as in saying what ails you, you do not do such a thing, citing a verse of a rajaz-poet addressing his she-camel; and it is kept at other times because the phrase means what stopped you, citing the verses of al-A'raf and al-Hijr and a verse of al-Farazdaq, and that the particle ba' enters the predicate after "is there" because a question is close to a denial. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 300} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 301} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 302} The editor in the margin explains the two verses, the rajaz-poet's story, al-Farazdaq's lines and the variants, and differs from Ibn Barri's and Ibn al-A'rabi's explanations; this is his estimate. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 300} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 301} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 302} He mentions the view of some grammarians, al-Kisa'i by al-Farra''s statement in the margin, that it was put in because the sense is what ails you in not fighting, and answers that were this allowed, what ails you that you stood would be allowed, and it is not, because prevention applies to the future. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 302} Others, al-Akhfash per the margin, said it is superfluous after the phrase, as it is added elsewhere, citing a line of al-Farazdaq; others denied it, saying it cannot be made superfluous when the meaning is sound and needed, the sense being what prevents us from not fighting. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 302} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 303} The editor suggests another understanding of the line and says al-Akhfash erred in citing it; this is his estimate. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 303} This is a linguistic discussion the museum reports from al-Tabari and the grammarians, with no verdict by the museum.

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-246|2:246]]) from p. 304: al-Tabari relates that some answered those who said a particle is implied by arguing that, were it so, the phrase "among others" could not have been put before it in the poet's line; the editor says this is taken word for word from al-Farra'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 304} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 305} Al-Tabari explains their saying that they had been driven from their homes and sons as meaning those of their men and women who were overcome, driven out or taken captive; the words look general but are particular, since those who spoke to their prophet were in their homes, and only those captured and subdued had been driven out. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 305} He explains the turning away as meaning that when fighting their enemy was prescribed for them they turned their backs and wasted what they had asked of their prophet, save a few, the ones who crossed the river with Talut (Saul), a reason he promises to give later. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 305} He says God knows those who wronged themselves, who broke what they promised and opposed the command of their Lord. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 305} He takes this as a rebuke to the Jews who lived near the Prophet in Medina, for denying him and opposing their Lord's command: they had disobeyed over something they asked God to prescribe at the start, so they are likelier to disobey over what He prescribed to them at the start; and he says something understood is left out of the passage, namely that their prophet asked his Lord, who sent them a king and prescribed fighting. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 305} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 306} This is al-Tabari's choice and argument, and the museum gives no judgement of anyone.
