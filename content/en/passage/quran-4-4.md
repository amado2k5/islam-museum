## Summary
Verse 4 of [[passage/quran-4|Surat an-Nisa]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 4 of 176 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**The sense of nihla (pp. 552-553, reports 8506-8509).** Al-Tabari says the command to give the dowries as a nihla means: give women their dowries as an obligatory gift and a binding duty; one says "so-and-so gave so-and-so such a thing as a nihla". The notes: the manuscript reads "obligatory upon him" with a mark of error over "upon him", and the printed edition is right; "nihla" with a kasra is a verbal noun like "hikma", and "nuhl" with a damma is a verbal noun like "hukm". Qatada (8506), through Bishr ibn Mu'adh from Yazid ibn Zuray' from Sa'id: an obligation; Ibn 'Abbas (8507), through al-Muthanna from Abu Salih from Mu'awiya ibn Salih from 'Ali ibn Abi Talha: nihla is the dowry; Ibn Jurayj (8508), through al-Qasim from al-Husayn from Hajjaj: a named obligation; Ibn Zayd (8509), through Yunus from Ibn Wahb: nihla in Arab speech is the obligatory, meaning he does not marry her except with something due to her, a dowry he names for her as due, and it is not right for anyone after the Prophet to marry a woman except with an obligatory dowry, nor for the naming of the dowry to be a falsehood without right.

**Second view: the guardians are addressed, and the practice of shighar (pp. 553-554, reports 8510-8511).** Others said the guardians of women are meant, since they used to take the women's dowries. Abu Salih (8510), through al-Muthanna from 'Amr ibn 'Awn from Hushaym from Sayyar: when a man gave his unmarried woman in marriage he took her dowry for himself, and God forbade them and the verse came down. The note says the printed edition has "ayyima" with a final ta, an error; an "ayyim" among women is one without a husband, virgin or not, and among men one without a wife. Others said it concerned the guardians of women when a man gave his sister to another man on the condition that the other give him his sister and no considerable dowry pass between them, and they were forbidden this; the note says this is the "shighar", in which each side marries his ward or unmarried woman to the other without dowry, one of the marriages of the Jahiliyya, which the Messenger of God forbade. Muhammad ibn 'Abd al-A'la (8511), through al-Mu'tamir ibn Sulayman from his father: a Hadrami claimed that some people would have a man give his sister and take the other's sister without taking a considerable dowry, and the verse came down.

**Al-Tabari's choice (p. 554).** Abu Ja'far says the best of the interpretations is his own, because God opened this verse by addressing those who marry women, forbade them to wrong or be unjust to them and showed them the way out of wronging them, and nothing in the verse indicates that the address turned from them to others; so those told to marry are those told to give the dowries, the sense being: give those you have married their dowries as a gift, because He said at the start of the verse "marry" and not "give in marriage", which would turn the command to give towards the women's guardians and away from their husbands. This is a command from God to the husbands of women who were consummated with and had a dowry named, to give them their dowries, excluding women divorced before consummation who had no dowry named in the contract. This is al-Tabari's choice and argument, given as transmitted, and the museum gives no verdict on questions of dowry. The note says the manuscript omitted the word "verse" and the printed edition made it "at the first", and the context requires what the editor placed in brackets.

**Whom the clause on a woman giving up part of her dowry addresses, and the reports on "wholesome" (pp. 555-557, reports 8512-8522).** Al-Tabari says the sense is: if your wives give you, men, something of their dowries, their selves pleased with that, eat it as wholesome and agreeable. 'Ikrima (8512), through Muhammad ibn 'Abd al-A'la from Bishr ibn al-Mufaddal from 'Umara: the dowry; 'Ikrima (8513), through Muhammad ibn al-Muthanna from Harami ibn 'Umara from Shu'ba from 'Umara: the dowries, the note saying Harami ibn 'Umara ibn Abi Hafsa al-'Ataki, Abu Rawh, narrated from Shu'ba, Ahmad calling him truthful with some heedlessness, and that he has an entry in al-Tahdhib; that the 'Umara narrating from 'Ikrima is his father 'Umara ibn Abi Hafsa al-'Ataki, reliable; and that the editor knows of no 'Umara among those 'Ikrima narrated from, so he thinks the phrase "'Ikrima from 'Umara" a copyist's error, either a repetition of "'Umara" or an error for "from Ibn 'Abbas", and put it in brackets. Sa'id (8514), through al-Muthanna from al-Himmani from Sharik from Salim: the spouses. Ibrahim (8515), through al-Muthanna from 'Amr ibn 'Awn from Hushaym from 'Ubayda: Ibrahim said to me, have you eaten of the wholesome? I said, what is that? He said, your wife gave you of her dowry. Ibrahim (8516), through Ibn Humayd from Jarir from Mansur: a man came in on 'Alqama while he was eating food before him of something his wife had given him from her dowry or otherwise, and 'Alqama said, come near and eat of the wholesome. Ibn 'Abbas (8517), through al-Muthanna from 'Abd Allah ibn Salih from Mu'awiya ibn Salih from 'Ali ibn Abi Talha: when it is without harm or deception, it is wholesome as God said. Ibn Jurayj (8518), through al-Qasim from al-Husayn from Hajjaj: the dowry. Ibn Zayd (8519), through Yunus from Ibn Wahb: after you have made it due to them and handed it over, eat it; the note saying the printed edition has an incomplete sentence giving only the verse's text, and he set the manuscript's with "eat it", which the copyist dropped. Muhammad ibn 'Abd al-A'la (8520), through al-Mu'tamir from his father: a Hadrami claimed that some people felt guilt over asking back anything of what they had given their wives, and the verse came down; the note says the printed edition has "go back" and he kept the manuscript. Qatada (8521), through Bishr ibn Mu'adh from Yazid from Sa'id: whatever she gives freely without constraint or humiliation, God has made it lawful for you to eat as wholesome; the note says the manuscript has "without his mention" and the printed edition is right. Others said the guardians of women are meant, and they were told: if the selves of the women whose marriage-bond is with you are pleased about their dowries, eat it as wholesome. Abu Salih (8522), through Ya'qub ibn Ibrahim from Hushaym from Sayyar: when a man gave his daughter in marriage he went to her dowry and took it, and this verse came down about the guardians. Al-Tabari says the better of the two interpretations is the one he gave, that the verse addresses the husbands, because the verse opens by mentioning them and the clause is in its context.

**The singular of the word for self, and transferring the act of selves to their owners (pp. 557-559).** Al-Tabari puts an objector's question: how was the word for self said in the singular when the sense is for many, as you know the sense is that their selves were pleased? He answers that transferring an act of the selves to their owners is common in Arab speech, as they say "I was narrow in this matter in arm" and "my eye was cooled by it", the sense being that his arm's length narrowed and his eye cooled; and he cites a line of al-Qutami about a camel too fat and strong for the one who tried to control and mount her, which is described, not reproduced. The editor's note traces it in al-Qutami's Diwan (44), al-Farra (1: 256) and Lisan, and says it recurs in the commentary (20: 319) and was used earlier (1: 446, note 6); it is from a poem glorifying Zufar ibn al-Harith, preceded by two lines on the camel's fatness; he glosses the words for plaster, palace and thick-fleshed; and he records that Ibn Barri rejected the explanation of the phrase "to you, to you" as "take her", saying Sibawayh and all the Basrans hold it means "stand back" and is not transitive, and that Abu 'Amr al-Shaybani transmitted a different word; the editor holds the commentators' explanation right and good and Ibn Barri's correction not. So the attribute of the arm was transferred to the owner of the arm and the arm then put as a specifier of the verb's place, and likewise the self was put in the singular in the verse as a specifier (the note says "tafsir" and "mufassar" are the Kufans' terms for the specifier and the specified, and refers to al-Farra, 1: 256). As for the singular for "selves", he meant inclination, and inclination can be plural, as in a line of 'Alqama ibn 'Abada (Alqama al-Fahl) describing the carcasses of exhausted camels dead on the road, whose hide is now only grease, and in a line of al-Musayyab ibn Zayd Manat al-Ghanawi about men some of whose people were captured and whose people then killed some of their captors: do not deny the killing, we were taken captive, and a bone is in your throats; both lines are described, not reproduced. The editor's notes: 'Alqama's line is in his Diwan (27), Sharh al-Mufaddaliyyat (777) and Sibawayh (1: 107), will recur in the commentary (10: 17, Bulaq), and is from a poem to al-Harith ibn Jabala al-Ghassani when he captured his brother Shas and 'Alqama travelled to him to ask for his release, the witness being "its hide" meant for "hides"; al-Musayyab's line is in Sibawayh (1: 107), Sharh al-Mufaddaliyyat (778) and Lisan, the witness being "in your throat" meant for "throats". Some Kufan grammarians said both plural and singular are allowed for the word for self here, as one says "I was narrow in arm" in several forms, because it is attributed to you and to whom you report about, so the singular suffices for the plural and no one supposes it is not plural in sense since a plural precedes it. Abu Ja'far says the right view, in his opinion, is that the word for self takes the place of nouns that come in a singular form conveying its meaning, and that it is plural in sense for all.

**The sense of "wholesome" (pp. 559-560).** Al-Tabari says the word is taken from "I tarred the camel", when it was mangy and was treated, and cites a line of Duraid ibn al-Simma about a woman tarring a camel, described, not reproduced. The editor's note traces it in al-Shi'r wa-l-Shu'ara' (302), al-Aghani (10: 22) and Lisan, from lines he composed when he passed al-Khansa' bint 'Amr ibn al-Sharid while she was tarring a camel of hers; he then asked her father for her hand and was refused, and lampooned her, claiming she refused him because he was an old man; al-Khansa' was asked whether she would answer him, and said she would not combine rejecting him with lampooning him; and he glosses "naqab" as the start of war when it appears. So the sense of the command to eat is: eat it as a healing remedy. One says "the food was wholesome for me", that is, became a remedy and healing treatment; a form with the kasra exists but is rare; those who say it one way say "yahna'uni and yamra'uni", those who say the other way "yahnini and yamrini", and used singly they say "this food cured me". One says "I tarred the people" when one supported them, and some Arabs have been heard to say "I was named Hani' so that I may support", meaning provide and suffice. These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 560}
