## Summary
Verse 124 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 124 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**The meaning (p. 173).** Abu Ja'far says the meaning is: God helped you at Badr when you were humbled, when you said to the believers among your Companions that it should suffice them for their Lord to aid them with a force of angels, three thousand sent down; that was the day of Badr. The exegetes then differ on which day they were promised the angels' presence. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 173}

**First view: the promise was at Badr and its condition was not met (pp. 173-174).** Some said God promised the believers at Badr to aid them with His angels if the enemy came upon them at once; they did not, so the aid was not sent. In his note the editor says the manuscript has a plainly wrong form here, is full of slips at this place, and that he corrected what was evident without marking many similar cases.
- From Humayd ibn Mas'ada, Bishr ibn al-Mufaddal, Dawud, Amir (7743): the Muslims were told that Kurz ibn Jabir al-Muharibi was to reinforce the polytheists, and that distressed them, so the verses were said to them; the defeat then reached Kurz and he turned back and did not reinforce them with the five thousand. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 173}
- From Ibn al-Muthanna, Abd al-A'la, Dawud, Amir (7744): the same, adding that those who would come upon them at once were Kurz and his men, to whom the defeat reached, so they did not reinforce them and the five thousand did not come down; afterwards the Muslims were reinforced with a thousand, making four thousand angels with them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 174}
- From Muhammad ibn Sinan, Abu Bakr al-Hanafi, Abbad, al-Hasan (7745): this is the day of Badr. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 174}
- From Ya'qub, Ibn Ulayya, Dawud, al-Sha'bi (7746): the Muslims were told that Kurz ibn Jabir al-Muharibi meant to reinforce the polytheists at Badr and it distressed them, so God sent down the verses; the polytheists' defeat reached him and he did not reinforce his men, and the Muslims were not reinforced with the five thousand. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 174}

**Second view: they were steadfast and God reinforced them (pp. 174-176).** Others said the promise was made at Badr, the believers were steadfast and godwary, and God reinforced them with His angels as He had promised:
- From Abu Kurayb, Yunus ibn Bukayr, Ibn Ishaq, Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr, from some of Banu Sa'ida (7747): I heard Abu Usayd Malik ibn Rabi'a, after he lost his sight, say: if I were with you at Badr now with my sight, I would tell you of the pass the angels came out of, without doubt or dispute. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 174} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 175}
- From Ibn Humayd, Salama, Ibn Ishaq (7748): the same from Abu Usayd, who was at Badr. The editor says it is in Ibn Hisham's Sira (2: 286), that it comes later with another chain in different wording and attributed to Uhud and not Badr, and that he will comment on it there (the number of that later report is not clear in the scanned print). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 175}
- From Ibn Humayd, Salama, Ibn Ishaq, Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr, who was told it from Ibn Abbas (7749): a man of Ghifar said: my cousin and I went up a mountain overlooking Badr, both of us polytheists, waiting to see who would be defeated so we could plunder with the plunderers. While we were on the mountain a cloud came near and we heard the neighing of horses, and I heard a voice say "advance, Hayzum". My cousin's heart's covering was torn off and he died on the spot; I nearly perished but held on. The notes explain the word for defeat and how it is used in asking who gets the victory; that the call is one used to urge horses forward; that Hayzum is the name of a horse among the angels' horses that day, said to be Jibril's; that the manuscript has a corrupted reading here; and that the heart's covering is likened to a woman's veil. The report is in Ibn Hisham's Sira (2: 285). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 175}
- From Ibn Humayd, Salama, Ibn Ishaq, al-Hasan ibn Umara, al-Hakam ibn Utayba, Miqsam, freedman of Abdullah ibn al-Harith, Ibn Abbas (7750): the angels fought on no day except Badr; on the other days they were numbers and reinforcement and did not strike. In Ibn Hisham's Sira (2: 286). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 175}
- From Ibn Humayd, Salama, Ibn Ishaq, his father Ishaq ibn Yasar, men of Banu Mazin ibn al-Najjar, Abu Dawud al-Mazini, who was at Badr (7751): I was following one of the polytheists at Badr to strike him when his head fell before my sword reached him, and I knew that someone else had killed him. In Ibn Hisham's Sira (2: 286). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 176}
- From Ibn Humayd, Salama, Ibn Ishaq, Husayn ibn Abd Allah ibn Ubayd Allah ibn Abbas, Ikrima, freedman of Ibn Abbas, Abu Rafi', freedman of the Prophet (7752): I was a slave of al-Abbas ibn Abd al-Muttalib, and Islam had entered our household; al-Abbas, Umm al-Fadl and I became Muslim, but al-Abbas feared his people, kept his Islam hidden, and had much money spread among them. Abu Lahab stayed behind from Badr and sent al-As ibn Hisham ibn al-Mughira in his place, as all did, no man staying behind without sending another. When the news came of the Quraysh's losses we felt strength and honour in ourselves. I was a weak man making arrows, carving them in the room of Zamzam; as I sat there with Umm al-Fadl, pleased with the news, Abu Lahab came dragging his feet and sat at the tent-rope of the room, his back to mine. People said Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith ibn Abd al-Muttalib had arrived. Abu Lahab said: come to me, nephew, you have the news. He sat with him while people stood about, and Abu Lahab asked how it had gone with the people. He replied: nothing, by God, except that we met them and gave them our shoulders, and they killed and captured us as they liked, and by God... The report continues on p. 177. The editor says the printed wording for the phrase on strength and honour is of no use, that the manuscript's is a corrupt form, and that he adopted Ibn Hisham's, and explains the tent-rope as the lowered side of the room, from the rope that fastens a tent to the ground. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 176}

**The close of Abu Rafi's report (p. 177).** Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith finishes his answer: "even so I do not blame the people; we met white men on piebald horses between sky and earth, who spared nothing and before whom nothing stood". Abu Rafi' lifted the tent-rope of the room and said: those are the angels. The editor says report 7752 is in Ibn Hisham's Sira (2: 301) with slight differences, that the phrase used here is said of a sword whose edge nothing turns back, and that he kept the wording of the manuscript and the Sira because the printed text had not understood it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 177}

**The capture of al-Abbas (p. 177).** From Ibn Humayd, Salama, Ibn Ishaq, al-Hasan ibn Umara, al-Hakam ibn Utayba, Miqsam, Ibn Abbas (7753): the one who captured al-Abbas was Abu al-Yasar Ka'b ibn Amr, a brother of Banu Salima, a compact man while al-Abbas was bulky. The Prophet asked him how he had captured al-Abbas, and he said a man had helped him whom he had never seen before or since, of such and such appearance; the Prophet said a noble angel had helped him. The editor says he did not find it in the printed Sira of Ibn Hisham but in al-Tabari's History (2: 288-289), that he kept the History's form as better Arabic, and that he did not find the word for "compact" in the dictionaries he had, taking its sense from the context as the opposite of bulky. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 177}

**Reports on the growing number (pp. 177-178).**
- From Bishr, Yazid, Sa'id, Qatada (7754; the number is unreadable and inferred from the sequence): they were reinforced with a thousand, then it became three thousand, then five thousand, and that was Badr. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 177} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 178}
- From Ammar, al-Rabi' (7755; the scanned print reads Ibn Abi Najih in the chain where this chain usually has Ibn Abi Ja'far): the like. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 178}
- From Muhammad ibn Sa'd, his father, his uncle, his father, his grandfather, Ibn Abbas (7756): they came to Muhammad marked with signs. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 178}
- From Muhammad ibn Bashshar, Sufyan, Ibn Khuthaym, Mujahid (7757): the angels fought only on the day of Badr. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 178}
The editor notes that a link ("he said, my father told me") dropped out of the printed text and that the manuscript is right; it is a recurring chain in the commentary. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 178}

**The view that the promise was conditional and was met at the Confederates (pp. 178-179).** Others said God promised at Badr to reinforce them in all their wars if they were steadfast in obeying Him and fighting His enemies and wary of what He forbade; they were so only on the day of the Confederates, when He reinforced them during the siege of Qurayza:
- From Muhammad ibn Umara al-Asadi, Ubaydallah ibn Musa, Sulayman ibn Zayd Abu Idam al-Muharibi, Abdullah ibn Abi Awfa (7758): we besieged Qurayza and al-Nadir as long as God willed and no victory came, so we returned; the Prophet called for water and was washing his head when Jibril came and said: you have laid down your arms but the angels have not laid down theirs. The Prophet wrapped his head in a cloth without washing it and called us, and we rose weary and spent, caring nothing for the journey, until we reached Qurayza and al-Nadir; that day God reinforced us with three thousand angels and gave us a slight victory, and we returned with favour and grace from God. In his notes the editor says the manuscript and print erred over the name of Ubaydallah ibn Musa al-Abdi (his biography is under number 5796 as printed); that Sulayman ibn Zayd Abu Idam al-Muharibi is described by Yahya ibn Ma'in as not reliable and a liar and by al-Nasa'i as abandoned in hadith; that Abdullah ibn Abi Awfa al-Aslami witnessed the Pledge of Ridwan and died in 88 AH; and that, though the chain does not stand, its meaning seems likely true since it agrees with sound reports on the Qurayza campaign from others than Ibn Abi Awfa. Another note sets out his reading of an indistinct word in the report and its derivation, and says al-Suyuti quotes the report from Ibn Jarir in al-Khasa'is al-Kubra (1: 233). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 178} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 179}

**The view that they were not reinforced at Uhud (pp. 179-180).** Others spoke along these lines but said the people were not steadfast or wary and so were reinforced with nothing at Uhud:
- From al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hajjaj, Ibn Jurayj, Amr ibn Dinar, Ikrima (7759): the promise was for Badr; they were not steadfast or wary so were not reinforced at Uhud, and had they been, they would not have been routed. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 179}
- From Ibn Bashshar, Abd al-Rahman, Sufyan ibn Uyayna, Amr ibn Dinar, Ikrima (7760): they were not reinforced at Uhud even by one angel, or he said except by one angel; Abu Ja'far is unsure which. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 180}
- From al-Husayn ibn al-Faraj (so printed here; possibly the same name printed differently in report 7737), Abu Mu'adh, Ubayd ibn Sulayman, al-Dahhak (7761): this was a promise from God at Uhud, made to His Prophet, that if the believers were wary and steadfast He would reinforce them with five thousand; the Muslims fled at Uhud and turned their backs, and God did not reinforce them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 180}
- From Yunus, Ibn Wahb, Ibn Zayd (7762): they said to the Prophet, looking at the polytheists, "will God not reinforce us as at Badr?" He replied that the question was about three thousand angels and that at Badr He had reinforced them with a thousand; the increase came on condition of steadfastness and godwariness, and on the condition that the enemy came upon them at once. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 180}

**Al-Tabari's preference (pp. 180-181).** Abu Ja'far says the soundest view is that God reported that His Prophet asked the believers whether three thousand angels would suffice; God then promised them three thousand, and after that five thousand if they were steadfast against their enemies and wary of God. Nothing in the verse shows that they were reinforced with three or five thousand, or that they were not; either may be, and no sound report has reached him that establishes either, and nothing may be said on this without a report on which proof stands. But the Qur'an does show that they were reinforced at Badr with a thousand angels, in [[passage/quran-8-9|8:9]]; as for Uhud, the indication that they were not reinforced is clearer, since had they been they would not have been routed or suffered what they did. The editor refers to the earlier explanations of reinforcement, steadfastness and godwariness. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 180} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 181}

**The word for "at once" (pp. 181-183).** Al-Tabari notes that the exegetes differ about the word near the end of verse 125 describing how the enemy comes. Some said it means "from this direction of theirs":
- From Humayd ibn Mas'ada, Yazid ibn Zurayʿ, Uthman ibn Ghiyath, Ikrima (7763); Bishr, Yazid, Sa'id, Qatada (7764, number unreadable and inferred); al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, Ma'mar, Qatada (7765); Muhammad ibn Sinan, Abu Bakr al-Hanafi, Abbad, al-Hasan (7766, number unreadable and inferred); Ammar ibn al-Hasan, Ibn Abi Ja'far, his father, al-Rabi' (7767); Muhammad ibn al-Husayn, Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal, Asbat, al-Suddi (7768): "from this direction of theirs". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 181} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 182}
- From Muhammad ibn Sa'd, his father, his uncle, his father, his grandfather, Ibn Abbas (7769): "from this journey of theirs"; and it is said, not from Ibn Abbas, rather "from this anger of theirs". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 182}
- From Yunus, Ibn Wahb, Ibn Zayd (7770, number unreadable and inferred): "from this direction of theirs". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 182}

Others said it means "from this anger of theirs":
- From Muhammad ibn al-Muthanna, Abd al-A'la, Dawud, Ikrima (7771): that was the day of Uhud; they were angry about what they had met at Badr. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 182}
- From Muhammad ibn Umara, Sahl ibn Amir, Malik ibn Mighwal, Abu Salih, freedman of Umm Hani (7772): "from this anger of theirs". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 182}
- From Muhammad ibn Amr, Abu Asim, Isa, Ibn Abi Najih, Mujahid (7773): it is the unbelievers' anger; they did not fight the Muslims at that hour, and that was Uhud. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 182}
- From al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hajjaj, Ibn Jurayj, Mujahid (7774): "from this anger of theirs". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 182}
- From al-Husayn ibn al-Faraj, Abu Mu'adh, Ubayd ibn Sulayman, al-Dahhak (7775): from their direction and their anger. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 183}

**The root of the word and what follows from it (pp. 183-184).** Abu Ja'far says the root meaning is the beginning of an undertaking that one takes up and then joins to another step, as a pot "boils over" when what is in it begins to bubble and carries on, and as one says "I went to so-and-so from my first start", meaning from the direction in which I began. So those who said "direction" mean that Kurz ibn Jabir and his men would come at Badr from the start of their setting out to help their fellow polytheists; those who said "anger" mean that the Quraysh unbelievers and their followers would come at Uhud from the start of their anger for those killed at Badr. From this difference they differed on whether God reinforced the believers with angels at Uhud. Some said He did not, because the believers were not steadfast or wary, as some archers left the Prophet's command at the post where he told them to hold and abandoned it seeking spoils, so some Muslims were killed and the polytheists got what they got; God had promised His Prophet reinforcement only if they were steadfast and wary. Of those who said it concerned Badr and Kurz, some said Kurz and his men did not come to reinforce the polytheists and God did not reinforce the believers with angels, since the promise was conditional on their coming. Those who said God did reinforce the Muslims at Badr argued from the mention in [[passage/quran-8-9|8:9]] of a thousand angels: the thousand did come, and the conditional promise concerns only what was beyond the thousand, since God does not break His promise. Al-Tabari then gives the readings of the participle for marking: most Medina and Kufa readers have a fatha on the waw, meaning God marked them; some Kufa and Basra readers have a kasra, meaning the angels marked themselves; the discussion continues on p. 185. In the notes the editor says the print and manuscript differ at several places and that he chose what he judged right (on seeking spoils, on "so some were killed" and on "beyond the thousand"), and that he read the phrase on their differing in interpretation according to the copyist's habit of leaving out the dot of a letter. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 183} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 184}

**Al-Tabari's choice of reading (p. 185).** Abu Ja'far says the better of the two readings is that with a kasra on the waw, because of the converging reports from the Prophet's Companions and the exegetes among them and the Followers after them that the angels themselves marked themselves, without ascribing the marking to God or to any other creature. He answers those who said the kasra suits humans but not angels: it is not impossible that God enabled the angels to mark themselves as He enabled humans, out of their wish to obey their Lord, so that the marking is ascribed to them even though God set its causes; and if they are described as doing it to draw near to their Lord, that is a higher praise of them for choosing obedience than saying it was done to them. He then turns to the reports. In two notes the editor explains that the printed text at one place was corrupt and the manuscript likewise (with a further slip), so he added the word "Companions" in brackets and the sentence ran straight, and that a smudged word in the manuscript should be read as "like". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 185}

**Reports that the angels marked themselves (pp. 186-188).**
- From Ya'qub, Ibn Ulayya, Ibn Awn, Umayr ibn Ishaq (7776): the first use of wool that day, meaning Badr, was when the Prophet said: "Mark yourselves, for the angels have marked themselves." In his note the editor gives biographies of Ibn Awn and Umayr ibn Ishaq, quotes Abu Hatim and al-Nasa'i that they know of no one who narrated from Umayr except Ibn Awn, and Ibn Ma'in saying he is trustworthy and also that his report is worth nothing but is written down; the editor concludes the report is mursal and from a man whose reports are written down but not used as proof. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 186}
- From Abu Kurayb, Mukhtar ibn Ghassan, Abd al-Rahman ibn al-Ghasil, al-Zubayr ibn al-Mundhir, his grandfather Abu Usayd, who was at Badr (7777): if my sight were released and you came with me to Uhud, I would show you the pass from which the angels came out in yellow turbans thrown between their shoulders. The editor sets out how he fixed an unclear word in the manuscript and printed text, relying on Sahl ibn Sa'd's version in the earlier reports 7747 and 7748; notes the dispute whether Abu Usayd was al-Zubayr's father or grandfather; and observes that this report came earlier with the chains of Abu Kurayb and Ibn Humayd, there attributed to Badr and not Uhud, which he holds to be the sound one; he suspects a slip by a copyist or narrator here and that the right reading is "to Badr". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 186}
- From Muhammad ibn Amr, Abu Asim, Isa, Ibn Abi Najih, Mujahid (7778, number unreadable, inferred from the sequence): "marked": the tails and forelocks of their horses were cut and had wool or dyed wool in them, and that is the marking. The editor holds the manuscript's omission of "or" to be right and says the word used means coloured, dyed wool. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 187}
- From Ibn Humayd, Hakkam, Anbasa, Muhammad ibn Abd al-Rahman, al-Qasim ibn Abi Bazza, Mujahid (7779, inferred): their tails and manes were cut and had wool or dyed wool in them; that is the marking. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 187}
- From Bishr, Yazid, Sa'id, Qatada (7780): we were told that their sign that day was wool in the forelocks and tails of their horses, and that they rode piebald horses. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 187}
- From al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, Ma'mar, Qatada (7781): their sign was wool in their forelocks. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 187}
- From Ammar (reported as "I was told"), Ibn Abi Ja'far, his father, Layth, Mujahid (7782): their horses had cut manes and their forelocks and tails were marked with wool and dyed wool. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 187}
- From Ammar, Ibn Abi Ja'far, his father, al-Rabi' (7783): they were that day on piebald horses. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 187}
- From al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hushaym, Juwaybir, al-Dahhak and some of their elders, al-Hasan (7784): like Ma'mar's report from Qatada. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 187}
- From Muhammad, Ahmad, Asbat, al-Suddi (7785): "marked". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 188}
- From Muhammad ibn Sa'd, his father, his uncle, his father, his grandfather, Ibn Abbas (7786): they came to the Prophet marked with wool, so he and his Companions marked themselves and their horses with their sign in wool. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 188}
- From Abu Kurayb, Ibn Yaman, Hisham ibn Urwa, Abbad ibn Hamza (7787): the angels came down in al-Zubayr's sign, in yellow turbans; al-Zubayr's turban was yellow. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 188}
- From Ya'qub ibn Ibrahim, Hushaym, Juwaybir, al-Dahhak (7788): with wool in the forelocks and tails. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 188}
- From al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, Ma'mar, Hisham ibn Urwa (7789): the angels came down at Badr on piebald horses in yellow turbans, and al-Zubayr wore a yellow turban that day. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 188}
- From Ahmad ibn Yahya al-Sufi, Abd al-Rahman ibn Sharik, his father, Hisham ibn Urwa, Urwa, Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr (7790): al-Zubayr had a yellow wrap at Badr and wound it as a turban, and the angels came down on the Prophet in yellow turbans. The editor's note on this chain gives the biography of Ahmad ibn Yahya al-Sufi (Abu Hatim called him trustworthy) and begins that of Abd al-Rahman ibn Sharik, which continues on the next page (p. 189): al-Bukhari in al-Adab and Abu Kurayb narrated from him; Abu Hatim called him weak in hadith; Ibn Hibban listed him among the trustworthy and said he sometimes erred. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 188}

**What these reports indicate (p. 189).** Abu Ja'far says these reports, some of them from the Prophet telling his Companions to mark themselves because the angels had, Abu Usayd's words on the yellow turbans, and the words of those who glossed the participle as "marked", all point to the soundness of the reading he chose: that the marking was by the angels themselves. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 189}

**The case for the other reading (pp. 189-190).** He thinks those who read it with a fatha took it from what is reported:
- From Humayd ibn Mas'ada, Yazid ibn Zuray', Uthman ibn Ghiyath, Ikrima (7791): they bore the sign of fighting. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 189}
- From Bishr, Yazid, Sa'id, Qatada (7792, inferred): they bore the sign of fighting, and that was at Badr. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 189}
They said the sign of fighting was on them, not that they marked themselves, so the marking is ascribed to whoever gave them that sign. Abu Ja'far adds that the word for sign means a mark, saying "a good sign" in two forms, and cites a line of poetry (the editor names the poet as Usayd ibn Hanqa al-Fazari) describing a boy whom God endowed with beauty and whose mark the eye cannot miss; the editor notes it was traced and explained earlier, in volume 5, pp. 594-595. He says the sense is a mark of beauty, and that when a man is given a mark by which he is known in war or elsewhere one says he marked himself. The editor refers to the earlier treatment of the word "sign" (volume 5, p. 594) and of the verb "to mark" (volume 5, pp. 251-257). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 190}
