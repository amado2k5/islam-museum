## Summary
Verse 225 of [[passage/quran-2|Surat al-Baqara]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 225 of 286 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

## In al-Tabari's commentary

Al-Tabari reports the interpreters' differences over the meaning of the word for "idle talk" in the verse ([[passage/quran-2-225|2:225]]). Some said: God will not hold you to oaths your tongues slip out in haste, so as to require expiation if you did not intend to swear, like someone saying "I did this, by God" or "I shall not do it, by God" by a slip of the tongue. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 427} From Ibn 'Abbas: it is saying "yes, by God" and "no, by God" (4373); from 'A'isha, in several reports to the same effect, including that 'Urwa asked her and she said it is "no, by God" and "yes, by God", the sort of thing people say back and forth (4374 to 4378). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 428} In the report of 'Ata', he entered with 'Ubayd ibn 'Umayr on 'A'isha, who said it is "no, by God" and "yes, by God", not among the oaths you have tied, or in another wording, what the heart has not bound itself to (4379, 4380, 4381). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 428} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 429} Ibrahim al-Sa'igh reported from 'Ata' from 'A'isha as a statement of the Prophet that it is what a man says in his house, "no, by God" and "yes, by God" (4382); the editor gives the narrators' biographies and says Abu Dawud judged it to stop at 'A'isha, and that Malik and al-Bukhari reported it as her own statement. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 429}

From 'Urwa from 'A'isha: it is people disputing a matter, one saying "no, by God", "yes, by God" and "never, by God", without their hearts binding themselves (4383); the editor explains the verb for disputing. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 430} From al-Sha'bi: it is a man's saying "no, by God" and "yes, by God", as part of his speech, with no expiation in it (4384, 4385, 4386, 4387). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 430} From Abu Qilaba, who hopes it is "a manner of speech" and not an oath (4388); the editor says the sense is one of the Arabs' idioms, like "woe to you", where no supplication is meant. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 430} Likewise from Abu Salih (4389); from 'A'isha by many chains through 'Ata' and Hisham ibn 'Urwa (4390 to 4400); from 'Ikrima and al-Sha'bi (4392, 4393, 4398); and some wordings add "so long as the heart has not bound itself". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 430} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 431} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 432} From Mujahid: two men bargain, one saying "by God I shall not sell you for such and such", the other "by God I shall not buy it for such and such"; this is idle talk and one is not held to it (4401). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 432}

Others said the idle talk in an oath is that a person swears thinking it is as he swears and then it proves otherwise. From Abu Hurayra (4402) and from Ibn 'Abbas: swearing about something he thinks true that is not (4403); in another report from him, swearing out of harm not to do something, then seeing another course is better, so he expiates and does the good; and part of the idle talk is swearing to something he thinks true and having erred, and on him is expiation but no sin (4404). The editor says the manuscript and printed edition read "erred in his oath" while al-Durr al-Manthur reads "erred in his thought", which is likelier, and refers to al-Tabari's later comment. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 432} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 433} From Sulayman ibn Yasar: an error, not deliberate (4405). From al-Hasan: you swear to something which you imagine is as you swear and it is not, so God does not hold him to it and there is no expiation; the holding to account and expiation concern what is sworn knowingly (4406 to 4409). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 433} From Mujahid (4410, 4411, 4412), with the example of swearing that this house belongs to so-and-so when it does not. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 433} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 434} From Ibrahim al-Nakha'i (4413, 4414, 4415), in one report adding that he liked to expiate. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 434} The note gives the biography of the narrator al-Ju'fi and the praise of the authorities for him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 434}

To finish the second view, that the idle talk is a man's swearing when he thinks he is truthful and it proves otherwise: from Ibrahim al-Nakha'i (4416), Abu Malik (4417, 4426, 4427), Ziyad (4418), Qatada: an error and not deliberate, so no expiation or sin (4419, 4423), al-Hasan (4420), Zurara ibn Awfa (4421), al-Sha'bi: a man swears not falling short of the truth and it proves otherwise (4422), al-Suddi (4424), al-Rabi' (4425), Yahya ibn Sa'id (4428), al-Hasan and Qatada (4429), Makhul: an error and not deliberate, the expiation being for what hearts have bound (4430, 4431), and Ibrahim: if he swears knowing he lies, that is what he is held to (4432). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 434} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 435} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 436} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 437} The notes correct the name Ibn Idris in 4417, complete the name of al-Tabari's teacher in 4418, give the biography of 'Umar ibn Bashir in 4422, and say of the chain in 4428 that he could not work out its sense and left it as it is. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 435} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 436} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 437}

Others said the idle talk is among the oaths a person swears in anger, without his heart binding or resolving, only as filler in speech. From Ibn 'Abbas: the idle oath is that you swear while angry (4433); from Tawus: every oath a man swears in anger has no expiation (4434). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 437} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 438} The editor bracketed "from Tawus" in 4433 because the copies dropped it, as al-Bukhari and al-Bayhaqi show, and notes a slip in the name Wasim in the manuscript and printed text. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 438} Their argument is what Ibn 'Abbas reported from the Prophet, "there is no oath in anger" (4435); the editor judges the chain sound, while Ibn Hajar called it weak because of Sulayman ibn Abi Sulayman, with whom the editor disagrees. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 439}

Others said the idle talk in an oath is swearing to do what God forbade or to leave what He commanded. From Sa'id ibn Jubayr: a man swears to a disobedience and does not keep it and expiates his oath (4436 to 4441); in some wordings he owes the expiation (4438), in others God does not hold him to leaving it (4441). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 439} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 440} It is reported that a woman swore not to speak to her granddaughter and went to Sa'id ibn al-Musayyab, Abu Bakr and 'Urwa ibn al-Zubayr, who said, there is no oath in disobedience and no expiation for her (4442). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 440} The editor gives biographies of some narrators of these reports and corrections of their wording. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 440}

To finish the third view, that the idle talk is swearing to do what God forbade: from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, a man swears to a disobedience and God does not hold him to leaving it; he expiates and leaves the disobedience (4443 to 4446); in 4445 he cites the verse in al-Ma'ida ([[passage/quran-5-89|5:89]]) and says God does not hold him for cancelling it but for carrying it through. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 440} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 441} From Masruq: does he expiate for the Devil's footsteps? There is no expiation on him (4447); likewise from Ibn 'Abbas (4448). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 441} From al-Sha'bi: its expiation is to repent of it (4449), and he used to say to leave the disobedience without expiating (4450); from Masruq: every oath you may not fulfil has no expiation (4451). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 442} Their evidence is what 'Abdallah ibn 'Amr reported from the Prophet: whoever vows what he does not own has no vow; whoever swears to disobey God has no oath; whoever swears to cut off kin has no oath (4452); the editor says al-Hakim and al-Bayhaqi report it, that al-Dhahabi objected to a discarded narrator in the chain, and that its meaning is established by several lines in the Musnad. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 442} From 'A'isha as the Prophet's saying: whoever swears to cut off kin or to disobey, his keeping faith is to break the oath and go back on it (4453); the editor says it is very weak because Haritha ibn Muhammad is weak. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 442} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 443}

Others said the idle talk is any oath a man attaches to his speech without intending to bind himself. From Ibrahim (4454, 4455); Mujahid: two men haggle and each says "by God, I shall not buy it" and "by God, I shall not sell" at such a price (4456); 'A'isha: oaths of idle talk are those said in jest, wrangling, quarrel and talk the heart does not rely on (4457). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 443} Their evidence is the mursal report from al-Hasan al-Basri that the Prophet passed by men at archery and one shot and said "I hit, by God", then "I missed"; a Companion said the man broke his oath, and he said, no, the oaths of archers are idle talk, with neither expiation nor penalty (4458); the editor calls it a weak mursal because of the unknown link, and notes that Ibn Kathir copied it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 444}

Others said it is what takes the form of a prayer by the swearer against himself if he does not do such and such, or of unbelief and association. From Zayd ibn Aslam: like a man's saying "may God blind me if I do not do such", and were God to hold you to this He would leave you nothing (4459, 4460); and like saying "he is an unbeliever", which He does not hold against him unless it comes from his heart (4461); and from Ibn Zayd (4462). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 444} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 445} Others said it is what carries an expiation, from Ibn 'Abbas (4463) and al-Dahhak (4464). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 445} Others said it is what the swearer broke by forgetting, from Ibrahim (4465). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 445} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 446}

Al-Tabari then begins to state his view: the Arabic word for "idle talk" is any speech that is blameworthy, void of meaning and abandoned; one says a man "talks idly" when he says something ugly; he cites two verses ([[passage/quran-28-55|28:55]] and [[passage/quran-25-72|25:72]]), phrases heard from the Arabs, and a line of rajaz by Ru'ba. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 446}
