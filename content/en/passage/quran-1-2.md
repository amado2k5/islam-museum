## In al-Tabari's commentary
In "the interpretation of the Opening of the Book" al-Tabari begins with [[passage/quran-1-2|"praise be to God"]]: it means thanks given purely to God, and not to what is worshipped besides Him or anything He has created, for His countless favours: making the organs sound for His obedience, enabling the limbs to perform His duties, spreading provision in this world without any claim on His part, and alerting them to the ways that lead to the lasting bliss; so praise is His for all of it, first and last. He reports from Ibn Abbas that Gabriel told Muhammad to say it and that its meaning is thanks to God, humility before Him and acknowledgment of His favour, guidance and beginning. The editor repeats that the chain is weak and notes that Ibn Kathir, al-Suyuti and al-Shawkani quote it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 135}
He gives a hadith through al-Hakam ibn Umayr, a Companion, that the Prophet said that when you say "praise be to God, Lord of the worlds" you have thanked God and He increases you. The editor says the chain is very weak: it has Isa ibn Ibrahim, whom the critics call unacceptable and abandoned, and his teacher Musa ibn Abi Habib, so much so that some hadith scholars doubted the Companion existed; he sets out the critics' disagreement and says Ibn Hajar established his companionship. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 136}
He notes it has been said that "praise be to God" is praise of Him by His names and fair attributes and "thanks to God" is praise by His favours, and reports from Ka'b al-Ahbar that "praise be to God" is praise of God without saying which. The editor remarks that Ka'b's word was never proof in commentary. He gives a hadith from al-Aswad ibn Sari' that the Prophet said nothing is dearer to God than praise, and for that He praised Himself in "praise be to God"; the editor calls this chain sound and adds the Musnad version and a similar one from Ibn Mas'ud. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 137}
Al-Tabari says the Arabic scholars do not differ that one may say "praise be to God, in thanks", which shows that praise can stand in the place of thanks and thanks in the place of praise; otherwise the verbal noun of "I thank" could not follow "praise". The editor notes that scholars have contradicted him, as in the commentaries of al-Qurtubi and Ibn Kathir, but judges his argument stronger. He then answers why the definite article is put on "praise" and not "praise to God": it shows that the sense is all praise and perfect thanks belong to God, while without it only the speaker's own praise would be meant, since "praise to God" means "I praise God with praise". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 138} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 139}
For this sense the readers and scholars of the community have consistently read "praise" in the nominative, not the accusative; he says anyone who deliberately recites it with the accusative, knowing the error, deserves punishment. He takes up the question whether the words are God's or Gabriel's or Muhammad's, since the surah says "You alone we worship" while God is the One worshipped. His answer is that all of it is God's speech: He praised Himself as He deserves and then taught His servants and made it their duty to recite it, as a trial, so it is as though He said "say this and say that". Asked where the word "say" is, he answers that the Arabs omit a word when its place is known and the listener understands, particularly when the omitted word is a saying; he cites lines of a Banu Amir poet about a grave and another about a woman's husband "wearing a sword and a spear", meaning carrying a spear, and the farewell to a traveller, "accompanied, in health". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 139} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 140}
So the omission in "praise be to God, Lord of the worlds" is made good by "You alone we worship", which shows it is part of what He told His servants; and the report from Ibn Abbas that Gabriel told Muhammad to say it supports this, since Gabriel taught him only what he was told to teach. He then begins "the interpretation of 'Lord'". The sense of the name "Allah" was set out under the basmala and need not be repeated. In Arabic "rabb" has several senses: the obeyed master is called a rabb, as in a line of Labid on the fall of the lord of Kinda, and one of al-Nabigha; and the man who sets a thing right is also called a rabb. The editor comments on who the two lines have in mind. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 141}
Al-Tabari completes the senses of "rabb" among the Arabs. He cites a line describing people who poured clarified butter into a skin that had not been treated, meaning one that had not been put right; from this comes the saying that a man "rabbs" his favour to another when he tries to mend and keep it up. He cites a line of Alqama ibn Abada, addressed to al-Harith ibn Abi Shamir al-Ghassani, whose sense is that the poet's affairs passed to the praised man after earlier kings who neglected them. He adds that the owner of a thing is called its rabb, and that other uses all go back to these three: the obeyed master, the one who sets right, and the owner. So God is Lord of the worlds in all senses: the master with no equal, the one who puts right the affairs of creation by His favours, and the owner to whom creation and command belong. In his note the editor explains the root and the treatment of the butter skin. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 142}
He gives Ibn Abbas's report that Gabriel told Muhammad to say it, and that it means praise be to God who owns all creation, the heavens and the earths and those in them and between them, known and unknown, and that his Lord is like nothing. Under "the interpretation of 'the worlds'" he says "alamun" is the plural of "alam", a plural with no singular of its own form, like "anam", "rahṭ" and "jaysh"; it is a name for the kinds of nations, each kind being a "world" and the people of each age within each kind being the "world" of that age, so humankind is a world, the jinn a world, and likewise the other kinds. He cites a line of al-Ajjaj that makes a tribe the head of this "world", and says this is the view of Ibn Abbas and Sa'id ibn Jubayr and the sense of what most commentators say. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 143}
He then gives reports: from Ibn Abbas through al-Dahhak that it is all creation; from Ikrima and Sa'id ibn Jubayr through Ibn Abbas that it is the Lord of jinn and humans; from Sa'id ibn Jubayr that each nation among them is a world by itself; from Mujahid that it is humans and jinn; and from Qatada that every kind is a world. The editor judges some chains sound and others weak, noting that Sufyan al-Thawri's transmission from Mujahid is cut because al-Thawri was born only shortly before Mujahid died, and that the commentary of Ata ibn Dinar from Sa'id ibn Jubayr is a written sheet he took from the register. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 144} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 145}
He gives a report from Abu al-Aliya that humans are a world, the jinn a world, and beyond them eighteen thousand or fourteen thousand worlds (the narrator is unsure) of angels on the earth, with a quarter of the earth in each corner holding three and a half thousand worlds, created for His worship; and one from Ibn Jurayj that it is jinn and humans. The editor quotes Ibn Kathir's judgement that Abu al-Aliya's report is strange and needs a sound proof, and agrees. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 146}
