**Commentary on verse 198 (pp. 324-326)**

God says to His prophet Muhammad: say to the polytheists, if you call your gods, polytheists, to guidance, which is straightness to what is right, they will not hear, meaning they will not hear your call, "and you see them looking at you but they do not see". This is address from God to His prophet: you see, Muhammad, their gods looking at you though they do not see, and that is why it is singular; had the Prophet been commanded to address the polytheists, it would have said "and you see them looking at you" in the plural. The editor explains that the address was first to the polytheists all together and then turned to the singular for the Messenger of God, and that the manuscript has a blank of about a word. Report 15533, from al-Suddi: "if you call them to guidance they do not hear, and you see them looking at you but they do not see", he said these are the polytheists, which may mean by "these are the polytheists" God's words "if you call them to guidance they do not hear". Mujahid used to say, in report 15534, of "and you see them looking at you but they do not see": when you call them to guidance. It is as if Mujahid took the sense to be that you see the polytheists looking at you though they do not see, which is one reading, but the speech is in the course of reporting about the gods and so fits their description better. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 324} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 325}

If someone says: what is the sense of "you see them looking at you but they do not see", and can something look at something and not see it? He answers: the Arabs say of a thing that faces or is level with another that it "looks at" it, and one says "so-and-so's house looks at my house" when it faces it; it is reported of them "when you come to such and such a place and the mountain looks at you, take right or left"; and I was told from Abu Ubayd that al-Kisa'i said "the wall looks at you" when it is near you where you see it, and from this the verse of the poet saying when the lands of the sons of so-and-so or of the sons of Sabah "looked" with an eye, meaning their herbage faced and lay level. So the sense is: you see, Muhammad, the gods of these idol-worshipping polytheists facing you and level with you though they do not see you, since they have no eyes. It said "them" and not "it" because they are images shaped on the forms of the children of Adam. The editor says he did not know the poet, that the line is in Abu Zayd's Nawadir, the Asas al-Balagha and the Maqayis, and sets out what al-Zamakhshari and Ibn Faris say of the land that "looks" when its herbage shows, all taken as metaphor and likeness. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 325} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 326}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
