## Summary
Verse 39 of [[passage/quran-8|Surat al-Anfal]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 39 of 75 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**Commentary on verse 39 (pp. 537-544)**

Al-Tabari says on "fight them until there is no trial and the religion is all God's; and if they stop, God sees what they do" [[passage/quran-8-39|8:39]]: he says to the believers, if these return to war on you, you have seen My way with whoever fought you of them at Badr, and I will return with the like against whoever makes war on you of them, so fight them until there is no polytheism and none is worshipped but God alone with no partner, so that affliction is lifted from God's servants on earth, which is the trial; "and the religion is all God's" meaning until obedience and worship are all pure for God and none other. The editor refers to the earlier commentary on "trial" and "religion". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 537} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 538}

The interpreters spoke likewise; report 16076, from Ibn Abbas: until there is no polytheism; report 16077, from al-Hasan: the trial is polytheism; report 16078, from Qatada: until there is no polytheism and the religion is all God's, until it is said, there is no god but God, for which God's Prophet fought and to which he called; report 16079, from al-Suddi: until there is no polytheism; report 16080, from al-Hasan: until there is no affliction; report 16081, from Ibn Jurayj: that no believer be tempted from his religion and that oneness belongs to God purely with no partner in it, and that what is besides Him of rivals is cast off, the editor thinking this report is stitched from two reports, the text of the first lost and the text of Ibn Hisham remaining for the second; report 16082, from Ibn Zayd: until there is no unbelief, and the religion is all God's, so that there is no unbelief alongside your religion. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 538} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 539}

Report 16083, from Hisham ibn Urwa from his father: Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan wrote to him asking about several things, and Urwa wrote back about the Prophet's leaving Mecca, beginning with praise of God and describing the Prophet in the best terms with prayer for him, then said: God gave him prophethood, and when he called his people to the guidance and light God sent him with, they did not at first withdraw from what he called them to and almost listened to him, until he mentioned their idols; people of Quraysh from al-Ta'if with wealth came, some people denied that and were hard on him, disliked what he said and set on those who obeyed them against him, so most people turned away from him and left him, except those God kept from among them, and they were few. He stayed so for as long as God decreed, then their chiefs plotted to tempt those who followed him from God's religion among their sons, brothers and tribes, and it was a trial of violent shaking, so some were tempted and God protected whom He willed. When that was done to the Muslims, the Prophet told them to leave for the land of Abyssinia, where there was a righteous king called the Negus in whose land none was wronged, and who was praised for his goodness; the land of Abyssinia was a trading place for Quraysh and a dwelling for their merchants, who found ease of provision, safety and good trade there, so the Prophet ordered them to it and most of them went there when they were overpowered at Mecca and he feared the trial for them, while he stayed and did not leave. The editor corrects readings of the printed edition by the manuscript and al-Tabari's History, and explains "turned away from him" and "ease". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 539} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 540}

The rest of report 16083: he stayed so for years, they being hard on those of them who became Muslim, then Islam spread among them and men of their nobles and protectors entered it, and when they saw that they relaxed their hold on the Prophet and his Companions. The first trial was the one that sent those who went out of the Companions to the land of Abyssinia, fearing it and fleeing from the trials and shaking they were in; when they were relaxed on and some entered Islam, talk went round of their relaxing, and it reached those in Abyssinia, so they returned to Mecca and almost felt safe there, and they kept growing and increasing. Many of the Ansar at Medina became Muslim and Islam spread in Medina, and the people of Medina began to come to the Prophet at Mecca; when Quraysh saw that, they urged each other on to tempt them and be hard on them, and seized them and were keen to tempt them, and severe hardship struck them, and that was the last trial. So there were two: a trial that sent out those who went to Abyssinia, and a trial when they returned and saw those who came to them from Medina. Then seventy leaders, heads of those who became Muslim, came to the Prophet from Medina, met him at the pilgrimage and pledged to him at al-Aqaba, giving their covenants that we are of you and you are of us and that whoever of your companions comes to us, we will protect you as we protect ourselves; and Quraysh grew harder on them then, and the Prophet told his Companions to leave for Medina, which is the last trial in which he took his Companions out, and he went out, and it is the one about which God sent down the verse. The editor explains "protectors", "relaxing" and "urging", corrects the printed edition from al-Tabari's History and Ibn Kathir, and notes that al-Tabari gave Urwa's letter in pieces in his commentary and his History, and that he hopes someone will gather its pieces to bring out Urwa's letter to Abd al-Malik whole, being among the first things written on the Prophet's biography. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 541} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 542}

Report 16084, from Urwa ibn al-Zubayr: that he wrote to al-Walid in answer to his question about the Prophet's leaving Mecca, then gave something like it. The editor identifies Abd al-Rahman ibn Abi al-Zinad, whom al-Tirmidhi held trustworthy and Ibn Ma'in, Ahmad, Ibn al-Madini and Ibn Sa'd weakened, and says this report has the addressee as al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik while the earlier chain is sounder, so he fears this is among what Ibn Abi al-Zinad's report from his father is confused in. Report 16085, from Mujahid on "fight them until there is no trial": Isaf and Na'ila, two idols that were worshipped. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 542} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 543}

As for "if they stop", its meaning is: if they stop the trial, which is polytheism toward God, and come to the true religion with you, "God sees what they do", meaning that God is not hidden from what they do of leaving unbelief and entering the religion of Islam, for He sees them and sees your deeds, and all things are plain to Him, not absent from Him, nor does an atom's weight escape Him in the heavens or the earth, nor smaller nor greater, but is in a clear book. Some said its meaning is: if they stop fighting. Abu Ja'far said: what we said is the better, because even if the polytheists stop fighting, it was an obligation on the believers to fight them until they became Muslim. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 543} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 544}

We stop here at the foot of p. 544, in the commentary on 8:39; the commentary on 8:40 begins on this page and is in the 8:40 file. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 544}
