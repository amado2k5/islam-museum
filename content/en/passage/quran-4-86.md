## Summary
Verse 86 of [[passage/quran-4|Surat an-Nisa]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 86 of 176 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**Commentary on the opening of the verse (p. 586, notes 1-3).** Al-Tabari says the greeting means that long life, survival and safety are invoked for a person; whoever has this invoked for him should invoke for the one who did so something finer than he invoked, or give the greeting back as it was. The editor explains that the sense of the word is survival and safety from afflictions; that the manuscript has an unclear phrase which the first printer handled acceptably; that the print changed a sentence while the manuscript is right; and that the copyist wrote a verb with an alif after the waw, which led to the error. Al-Tabari then records the interpreters' disagreement about the finer form and the equal form. Some said the finer is for the person greeted, told "peace be upon you", to say "and upon you peace and God's mercy", adding to the invocation, and the return is to say the same as was said to him, or "and upon you peace". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 586}

**Reports 10033-10038 (pp. 586-587).** Muhammad b. al-Husayn (10033, from Ahmad b. Mufaddal, from Asbat) reports from al-Suddi that when someone greets you, say "and upon you peace and God's mercy", or confine yourself to "peace be upon you" as he said to you. Al-Qasim (10034, from al-Husayn, from Hajjaj, from Ibn Jurayj) reports from Ata' that this concerns the people of Islam, and al-Muthanna (10035, from Suwayd, from Ibn al-Mubarak, from Ibn Jurayj, as read to him) the same from Ata'. Ibn Waki' (10036, from his father, from Sufyan, from Abu Ishaq) reports that Shurayh used to answer with "peace be upon you" as he was greeted; (10037, from his father, from Ibn Awn and Isma'il b. Abi Khalid) that Ibrahim used to answer "peace and God's mercy be upon you"; and (10038, from his father, from Sufyan, from Atiyya) that Ibn Umar used to answer "and upon you". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 587}

**Second view and reports 10039-10043 (pp. 587-588).** Others said the sense is that the Muslims answer Muslims with something finer, and answer the people of disbelief with the like. Ishaq b. Ibrahim b. Habib b. al-Shahid (10039, from Humayd b. Abd al-Rahman, from al-Hasan b. Salih, from Simak, from Ikrima) reports from Ibn Abbas that whoever among God's creation greets you, answer him even if he is a Zoroastrian, citing the verse. Muhammad b. Bashshar (10040, from Salim b. Nuh, from Sa'id b. Abi Aruba) reports from Qatada that the finer is for Muslims and the like for the People of the Book, and Bishr b. Mu'adh (10041 and 10042, from Yazid, from Sa'id) the same from Qatada. Yunus (10043, from Ibn Wahb) reports from Ibn Zayd that he heard his father say it is a duty of every Muslim greeted with a greeting to greet with something finer, and if someone not of Islam greets him, to answer with what he said. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 588}

**Al-Tabari's choice (p. 588, note 1).** Abu Ja'far al-Tabari says the better of the two interpretations is that of those who said this concerns the people of Islam, so a Muslim answers a Muslim with a greeting finer than his or like it. He reasons that the sound reports from the Prophet make it obligatory for every Muslim to answer any disbeliever's greeting with less than his greeting, and that God commanded in the verse the finer or the like without distinguishing, with no evidence that would make sound the view assigning the finer to Muslims and the like to the people of disbelief. He holds that the choice belongs to the one greeted between the finer and the like, except where the sunna specifies, and that the sunna specified the people of disbelief by forbidding the finer or the like, allowing only "and upon you", so none should exceed the limit the Prophet set, while for Muslims the one greeted has the choice God gave. This is al-Tabari's choice; the museum gives no verdict. The editor says the clause on a binding report is printed one way and in the manuscript another, both unsound, and that he preferred what he gave as nearer to the context. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 588}

**Report 10044 from Salman al-Farisi (p. 589, note 1).** Al-Tabari says a report from the Prophet on this matter agrees with what he said. Musa b. Sahl al-Ramli (10044, from Abd Allah b. al-Sari al-Antaki, from Hisham b. Lahiq, from Asim al-Ahwal, from Abu Uthman al-Nahdi) reports from Salman al-Farisi that a man came to the Prophet and said "peace be upon you, Messenger of God", and he answered "and upon you, and God's mercy"; another came and added "and God's mercy" and he said "and upon you, and God's mercy and blessings"; another added "and blessings" and he said "and upon you". The man said: so-and-so and so-and-so came, greeted you and you answered them with more than you answered me! He said: you left us nothing, for God commanded answering with something finer or giving the greeting back, and we gave it back to you. The editor says Abd Allah b. al-Sari is weak and was a righteous man, as they said; quotes Abu Nu'aym that he narrates objectionable reports, and Ibn Hibban that he narrated from Abu Imran wonders that are certainly fabricated; refers to al-Tahdhib and Ibn Abi Hatim; and says he was not alone in narrating it from Hisham b. Lahiq. On Hisham b. Lahiq there is disagreement: Ahmad says he wrote hadiths from him and there was no harm, but that he raised from Asim hadiths that were not raised, tracing them to Salman, and Shababa objected to a hadith of his; the editor refers to al-Bukhari's Tarikh, Ibn Abi Hatim and Lisan al-Mizan (al-Nasa'i strengthened him, Ibn Hibban listed him among both the reliable and the weak, and Ibn Adi said his hadiths are fine and he hoped no harm), and concludes that the criticism concerns error and not doubt of his truthfulness, and that his hadith is apparently good (hasan). (The note continues on p. 590.) These reports and the editor's judgements are given as transmitted; the museum gives no verdict. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 589}

**Rest of the note: tracing the report (p. 590).** The editor says Ibn Kathir mentioned the report from this place in al-Tabari, then quoted Ibn Abi Hatim as narrating it, with the chain suspended, through Abd Allah b. al-Sari al-Antaki with the same chain, and then Ibn Mardawayh through Abd Allah b. Ahmad b. Hanbal from his father from Hisham b. Lahiq; Ibn Kathir said he did not see it in the Musnad, and the editor agrees. He says al-Suyuti cited it in al-Durr al-Manthur and ascribed it to Ahmad in al-Zuhd, to Ibn al-Mundhir and to al-Tabarani, with a good chain, and that al-Haythami in Majma' al-Zawa'id said al-Tabarani narrated it, with Hisham b. Lahiq in it, strengthened by al-Nasa'i whose hadith Ahmad left, and the other men being men of the Sahih; the editor holds that al-Haythami's statement that Ahmad left Hisham's hadith is not good, since the text established from Ahmad in al-Bukhari and Ibn Abi Hatim does not show it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 590}

**Obligation to answer a greeting and reports 10045-10046 (p. 590, note 1).** Al-Tabari asks whether answering the greeting is obligatory as God commanded in His book, and answers yes, as a group of the earlier authorities said. Al-Muthanna (10045, from Suwayd, from Ibn al-Mubarak, from Ibn Jurayj) reports from Abu al-Zubayr that he heard Jabir b. Abd Allah say he saw him only treating it as obligatory (the editor: that is, obligatory to answer the greeting), and al-Muthanna (10046, from Suwayd b. Nasr, from Ibn al-Mubarak, from Sufyan, from a man) reports from al-Hasan that giving the greeting is voluntary and answering it is a duty. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 590}

**End of the verse (pp. 591-592, notes 1-3, 1).** Al-Tabari says the sense is that God is guardian over everything you do, obedience and disobedience, until He repays you. Muhammad b. Amr (10047, from Abu Asim, from Isa, from Ibn Abi Najih) reports from Mujahid that the word means guardian, and al-Muthanna (10048, from Abu Hudhayfa, from Shibl) the same. Al-Tabari holds that the origin of this attribute here is a form on the pattern of "fa'il" from the noun for reckoning, which means counting; one says "I took account of so-and-so for such and such" and he is his accountant when he is the keeper of his account. Some Basran language scholars (the editor says it is Abu Ubayda in Majaz al-Qur'an) claimed it means "sufficient", from "the thing sufficed me"; al-Tabari calls this an error, since one does not say the verb in a form with "upon" to produce this noun, and one says only that He is his sufficiency and his accountant. The editor refers to the earlier explanation of this word and of reckoning (page numbers garbled and not given) and says print and manuscript have unsound verb forms in two places and that his reading follows the context. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 592}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 592}
