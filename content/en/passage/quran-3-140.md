## Summary
Verse 140 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 140 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**The difference in reading (p. 236).** Abu Ja'far says the reciters differed in reading this place. Most reciters of the Hijaz, Medina and Basra read it with a fatha on the first letter of the word for the wound in both places, meaning killing and wounds: if killing and wounds touch you, Companions of Muhammad, the same has touched the people of your polytheist enemies. Most reciters of Kufa read it with a damma in both places (the bracketed words are the editor's addition, which he inferred from the context). The editor notes that a passage has dropped out through the copyist, the explanation of the damma reading, and that al-Tabari perhaps cited here what al-Farra' said in Ma'ani al-Qur'an (1: 234); the note continues on the next page. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 236}

**Close of the note on the two readings (p. 237).** The editor finishes his quotation from al-Farra': the companions of Abd Allah (ibn Mas'ud) read the word with a damma, as if the damma form meant the pain of the wounds and the fatha form meant the wounds themselves. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 237}

**Al-Tabari's choice between the readings (p. 237).** Abu Ja'far says the sounder of the two readings, in his view, is the fatha in both places, because the interpreters agreed that the sense is killing and wounds, which shows the reading is the fatha. Some of the grammarians held that the two forms are two dialectal variants with one meaning; what is known among those who know the speech of the Arabs, he says, is what he stated. The editor notes that his wording here points to a gap in al-Tabari's text. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 237}

**Wounds and killing (pp. 237-238).**
- Muhammad ibn Amr from Abu Asim from Isa from Ibn Abi Najih from Mujahid (7893), and al-Muthanna from Abu Hudhayfa from Shibl from Ibn Abi Najih from Mujahid (7894): wounds and killing. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 237}
- Muhammad ibn Sinan from Abu Bakr al-Hanafi from Abbad from al-Hasan (7895): if they kill some of you at Uhud, you killed some of them at Badr. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 237}
- Bishr from Yazid from Sa'id from Qatada (7896): the word means the wound, and that was Uhud, when killing and wounding spread among the Prophet's Companions; God informed them that the people had suffered the like, and that what struck them was a punishment. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 237}
- Al-Muthanna from Ishaq from Ibn Abi Ja'far from his father from al-Rabi' (7897): that was Uhud; the report is a consolation to the Companions of Muhammad and an urging to fight. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 238}
- Muhammad ibn al-Husayn from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal from Asbat from al-Suddi (7898): the word means the wounds. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 238}
- Ibn Humayd from Salama from Ibn Ishaq (7899): wounds, and the like for the people. The editor says the report is in Ibn Hisham's Sira (3: 116) and continues the reports ending at 7891. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 238}
- Al-Muthanna from Ishaq from Hafs ibn Umar from al-Hakam ibn Aban from Ikrima from Ibn Abbas (7900): the Muslims slept with their wounds, meaning on the day of Uhud; Ikrima said this verse and [[passage/quran-4-104|4:104]] were sent down about them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 238}

**The meaning of "touch" (p. 238).** Abu Ja'far says "touch" here means "befall"; Muhammad ibn Sa'd from his father, his uncle, his father and grandfather from Ibn Abbas (7901) gives the same. The editor refers to the earlier explanation of the word. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 238}

**The alternation of the days (p. 239).** Abu Ja'far says the days meant are the days of Badr and Uhud, and that God causes them to alternate among the people, who are the Muslims and the polytheists: at Badr God gave the Muslims the turn, so that they killed seventy and captured seventy, and at Uhud He gave the polytheists the turn, so that they killed seventy of the Muslims besides the wounded. He explains the expression "God gave a man the turn against another" as prevailing over him and taking back what he had suffered. The editor notes that the bracketed words are his addition required by the context of al-Tabari's explanation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 239}

**Reports on the alternation (pp. 239-241).**
- Al-Hasan (7902): God made the days alternate, and gave the unbelievers the turn over the Prophet's Companions at Uhud. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 239}
- Qatada (7903): without the alternations the believers would not be harmed, but the unbeliever has the turn against the believer and the believer is tried through the unbeliever, so God may know who obeys Him and who disobeys, and the truthful from the liar. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 239}
- Al-Rabi' (7904): God gave His Prophet and Companions victory over the polytheists at Badr and gave the enemy the upper hand at Uhud; what befell the Muslims at Uhud was a punishment for disobeying the Messenger. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 240}
- Al-Suddi (7905): a day for you and a day against you. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 240}
- Ibn Abbas, by two chains (7906, 7907): God gave the polytheists the turn over the Prophet at Uhud; the second says Uhud answered Badr, the believers were killed at Uhud and God took martyrs from them, while the Messenger prevailed over the polytheists at Badr. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 240}
- Ikrima from Ibn Abbas (7908): when the fighting of Uhud ended and the Muslims had suffered, the Prophet climbed the mountain; Abu Sufyan came calling that war goes by turns, a day for us and a day for you. The Prophet told his Companions to answer, and they said the two sides were not equal, their dead in paradise and the other side's dead in the fire. Abu Sufyan then invoked al-Uzza and Hubal, and the Prophet had them answer that God is their protector and that He is higher and more majestic; Abu Sufyan ended by naming the Lesser Badr as the meeting place. Ikrima said the verse was sent down about them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 240}
- Ibn Jurayj from Ibn Abbas (7909): God gave the turn over the Prophet at Uhud. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 241}
- Ibn Ishaq (7910): He turns them among people for trial and purification; the editor says the report is in Ibn Hisham (3: 116-117) and continues 7899. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 241}
- Muhammad, that is Ibn Sirin, through Ibn Awn and Hammad ibn Zayd (7911): it means the rulers. The editor gives notes on Ibrahim ibn Abd Allah (said to be Abu Shayba al-Absi, died 265) and on Abd Allah ibn Abd al-Wahhab al-Hajabi (who narrated from Malik and Hammad ibn Zayd, whom al-Bukhari narrated from, died 228), and states that "Muhammad" is Ibn Sirin. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 241}

**Knowing and believing in the clause (pp. 241-242).** Abu Ja'far says the sense is that God sets the days in turn so as to distinguish the believers, and that the conjunction shows the speech is joined to what precedes it and awaits a following statement, with the lam attached to it. The editor notes that the conjunction dropped out of the printed text and the manuscript and that he restored it, and he follows the manuscript elsewhere. Al-Tabari then raises an objection: one does not say "I asked and I knew Abd Allah" meaning his person, only his description, so how can knowing take a definite noun? He answers that "those who" carries the sense of "who" and "which of", as does the definite article, citing [[passage/quran-29-3|29:3]] and [[passage/quran-18-12|18:12]], and likewise "I asked to know Abd Allah from Amr", meaning to tell one from the other. The word for "which" is put in the nominative, while the article, "the one who" and "who", when placed in its stead, take the accusative because the knowing falls on them. The editor refers to al-Farra' (Ma'ani al-Qur'an 1: 234-235) and flags two scribal slips against the recitation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 242}

**Meaning of the whole (pp. 242-243).** Abu Ja'far says the sense is that God alternates the days among people so that the believers among you may be distinguished from the hypocrites, and the second term is dropped because the speech shows it, as if it were said: so that it may be clear which of you is the believer. On taking martyrs, it means honouring with martyrdom whom He wishes to honour; "martyrs" is the plural of "martyr" (the editor refers to the earlier explanation). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 243}
- Ibn Ishaq (7912): He separates the believers from the hypocrites and honours with martyrdom those of the people of faith He chooses; Ibn Hisham (3: 117), continuing 7910. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 243}
- Ibn Jurayj (7913): the Muslims used to ask their Lord for a day like Badr on which to fight the polytheists, try themselves well and seek martyrdom; they met the polytheists at Uhud and God took martyrs from them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 243}
- Qatada (7914): God honoured His friends with martyrdom at the hands of their enemy, and the outcomes of affairs finally return to the people of obedience to Him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 243}
- Ibn Abbas (7915) and al-Dahhak (7916): they used to ask for martyrdom, met the polytheists at Uhud and God took martyrs from them, those to whom [[passage/quran-2-154|2:154]] refers. In a note the editor says the printed text differs from the manuscript, that he judged "the polytheists" the right reading over "the Muslims" because the copyist often slipped, and that al-Suyuti (al-Durr al-manthur 2: 79) dropped the word. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 244}

**The wrongdoers (p. 244).** Abu Ja'far says those meant are the ones who wronged themselves by disobeying their Lord. Ibn Ishaq (7917) says they are the hypocrites who show obedience on their tongues while their hearts persist in disobedience (Ibn Hisham 3: 117, continuing 7912). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 244}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
