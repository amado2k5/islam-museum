## Summary
Verse 8 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 8 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

## Verse 8 in al-Tabari's commentary (Shakir ed., volume 6, pp. 211-218)

For [[passage/quran-3-8|3:8]] al-Tabari says that the rooted, having believed in the resembling and that it and the firm are from their Lord's sending down and revelation, also address their Lord out of desire that He turn away from them what afflicted those whose hearts swerved, who followed the resembling to seek discord and to seek its interpretation that only God knows: In his gloss the plea means: do not make us like those whose hearts swerved from the truth and so barred others from Your path, and do not turn our hearts away from the guidance by which You gave us success in believing in the firm and the resembling of Your Book. He explains the request for mercy from God's presence as a request for success and steadfastness in what they hold, affirming both the firm and the resembling, and the close of the verse as meaning that God is the one who gives His servants success and rightness in holding to His religion and in believing His Book and messengers. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 211} In 6649, Muhammad ibn Jafar ibn al-Zubayr (through Ibn Humayd, Salama and Ibn Ishaq): that is, do not turn our hearts even if we incline by our deeds. The editor says the print wrote 'by our bodies', which has no meaning and is a corruption of the report from Ibn Ishaq; the right wording is from the manuscript and the Sira of Ibn Hisham (2: 226); the word means acts, so they ask God to keep their hearts firm in faith even if their deeds incline to some disobedience. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 212}

Al-Tabari then says that God's praise of these people for asking that He not turn their hearts, and that He give them mercy from Himself to help them stay firm in their sound insight into the truth, shows the error of those he calls the ignorant among the Qadariyya, who say that God's turning the heart of a servant from obedience, and inclining it away, is injustice. His argument is that if it were as they say, those who made this plea would deserve blame rather than praise, since they would be asking their Lord not to wrong them, which would be ignorance in the asker because God does not wrong His servants; He told them so and denied it of Himself ([[passage/quran-41-46|41:46]]), so there is no reason to ask Him to be as He has told them He is. He holds that the falsity of their claim is clear evidence that God's turning of the heart of one whose heart He turns is just, and that whoever asked not to be turned deserved praise for directing his desire to the one who can answer and placing his request where it belongs, with many reports of the Prophet's own request to his Lord for this. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 213} This is al-Tabari's argument in a question of theology; the museum reports it as his and gives no verdict. In a note (p. 212) the editor says the Qadariyya are the deniers of destiny and of the attributes and that he means the Mu'tazila, and that the print wrote a word without its preposition, the right form being in the manuscript.

In 6650, Umm Salama (through Abu Kurayb, Waki, Abd al-Hamid ibn Bahram and Shahr ibn Hawshab): the Messenger said, 'O turner of hearts, make my heart firm on Your religion,' and then recited the verse ([[passage/quran-3-8|3:8]]) to its end. In 6651, Asma, by the same route, in similar words. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 213} Of the first the editor says the chain is sound, that Abd al-Hamid ibn Bahram and Shahr ibn Hawshab are reliable in his view, and that the report is abridged and comes at greater length in 6652 and abridged in 6658. Of the second he says the chain is also sound, but that here Shahr narrates from Asma bint Yazid ibn al-Sakan al-Ansariyya, where the one before has him narrating from Umm Salama, the Mother of the Believers; that he found it from Asma only in this report of al-Tabari and in one Ibn Kathir mentions (2: 102) from Ibn Mardawayh through Muhammad ibn Bakkar, in which the chain reads 'from Umm Salama, from Asma bint Yazid'; and that Ibn Kathir adds that al-Tabari also relates it from Asad ibn Musa. The editor holds that 'from Umm Salama, from Asma' in Ibn Mardawayh's report is beyond doubt an error, apparently the scribes' addition of the word 'from', the right reading being 'Umm Salama Asma'. He notes that Asma bint Yazid ibn al-Sakan al-Ansariyya is a well-known Companion, a cousin of Mu'adh ibn Jabal whose byname was Umm Salama; that Shahr was known for narrating from her, Ibn al-Sakan calling him the one who narrated most from her, and that he was her client; and that he has never heard that the Mother of the Believers narrated from this Asma. He finds Ibn Kathir's pointing to al-Tabari's two reports through Asad ibn Musa and al-Hajjaj ibn al-Minhal problematic, since it suggests they are like Ibn Mardawayh's, and thinks Ibn Kathir took 'Umm Salama' in the three reports (6650, 6652, 6658) to be Asma; if so he erred, since in all three she is the Mother of the Believers beyond doubt. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 214}

In 6652, Shahr ibn Hawshab said he heard Umm Salama relate (through al-Muthanna, al-Hajjaj ibn al-Minhal and Abd al-Hamid ibn Bahram al-Fazari) that the Messenger often said in his supplication, 'O God, turner of hearts, make my heart firm on Your religion.' She said: I asked, O Messenger of God, can the heart be turned? He said: yes, there is no human being God created but his heart is between two of His fingers; if He wills He sets it upright and if He wills He turns it. He then voiced the plea of the rooted in the first person plural, asking his Lord for steadfastness and mercy from Himself. She said: will you not teach me a prayer to pray for myself? He said: yes, say, 'O God, Lord of the Prophet Muhammad, forgive my sin, remove the anger of my heart, and protect me from misleading trials.' {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 214} This is the longer report the editor pointed to, and it uses the language of fingers as it was transmitted; the museum passes it on as reported, with no verdict. The editor notes that Ahmad gave it abridged in the Musnad of Umm Salama (6: 294) from Waki, that Abu Kurayb added the reading of the verse, that Ahmad also gave it (6: 301-302) through Hashim ibn al-Qasim Abu al-Nadr from Abd al-Hamid with an ending 'as long as You keep us alive', and (6: 315) abridged through Mu'adh ibn Mu'adh from Abu Ka'b the silk-seller from Shahr; that Abd Allah ibn Ahmad asked his father about Abu Ka'b, who said he was reliable and named him Abd Rabbih ibn Ubayd; that al-Tirmidhi gave it (4: 266) through Abu Musa al-Ansari from Mu'adh ibn Mu'adh and called it good; and that Abu Ka'b, Abd Rabbih ibn Ubayd al-Azdi al-Jarmuzi, was declared reliable by Yahya ibn Said, Ibn Ma'in and others. He records that al-Haythami gave it three times in Majma al-Zawaid (10: 176, 7: 210, 6: 325), faulting it in two places for Shahr ibn Hawshab, 'weak though declared reliable', and saying of the last that its chain is good; that al-Suyuti (2: 8) added Ibn Abi Shayba; that Ibn Khuzayma gave it in Kitab al-Tawhid (p. 55) through Ibn Wahb, Ibrahim ibn Nashit al-Wa'lani and Abd Allah ibn Abd al-Rahman ibn Abi Husayn al-Makki from Shahr from Umm Salama in similar words, a sound chain; that al-Ajurri gave it in al-Shari'a (p. 316) by two other routes; and that the print had transposed a phrase where he kept the manuscript's order. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 215}

In 6653, Jabir (through Muhammad ibn Mansur al-Tusi, Muhammad ibn Abd Allah al-Zubayri, Sufyan, al-A'mash and Abu Sufyan): the Messenger often said, 'O turner of hearts, make my heart firm on Your religion,' and one of his household said to him: do you fear for us when we have believed in you and what you brought? He said: the heart is between two of the fingers of the Merciful, who does so with them, and Abu Ahmad moved his two fingers; Abu Jafar (al-Tabari) said that al-Tusi pressed his two fingers together. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 215} The editor says al-Tusi is Muhammad ibn Mansur ibn Dawud, the devout, al-Tabari's teacher, reliable, praised by Ahmad and declared reliable by al-Nasa'i and others; that the manuscript's wording is the right one and the print's differs; and that the verb for the gesture means to gather, that is, he pressed his two fingers together. He adds that al-Hakim gave it in al-Mustadrak (2: 288-289) through al-A'mash and graded it sound on Muslim's condition, though the start of the chain from al-Hakim to al-A'mash is not given because the manuscripts of the Mustadrak have a lacuna there, which the publisher filled from al-Dhahabi's abridgement; that al-Suyuti (2: 9) added al-Tabarani in al-Sunna; and that al-Tirmidhi alluded to it (3: 199). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 216}

In 6654, Anas (through Said ibn Yahya al-Umawi, Abu Mu'awiya, al-A'mash and Abu Sufyan): the Messenger often said, 'O turner of hearts, make my heart firm on Your religion.' We said: we have believed in you and affirmed what you brought, and is it feared for us? He said: yes, hearts are between two of the fingers of God, who turns them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 216} The editor says Ahmad gave it in the Musnad (numbers 12133 and 13731) and al-Tirmidhi (3: 199) through Hannad from Abu Mu'awiya, calling it good and sound, and noting that more than one narrated it from al-A'mash from Abu Sufyan from Anas, that some narrated it from Jabir, and that Abu Sufyan's report from Anas is sounder. The editor takes this as an attempt to fault the hadith before it, and says the fault does not stand: Abu Sufyan Talha ibn Nafi is a reliable Follower who heard from both Jabir and Anas and is used by the compilers of the six books, and a Follower often hears one hadith from two Companions. He adds that al-Hakim gave it abridged (1: 526) and graded it sound along with al-Dhahabi; that Ibn Majah gave it at greater length (number 3834) by another route, through al-A'mash from Yazid al-Raqashi from Anas, and that al-Busiri said in his additions that the hadith turns on Yazid al-Raqashi, who is weak, which the editor calls a slip since it also comes through al-A'mash from Abu Sufyan from Anas, so al-Raqashi is not alone in it; that al-Bukhari joined the two routes in al-Adab al-Mufrad (p. 100); and that al-Suyuti (2: 8) added Ibn Abi Shayba.

In 6655, al-Nawwas ibn Sam'an al-Kilabi (through Muhammad ibn Abd Allah ibn Abd al-Hakam from Bishr ibn Bakr, and Ali ibn Sahl from Ayyub ibn Bishr, both from Ibn Jabir, from Busr ibn Ubayd Allah, from Abu Idris al-Khawlani): I heard the Messenger say, 'There is no heart but is between two of the fingers of the Merciful; if He wills He sets it upright and if He wills He turns it.' He used to say, 'O turner of hearts, make our hearts firm on Your religion,' and 'the balance is in the hand of the Merciful, raising some peoples and lowering others until the Day of Resurrection.' {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 217} The editor says Bishr ibn Bakr al-Tinnisi is reliable and trusted, that al-Shafi'i and al-Humaydi narrated from him and al-Bukhari used him; that he found no narrator named Ayyub ibn Bishr, only Ayyub ibn Bashir, who does not belong to this class, and that it may be Ayyub ibn Suwayd al-Ramli, who narrates from Ibn Jabir and from whom his townsman Ali ibn Sahl al-Ramli could narrate, though a corruption of 'Suwayd' into 'Bishr' is hard; that Ibn Jabir is Abd al-Rahman ibn Yazid ibn Jabir al-Azdi al-Shami al-Dara'ani, reliable and used by the compilers, whom Ibn al-Madini placed in the second class of the jurists of Syria after the Companions; that Busr ibn Ubayd Allah al-Hadrami is a reliable Follower whom Abu Mushir called the best preserver of Abu Idris al-Khawlani's reports, and that the print wrote his name 'Bishr', a misspelling, while some references give his father's name as Abd Allah, also an error; that Abu Idris al-Khawlani is A'idh Allah ibn Abd Allah, already treated, and al-Nawwas a well-known Companion. He lists Ahmad (Musnad number 17707) through al-Walid ibn Muslim from Ibn Jabir; Ibn Majah (number 199) through Sadaqa ibn Khalid, with al-Busiri calling the chain sound; Ibn Khuzayma in al-Tawhid (p. 54) and al-Ajurri in al-Shari'a (pp. 317-318) through al-Walid; and al-Hakim in the Mustadrak (2: 289) and al-Bayhaqi in al-Asma wa-l-Sifat (p. 248) through Muhammad ibn Shu'ayb ibn Shabur from Ibn Jabir, which al-Hakim and al-Dhahabi graded sound on the condition of the two shaykhs. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 217} He adds that al-Hakim also gave it (4: 321 and 1: 525) through al-Asamm, by al-Tabari's own teacher's route and through Bahr ibn Nasr from Bishr ibn Bakr, calling it sound on Muslim's condition; the editor finds it strange that al-Dhahabi agreed, since Muslim did not use Bishr ibn Bakr and neither of the two shaykhs used Ibn Shabur; and that al-Suyuti (2: 9) added al-Nasa'i in the Sunan al-Kubra. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 218}

In 6656, Samura ibn Fatik al-Asadi, a Companion of the Messenger (through Umar ibn Abd al-Malik al-Ta'i, Muhammad ibn Ubayda, al-Jarrah ibn Malih al-Bahrani, al-Zubaydi and Juwaybir), from the Prophet: the balances are in God's hand, raising some peoples and lowering others, and the heart of Adam's son is between two of the fingers of the Merciful; when He wills He turns it, and when He wills He sets it upright. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 218} The editor says Umar ibn Abd al-Malik ibn Hakim al-Ta'i al-Himsi, al-Tabari's teacher, has only a brief entry in al-Tahdhib saying al-Nasa'i narrated from him and called him sound; that he does not know who Muhammad ibn Ubayda is and found no entry for him beyond the Tahdhib naming him as Umar's teacher, and that the attribution given there has no meaning and may be a corruption of one naming a village in Yemen twenty miles from Sanaa, as in Mu'jam al-Buldan; that al-Jarrah ibn Malih al-Bahrani al-Himsi is reliable and well known among the Syrians and is not the father of Waki ibn al-Jarrah; that al-Zubaydi is Muhammad ibn al-Walid ibn Amir Abu al-Hudhayl, judge of Homs, reliable and firm, whom Ibn Sad called the most learned of the Syrians in fatwa and hadith and whom al-Awza'i preferred above all who heard from al-Zuhri; and that 'Juwaybir', as it stands in al-Tabari, is probably a scribes' corruption, since Juwaybir ibn Said al-Azdi is very weak and has no part in this hadith, which is known from Jubayr ibn Nufayr, as will come.

We stop at the end of p. 218, within the editor's notes on report 6656, which continue on the next page. These are reports as transmitted, al-Tabari's choices and arguments are his, and the editor's notes and references are his, with no museum verdict.
