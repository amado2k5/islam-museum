## Summary
Verse 4 of [[passage/quran-13|Surat ar-Ra'd]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 4 of 43 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**Commentary on verse 4 (pp. 330-346)**

On "and in the earth are neighbouring tracts, and gardens of grapes, and crops and palms, clustered and unclustered, watered with one water; and We favour some of them over others in produce; in that are signs for people who understand", al-Tabari says: "and in the earth are neighbouring tracts", pieces of it near to one another by adjacency and differing in quality in spite of their adjacency and nearness, so that one piece is salt marsh which grows nothing next to a good piece which grows and is useful. The interpreters said the same: {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 330}

Report 20067, from Mujahid, through Abu Kurayb, Waki', Sufyan and Layth: "and in the earth are neighbouring tracts", the salt marsh and the good land, the salty and the good. Reports 20068 and 20069, from Mujahid, through Ahmad ibn Ishaq, Abu Ahmad, Sufyan and Layth, and al-Muthanna and Abu Nu'aym: salt marshes and good land. Report 20070, from Ibn Abbas, through al-Hasan ibn Muhammad, Sa'id ibn Sulayman, Ishaq ibn Sulayman and Abu Sinan: the good land and the salt marsh. Report 20071, from Ibn Abbas, through Muhammad ibn Sa'd, his father, his uncle and his father from his father: "and in the earth are neighbouring tracts", meaning the salt land and the good land, which lie together side by side, some favoured over others in produce. The editor explains that "sabkha" is salty land with salt and seepage which hardly grows anything but some trees, and "'adhiya" land of generous growth that is not saline, and notes that he removed an obvious repetition from the manuscript and printed text caused by the copyist's slip. Report 20072, from Ibn Abbas, through al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hajjaj and Ibn Jurayj: {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 331}

"neighbouring tracts", the good land and the salt marsh lying together, this one grows and this one beside it does not. Reports 20073 to 20075, from Mujahid, through al-Hasan ibn Muhammad, Shababa and Waraqa; al-Muthanna, Abu Hudhayfa and Shibl; and Ishaq, Abd Allah and Waraqa: "neighbouring tracts", its good and sweet, and its bad, the salt marshes. Reports 20076 and 20077, from Qatada, through Bishr, Yazid and Sa'id, and Muhammad ibn Abd al-A'la, Muhammad ibn Thawr and Ma'mar: villages near each other. Report 20078, from al-Dahhak, through al-Muthanna, Amr, Hushaym and Abu Ishaq al-Kufi: the salt land, followed by the good land. The editor says Abu Ishaq al-Kufi is Abd Allah ibn Maysara al-Harithi, who is weak. Report 20079, from al-Dahhak, through al-Husayn ibn al-Faraj, Abu Mu'adh and Ubayd ibn Sulayman: {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 332}

"and in the earth are neighbouring tracts", meaning the salt land and the good land lying side by side. Report 20080, from Ibn Abbas, through al-Harith, Abd al-Aziz, Isra'il, Ata' ibn al-Sa'ib and Sa'id ibn Jubayr: one land grows sweet and another sour, adjoining, watered with one water. Report 20081, from Ibn Abbas, the same, through Ahmad ibn Ishaq, Abu Ahmad and Isra'il: this one sweet and that one sour, though watered with one water, and they lie together. Report 20082, from Ibn Shawdhab, through Abd al-Jabbar ibn Yahya al-Ramli and Damra ibn Rabi'a: good land and salty; the editor says he found no biography of Abd al-Jabbar ibn Yahya al-Ramli, a teacher of al-Tabari. On "and gardens of grapes, and crops and palms, clustered and unclustered, watered with one water, and We favour some of them over others in produce" he says, mighty be His praise: in the earth, along with the pieces that differ in character, by salinity and sweetness, foulness and goodness, though adjoining and near one another, there are also gardens of grapes, crops and palms, near in creation but differing in tastes and colours, though all share one drink: some of them are sweet in taste, fair in appearance and fragrant, and some sour in taste and without scent. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 333}

The interpreters said the same: report 20083, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, through Ibn Humayd, Jarir and Ata' ibn al-Sa'ib: "and gardens of grapes, and crops and palms, clustered and unclustered", he said, together and not together; "watered with one water, and We favour some of them over others in produce", he said, the one land may have peaches, pears, white and black grapes, some bearing more than others, some sweet and some sour and some better than others. Reports 20084 to 20086, from Mujahid, through al-Hasan ibn Muhammad, Shababa and Waraqa; al-Muthanna, Abu Hudhayfa and Shibl; and Ishaq and Abd Allah: "and gardens", he said, and what goes with them. The readers differed on reading "and crops and palms". Most readers of Medina and Kufa read "wa-zar'in wa-nakhilin" in the genitive, joined to "grapes", in the sense: in the earth are neighbouring tracts and gardens of grapes and of crops and palms. Some readers of Basra read "wa-zar'un wa-nakhilun" in the nominative, joined to "gardens", in the sense: in the earth are neighbouring tracts and gardens of grapes, and in it also crops and palms. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 334}

Al-Tabari says: the correct view is that these are two readings close in meaning, each read by well-known readers, and whichever the reader uses is right. For crops and palms, if they are in gardens, are in the earth, and if they are in the earth, the earth without a garden, then it is the same whether they are described as being in a garden or in land. As for "palms, clustered and unclustered", "sinwan" is the plural of "sinw", palms joined by one root; singular, dual and plural are distinguished only by the case ending on the nun, which in the dual is always kasra and in the plural is inflected in all cases; the like is "qinwan", whose singular is "qinw". The interpreters said the same: report 20087, from al-Bara', through Abu Kurayb, Waki', Sufyan and Abu Ishaq: "sinwan", he said, together, "and not sinwan", separate. Report 20088, from al-Bara', through Ibn Humayd, Yahya ibn Wadih, al-Husayn and Abu Ishaq: "sinwan" is a palm beside which are palms at its root, "not sinwan" the palm by itself. Report 20089, from al-Bara' ibn Azib, through Muhammad ibn Bashshar, Abu Asim, Sufyan and Abu Ishaq: "sinwan" are two palms with one root, "not sinwan" a palm or two palms apart. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 335}

Report 20090, from al-Bara', through Muhammad ibn al-Muthanna, Muhammad ibn Ja'far, Shu'ba and Abu Ishaq: a palm that has palms beside it, "and not sinwan" scattered palms. Report 20091, from al-Bara', through al-Hasan ibn Muhammad, Amr ibn al-Haytham Abu Qatan, Yahya ibn Abbad, Affan, Shu'ba and Abu Ishaq: "sinwan" is a palm with palms beside it, "not sinwan" the scattered; the editor gives notes on the narrators. Report 20092, from al-Bara', through al-Hasan, Shababa, Isra'il and Abu Ishaq: "sinwan" are three, four or two palms of one root, "not sinwan" the scattered. Report 20093, from al-Bara', through Ahmad ibn Ishaq, Abu Ahmad, Sufyan, Sharik and Abu Ishaq: two palms with one root, "not sinwan" the scattered. Report 20094, from Ibn Abbas, through al-Muthanna, Abd Allah ibn Salih, Mu'awiya and Ali: "sinwan", he says, together. Report 20095, from Ibn Abbas, through Muhammad ibn Sa'd, his father, his uncle and his father from his father: by "sinwan" he means the palm from whose root palms come out, some bearing and some not, so its root is one and its tops separate. Report 20096, from Ibn Abbas, through al-Harith, Abd al-Aziz, Isra'il, {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 336}

Ata' ibn al-Sa'ib and Sa'id ibn Jubayr: palms in one root, "not sinwan", scattered palms. Report 20097, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, through Ibn Humayd, Jarir and Ata': together and not together. Report 20098, from al-Bara', through al-Muthanna, al-Nufayli, Zuhayr and Abu Ishaq: "sinwan" is what has one root and is separate, "not sinwan" what grew by itself; the editor gives notes on al-Nufayli and Zuhayr ibn Mu'awiya al-Ju'fi. Reports 20099 to 20101, from Mujahid, through al-Hasan ibn Muhammad, Shababa and Waraqa; al-Muthanna, Abu Hudhayfa and Shibl; and Ishaq and Abd Allah: "sinwan" are two palms or more in one root, "not sinwan" one alone. Reports 20102 and 20103, from al-Dahhak, through Abu Kurayb, Waki' and Salama ibn Nubayt, and al-Muthanna, Amr ibn Awn, Hushaym and Juwaybir: "sinwan" is the together, with one root, "not sinwan" the separate in root. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 337}

Report 20104, from Qatada, through Bishr, Yazid and Sa'id: as for "sinwan", it is two or three palms with one root and various branches, "not sinwan" the single palm. Report 20105, from Qatada, through Muhammad ibn Abd al-A'la, Muhammad ibn Thawr and Ma'mar: "sinwan" is the palm at whose root are two or three palms of one root. Report 20106, from Ibn Zayd, through Yunus and Ibn Wahb: "sinwan" are two or three palms in one root, which people count as sinwan. Report 20107, from Ma'mar, through Ibn Abd al-A'la and Muhammad ibn Thawr: a man told me that there was a quarrel between Umar ibn al-Khattab and al-Abbas, and al-Abbas was hasty toward him; Umar came to the Prophet and said: O Messenger of God, have you not seen what Abbas did to me and did! I wanted to answer him but remembered his place with you and held back. He said: God have mercy on you, a man's uncle is the like of his father. The editor says this report is weak because the man who narrated from Umar is unknown, that the reports that follow are all mursal, and that the saying "a man's uncle is the like of his father" is reported by Muslim in his Sahih from the hadith of Abu al-Zinad from al-A'raj from Abu Hurayra, by Ahmad in his Musnad, and by al-Tirmidhi in the chapter on the merits of al-Abbas, who calls it hasan gharib, {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 338}

and that Ahmad also reports a long hadith in the Musnad of Ali whose chain is weak because of a break. Report 20108, from Qatada, through al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq and Ma'mar: "sinwan" is a palm at whose root are two or three palms of one root, and it has the same story between Umar and al-Abbas and his words "God have mercy on you, a man's uncle is the like of his father"; the editor says it is mursal and was reported by Ibn Sa'd in al-Tabaqat. Report 20109, from Mujahid, through Abd al-Razzaq, Ibn Uyayna and Dawud ibn Shabur: the Prophet said, you harm me through al-Abbas, for he is what remains of my forefathers, and a man's uncle is the like of his father. Report 20110, from Ata' and Ibn Abi Mulayka, through Ya'qub, Hushaym and Hajjaj: the Messenger of God said to Umar, Umar, did you not know that a man's uncle is the like of his father? These two are mursal reports. Report 20111, from Mujahid, through al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hajjaj, Ibn Jurayj and al-Qasim ibn Abi Bazza: "sinwan" are three palms in one root, like three sons of one mother and father who differ in their works as the fruit of these three palms in one root differs; {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 339}

Ibn Jurayj said Mujahid said: like the righteous of Adam's children and their wicked, their father being one. Report 20112, from Mujahid, through al-Hasan ibn Muhammad, Hajjaj ibn Muhammad, Ibn Jurayj and Ibrahim ibn Abi Bakr ibn Abd Allah, is similar. Report 20113, from al-Hasan, through al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hajjaj and Abu Bakr ibn Abd Allah: this is a parable God struck for the hearts of Adam's children. The earth in the hand of the Merciful was one clay; He spread it out and flattened it, and it became neighbouring tracts; water comes down on it from the sky, and this one brings out its flowers, fruit, trees and plants and revives its dead land, and that one brings out its salt marsh, salt and foulness, and both are watered with one water. Had the water been salty it would have been said that this one became saline because of the water! So the people were created from Adam, and a reminder comes down on them from heaven: some hearts soften, humble themselves and submit, and some hearts harden, play, forget and grow coarse. Al-Hasan said: by God, no one sat with the Qur'an but rose from it with an increase or a loss; God said [[passage/quran-17-82|17:82]]. The editor gives remarks on the narrators and on the word "istasbakhat". On "watered with one water" the readers differed over "tusqa". Most readers of Medina and Iraq, of Kufa and Basra, read "tusqa" with a ta', meaning the gardens, crops and palms are watered. Some used to say that "tusqa" was said, and the discussion continues on the next page. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 340}

Some Meccans and Kufans read "yusqa" with a ya'. The grammarians differed over the reason for the masculine when it is so read, though it is a report about the gardens, grapes, palms and crops that they are watered with one water. One of the Basran grammarians said: when it is read with the ta', it goes with "grapes", as "al-an'am" was made masculine in God's words "from what is in its bellies" in Sura al-Nahl and then feminine afterwards, when He said "and on them and on ships you are carried" [[passage/quran-23-22|23:22]]; so whoever says "yusqa" with the ya' makes "grapes" among the words treated as masculine and feminine, like "al-an'am". One of the Kufan grammarians said: whoever says "tusqa" goes to the feminine of "crops, gardens and palms", and whoever makes it masculine goes to the sense that all of it is watered with one water while its produce differs, sour and sweet, and in this is a sign; this is al-Farra's saying in Ma'ani al-Qur'an. Al-Tabari says: the better of the two readings for me to recite is that of those who read it with the ta', "tusqa bi-ma'in wahid", the sense being that the gardens, palms and crops are watered with one water, because "tusqa" comes after they have been mentioned and they are a plural of non-humans. The other way is not excluded, in the sense that all of that is watered with one sweet water and not salt. The interpreters said the same: {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 341}

Report 20114, from Mujahid, through al-Hasan ibn Muhammad, Shababa, Waraqa and Ibn Abi Najih: "watered with one water", the water of the sky, like the righteous of Adam's children and their wicked, their father being one. Reports 20115 and 20116, from Mujahid, through Abu Kurayb, Waki', Sufyan and Layth, and Ahmad ibn Ishaq and Abu Ahmad: the water of the sky. Report 20117, from al-Dahhak, through al-Muthanna, Amr, Hushaym and Abu Ishaq al-Kufi: rain water; the editor says Abu Ishaq al-Kufi is weak and feeble in hadith and that "al-Sufi" in the printed text and the manuscript is a plain error. Reports 20118 to 20120, from Mujahid, through al-Muthanna, Suwayd, Ibn al-Mubarak and Ibn Jurayj; Abu Hudhayfa and Shibl; and al-Qasim, al-Husayn and Hajjaj: the water of the sky, like the righteous of Adam's children and their wicked, their father being one. Report 20121, from Ibn Shawdhab, through Abd al-Jabbar ibn Yahya al-Ramli and Damra ibn Rabi'a: with the water of the sky. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 342}

On "and We favour some of them over others in produce" the readers differed. Most readers of Mecca, Medina and Basra and some of Kufa read "nufaddilu" with a nun, meaning We favour some over others in produce; most Kufan readers read "yufaddilu" with a ya', going back to "He covers the night with the day". Al-Tabari says: they are two widespread readings with one meaning, and whichever the reader uses he is right; but the ya' is the better of them for me, because it is in the course of a speech that begins "God is He who raised the heavens", and reading it with the ya' is therefore preferable. The sense of the speech is: the gardens of grapes, crops and palms, clustered and unclustered, are watered with one sweet water, not salt, and God makes their tastes differ, favouring some over others in taste, this one sweet and that one sour. Report 20122, from Ibn Abbas, through Abu Kurayb, Waki', Sufyan, Ata' and Sa'id ibn Jubayr: "and We favour some of them over others in produce", he said, the Persian and the daqal, the sweet and the sour. The editor says he found no one who mentions a "Persian" date and thinks it is the "barni", a yellow round date of pure sweetness, and that "daqal" is the worst kind of date. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 343}

Report 20123, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, through Ibn Humayd, Jarir and Ata' ibn al-Sa'ib: "and We favour some of them over others in produce", he said, the one land may have peaches, pears, white and black grapes, some bearing more than others, some sweet and some sour and some better than others. Report 20124, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, through al-Muthanna, Arim Abu al-Nu'man, Hammad ibn Zayd and Ata': barni and so and so, some better than others. Report 20125, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, through Muhammad ibn Bashshar, Mu'ammal, Sufyan and Ata': this sour, this sweet, this bitter. Report 20126, from Abu Hurayra, through Mahmud ibn Khidash, Sayf ibn Muhammad, al-A'mash and Abu Salih: the Prophet said on "and We favour some of them over others in produce": the daqal and the Persian, the sweet and the sour. In a long note the editor says that Mahmud ibn Khidash al-Taliqani, a teacher of al-Tabari, is truthful and reliable; that Sayf ibn Muhammad al-Thawri, the nephew of Sufyan al-Thawri, was weakened by Ahmad, whose son Abd Allah relays that he called him a liar, while Ibn Ma'in called him a lying, foul old man and al-Nasa'i called him weak and abandoned; that al-Tirmidhi reports it from Mahmud ibn Khidash by this chain and calls it hasan gharib, at which the editor wonders, that al-Tirmidhi should call good a chain containing this narrator; and that he corrected the printed text's form of Sayf's name. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 344}

Report 20127, from Abu Hurayra, from the Prophet, through Ahmad ibn al-Hasan al-Tirmidhi, Sulayman ibn Ubayd Allah al-Raqqi, Ubayd Allah ibn Amr al-Raqqi, Zayd ibn Abi Unaysa, al-A'mash and Abu Salih: on "and We favour some of them over others in produce" he said: the daqal and the Persian, the sweet and the sour. The editor says Ahmad ibn al-Hasan al-Tirmidhi, a teacher of al-Tabari, was one of the vessels of hadith; that Sulayman ibn Ubayd Allah al-Raqqi was called truthful by Ibn Abi Hatim and listed by Ibn Hibban among the reliable, while al-Nasa'i called him not strong and Ibn Ma'in said he was nothing; that al-Dhahabi mentions the report in al-Mizan and says al-Uqayli said that only Sulayman brought it and that it is known through Sayf ibn Muhammad from al-A'mash, and al-Dhahabi adds that Sayf is ruined; and that the editor wonders how al-Tirmidhi could call it good with these defects. On "in that are signs for a people who understand" he says, mighty be His praise: in God's, mighty and exalted, making these neighbouring tracts of earth differ from one another, and the fruits of their gardens and their crops as we have described and explained, there is a clear proof and lesson for people who understand that difference: that the One who made them differ in this way is the One who makes His creatures differ in what He apportions them of guidance and misguidance, success and abandonment, giving success to this one and abandoning that, guiding this and leading that astray, {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 345}

and had He willed He would have made them all alike, as, had He willed, He would have made equal all the produce of the Garden, which drinks one drink and is watered with one watering, though it differs in produce. The editor notes that the word "one" in brackets is a necessary addition. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 346}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
