**Commentary on verse 171 (pp. 217-221)**

Al-Tabari says God is addressing His Prophet Muhammad: remember, Muhammad, when We uprooted the mountain and raised it above the Children of Israel like a canopy of cloud, and said to them "take what We gave you with strength", of Our obligations and the rulings of Our Book that We laid on you: accept it and act with diligence to fulfil it, without shortfall or delay; "and remember what is in it", the covenants and pledges We took from you to act by it, "so that you may fear God", that is, may fear your Lord and dread His punishment for leaving off acting on it, when you recall the pledges taken from you in it. The editor refers to the earlier explanations of "with strength". The people of interpretation say the same. Report 15331, from Ibn Abbas: Moses said to them "take what We gave you with strength", meaning to act by the Book, otherwise the mountain would fall on them and destroy them; they said, rather we will take what God gave us with strength, and afterward they broke their pledge. Report 15332: this is God's saying in [[passage/quran-4-154|4:154]]; "or I will let it down on you". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 217} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 218}

Report 15333, from Ibn Abbas through Amir: I know, of God's creation, why the Jews prostrate on the edge of their faces: when the mountain was raised above them they prostrated and kept looking at the mountain for fear it would fall on them; it was a prostration God was pleased with, and they made it a custom. Report 15334 is like it. The editor identifies the narrators: Ishaq ibn Shahin al-Wasiti, a teacher of al-Tabari, Khalid ibn Abd Allah, Dawud ibn Abi Hind, and Amir, who is al-Sha'bi. Report 15335, from Qatada: "with strength" means earnestly; a mountain God pulled from its base and set above their heads, saying: you will take My command or I will cast it on you. Report 15336, from Mujahid: as butter is churned out, and the editor explains that to churn butter is to shake the skin so that its butter comes out; from Ibn Jurayj: they had refused to accept the Torah or believe in it, and were told they would believe in it and accept it or it would fall on them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 218} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 219}

Report 15337, from Abu Bakr ibn Abd Allah: Moses said, this is God's book, will you accept it with what is in it, for it explains what is lawful and forbidden to you and what He commands and forbids; they said, unroll it for us, and if its obligations are light and its limits easy we will accept it; he said, accept it as it is; they said, no, not until we know what is in it and what its limits and obligations are. They went back to Moses several times, so God inspired the mountain, which was torn up and rose into the sky until it stood between their heads and the sky, and Moses said: do you not see what my Lord says? If you do not accept the Torah with what is in it, I will cast this mountain on you. Al-Hasan al-Basri related that when they looked at the mountain each man fell prostrate on his left brow, watching the mountain with his right eye for fear it would fall on him, and so there is no Jew on earth who prostrates except on his left brow; they say this is the prostration by which the punishment was lifted from us. Abu Bakr said: when the tablets, on which God's book was written by His hand, were unrolled, there was no mountain, tree or stone on earth that did not shake, and no Jew today, small or great, to whom the Torah is recited but shakes and nods his head at it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 219}

Al-Tabari then gives the differences of those versed in the Arabs' speech over the meaning of "nataqna". Some Basrans (the editor says this is Abu Ubayda) said it means "We raised", citing a line by al-Ajjaj describing his camel and the hard going of its journey, and a line by Ru'ba in a poem in which he boasts of his people, saying that others have tried their most weighty wits; the editor explains the words for the saddle blanket, the saddle frame and the heaviest. A second view is reported from the same speaker: that the root means anything you tear from its place and throw, whence a woman with many children is called "natiq" since she throws her offspring, and he cited a line by al-Nabigha about the Banu Asad; the editor gives the setting of the poem and the story of al-Nabigha with Zur'a ibn Amr and the alliance of Banu Dhubyan with Banu Asad, and the variants of the line in the divan and the dictionary. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 219} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 220}

Another of them said that in this place it means "and We raised it", and one says "the journey shook me", meaning moved me, and "he did not shake his foot", meaning he does not spur; and a mount "shakes" its rider when it runs with him and tires him until he pants; and "the woman shook", having many children. Some Kufans said "We hung the mountain over them and raised it", and a woman with many children is "mintaq", and they heard "he took the sack and shook out what was in it", meaning scattered what was in it. The editor notes that al-Tabari did not explain "canopy" here and refers to where it was explained earlier. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 221}

The editor goes on in the footnote with the lines of al-Nabigha, explaining that the last line describes them in ease and soft living, their mothers having lived well so that their children were many; he explains that a woman is said to have "dahaqat" when she bears her children one after another, that a "mudhkar" is a woman who bears males, and that the divan and other books give the variant "she overflowed upon you", meaning she spread wide and prevailed as a vessel overflows and covers and drowns what is around it. With this the commentary on the verse ends. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 221}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
