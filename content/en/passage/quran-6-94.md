## Summary
Verse 94 of [[passage/quran-6|Surat al-An'am]], a Meccan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 94 of 165 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

On "you have come to us alone as we created you at first, and left what we gave you behind your backs" al-Tabari says: this is God's report of what he will say on the day of resurrection to these who set gods and rivals equal to him, telling his servants that he will say to them on their coming to him "you have come to us alone". By "alone" he means single, with no wealth with them, no women, no slaves and nothing of what God gave them in this world, "as we created you at first", naked, uncircumcised, barefoot, as their mothers bore them and as he created them in their mothers' wombs, with nothing on them or with them of what they used to boast of in this world. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 543}

The editor says the printed text has "no furniture and no companion", the right being the manuscript's, meaning their women and servants, and quotes al-Baghawi: single, with no wealth, no spouse, no child and no servants; that "ghulf" is the plural of "aghlaf" and "ghurl" of "aghral", the uncircumcised, and that Muslim's hadith from A'isha says people are gathered on the day of resurrection barefoot, naked and uncircumcised.

"Furada" is a plural whose singular is "fard", and al-Tabari cites a line of al-Nabigha al-Dhubyani describing a wild bull with marked legs and a lean belly like a polisher's lone sword. "Farad" and "farid" are like "wahad", "wahid" and "wahid" in the singular of "awhad", and "farad" may be pluralised "furad" as "wahad" is "wuhad", from which is a line of the poet Tamim ibn Ubayy ibn Muqbil describing gnats, blue-eyed, over a horse's chest, singly and in pairs. Yunus al-Jarmi was reported to say that "furad" is the plural of "fard", as "tu'm" and "tu'am" are for the plural, and from it "furada", "rudafa" and "qurana". One says "a man is fard" and "a woman is fard" when she has no brother, and "the man was alone, he is alone" meaning he was single, "farid". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 543}

The editor says of Nabigha's line that it is in his diwan and Lisan, from his famous poem in which he excused himself to al-Nu'man ibn al-Mundhir, with the description of the bull before it; that Wajra is a place between Mecca and Basra, a pasture of wild animals, "mawshi akari'uh" means black only in its legs, "tawi al-masir" a lean belly, and he describes the bull's whiteness and gleam like a freshly polished sword.

13570, from al-Qurazi: A'isha, wife of the Prophet, read "you have come to us alone as we created you at first" and said: oh the shame, men and women are gathered together looking at one another's nakedness! and God's messenger said: "each of them that day has a concern enough for him"; men will not look at women nor women at men, each busy with himself. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 544}

The editor says the line is by Tamim ibn Ubayy ibn Muqbil and came earlier in another version, that he found no ancient grammarian called Yunus al-Jarmi and that Yunus ibn Habib was a Dabbi, not a Jarmi, and hopes some reader will guide him to the truth; that print has "al-ghawani" and the manuscript an unpointed "al-'awani" and the right reading is "al-qurana", as one says "they came qurana", joined, and Dhu al-Rumma said so; and in 13570 he identifies Amr ibn al-Harith ibn Ya'qub al-Ansari al-Misri, trusted, and Ibn Abi Hilal, Sa'id ibn Abi Hilal al-Laythi al-Misri, trusted, noting that al-Hakim in the Mustadrak identified al-Qurazi as Uthman ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Qurazi, of whom he found no entry, and that "al-Qurtubi" in print and manuscript is an error; that al-Hakim related it through Abd Allah ibn Wahb from Amr ibn al-Harith without "Ibn Zayd said" and called its chain sound, while al-Dhahabi commented that it is sound with a break; that he thinks "Ibn Zayd said" an addition by the copyist; and that the break is that al-Qurazi did not hear from A'isha.

As for "and left what we gave you behind your backs" he says: you left behind, people, what we established you in in this world of which you used to boast, behind you in this world, not carrying it with you. This is God's reproach of these polytheists for their boasting in this world over their wealth. Everything you gave to another and made his property you have "khawwalta" him; one says "the man was haughty with the utmost conceit", "khail". He cites the line of Abu al-Najm in a rajaz praising God the generous giver, that he gave and did not stint, heaps of high humps from the gift of the giver. It is reported that Abu Amr ibn al-Ala used to recite the line of Zuhayr in which the noble, if asked for their wealth to be made over, make it over, if asked they give, and if they are asked for the lots they choose the fat ones. 13571, from al-Suddi: "and left what we gave you", of wealth and servants, "behind your backs", in this world. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 546}

The editor says print and manuscript have "everything whom you owned", an utter error, the right as he set; that Abu al-Najm's rajaz opens "praise be to God, the generous giver", "kum al-dhura" being great humps, plural of "kauma'", and "khawal" with a kasra on the waw; that Zuhayr's line is in his diwan and Lisan from his famous poem on Harim ibn Sinan and al-Harith ibn Awf, recalling their people's generosity in the time of drought; that a transmission other than Abu Amr's is "if they are lent camels they lend them", "istakhbala" meaning to borrow a she-camel for her milk and wool and so he lent it, "istikhwal" being the same, and al-Asma'i reported Abu Amr saying that had he recited it he would have recited "if they are made owners of the wealth they make owners"; and "yaysiru" is from the "maysir" in which the camels' lots are divided, and "yughlu" means to choose the fattest camels for slaughter, so they slaughter only the costly.

On "we do not see with you your intercessors whom you claimed to be partners in you" al-Tabari says: he says to these who set rivals equal to their Lord on the day of resurrection: we do not see with you your intercessors whom you used to claim in the world would intercede for you with your Lord on the day of resurrection. It is said this verse came down about al-Nadr ibn al-Harith for his saying that al-Lat and al-Uzza would intercede for him with God on the day of resurrection, and it was said that this was the saying of all idol-worshippers. 13572, from al-Suddi: the polytheists claimed they worshipped the gods because they were intercessors who would intercede for them with God and that these gods were God's partners; 13573, from Ikrima: al-Nadr ibn al-Harith said "al-Lat and al-Uzza will intercede for me"! and this verse came down, "you have come to us alone as we created you at first", to "partners". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 547}

The editor refers for "intercessor" to an earlier passage.

On "the bond between you is cut and what you claimed has left you" he says, reporting what he will say on the day of resurrection to these polytheists who set rivals equal to him: "the bond between you is cut" means their ties which were between them in the world have gone that day, so there is no tie, no affection and no mutual help between them, though in the world they kept ties and helped one another; all that vanished in the next world, none of them helping his companion or joining him. 13574 and 13575, from Mujahid: "bayn" is their ties in the world; 13576 and 13577, from Qatada: your tie, what tie there was between you; 13578, from Ibn Abbas: meaning kinship and standing; 13579, from al-Suddi: what was between you is cut; 13580, from Abu Bakr ibn Ayyash: ties in the world. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 549}

The editor refers for "bayn" to an earlier passage and says the chain of 13580 is broken, as he noted before, and that Abu Bakr ibn Ayyash ibn Salim al-Asadi is trusted and well known.

The readers differed in the reading of "baynakum". Most readers of Medina read it in the accusative, meaning what is between you is cut, and most readers of Mecca and the two Iraqs in the nominative, meaning your tie is cut. Abu Jafar said: the right for me is to say that these are two well-known readings with agreeing sense, so whichever the reader reads he is right, since the Arabs sometimes put "bayn" in the accusative in the place of a noun, it being heard from them "he came to me towards you, beneath you, equal to you" in the accusative in place of the nominative, and they are also reported to put it in the nominative when the verb is for it and it is made a noun, and he recites a line of Muhalhil describing his people's spears as like the ropes of a deep well that drag along its sides, though the accusative is the commoner among them, both when it is an adverb and when a noun. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 550}

The editor says print has "my return towards you", a plain error, the right being in al-Farra's Ma'ani al-Qur'an; that Muhalhil's line is in al-Qali's Amali and Lisan, from his famous poem he said when he avenged his brother Kulayb Wa'il, with an earlier line on ransoming the men of Thaqiqa the day they came like forest lions; that "ashtan" are tightly twisted ropes used for drawing water, singular "shatan", "jal" the side of a well, and "jurur" a description of a well deep in its bottom since its bucket is dragged on its lip, he describing the length of their spears and the movement of their hands in thrusting and then drawing them out of the body of whom they struck.

As for "and what you claimed has left you" he says: what you claimed of your gods to be a partner of your Lord and an intercessor for you with your Lord has turned from your way and course, and will not intercede for you today. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 550}

The editor refers for "error" to earlier passages.

Verse 94 closes here on p. 550, and al-Tabari goes on to [[passage/quran-6-95|6:95]].
