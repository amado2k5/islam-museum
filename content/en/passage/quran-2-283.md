## Summary
Verse 283 of [[passage/quran-2|Surat al-Baqara]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 283 of 286 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

([[passage/quran-2-283|2:283]]), pp. 94 to 100. Al-Tabari begins with the reading of the word for the scribe: the readers of all the regions read it as the one who writes for you the document of the debt; a group of the early readers read it as the writing itself, meaning that they would have no way to write the debt down, either for lack of inkwell and sheet or for lack of one to write even if they had them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 94} His choice: the reading of the regions is the only one permitted in his view, because it is so in the Muslims' copies of the Qur'an; this is his own choice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 94} He then gives the sense: if you are on a journey and find no one to write for you and have no way to write down the debt you were told to record and have witnessed, then take pledges for your debts, which you receive from those you lend to, as a security for your property. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 94} The editor adds a bracketed clause at the start of the explanation and says it is necessary for the sentence to stand; this is his. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 94}

In 6435, al-Dahhak (through al-Muthanna, Ishaq, Abu Zuhayr and Juwaybir): whoever is on a journey and sells for a term and finds no scribe is given leave to take pledges, and if he finds a scribe he may not take a pledge. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 95} In 6436, al-Rabi' (through Ammar): a scribe who writes for you. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 95} In 6437, al-Dahhak (through Yahya ibn Abi Talib): every sale for a term is one God ordered to be written and witnessed, and that is for residence; if people are travelling and have sold for a term and find no scribe, there are the pledges. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 95} The editor fears that the copyist dropped a word, which he sets in brackets; this is his. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 95}

Al-Tabari then lists those who interpreted on the reading he has related: 6438, Ibn Abbas (through Abu Kurayb, Hushaym, Yazid ibn Abi Ziyad and Miqsam): it means the scribe, the sheet, the inkwell and the pen; 6439, Ibn Abbas (through Yaqub, Ibn Ulayya and Ibn Jurayj from his father): he read it so and said that a man may find a sheet and no scribe; 6440, Mujahid used to read it so and say that a scribe may be found when no sheet or ink is; 6441, Mujahid (through al-Muthanna, Abu Hudhayfa, Shibl and Ibn Abi Najih): it means ink, so if they find no ink then the pledges come in, and he added that a pledge is only in travel; 6442, Abu al-Aliyya (through al-Muthanna, al-Hajjaj, Hammad ibn Zayd and Shuayb ibn al-Habhab): he read it so and said an inkwell may be found and no sheet. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 96}

Next, the readers' difference over the plural of the word for pledge. Most readers of the Hijaz and Iraq read the plural on the pattern of rihan, plural of rahn, as kibash is the plural of kabsh, bighal of baghl and ni'al of na'l. Another group read it with two dammas, ruhun, as the plural of a plural, and some explained it as a plural of rahn like suqf from saqf. Others read it with a quiet ha, ruhn, as the plural of rahn as suqf is of saqf, saying they know no noun of the fa'l pattern pluralised on fu'ul and fu'l except rahn and saqf. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 96} Abu Jafar says the better reading is the rihan one, because it is the known plural of nouns of that pattern, like habl and hibal, ka'b and ki'ab, while the fu'ul or fu'l plural is odd and rare, found in a few words such as saqf, qalb (the heart of the palm), jadd (in the sense of fortune), and further examples of that kind; this is his choice and argument. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 96} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 97} In the notes to p. 96 the editor says some of these forms are strange and absent from the dictionaries, that the plurals recorded for jadd are ajdad, ajudd and judud, that the print has a wrong letter in one word and the manuscript is undotted; this is the editor's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 96}

Al-Tabari supposes that what led those who read the damma form, despite its oddness, is that they found rihan used for horse races and wished to turn the word away from that ambiguity, and found ruhun said as a plural of rahn, as in a line of poetry which the printed text gives to a poet whose name the scan does not make clear, in which the pledges are said to have been forfeited at the beloved's side out of the poet's heart; this is his supposition. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 97} The editor traces the line in his note to Ibn al-Shajari's anthology, Lubab al-Adab and Lisan al-Arab (under the root for pledge), says the wording there differs and is better in his view, explains forfeiture of a pledge as the creditor owning it when the debtor cannot redeem it by the stated time, a custom of the pre-Islamic period that Islam voided, and explains the line as the poet leaving the beloved after the pledges, loves and covenants, all of which went as a pledge goes from the one who holds it; this is the editor's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 97}

Then al-Tabari explains the next clause: if the debtor is trusted by the owner of the money and the creditor took no pledge from him on the journey because of his trustworthiness, let the debtor fear his Lord regarding the debt he owes, neither denying it, nor putting it off, nor trying to take it away, which would expose him to a punishment he cannot meet, and let him pay the debt he was trusted with. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 97} He recalls that he has already discussed those who held that this ruling abrogates the earlier verse's command of witnesses and writing, and has shown which view is sounder, so he does not repeat it; the editor refers to pp. 53 to 55. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 98} In the notes to p. 97 the editor explains the phrase for a debtor who withholds a right, and the phrase meaning to have no power over, and says the particle in the latter is missing in both manuscript and print and that what he set is nearer to correct; this is his. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 97}

In 6443, al-Dahhak (through Yahya ibn Abi Talib, Yazid and Juwaybir): this means trusting one another on a journey, not in residence, where, if he can find a scribe, he may not take a pledge nor trust the other. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 98} Al-Tabari says that what al-Dahhak said, that a creditor may not trust a debtor when he can reach a scribe, a document and witnesses, even on a journey, is as he said, for the reason already shown; but his saying that the pledge is likewise, so that the owner of the right may not take a pledge if he can reach a scribe and a witness, whether at home or on a journey, is in his view a saying without meaning, because of the sound report in 6444 that the Prophet bought food on credit and pledged his armour for it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 98} So he holds that a man may give a pledge for what he owes and take one for what is owed him, on a journey or at home; the Prophet, he says, was not without scribe or witness when he pledged, since neither was ever out of reach in his city; but those who sell with a pledge, where they can reach a scribe and witness or the sale or debt is for a set term, should write it and have witnesses over the money and the pledge, and leaving the writing and witnessing is allowed only where there is no way to them. This is al-Tabari's own reasoning, reported here as transmitted with no museum legal ruling. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 98} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 99} The editor notes that al-Tabari gives 6444 without a chain, that al-Bukhari in his Sahih and Muslim in his Sahih narrated it by several routes from Aisha, mother of the believers, and that it is also in al-Bayhaqi's Sunan; he explains that the word for deferral means putting off and a sale on credit; this is the editor's, and in another note he says he kept the manuscript's wording in one phrase. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 98} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 99}

He then explains the last sentence of the verse as addressed to the witnesses whom the borrower and the owner of the money were told to call: do not conceal testimony after you have testified before the judges, and answer the one you witnessed for when he calls you to give it against his opponent before the judge who will take his right. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 99} God, he says, tells the witness what is upon him for concealing it and refusing to give it when the one who asked for it needs it before a judge or ruler: the sinful heart means a heart that is corrupt, earning by the concealment disobedience to God. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 99} In 6445, al-Rabi' (through al-Muthanna, Ishaq, Ibn Abi Jafar from his father): it is not lawful for anyone to conceal testimony he holds, even if it is against himself and his parents, and whoever conceals it has committed a great sin. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 99} In 6446, al-Suddi (through Musa, Amr and Asbat): corrupt of heart. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 100} In 6447, Ibn Abbas (through al-Muthanna, Abu Salih, Muawiya from Ali): the greatest of the major sins is associating partners with God, for which he cites [[passage/quran-5-72|5:72]], then false witness and concealing testimony, linking the last to this verse's wording on sin. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 100} The editor says the manuscript and print give the other verse's wording differently, which is not a reading but a copyist's slip; this is his. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 100} Al-Tabari adds that it is also reported from Ibn Abbas that a witness must testify wherever he is called to testify and report wherever he is asked; and 6448, Ibn Abbas (through al-Muthanna, Suwayd, Ibn al-Mubarak, Muhammad ibn Muslim and Amr ibn Dinar): if you hold testimony and are asked, tell it, and do not say 'tell it to the governor'; tell him, perhaps he will think again or turn back. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 100}

Finally he explains the verse's close: God knows what you do in your testimony, whether upholding it or concealing it when the asker needs it, and the rest of your hidden and open deeds; He counts it all against you to repay you, good or ill, as you deserve. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 100} The commentary on 2:283 ends here. These are reports as transmitted, al-Tabari's choices and arguments are his, and the editor's notes are his, with no museum verdict and no legal ruling.
