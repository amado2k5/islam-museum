## Summary
Verse 13 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 13 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

## Verse 13 in al-Tabari's commentary (Shakir ed., volume 6, pp. 229-236)

For [[passage/quran-3-13|3:13]] al-Tabari says the sense is: say, Muhammad, to the disbelieving Jews who live around your city that they had a sign and a proof of the truth of what you said, that they would be overcome, and a lesson. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 229} In 6673, Qatada (through Bishr, Yazid and Said): a lesson and a matter for reflection. In 6674, al-Rabi (through al-Muthanna, Ishaq and Ibn Abi Jafar from his father): the same, except that he said 'something to reflect on'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 230}

Al-Tabari explains the two parties as two groups and factions, and the word for a party as a gathering of people; he says they met for war, one being the Messenger and those with him who were at the battle of Badr, the other the polytheists of Quraysh, so that the first is a company that fights in obedience to God and for His religion and the other disbelieves. The editor refers, for the word for a party and for the path of God, to earlier places in the commentary. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 230} In 6675, Ibn Abbas (through Abu Kurayb, Yunus ibn Bukayr, Ibn Ishaq, Muhammad ibn Abi Muhammad and Said ibn Jubayr or Ikrima): the first were the Messenger's Companions at Badr, the other the party of the disbelievers of Quraysh. In 6676, Ibn Abbas (through Ibn Humayd, Salama and Ibn Ishaq, by the same chain): the same. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 230} The editor notes that these two reports are in Ibn Hisham's Sira (3: 51) with a difference in wording owing to the difference in the narration. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 230} In 6677, Ikrima (through al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hajjaj and Ibn Jurayj): Muhammad and his Companions, and Quraysh at Badr. In 6678, Mujahid (through Muhammad ibn Amr, Abu Asim, Isa and Ibn Abi Najih): in Muhammad and his Companions and the polytheists of Quraysh at Badr. In 6679, Mujahid (through al-Muthanna, Abu Hudhayfa, Shibl and Ibn Abi Najih): the same. In 6680, Mujahid (through al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, al-Thawri and Ibn Abi Najih): that was Badr, when the Muslims and the disbelievers met. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 231}

Al-Tabari then explains why the second party is in the nominative after the word for 'two parties' has come: it is on the pattern of a fresh start, meaning that one of them fights. He cites a line of Kuthayyir in which the poet describes himself as a man with two legs, one sound and the other struck by time so that it withered, and a line that the commentary ascribes to Ibn Mufarrigh dividing a people between a sound leg and a leg afflicted by doubt. He says the Arabs do this with anything repeated that follows a like term when it carries a predicate: they sometimes return it to the case of the first and sometimes begin it afresh in the nominative, and they put it in the accusative with a complete or defective verb; the genitive also occurs, returning to the beginning of the sentence. He says the genitive here would be permissible, returning to 'the two parties', but that he does not allow reading it so because the authoritative reciters are agreed against it, and that the accusative too would be permissible, as a state of the two differing parties. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 232} The editor gives the sources for the first line (Kuthayyir's Diwan, al-Farra's Ma'ani al-Qur'an, Abu Ubayda's Majaz al-Qur'an, Sibawayh, Khizanat al-Adab), sets out lines of the poem and the commentators' readings of them, from al-A'lam's view that the poet wished one of his legs to be lamed while he was with his beloved and his she-camel to stray so that he could not leave her, to other views, and says that he has his own view of the lines, which this is not the place to give. Of the second line he says he could not identify an ascription to Ibn Mufarrigh and that it is without doubt by al-Najashi al-Harithi, from his poem on Mu'awiya and Ali; that the Azd tribes named fought at Siffin, Azd Shanu'a with the Syrians and Azd Uman with the Iraqis; that the version in al-Wahshiyyat and Ibn al-Shajari's Hamasa differs from al-Tabari's; and that al-Najashi was with Ali. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 232}

On p. 233 al-Tabari sets out the reciters' difference over the verb of seeing in the verse. The people of Madina read it with ta, so that the sense is that the Jews had a lesson in seeing the polytheists twice the Muslims with their own eyes; he means their warning in what they saw of the few Muslims, the many polytheists, and the victory of the few over the many. Most reciters of Kufa and Basra and some Meccans read it with ya, so that the Muslims who fight in God's path see the disbelieving company as twice the Muslims in size; the verse on their reading says that the Jews had a lesson in two parties that met, in which the Muslims, though few, saw the polytheists, though many. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 233}

Al-Tabari then puts a question in a questioner's voice, about the sense of the ya reading and which party saw the other as double, and answers that the interpreters differed. Some said that the party which saw the other as double was the Muslim one, which saw the polytheists' number as twice its own; God made them few in its eyes until it saw them as twice its number, and then in another state made them fewer still, so that it saw them equal to its own. The editor notes that the polytheists were many times their number and that God showed them only twice it, as will be explained. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 233} In 6681, Ibn Mas'ud (through Musa, Amr, Asbat, al-Suddi and Murra al-Hamdani): this is the day of Badr; Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud said: we looked at the polytheists and saw them double us, then we looked and did not see them exceed us by a single man, and that is God's word in al-Anfal ([[passage/quran-8-44|8:44]]). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 234}

Al-Tabari spells out the sense on this reading: the party of few sees the party of many as multiples, so that it sees it exceed its own number by one equal only; so they see them as twice their number, one of the two being a number equal to the seeing party and the other the extra that doubles it, which is one of the two senses of the lessening that God told the believers He had done in their eyes; the other is the second lessening, as Ibn Mas'ud said, that He showed them the polytheists equal to their own number, not more. The editor says the print changed a phrase because the printer missed the sense of restriction in it, and that the manuscript's reading is right. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 234}

Others of this camp said that those who saw the polytheists as twice themselves were the Muslims, but they saw them as they were, not reduced in their eyes, and God supported them with His victory; this is why God said to the Jews that they had a lesson in them, warning them lest the like of what He brought on the people of Badr at their hands come on them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 235} In 6682, Ibn Abbas (through Muhammad ibn Sad, his father, his uncle, his father and his grandfather): it was sent down about the lightening on the day of Badr, when the believers were three hundred and thirteen and the polytheists twice that, six hundred and twenty-six, and God supported the believers; this was the lightening for them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 235} The editor says the print has a very corrupt phrase at the start, because the publisher could not read the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 235}

Al-Tabari remarks, in his own comment, that this report is contrary to what the reports agree on about the polytheists' number at Badr, since people differed over it in two ways: some said they were a thousand, others between nine hundred and a thousand. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 235} For those who said a thousand: in 6683, Ali (through Harun ibn Ishaq al-Hamdani, Musab ibn al-Miqdam, Israil, Abu Ishaq and Haritha): the Messenger set out for Badr and we reached it before the polytheists and found two men there, a Qurashi and a freedman of Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt; the Qurashi escaped and we took the freedman and kept asking how many the people were, and he said: many, and fierce in might. Whenever he said that the Muslims beat him, until they brought him to the Messenger, who asked him and got the same answer; the Prophet pressed him to say how many they were but he refused. Then he asked how many camels they slaughtered, and he said ten each day, and the Messenger said: the people are a thousand. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 236} The editor says both manuscript and print have a plainly wrong word in one place, the right one being from al-Tabari's history (2: 269), that the plural in the report means slaughter camels, male or female, and that the history has 'ten', which is better. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 236} In 6684, Abd Allah (through Abu Said ibn Yusha al-Baghdadi, Ishaq ibn Mansur, Israil, Abu Ishaq and Abu Ubayda): we took a man of the polytheists prisoner on the day of Badr and asked how many they were, and he said a thousand. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 236} The editor says he found no entry for Abu Said ibn Yusha al-Baghdadi in the books at hand. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 236}

We stop on p. 236 before report 6685 (Urwa ibn al-Zubayr's account of the Companions sent to the water of Badr), which opens the reports of those who said the polytheists numbered between nine hundred and a thousand. These are reports as transmitted, al-Tabari's choices and arguments are his, and the editor's notes and references are his, with no museum verdict.
