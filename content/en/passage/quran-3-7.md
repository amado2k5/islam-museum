## Summary
Verse 7 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 7 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

([[passage/quran-3-7|3:7]]), pp. 169 to 173. Al-Tabari says the God from whom no thing, earthly or heavenly, is concealed is the one who sent down the Qur'an, by 'the Book' meaning the Qur'an, and that he has already covered the reason the Qur'an is called a book. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 169} The editor refers to part 1 (p. 99), part 3 (p. 86) and the language indexes. He then explains that the firm verses are verses of the Book, that is, of the Qur'an, and that the firm ones are those made firm by explanation and detail, whose arguments and proofs are established for what they are proofs of: lawful and forbidden, promise and threat, reward and punishment, command and prohibition, report and parable, exhortation and lesson, and the like. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 170}

God then describes these firm verses as the foundation of the Book, which al-Tabari explains as the root of the Book in which are the pillar of the religion, the obligations, the limits and everything people need in the matter of their religion and the duties laid on them in this world and the next. They are called by the word for 'mother' because they are the bulk of the Book and the place its people resort to when they need it; so the Arabs call what gathers and forms the greater part of something its 'mother': the banner that gathers the people in camps is their mother, and the manager who stands for most of the affairs of a village or town is its mother. The editor refers to part 1 (pp. 107-108), and al-Tabari points to his earlier explanation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 170}

Al-Tabari explains why the word for mother is singular and not the plural 'mothers' though the pronoun is plural: he meant that all the firm verses together are the mother, not that each verse is a mother; had each verse been a mother the plural would have been said. He compares the sura of the Believers, where the son of Mary and his mother were made one sign in the singular and not two signs ([[passage/quran-23-50|23:50]]), because the meaning is that together they are one sign, the sense being one in what they were made for as a lesson to creation; had he meant each one on its own he would have said two signs, since in each was a lesson for people: Mary gave birth without a man, and her son spoke in the cradle as a baby. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 171} The editor corrects in a footnote a meaningless sentence in the print caused by the copyist's reading of the manuscript, and another where the right wording is in the manuscript.

He then relays, without naming him, the view of a Basran grammarian: the singular was said by way of quotation, as a man says 'I have no helpers' and you answer 'I am your helpers', or 'I have no equal' and you say 'we are your equal'; he says it resembles 'leave me from two dates'; and he cites two lines of rajaz verse by a man of Fuqays about a woman who presented herself to him at a camping place as a tethered filly does at her pasture, in a way that fell short of nothing in killing him, the wording being by way of quotation because it had been accusative before, as one says 'the call went out: the prayer, the prayer', quoting the caller. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 172} He adds that some said the line has a different particle in the speaker's dialect and that the accusative is of command, as in 'a beating for Zayd'. In footnotes the editor identifies the poet as Mantur ibn Marthad ibn Farwa al-Fuqasi al-Asadi (also called Mantur ibn Habba al-Fuqasi al-Asadi, Habba being his mother), refers to the Majalis of Thalab (p. 602), Sharh Shawahid al-Shafiya (pp. 248-251), Sirr Sina'at al-I'rab (1: 177-179, 235) and Lisan al-Arab, explains the word for a tether as the rope let out for an animal to graze on and the verb 'did not fall short', notes the variant wording in Thalab's Majalis, and criticises an earlier editor's correction while showing where the copyist misread the manuscript.

Al-Tabari rejects this, as his own judgement, with the words 'this is a saying with no meaning', because all these examples are quotations by their reporters of another's words and the expressions he spoke, and it is known that God did not quote from anyone the saying about the mother, so it cannot be said that He put it by way of quotation from one who said it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 172} He then explains the word for 'others' as the plural of the feminine 'other'; the editor refers to part 3 (p. 459) and gives in a footnote his reason for preferring that reading.

Al-Tabari gives the difference among the people of Arabic on why this word is not declined: some said it is not declined because it is an adjective whose singular is the feminine 'other', as 'jama'' and 'kuta'' are not declined, being adjectives; others said it is not declined because of the letter added in its singular, its plural being built on the singular in leaving off the ending, as 'hamra' and 'bayda' are left undeclined in the indefinite and the definite for the added lengthening and hamza, after which the two plurals part: the plural of the feminine 'other' is built on its singular, so it is the pattern 'ukhar' and is not declined, whereas the plural of 'hamra' and 'bayda' is built against their singulars and is declined, as 'humr' and 'bid'; so their agreement in the singular makes them agree there and their difference in the plural makes their declension differ. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 173} The editor says he left one word in al-Tabari's sentence as it stands because he could not decide its sense, and that the word for 'pattern' in it is probably the copyist's addition.

He then explains the resembling verses as resembling in recitation and differing in meaning, as in the report of the fruit of paradise resembling in sight and differing in taste ([[passage/quran-2-25|2:25]]), and as in the saying of the Children of Israel about the cow that it resembled others to them in description though its kinds differed ([[passage/quran-2-70|2:70]]). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 173} The editor refers to part 1 (pp. 384-385) and part 2 (pp. 209-211).

On p. 174 al-Tabari sums up the sense of the first sentence: the one from whom nothing, earthly or heavenly, is hidden is the one who sent down the Qur'an to you, Muhammad, in which are verses made firm by explanation that are the foundation of the Book on which you and your community rest in religion and to which you and they resort in the laws of Islam He made obligatory for you, and other verses that resemble one another in recitation and differ in meaning. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 174}

He then says the interpreters differed over which verses of the Book are the firm and which the resembling, and he sets out five views, each given as transmitted.

First view: the firm verses are those acted upon, namely the abrogating ones or those that establish rulings, and the resembling ones are those left unacted on, the abrogated. In 6573, Ibn Abbas (through Yaqub ibn Ibrahim, Hushaym and al-Awwam from someone who told him): the firm verses are the three verses in the sura of the Cattle starting at [[passage/quran-6-151|6:151]], and the passage in the sura of the Night Journey starting at [[passage/quran-17-23|17:23]] to the end of that run. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 174} In a footnote the editor corrects a word in the print from al-Durr al-Manthur (4: 2); he fears that 'from Abu Ishaq' dropped from the chain after al-Awwam (ibn Hawshab, who narrates from Abu Ishaq al-Sabii), that the one Abu Ishaq narrates from in this report is Abd Allah ibn Qays (referring to Tahdhib al-Tahdhib 5: 365), and that al-Hakim gave the report in al-Mustadrak (2: 288) through Ali ibn Salih ibn Hayy from Abu Ishaq from Abd Allah ibn Qays from Ibn Abbas, calling it sound, with al-Dhahabi agreeing; but he did not put the suspected gap into the text.

In 6574, Ibn Abbas (through al-Muthanna, Abu Salih, Muawiya ibn Salih and Ali ibn Abi Talha): the firm are its abrogating verses, its lawful and unlawful, its limits and obligations, what is believed and acted on; the resembling are its abrogated verses, its advanced and delayed, its parables and oaths, what is believed and not acted on. In 6575, Ibn Abbas (through Muhammad ibn Sad, his father, uncle, father and grandfather): the firm are the abrogating verse by which one is bound and acts, and the resembling are the abrogated ones by which one is not bound. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 175} In 6576, al-Suddi (through Musa, Amr and Asbat, from Abu Malik and Abu Salih from Ibn Abbas, from Murra al-Hamdani from Ibn Masud, and from some Companions): the firm are the abrogating verses acted on, the resembling the abrogated. In 6577, Qatada (through Bishr, Yazid and Said): the firm is the abrogating that is acted on, in which God made lawful and unlawful, and the resembling the abrogated, believed in and not acted on. In 6578, Qatada (through al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq and Mamar): the firm is what is acted on. In 6579, al-Rabi' (through al-Muthanna, Ishaq, Ibn Abi Jafar and his father) the same.

On p. 176: 6580, al-Dahhak (through al-Muthanna, Amr, Hushaym and Juwaybir): the abrogating verses, and the resembling what was abrogated and left in recitation. 6581, al-Dahhak ibn Muzahim (through Ibn Waki, his father and Salama ibn Nubayt): the firm is what was not abrogated, the resembling what was. 6582, al-Dahhak (through Yahya ibn Abi Talib, Yazid and Juwaybir): the abrogating and the abrogated. 6583, al-Dahhak (told through al-Husayn ibn al-Faraj, Abu Muadh and Ubayd ibn Sulayman): the abrogating that is acted on, and the abrogated that is believed and not acted on. 6584, al-Dahhak (through Ahmad ibn Hazim, Abu Nuaym and Salama): what was not abrogated and what was. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 176}

Second view: the firm are those in which God made firm the explanation of the lawful and unlawful, and the resembling are those of which one part resembles another in meaning though the wording differs. In 6585, Mujahid (through Muhammad ibn Amr, Abu Asim, Isa and Ibn Abi Najih): the firm are what has the lawful and unlawful, and everything else is resembling, confirming one another; he gave as examples places in the sura of the Cow ([[passage/quran-2-26|2:26]]), the Cattle ([[passage/quran-6-125|6:125]]) and Muhammad ([[passage/quran-47-17|47:17]]). In 6586, the same from Mujahid (through al-Muthanna, Abu Hudhayfa, Shibl and Ibn Abi Najih). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 177}

Third view: the firm are those that allow only one interpretation, and the resembling those that allow several. In 6587, Muhammad ibn Jafar ibn al-Zubayr (through Ibn Humayd, Salama and Muhammad ibn Ishaq): in the firm are the Lord's proof, the protection of servants and the repelling of opponents and falsehood, with no turning or distortion from what they were set for; the resembling have turning, distortion and interpretation, and in them God tested His servants as He tested them in lawful and unlawful, so they are not turned to falsehood or deflected from truth. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 177} The editor lists where Ibn Hisham's text differs from the print and manuscript in this report, corrects a phrase from the manuscript and Ibn Hisham, and says it is the remainder of the reports ending at 6571, from Ibn Ishaq's narration.

Fourth view: the firm is what God made firm of the stories of the nations and their messengers, detailing it for Muhammad and his community, and the resembling is what the wording of their stories resembles on repetition across the suras, told with the same words and different meanings or different words and the same meaning. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 178} In 6588, Ibn Zayd (through Yunus and Ibn Wahb): he read the opening of the sura of Hud ([[passage/quran-11-1|11:1]]), said the story of the Messenger takes twenty-four of its verses and that of Noah twenty-four, then mentioned the pointer to the news of the unseen ([[passage/quran-11-49|11:49]]), then Ad, Salih, Abraham, Lot and Shuayb; he said this is the explanation of how the sura's verses were first made firm and then detailed; and the resembling is the mention of Moses in many places, all of one meaning, giving as examples of resembling wording the different expressions for taking the family aboard the ark ([[passage/quran-23-27|23:27]], [[passage/quran-11-40|11:40]]), for putting in and drawing out the hand ([[passage/quran-28-32|28:32]], [[passage/quran-27-12|27:12]]), and for the staff as a snake and as a serpent ([[passage/quran-20-20|20:20]], [[passage/quran-7-107|7:107]], [[passage/quran-26-32|26:32]]). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 179} He adds that Hud has ten verses there, Salih eight, Abraham eight others and Moses four, all of it judging between prophets and their peoples in that sura until verse one hundred ([[passage/quran-11-100|11:100]]), and that on the resembling in the Qur'an, whomever God wants to try with affliction and misguidance asks: what is the matter with this that it is not so? In footnotes the editor says the sentence on the resembling interrupts the account of the stories and the counting of verses, sets out the places of the examples in order, corrects copyist slips and prefers 'explanation' in the reading, and found a parallel in al-Suyuti's al-Durr al-Manthur (3: 320) from Ibn Abi Hatim from Ibn Zayd, where he adds that his father Zayd ibn Aslam used to say it.

Fifth view: the firm is what the scholars knew the interpretation of and understood the meaning and commentary, and the resembling is what no one has a way to know, which God kept to Himself, such as the time of the coming of Jesus son of Mary, the time of the sun's rising from its setting place, the rising of the Hour and the end of the world. They said God called the separate letters at the start of some suras resembling because their wording is alike and they match the letters of the abjad reckoning; and some Jews in the Messenger's time hoped to learn from them the length of Islam and its people and the term of Muhammad and his community, and God gave the lie to their talk and told them that what they sought they could not reach from these letters nor from any others, and that only God knows it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 179} Al-Tabari says this view is reported from Jabir ibn Abd Allah ibn Ri'ab, that the verse was revealed about it, and that he gave the report from him and others in the commentary on the opening of the sura of the Cow. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 180} The editor refers to part 1 (pp. 224-245) and report 246 with its note, glosses 'akl' as the length of a life, fixes Ri'ab with a kasra, and corrects wording of the print from the manuscript.

Al-Tabari chooses, as his own preference, that what is reported from Jabir is nearest to the interpretation of the verse, because everything God sent down of the Qur'an to His messenger He sent as explanation for him and his community and guidance for the worlds, and it is not allowed that it contain what they have no need of, nor what they need and then have no way to know the interpretation of; so whatever creation needs is knowable. He gives the example of the verse in the Cattle on the appearance of one of the Lord's great signs, after which late belief is of no use ([[passage/quran-6-158|6:158]]): the Prophet told his community that this sign is the sun rising from where it sets; what people needed to know was the time when repentance profits, by description and without fixing the number of years, months and days, and God made that clear by the indication of the Book and the Messenger's explanation; what they do not need is the length of the period between the revelation of that verse and the occurrence of that sign, which is the knowledge God kept to Himself and veiled from them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 181} He adds that this and its like is what the Jews sought to learn about the duration of Muhammad and his community from the separate letters, and that they cannot reach its interpretation from them and that only God knows it. The editor refers to the commentary on that verse in al-Tabari's work (8: 71-77, Bulaq) and corrects print wording from the manuscript. On p. 180 he says the clause he set in brackets in al-Tabari's sentence is unclear, that he fears the copyist dropped lines there, that leaving it out would not harm the context, but he kept it between brackets to show its place.

Al-Tabari goes on: if the resembling is what we have described, everything else is firm, since it is either firm with a single meaning and no other interpretation, needing no explanation once heard, or firm though having many aspects and interpretations, with the indication of the intended meaning coming either from God's explanation or from His messenger's explanation to his community; and knowledge of it will not escape the scholars of the community. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 182}

He then begins the clause about the foundation of the Book, referring to his earlier explanation, and relays the interpreters' difference. Some said: they are those with the obligations, limits and rulings, in line with al-Tabari's own saying. In 6589, Yahya ibn Yamar (through Imran ibn Musa al-Qazzaz, Abd al-Warith ibn Said and Ishaq ibn Suwayd): they are those with the obligations, the limits and the pillar of the religion; he gave an illustration, saying the mother of the towns is Mecca, the mother of Khurasan is Marw, and the mother of travellers is the one they entrust their affairs to and who cares for them on the road. In 6590, Ibn Zayd (through Yunus and Ibn Wahb): they are the whole of the Book. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 182} The editor says the biographies of Imran and Abd al-Warith were given at report 2154.

Others said that the foundation of the Book means the openings of the suras, from which the Qur'an is drawn out. In 6591, Abu Fakhita (through Imran ibn Musa, Abd al-Warith ibn Said and Ishaq ibn Suwayd): the mother of the Book is the sura openings, from which the Qur'an is drawn; from the letters opening the sura of the Cow the Cow was drawn, and from those opening Al Imran, Al Imran ([[passage/quran-2-1|2:1]], [[passage/quran-3-1|3:1]]). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 183} The editor says Abu Fakhita is Said ibn Ilaqa al-Hashimi, freedman of Umm Hani, a reliable narrator with an entry in al-Tahdhib, and that al-Suyuti gave this report with 6589 in al-Durr al-Manthur (4: 2) as one abridged report from Ishaq ibn Suwayd, that Yahya ibn Yamar and Abu Fakhita disputed over this verse.

Al-Tabari then explains the clause about those whose hearts incline: the meaning is those in whose hearts there is a turning from the truth and a deviation from it. He gives the verb's forms in the language: so-and-so 'zagha' from the truth, he deviates, with noun forms zaygh, zayghan, zaghugha and zuyugh, and God 'azagha' him when He makes him incline, which is the sense of the plea in 3:8 that God not turn hearts from the truth after guiding them ([[passage/quran-3-8|3:8]]). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 183} On p. 184: 6592, Muhammad ibn Jafar ibn al-Zubayr (through Ibn Humayd, Salama and Ibn Ishaq): a turning from guidance; the editor says it is the remainder of the reports ending at 6587. 6593, Mujahid (through Muhammad ibn Amr, Abu Asim, Isa and Ibn Abi Najih): doubt; 6594 the same (through al-Muthanna, Abu Hudhayfa and Shibl). 6595, Ibn Abbas (through al-Muthanna, Abd Allah ibn Salih, Muawiya ibn Salih and Ali ibn Abi Talha): of the people of doubt. 6596, al-Suddi (through Musa ibn Harun, Amr and Asbat, from Abu Malik and Abu Salih from Ibn Abbas, from Murra al-Hamdani from Ibn Masud and some Companions): the inclination is doubt. 6597, Mujahid (through al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hajjaj and Ibn Jurayj): doubt; and Ibn Jurayj said that those whose hearts incline here are the hypocrites. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 184}

Al-Tabari then explains their following of what resembles: they pursue what is alike in wording and shifting in meaning across the aspects of interpretation, so as to establish by the false interpretations they claim the error and deviation from the highway of truth they are on, confusing those whose knowledge of the aspects of interpretation and the turns of meaning is weak. In 6598, Ibn Abbas (through al-Muthanna, Abd Allah ibn Salih, Muawiya and Ali): they carry the firm onto the resembling and the resembling onto the firm and confuse, so God confused them. In 6599, Muhammad ibn Jafar ibn al-Zubayr (through Ibn Humayd, Salama and Ibn Ishaq): what has been turned and shifted, to confirm what they innovated and made up, so that they may have a proof for what they said and a doubt. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 185} The editor says Ibn Hisham has a different word and lacks 'turned', that it is the remainder of the reports ending at 6592, and that Ibn Hisham's text (2: 226) differs slightly, so he left the commentary's wording as it is since its narration from Ibn Ishaq differs from Ibn Hisham's. In 6600, Mujahid (through al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hajjaj and Ibn Jurayj): it is the door they strayed from and perished in, seeking its interpretation.

On p. 186: 6601, al-Suddi (through Musa ibn Harun, Amr and Asbat): they follow the abrogated and the abrogating and say: why was this verse acted on in such a way in place of that one, the first left and this one acted on? Why was this not acted on before the first, which was abrogated, came? And why does He threaten punishment for one who does an act that He punishes by fire, while elsewhere the same act does not make the fire obligatory? {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 186} The editor gives his readings of the manuscript and print at these two points.

Al-Tabari says the interpreters differed over whom the verse meant. Some said it meant the delegation of Najran from the Christians who came to the Messenger and argued with him, saying: do you not claim that Jesus is God's spirit and His word? They interpreted from that what they say of Jesus, which al-Tabari describes as disbelief in their saying. In 6602, al-Rabi' (through al-Muthanna, Ishaq, Ibn Abi Jafar and his father): the delegation that came to the Messenger from the Christians of Najran set about it and disputed with him, saying: do you not claim he is God's word and a spirit from Him? He said: yes. They said: then that is enough for us! So God sent down what He sent about those whose hearts incline, and then sent down the clarification of Jesus' likeness to Adam ([[passage/quran-3-59|3:59]]). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 187} This position, attributed to the Christians, is given as the narrators and al-Tabari relate it, with no museum verdict.

Others said it was revealed about Abu Yasir ibn Akhtab and his brother Huyayy ibn Akhtab and those with them who debated the Messenger about the length of his remaining time and his community's, wanting to learn it from the letters opening the suras; God made the matter of those whose hearts incline about them, namely these Jews whose hearts inclined from guidance and truth, and the 'resembling' means the meanings of these separate letters, open to being turned across various interpretations; al-Tabari had given the report earlier at the start of the sura of the Cow (report 246). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 187}

Others said it meant every innovator in his religion who invents an innovation contrary to what Muhammad was sent with, by an interpretation he draws from some verses open to interpretation, though God has made the explanation of it firm in His Book or on the tongue of His messenger. In 6603, Qatada (through al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq and Mamar): when he read this verse he said: if they are not the Haruriyya and the Saba'iyya, I do not know who they are. Al-Tabari then relays his further words: that in the people of Badr and al-Hudaybiya who witnessed the pledge of al-Ridwan, Emigrants and Helpers, there is information for whoever asks and a lesson for whoever takes one; that the Khawarij rose when the Messenger's Companions were many in Medina, Syria and Iraq and his wives alive, and that no male or female of those Companions went out as a Haruri, nor were they pleased with what they were on, but they spoke of what the Prophet had said in blame of them and his description of them, hated them in their hearts and opposed them with their tongues, and their hands were hard on them when they met; that had the Khawarij's cause been guidance it would have held together but it was error so it split, as whatever is not from God has much disagreement in it; that they had worked at the matter for a long time without prospering any day, and had they been on guidance God would have shown and supported it, but they were on falsehood that God refuted; that whenever a horn of theirs rose, God refuted their argument and spilled their blood, and if they hide it, it is a sore and grief in their hearts, and if they show it, God spills their blood; and then that Judaism, Christianity, the Haruriyya and the Saba'iyya are innovations that no book came down with and no prophet laid down. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 188} This is Qatada's wording as al-Tabari reports it, his own judgement and not the museum's.

In his footnotes on pp. 187-189 the editor explains that the Haruriyya are the Khawarij who gathered at Harura near Kufa, where their first assembly and their arbitration took place when they opposed Ali; that the Saba'iyya are ascribed to Ibn al-Sawda the Jew, Abd Allah ibn Saba', who, the editor relates, said to Ali 'you are you', meaning the imam has a divine part, and Ali sent him into exile to al-Mada'in; that he preferred the manuscript's spelling; that the lesson meant at Badr is the Companions' counsel to the Prophet to leave his first station, and at al-Hudaybiya the words of some of the pledgers in disliking the treaty the Prophet made with Quraysh, which he takes as proof against the Khawarij's and the Saba'iyya's claims; he glosses 'alasa' as turned and attempted, corrects 'aflaja' with a jim as 'made his proof prevail', and drops a superfluous waw in one place. He also explains that Judaism and Christianity at the end of the report mean what the Jews and Christians invented in saying that Uzayr and the Messiah are sons of God and in their other statements, which is the editor's gloss. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 189}

In 6604, Qatada (through Bishr, Yazid and Said): the people sought the interpretation, erred in it and met the trial, so they followed the resembling and perished by it; by my life, among the people of Badr and al-Hudaybiya, and he gave something like the report of Abd al-Razzaq from Mamar. In 6605, Aisha (through Muhammad ibn Khalid ibn Khidash and Yaqub ibn Ibrahim, Ismail ibn Ulayya, Ayyub and Abd Allah ibn Abi Mulayka): the Messenger recited the passage from the verse's start to its end, then said: when you see those who dispute about it, they are the ones God meant, so beware of them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 189} The editor notes that al-Tabari gives it here with eleven chains, all from Ibn Abi Mulayka but one (6611); that the narrators differ over whether he took it directly from Aisha or through al-Qasim, and that all are sound, as will follow; he gives Ibn Abi Mulayka as Abd Allah ibn Ubayd Allah, the Qurashi of Mecca, a reliable Follower who heard Aisha and others, and cites al-Tirmidhi (4: 80) on the two routes and that Yazid ibn Ibrahim was not alone in mentioning al-Qasim.

In 6606, Aisha (through Ibn Abd al-Ala, al-Mutamir ibn Sulayman, Ayyub and Ibn Abi Mulayka) the same, with 'dispute about it or dispute among themselves', and Matar said from Ayyub: so do not sit with them, they are those God meant, so beware them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 190} In a footnote the editor gives al-Hafiz's words in Fath al-Bari (8: 157) on Ibn Abi Mulayka's hearing from Aisha and the disagreement over this report, says Ahmad gave it in the Musnad (6: 48) and Ibn Majah (47) from Muhammad ibn Khalid ibn Khidash, al-Tabari's teacher, gives a note on Ibn Khidash, quotes Ibn Kathir (2: 97), notes Matar ibn Tahman al-Warraq (d. 125) and Ibn Hibban's Sahih (no. 75) and judges Ibn Hibban's wording there a slip of the pen or a copyist's lapse.

In the rest of his footnote on p. 191 the editor cites Abu Zura in Ibn Abi Hatim's al-Marasil (p. 78) that Matar did not hear anything from Anas and his report is mursal, and says that al-Tabari's report here states plainly that Matar heard the hadith from Ayyub with the additional wording, that al-Mutamir ibn Sulayman heard it from Ayyub in brief, with the wording of warning, and then from Matar al-Warraq from Ayyub at greater length, and that Matar and Ayyub were of one generation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 191}

Al-Tabari then gives further routes for the hadith of Aisha. In 6607 (through Ibn Bashshar, Abd al-Wahhab, Ayyub and Ibn Abi Mulayka, from Aisha, from the Prophet) with similar meaning; the editor says Abd al-Wahhab is Ibn Abd al-Majid al-Thaqafi, that Ibn Majah (47) gave it from Ahmad ibn Thabit al-Jahdari and Yahya ibn Hakam from Abd al-Wahhab, and that Ibn Kathir points to Ibn Majah's report and to Muhammad ibn Yahya al-Abdi's in his Musnad. In 6608 (through al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, Mamar and Ayyub) the like; the editor cites Ibn Kathir that Abd al-Razzaq gave it from Mamar from Ayyub and that more than one gave it from Ayyub, and notes that the Cairo manuscript of Abd al-Razzaq's commentary lacks the end of the sura of the Cow to the start of the Women. In 6609 (through Yunus, Ibn Wahb, al-Harith, Ayyub and Ibn Abi Mulayka), Aisha: the Messenger recited the whole verse and said: when you see those who follow what resembles and those who dispute about it, they are the ones God meant, they are those God spoke of, so do not sit with them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 191} The editor says al-Harith is Ibn Nabhan al-Jarmi of Basra, very weak; he quotes al-Bukhari that he is 'rejected in hadith' and Ahmad that he was a righteous man who did not know hadith; the hadith's basis is nonetheless sound through the other chains before and after.

On p. 192: 6610, Aisha (through Ibn Waki, Abu Usama and Yazid ibn Ibrahim, from Ibn Abi Mulayka, who said: I heard al-Qasim ibn Muhammad narrate from Aisha): the Prophet recited the verse and read on to the end of the verses and said: when you see those who follow what resembles, they are the ones God named, so beware of them. In 6611 (through Ali ibn Sahl, al-Walid ibn Muslim, Hammad ibn Salama, Abd al-Rahman ibn al-Qasim and his father, from Aisha), the Messenger cited the clause about those who follow the resembling and said: God has warned you, so when you see them, recognize them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 192} The editor says of 6610 that Ibn Waki is Sufyan ibn Waki, weak; Abu Usama is Hammad ibn Usama al-Kufi, a hafiz; Yazid ibn Ibrahim al-Tustari of Basra is reliable and firm, trusted by Ahmad, Waki and Abu Hatim; that this is one of the routes that add al-Qasim ibn Muhammad between Ibn Abi Mulayka and Aisha; that all are sound because Ibn Abi Mulayka heard it from Aisha and also from al-Qasim from Aisha and narrated it both ways. He gives its sources: Abu Dawud al-Tayalisi (1433), al-Bukhari (Fath 8: 157-159), Muslim (2: 303-304), Abu Dawud (4598), al-Tirmidhi (4: 80, who calls it good and sound) and Ibn Hibban (72), and says Yazid ibn Ibrahim was not alone in adding al-Qasim. Of 6611 he says it is a sound chain and a strong corroboration of Ibn Abi Mulayka's report through al-Qasim, refers to the narrators' earlier biographies, quotes Ibn Kathir (2: 98) that Ibn Mardawayh gave it by another route from al-Qasim, and explains her word 'cited' as quoting the verse in illustration.

On p. 193: 6612, Aisha (through Ali, al-Walid, Nafi ibn Umar and Ibn Abi Mulayka): when you see them, beware of them; then he quoted the clause about those with inclined hearts who follow the resembling, 'and do not act on its firm verses'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 193} In a long footnote the editor says Nafi ibn Umar ibn Abd Allah ibn Jamil al-Jumahi al-Qurashi of Mecca is reliable (Ahmad: firm, firm, sound in hadith); that the manuscript and print wrote 'Nafi from Umar', an error he corrected from the Fath (8: 157) and Ibn Kathir; and that both copies have a worse slip dropping Ibn Abi Mulayka and his statement of hearing from the chain, so he restored what dropped between two marks of addition on the strength of Ibn Kathir (2: 98), who reports that Ibn Jarir gave it from Rawh ibn al-Qasim and Nafi ibn Umar al-Jumahi, both from Ibn Abi Mulayka from Aisha, and that Nafi said: Aisha narrated to me.

On p. 194: 6613, Aisha (through Ahmad ibn Abd al-Rahman ibn Wahb, his uncle, Shabib ibn Said, Rawh ibn al-Qasim and Ibn Abi Mulayka): the Messenger was asked about this verse and said: when you see those who dispute about it, they are those God meant, so beware of them. In 6614 (through Muhammad ibn Abd Allah ibn Abd al-Hakam, Khalid ibn Nizar, Nafi and Ibn Abi Mulayka), Aisha would recite the verse and then say that the Prophet said: when you see those who dispute about it, beware of them, for they are those God meant. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 194} The editor says Ibn al-Madini declared Shabib ibn Said al-Habati reliable and his book sound; that Rawh ibn al-Qasim is reliable, as Ahmad and Ibn Main said; that Khalid ibn Nizar al-Ayli is reliable; that Ayyub, Nafi ibn Umar and Khalid ibn Nizar narrated from Ibn Abi Mulayka from Aisha directly without al-Qasim, and were followed by Abu Amir al-Khazzaz (Salih ibn Rustam) in al-Tirmidhi (4: 80), by Said ibn Mansur through Hammad ibn Yahya as Ibn Kathir reports, and by Ibn Jurayj, whom the Fath mentions without a source.

On p. 195: 6615, Aisha (through Ibn Waki, Yazid ibn Harun, Hammad ibn Salama, Ibn Abi Mulayka and al-Qasim, from the Prophet): they are those God named, so when you see them, beware of them. The editor says the chain is sound with al-Qasim added, which refutes al-Tirmidhi's remark that Yazid ibn Ibrahim alone added it, since Hammad ibn Salama corroborated him; he cites the Musnad of Abu Dawud al-Tayalisi (1432), notes that Abu al-Walid al-Tayalisi combined the two narrations as Ibn Abi Hatim has it in Ibn Kathir, that Hammad ibn Salama narrated through two teachers from al-Qasim, that Ibn Kathir mentions a further route of Ibn Mardawayh whose chain is not given, and that al-Suyuti (2: 5) also credits the basic report to al-Bayhaqi in al-Dala'il. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 195}

Then, on pp. 195-196, al-Tabari says as his own choice that what the verse's outward sense indicates is that it was revealed about those who disputed with the Messenger over the resembling in what was sent to him of God's Book, either about Jesus or about the length of his remaining time and his community's, and that it is likelier to be about those who disputed over his time and his community's, because the clause saying that God alone knows its interpretation shows it is a report about the term they sought to know through the resembling that only God knows; as for Jesus and his affair, God had informed His prophet Muhammad and his community and made it plain to them, so it is clear that the verse means only what was hidden of the terms. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 196} The editor corrects two wordings from the manuscript, glosses the word 'akl' as provision and hence the span of a life, and says the copyist is often careless and distorts in haste.

He then explains the aim of those with inclined hearts in following the resembling, saying the interpreters differed over it; the editor refers to his earlier explanations of the words for seeking and for discord (3: 508; 4: 163; 2: 443-444; 3: 565-571; 4: 301). Some said the meaning is seeking idolatry. In 6616, al-Suddi (through Musa ibn Harun, Amr ibn Hammad and Asbat): the wish for idolatry. In 6617, al-Rabi' (through al-Muthanna, Ishaq, Ibn Abi Jafar and his father): idolatry. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 196} Others said it is seeking the doubts. In 6618, Mujahid (through Muhammad ibn Amr, Abu Asim, Isa and Ibn Abi Najih): the doubts, by which they were destroyed; 6619 the same (through al-Muthanna, Abu Hudhayfa and Shibl), and 6620 (through al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hajjaj and Ibn Jurayj): the doubts, which are what they were destroyed by. In 6621, Muhammad ibn Jafar ibn al-Zubayr (through Ibn Humayd, Salama and Ibn Ishaq): confusion; the editor says it is the remainder of the reports ending at 6599. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 197}

Al-Tabari says, as his own choice, that the better of the two sayings is that its meaning is the wish for doubts and confusion, so that the sense is: those whose hearts have a turning from the truth and a leaning away from it follow, of the verses of the Book, what is alike in wording and allows a turner to turn it across the interpretations since it allows different meanings, wanting confusion for himself and others, arguing with it for the falsehood his heart leans to against the truth God made clear and plain by the firm verses of His Book. The editor explains in a footnote how the pronouns in the manuscript's wording are inconsistent and why he preferred the reading 'its turner'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 197}

On p. 198 he says that though the verse was revealed about the people of idolatry he mentioned, it concerns every innovator in God's religion whose heart leans to an innovation through his interpretation of some of the resembling verses, who then argues with it and disputes with the people of truth, turning from the plain proofs of the firm verses, wanting to confuse the believers and seeking knowledge of the interpretation of what resembled to him, whoever he is and whichever kind of innovator, of the Christians, the Jews, the Magians, or a follower of Saba, a Haruri, a Qadari or a Jahmi, as in the Prophet's saying in the report above. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 198} This generalisation and choice are al-Tabari's, and the museum passes no judgement on any group. In 6622, Ibn Abbas (through Yunus, Sufyan, Mamar, Ibn Tawus and his father), when the Khawarij and what they show at hearing the Qur'an were mentioned before him: they believe in its firm and perish at its resembling! And Ibn Abbas recited the clause saying that God alone knows its interpretation. The editor says the wording in the manuscript and print 'what they meet at flight' has no meaning; he judged that it concerns the Khawarij's humility, worship and submission when hearing the Qur'an, which is well known of them, those of whom the famous hadith says you would belittle your prayer beside theirs, and that Ibn Abbas's reply expresses wonder at their humility and their error in the innovative interpretation by which they made lawful the blood and property of Muslims; in another note he says he left the spelling of 'Saba'i' as it is there. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 198}

Al-Tabari completes on pp. 198-199, as his own choice, the reasoning for his preference: those among whom the verse was revealed were people of idolatry, and by seeking the interpretation they sought they meant only to confuse the Muslims and argue against them in order to turn them from the truth they held; so it makes no sense to say they did it wishing idolatry, when they were idolaters already. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 199}

He then takes up the seeking of interpretation and says the interpreters differed over the sense of the interpretation meant. Some said it is the term the Jews wanted to know, the end of the affair of Muhammad and his community, through the separate letters by the abjad reckoning and similar terms. In 6623, Ibn Abbas (through al-Muthanna, Abd Allah ibn Salih, Muawiya and Ali): its interpretation is the Day of Resurrection, and only God knows it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 199} Others said the sense is the outcomes of the Qur'an, saying they wanted to know when the abrogating verse would come to the rulings God had laid down for the people of Islam before it, abrogating what He had set down earlier. In 6624, al-Suddi (through Musa, Amr and Asbat): they wanted to know the interpretation of the Qur'an, which is its outcomes, and its interpretation is its outcomes: when would the abrogating come from it to abrogate the abrogated? {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 200} Others said the sense is seeking the interpretation of the resembling verses, interpreting them, since they have various aspects and turns, according to the inclination in their hearts and the error they had taken up. In 6625, Muhammad ibn Jafar ibn al-Zubayr (through Ibn Humayd, Salama and Ibn Ishaq): that is according to the error they had taken up in their saying..., pointing to their argument from the two verb forms in the first person plural found in the divine speech. The editor says the print has a word differing from the manuscript and the Sira of Ibn Hisham (2: 226), and that the mention of these two forms alludes to the opening of the long report 6543 (p. 153) in which Ibn Ishaq relays the Najran delegation's argument that God says 'we did, we commanded, we created, we decreed', that had He been one He would have said it in the singular, and that in their view He is Himself, Jesus and Mary. This position is attributed to those who held it in the report, with no museum verdict.

Al-Tabari says, as his own choice, that what Ibn Abbas said, namely that the seeking of interpretation which the people sought from the resembling is the knowledge of the end of the term and the time of the Hour, and what he has given from al-Suddi, that they sought to know a time that is coming before its arrival, is sounder, though al-Suddi overlooked the meaning by turning it to their having sought the time of the coming of the abrogating for what had been made firm before. He says he holds that the people's seeking knowledge of the coming time before its arrival, knowledge veiled from them and others, through the resembling verses, better fits the sense of this clause, because of what he showed earlier of God's report that none but God knows that interpretation; and there is no doubt that the sense of the two verb forms was known to many ignorant idolaters, let alone to believers and the firmly rooted. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 201} In notes on p. 200 the editor explains the grammar of the sentence, that 'coming' is an active participle, and that the prepositional phrase belongs to 'seeking'.

He then explains the clause that God alone knows the interpretation and the saying of the rooted: al-Tabari means that none knows the time of the Hour, the end of the term of Muhammad and his community, and what is to be, but God, apart from other humans who hoped to gain such knowledge by reckoning, astrology and soothsaying; and the rooted say they believe in it and that all is from their Lord, not knowing it, their superiority over others in knowledge there being the knowledge that God alone knows it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 201} He says the interpreters differed over whether 'the rooted' is joined to the name of God, so that they too have knowledge of the interpretation of the resembling, or is a new sentence reporting that they say they believe in the resembling and affirm that only God knows it. Some said: only God, alone in His knowledge, knows its interpretation; as for the rooted, the report about them begins anew, that they say they believe in the resembling and the firm and that all of it is from God.

In 6626, Aisha (through Muhammad ibn Abd Allah ibn Abd al-Hakam, Khalid ibn Nizar, Nafi and Ibn Abi Mulayka): their rootedness in knowledge was that they believed in its firm and resembling verses and did not know its interpretation. In 6627, from Ibn Tawus from his father (through al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq and Mamar): Ibn Abbas used to say the clause that God alone knows its interpretation, and then, with a conjunction before the verb, that the rooted say what they say, as in his reading. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 202} The editor says the print dropped the conjunction though it should stay since Ibn Abbas reads so, as will come on p. 204, that he thinks the original was 'Ibn Abbas used to read' and the copyist wrote 'say' in haste, and that he added the words 'in knowledge' in brackets because this is a reading and not an interpretation by Ibn Abbas. In 6628, Hisham ibn Urwa (through Yunus, Ibn Wahb and Ibn Abi al-Zinad): my father used to say of this verse that the rooted in knowledge do not know its interpretation but say that they believe in it and that all is from their Lord. In 6629, Abu Nahik al-Asadi (through Ibn Humayd, Yahya ibn Wadih and Ubayd Allah): you join this verse but it is cut off, so their knowledge ends at their saying. In 6630, Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz (through al-Muthanna, Ibn Dukayn and Amr ibn Uthman ibn Abd Allah ibn Mawhab): the knowledge of the rooted in the interpretation of the Qur'an ended at their saying they believe in it and that all is from their Lord. In 6631, Malik (through Yunus and Ashhab): then He began anew and said what He said about the rooted, and they do not know its interpretation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 203}

Others said the sense is that God and the rooted know its interpretation, and the rooted, with their knowledge and rootedness, say they believe in it and that all is from their Lord. In 6632, Mujahid from Ibn Abbas (through Muhammad ibn Amr, Abu Asim, Isa and Ibn Abi Najih): I am among those who know its interpretation. In 6633, Mujahid (by the same route): the rooted know its interpretation and say they believe; 6634 the same (through al-Muthanna, Abu Hudhayfa and Shibl); 6635, al-Rabi' the same (through Ammar ibn al-Hasan, Ibn Abi Jafar and his father). In 6636, Muhammad ibn Jafar ibn al-Zubayr (through Ibn Humayd, Salama and Ibn Ishaq): none knows the interpretation of what He intended but God and the rooted who say they believe and that all is from their Lord; how then could it differ, being one saying from one Lord? Then they returned the interpretation of the resembling to what they knew of the interpretation of the firm, which has only one interpretation, so the Book coheres and its parts confirm one another, the argument goes through, the excuse appears, falsehood is removed and disbelief is crushed. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 204} The editor says the wording is so in the manuscript, the print and Ibn Kathir (2: 100), that Ibn Hisham's Sira (2: 226) has another wording and both are right, that the part from 'all is from their Lord' to 'from one Lord' is an addition from Ibn Hisham's narration that the copyist dropped in haste, that 'zaha' means went away and was far, and that it is the remainder of the reports ending at 6625.

Al-Tabari then sets out the grammar: whoever takes the first view, that the rooted do not know the interpretation and God only reported their belief, makes 'the rooted' a subject by beginning the sentence in the Basrans' view, with their saying as its predicate; in the view of some Kufans it takes the nominative by the pronoun returning to them in the verb; in another's, by the whole clause of the report about them; and whoever takes the second view joins 'the rooted' to the name of God and so puts them in the nominative by the conjunction. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 204} He says, as his own choice, that the correct view is that they are nominative by the clause reporting them after them, because of what he showed before, that they do not know the interpretation of the resembling mentioned in the verse; and that, as it has reached him, it is also in the reading of Ubayy, which has a conjunction before their verb, as he reported of Ibn Abbas that he read it, and in the reading of Abd Allah ibn Masud, which has an exclusive form in place of the verse's, meaning that its interpretation is only with God, and then mentions their saying. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 204} The editor refers to his note on 6627.

He then begins the account of the meaning of 'interpretation' in the speech of the Arabs, saying it is explanation, return and destination, and mentions that one of the reporters recited a line of al-A'sha on it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 204}

On pp. 204-206 al-Tabari gives the meaning of 'interpretation' in the speech of the Arabs: explanation, return and destination. He mentions that one of the reporters recited a line of al-A'sha on it; he explains that it comes from 'the thing returned to such-and-such' when it came to it and went back, and that 'I made it return' means I brought it to it; that it was said of the phrase for the best interpretation that it means the best requital, since requital is where the people's affair returns ([[passage/quran-4-59|4:59]], [[passage/quran-17-35|17:35]]); and that the poet's 'the interpretation of her love' means the explanation and destination of her love, that is, her love was small in his heart and grew from small to great and kept growing until it was old, like the young camel that kept growing until it reached its mother's size. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 205} In notes on pp. 205-206 the editor gives the line's sources (the Diwan of al-A'sha, p. 88; Abu Ubayda's Majaz al-Qur'an 1: 86-87; al-Sahibi, p. 164; Lisan al-Arab), the other wording of the line in Lisan and al-Azhari's explanation of it, that separation weighed on the poet and he yearned for his beloved as a young camel yearns after being parted from its mother, and then keeps on and grows, al-Azhari saying he explained it because the reporters had stumbled over its meaning; he says he does not know the sense of the second word al-Tabari gives and fears the right reading is another, and corrects an extra waw in the manuscript. The line is also recited with another wording. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 206}

Al-Tabari then explains the rooted: the scholars who have mastered their knowledge and taken it in and memorised it, so that no doubt or confusion enters their knowing of what they know; the word comes from the thing taking root in the thing, its firmness and penetration, as one says faith took root in so-and-so's heart. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 206} The editor notes that one of the noun forms is not given in the dictionaries. He says a report was transmitted describing them from the Prophet. In 6637, Abu al-Darda and Abu Umama (through Musa ibn Sahl al-Ramli, Muhammad ibn Abd Allah, Fayyad ibn Muhammad al-Raqqi and Abd Allah ibn Yazid ibn Adam): the Messenger was asked who is rooted in knowledge, and he said: the one whose oath is kept, whose tongue is truthful, whose heart is upright and whose belly is restrained; that is the rooted in knowledge. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 206} The editor says al-Bukhari and Ibn Abi Hatim gave an entry for Fayyad ibn Muhammad al-Raqqi without criticism; that Ibn Abi Hatim records of Abd Allah ibn Yazid ibn Adam that his father said he did not know him and that a hadith of his was false; and that al-Dhahabi in al-Mizan and al-Hafiz in al-Lisan quote Ahmad that his hadiths were fabricated, with no kind word in his entry. This is the editor's judgement on the narrator, not the museum's.

In 6638, Anas ibn Malik, Abu Umama and Abu al-Darda (through al-Muthanna and Ahmad ibn al-Hasan al-Tirmidhi, Nuaym ibn Hammad, Fayyad al-Raqqi and Abd Allah ibn Yazid al-Awdi): the Messenger was asked about the rooted and said: the one whose oath is kept, whose tongue is truthful, whose heart is upright and whose belly and private part are restrained; that is the rooted. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 207} The editor says it is the earlier report with a slight addition and with Anas added; that 'al-Awdi' is most likely an error of one of the narrators and the right wording is 'ibn Adam' as in the earlier chain, since al-Awdi is another narrator who is reliable while the narrator here is, in his phrase, a liar; that Ibn Abi Hatim gave it through Muhammad ibn Awf al-Himsi from Nuaym ibn Hammad, that Ibn Kathir slipped in writing Ubayd Allah for Abd Allah, that al-Haythami in Majma al-Zawaid (6: 324) gives it from al-Tabarani with Wathila ibn al-Asqa added and says Abd Allah ibn Yazid is weak, that al-Suyuti (2: 7) credits it loosely to Ibn Jarir, Ibn Abi Hatim and al-Tabarani since Wathila is only in al-Tabarani's, and that a similar report is given from Ibn Asakir through al-Awdi.

Al-Tabari then says a group of the interpreters said that God named these people the rooted by their saying that they believe in all of it and that everything is from their Lord. In 6639, Ibn Abbas (through Ibn Waki, his father, Sufyan, Jabir and Mujahid): the rooted are those who say so. In 6640, al-Suddi (through Musa, Amr and Asbat): the rooted are the believers, who say they believe in it, in its abrogating and abrogated verses, and that all is from their Lord. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 208} In 6641, Ibn Jurayj (through al-Qasim, al-Husayn and Hajjaj): Ibn Abbas said Abd Allah ibn Salam said the rooted in knowledge, and their knowledge is their saying; Ibn Jurayj said they are those who say the plea that their hearts not be turned ([[passage/quran-3-8|3:8]]) and that God will gather people for a day of no doubt ([[passage/quran-3-9|3:9]]). The editor says the manuscript and print wrote the phrase without the conjunction and that he restored the verse's wording. Al-Tabari explains their saying that they believe in it as their affirming what resembled of the verses of the Book and that it is true, though they do not know its interpretation. In 6642, al-Dahhak (through Ahmad ibn Hazim, Abu Nuaym and Salama ibn Nubayt): the firm and the resembling.

Then he explains their saying that all is from their Lord: all the firm of the Book and all the resembling are from their Lord, which is its sending down and revelation to His prophet Muhammad. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 209} In 6643, Ibn Abbas (through Ibn Waki, his father, Sufyan, Jabir and Mujahid): what was abrogated of it and what was not. In 6644, Qatada (through Bishr, Yazid and Said): they said all is from their Lord, believing in its resembling and acting on its firm. In 6645, al-Rabi' (through Ammar ibn al-Hasan, Ibn Abi Jafar and his father): they say the firm and the resembling are from our Lord. In 6646, Ibn Abbas (through Muhammad ibn Sad, his father, uncle, father and grandfather): we believe in the firm and are bound by it, and believe in the resembling and are not bound by it, and it is all from God. In 6647, al-Dahhak (through Yahya ibn Abi Talib, Yazid and Juwaybir): they act on it; they say we act on the firm and believe in it, and we believe in the resembling and do not act on it, and all is from our Lord. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 209} The editor says the print wrote the two verbs with the third-person prefix and the context requires the first-person plural.

We stop at the end of p. 209; after it al-Tabari begins the Arabic grammarians' difference over the ruling on the word for 'all' when what it is attached to is left unstated. These are reports as transmitted, al-Tabari's choices and arguments are his, and the editor's notes and references are his, with no museum verdict.
