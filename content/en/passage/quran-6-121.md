## Summary
Verse 121 of [[passage/quran-6|Surat al-An'am]], a Meccan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 121 of 165 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

On verse 121, "do not eat of that over which God's name was not mentioned, it is indeed a transgression...": al-Tabari says the meaning is, believers, do not eat what died and was not slaughtered by you or by a monotheist who follows laws God laid down in a revealed book, for it is forbidden to you, nor what was dedicated to other than God, what idolaters slaughtered for their idols; eating that is "fisq", which here means the disobedience of unbelief. Before it, on the end of verse 120, "they will be repaid for what they used to earn": those who earn sin are those who do what God forbade, and God will repay them on the Day of Resurrection for the disobedience they did in this world. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 76}

He adds that "innahu" refers to the eating, though only the act was mentioned, as in "it increased them in faith" (3:173), where the saying is referred to though it was mentioned only through a verb; the editor notes that "the act" here means the verbal noun, and refers to al-Farra's Ma'ani al-Qur'an. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 76}

Al-Tabari then takes up "the devils inspire their allies", noting that the commentators differed over who is meant by the devils. Some said they were the devils of Persia and those of their religion among the Magians, and their allies the defiant idolaters of Quraysh, to whom they inspired adorned speech to dispute with the Prophet and his companions over eating carrion. Report 13805, from Ikrima, says that when the prohibition of carrion came down, Persia inspired its allies in Quraysh to quarrel with Muhammad and ask: what you slaughter is lawful and what God slaughters (Ibn Abbas adds: with a golden knife) is forbidden? The verse then came down. In the notes the editor corrects a misreading of the manuscript in the printed text, explains "shimshar" as a knife or blade and suggests this was a Magian belief that carrion is God's slaughter, and gives biographical notes on the chain. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 77}

The reports continue. Report 13806, from Amr ibn Dinar from Ikrima, says the idolaters of Quraysh corresponded with Persia against the Romans and Persia wrote back that Muhammad and his companions claim to follow God's command, yet what God slaughtered with a golden knife, meaning carrion, they would not eat, while what they slaughtered themselves they would; some Muslims felt something in their hearts over this, and the verse came down along with 6:112. Others said the devils meant are those who tempt the children of Adam and inspired their allies in Quraysh; so 13807, from Ikrima: how do you worship One whose killing you do not eat, while you eat what you killed? This reached the Prophet and the verse came down. From Ibn Abbas (13808): it is Iblis inspiring the idolaters of Quraysh, and the devils of the jinn inspire the devils of mankind. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 78}

He reports from Ibn Jurayj through Abd Allah ibn Kathir that the devils inspire the idolaters to say that what dies and what you slaughter are the same, and to dispute with Muhammad on that basis; and that on "if you obey them you are idolaters" their saying was that what God slaughtered, meaning carrion, you do not eat, while what you slaughtered by your hands is lawful. The same wording recurs in several reports: 13809 from Ibn Abbas, that the idolaters told the Muslims you do not eat what your Lord killed but eat what you killed; 13810 from him, that Satan commanded his allies to say that what God killed for you is better than what you slaughter with your knives; and 13811, that the idolaters argued with the Muslims in the same way and the verse came down. A note gives biographical details for the chain of report 13809. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 79}

The same appears in 13812 from Ibn Abbas on "the devils inspire": they say what God slaughtered, do not eat, and what you slaughtered, eat; in 13813 from Ikrima, that some idolaters came to the Messenger of God and asked who kills a sheep when it dies; he said God kills it; they said, then you claim what you and your companions kill is lawful and what God killed is forbidden? and the verse came down; in 13814 from al-Hadrami, that they said you eat what a falcon or a dog killed but not what God killed; in 13815 from Ibn Abbas the same with the comment that if you obey them in eating what I forbade you, you are then idolaters; in 13816 from al-Dahhak; and in 13817 from Mujahid, that their saying about carrion is the meaning of "if you obey them you are idolaters". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 80}

Report 13818 gives the same from Mujahid; 13819, from Qatada, that the idolaters disputed with them over slaughter, saying what you kill with your hands you eat, and what God killed you do not, meaning carrion. In 13820 Qatada says God's enemy Iblis inspired his allies among the misguided to dispute with Muhammad's companions about carrion, and that "if you obey them you are idolaters" came down; and Qatada added that idolatry, as far as he knew, was only ever by one of three things: calling on another god beside God, prostrating to other than God, or naming slaughtered animals for other than God. From al-Suddi (13821): the idolaters asked how they claim to follow God's good pleasure when they will not eat what God slaughtered but eat what they slaughtered, and God said if you obey them and eat carrion you are idolaters; similarly 13822 from Ibn Abbas, and 13823 from him through Sa'id ibn Jubayr, that what you killed has God's name mentioned over it and what died does not. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 81}

From al-Dahhak (13824): this concerns slaughter, and the idolaters told the Muslims, you claim God forbade you carrion and made lawful what you slaughter and forbade what He Himself slaughtered for you; how is this so when you worship Him? and the verse came down. Others said those who disputed were a group of Jews; so 13825, from Ibn Abbas, that the Jews came to the Prophet and said we eat what we kill and do not eat what God killed, and the verse came down. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 82}

Al-Tabari gives his choice: the most sound saying is that God reported that the devils inspire their allies to argue with the believers over their prohibition of eating carrion, in the manner of the disputes described. It is possible that those inspiring were human devils inspiring their own allies, possible that jinn devils inspired human allies, and possible that both kinds cooperated, as God reported in another verse, "thus We made for every prophet an enemy, the devils of humans and jinn, inspiring one another" (6:112). Indeed this is in his view the most probable interpretation, since God told His Prophet that He made for him enemies among the devils of jinn and humans as He did for earlier prophets, who inspire one another with false adornments of speech, and then told him that these devils inspire their human allies to argue with him and the believers who follow him about the carrion God forbade. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 83}

He then records the commentators' difference on what is meant by the prohibition of eating what God's name was not mentioned over. Some said these are animals the Arabs slaughtered for their gods; he gives (13826) Ibn Jurayj's question to Ata' about "eat of that over which God's name has been mentioned": he said it commands mentioning His name over drink, food and slaughter; and about "do not eat of that over which God's name has not been mentioned": he said it forbids the slaughter that was done in the pre-Islamic era on idols, which the Arabs and Quraysh slaughtered. Others said it is carrion, and report 13827, from Ata', begins; the editor notes in the footnote that this heading is missing in the manuscript and should be retained. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 83}

Report 13827 gives Ibn Abbas that what God's name was not mentioned over means carrion. Others said it means every slaughtered animal over which God's name was not mentioned. So (13828) a man asked al-Hasan about a bird of the kind called kara, some slaughtered with God's name mentioned and some where the name was forgotten, all mixed up; he said eat it, eat it, while Ibn Sirin, asked the same, recited "do not eat of that over which God's name has not been mentioned". Report 13829, from Abd Allah ibn Yazid al-Khatmi: eat from the slaughter of the People of the Book and of the Muslims, and do not eat what God's name was not mentioned over; and 13830, that when asked about a man who slaughtered and forgot to say the name, he recited the verse to its end. The editor's notes explain "kara" as the plural of the curlew, a bird whose flesh is eaten, and give biographical notes on the chains. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 84}

Al-Tabari's choice: God meant what was slaughtered for idols and gods, and what died, or was slaughtered by someone whose slaughter is not lawful. The saying that it means what a Muslim slaughtered and forgot to mention God's name over is far from the truth for its oddity and its departing from what the proof agrees on, that it is lawful; and he says he showed its error by analogy in his book "Latif al-qawl fi ahkam shara'i' al-din". As for "and it is a fisq", it means that eating what God's name was not mentioned over, of carrion and what was dedicated to other than God, is fisq. They differed on the meaning of fisq here: some said disobedience, which he gives (13831) from Ibn Abbas; others said unbelief. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 85}

As for "the devils inspire their allies", the dispute over the meaning has been given, and their inspiring is their pointing out to them what they pointed to, whether by word, message or writing. Report 13832, from Abu Zumayl: I was sitting with Ibn Abbas when a man came and said Abu Ishaq claims a revelation came to him tonight, meaning al-Mukhtar ibn Abi Ubayd; Ibn Abbas said he told the truth, and I recoiled; he said there are two kinds of inspiration, God's inspiration and the devil's, God's to Muhammad and the devils' to their allies, and recited the verse. The editor's notes give Abu Zumayl's biography and say al-Mukhtar ibn Abi Ubayd al-Thaqafi was killed by Mus'ab ibn al-Zubayr and his men in the year 67 of the Hijra, calling him a liar in the editor's judgement. "Allies" here are helpers and supporters, and "to dispute with you" means to quarrel with you. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 86}

As for "if you obey them you are idolaters", it means if you obey them in eating carrion and what your Lord forbade you; he gives (13833) Ibn Abbas, in eating what I forbade you, and (13834) al-Suddi, so you ate carrion. "You are idolaters" means you are then like them, since these people ate carrion counting it lawful; if you eat it in that way you have become like them, idolaters. The scholars then differed on whether anything of this verse's ruling was abrogated. Some said none was abrogated and it is decisive for what it was meant for, which is the view of most scholars, and the editor refers to al-Nahhas's "al-Nasikh wa-l-mansukh". From al-Hasan al-Basri and Ikrima it is reported (13835) that God said "eat of that over which God's name is mentioned", and then that was abrogated with an exception: "the food of those given the Book is lawful to you" (5:5). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 87}

Al-Tabari's choice: the verse is decisive for what it was sent down about and nothing of it was abrogated; the food of the People of the Book is lawful and their slaughter is valid, and this is apart from what God forbade in "do not eat of that over which God's name has not been mentioned", since by this verse God forbade carrion and what was dedicated to the false gods. The slaughter of the People of the Book is valid whether or not they mentioned the name, because they are monotheists with books from God whose rules they follow, slaughtering by their religions as a Muslim slaughters by his, unless the one omitting the name omitted it as a religious stance of denial or of worshipping something other than God; then eating his slaughter is forbidden whether or not he mentioned God. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v12 p. 88}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
