## In al-Tabari's commentary

The interpreters differed on the meaning of "ra'ina", which the believers were forbidden to say. Some said it means "do not say what is contrary", from Ata and Mujahid. Others said it means "lend us your hearing", that is listen to us and we will listen to you, from Ibn Abbas and Mujahid; al-Dahhak said a man among the polytheists would say "lend me your hearing". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 459} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 460}

They then differed on the reason for the prohibition. Some said it was a word the Jews used in mockery and abuse, so the believers were forbidden to say it to the Prophet, from Qatada, Atiyya, al-Dahhak from Ibn Abbas, and Ibn Zayd (who linked it to the verse in Surat al-Nisa and glossed "al-ra'in" as the one who errs; the editor says he found this nowhere else and that others derived it from ru'una, folly, ignorance and slackness). Others said it was a usage of the Ansar in the pre-Islamic period and they were forbidden it in Islam, from Ata; Abu al-Aliya reported that the Arab polytheists said it in conversation and were forbidden, and Ibn Jurayj that it is the speech of the mocker. Others said it was the speech of one particular Jew, Rifa'a ibn Zayd (of Banu Qaynuqa in al-Suddi's report, where al-Tabari observes that the correct name is Ibn al-Tabut, not Ibn al-Sa'ib), who addressed the Prophet with it abusively, and the Muslims took it from him, so God forbade them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 460} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 461} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 462} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 463}

Al-Tabari holds the right view is that it was a word God disliked them to say to His Prophet, like what is reported from the Prophet, "do not call the grape karm, but say habla", and "do not say 'my slave', but say 'my lad'", two words of one meaning where one is disliked and the other preferred in address (the editor gives the routes of both reports in the Musnad and the two Sahihs). He then puts the objection of what the prohibition of "ra'ina" signifies, once the reason for the grape and the slave is known. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 463}

Answering the objection, al-Tabari says the prohibition of "ra'ina" is like that of "karm" for the grape and "my slave" for the possessed: the Prophet disliked that some of God's servants be ascribed, in the sense of servitude, to other than God, and disliked the grape being described as generous. So "ra'ina" can mean "watch over us and we watch over you" (as in "may God keep you"), or "lend us your hearing", that is empty it to hear our speech, for which he cites the verse of al-A'sha. Since God had ordered the believers to revere His Prophet and forbidden raising their voices above his (the editor refers to the opening of Surat al-Hujurat), they were forbidden a word carrying the reciprocal sense "heed us and we heed you", and told to choose the best words and gentlest meanings in addressing him, and not to resemble the Jews in their "hear, not made to hear, and ra'ina". He sees support in the verse that follows, which says that the People of the Book and the polytheists do not wish any good to be sent down to the believers from their Lord. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 463} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 464} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 465}

He rejects what is reported from Mujahid, that "ra'ina" means "contrary", since "ra'aytu" in Arabic is either from care and watchfulness or from emptying the hearing, and has no sense of "I opposed" except on the reading with tanwin, meaning folly and ignorance, as Ibn Zayd said. As for what is reported from Atiyya, that it was a Jewish word of abuse and mockery which the believers used having taken it from them, he holds it is not permissible to describe the believers as taking the polytheists' speech without knowing its meaning; he allows what is reported from Qatada, that it was a sound Arabic word coinciding with a word of the Jews in another tongue that is abuse for them, so God forbade it to the believers lest those who meant otherwise dare to address the Messenger with it. He says no report has come on this by a route that makes it proof, so the better reading of the verse is the plain sense he described first. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 465} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 466}

He records that al-Hasan al-Basri read "ra'inan" with tanwin, from ru'una, a reading that departs from that of the Muslims and may not be used for its oddity; whoever gives tanwin does so because "do not say" governs it, whoever does not does so because it is a quoted command. He adds that Ibn Mas'ud's reading "ra'una", were it sound, would mean they were forbidden to use it among themselves, though he does not know it to be sound by a route that validates reports. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 466} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 467}

On "and say unzurna" he says: say to your Prophet, look on us and watch us, that we may understand what you say, and he reports from Mujahid that it means make us understand, explain to us. "Nazartuhu" means "I waited for him", for which he cites al-Hutay'a and the verse in Surat al-Hadid, "look on us that we may borrow of your light". He notes the reading "anzirna" with a cut hamza, meaning delay us, and holds it has no place here, since the Companions were told to draw near the Prophet and listen, not to hold back from him; the sound reading joins the hamza, meaning "wait for us". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 467} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 468}
