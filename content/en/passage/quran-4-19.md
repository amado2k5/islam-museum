## Summary
Verse 19 of [[passage/quran-4|Surat an-Nisa]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 19 of 176 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**The address and the forbidding of inheriting women (pp. 103-104).** Abu Jafar explains the address to the believers in [[passage/quran-4-19|4:19]] as meant for those who believed God and his Messenger, and the prohibition on taking women over as an inheritance by compulsion as meaning that it is not lawful for them to take over, by compulsion, the marriage of the wives of their relatives and fathers; the editor refers for the word for compulsion to earlier commentary (the scan reads volume 6 p. 565 and volume 4 pp. 297-298). He raises the question of how they inherited them, when women are heirs as men are, and answers that this was not about inheriting their property when they died. In the Jahiliyya, when a woman's husband died, his son or relative was more entitled to her than anyone else, and than she herself: if he wished he married her, and if he wished he held her back from others and did not marry her off until she died. God forbade this, banned marrying the fathers' wives and forbade holding them back from marriage. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 103} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 104}

**First view: inheriting the marriage of relatives' wives (pp. 104-108, reports 8869-8881).** Abu Kurayb (8869, from Asbat ibn Muhammad from Abu Ishaq al-Shaybani from Ikrima) reports from Ibn Abbas that when a man died his guardians were more entitled to his wife: some might marry her, they might marry her off, or leave her unmarried, and they held more right over her than her own family, and the verse came down about this. Ahmad ibn Muhammad al-Tusi (8870, from Abd al-Rahman ibn Salih from Muhammad ibn Fudayl from Yahya ibn Said from Muhammad ibn Abi Umama ibn Sahl ibn Hunayf from his father) reports that when Abu Qays ibn al-Aslat died his son wanted to marry his wife, which was theirs to do in the Jahiliyya, and the verse came down. Ibn Humayd (8871, from Yahya ibn Wadih from al-Husayn ibn Waqid from Yazid al-Nahwi) reports from Ikrima and al-Hasan al-Basri that a man would inherit the wife of a relative and hold her back until she died or returned his dowry, and God forbade it. Yaqub ibn Ibrahim (8872, from Ibn Ulayya from Sulayman al-Taymi) reports from Abu Mijlaz that the Ansar did this: a close relative inherited his kinsman's wife and was more entitled to her than her own guardian. Al-Qasim (8873, from al-Husayn from Hajjaj from Ibn Jurayj from Ata al-Khurasani) reports from Ibn Abbas that when a man's father or relative died he was more entitled to the wife, keeping her or confining her until she ransomed herself with her dowry or died and he took her wealth; Ibn Jurayj adds that Ata ibn Abi Rabah told him the people of the Jahiliyya confined the widow for a boy among them, that Mujahid said a son was more entitled to his father's wife, marrying her if she was not his mother or marrying her to his brother or nephew, and that Ikrima said the verse came down about Kubaysha bint Man ibn Asim of the Aws, whose husband Abu Qays ibn al-Aslat died and over whom his son spread his protection; she came to the Prophet and said she had neither inherited her husband nor been left free to marry. Muhammad ibn Amr (8874, from Abu Asim from Isa from Ibn Abi Najih) reports from Mujahid that the eldest son was more entitled to his father's wife, marrying her if she was not his mother or marrying her to whom he wished; the same is reported from Amr ibn Dinar by Muhammad ibn Amr (8875) and by al-Muthanna (8876, from Abu Hudhayfa from Shibl). Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (8877, from Ahmad ibn Mufaddal from Asbat) reports from al-Suddi that when a man's father, brother or son died, the heir who first cast his cloth over the widow was more entitled to marry her with the dead man's dowry or to marry her and take her dowry; if she got ahead of him and went to her family, they were more entitled. Al-Husayn ibn al-Faraj (8878, from Abu Muadh from Ubayd ibn Sulayman al-Bahili) reports from al-Dahhak that in Medina a man cast his cloth over a dead relative's wife and so inherited her marriage, which they counted as marriage, or kept her until she ransomed herself, and that this was in polytheism. Yunus (8879, from Ibn Wahb) reports from Ibn Zayd that among the people of Yathrib a son inherited his father's wife as he inherited his mother, and she could not refuse; if he wished he took her, if he disliked it he left her, and if he was a child she was held for him until he grew up. Muhammad ibn Saad (8880, from his father from his uncle from his father from his father) reports from Ibn Abbas that men of Medina cast a cloth over a dead relative's wife, inherited her marriage and held her until she paid a ransom. Ibn Waki (8881, from his father from Sufyan from Ali ibn Badhima) reports from Miqsam that in the Jahiliyya a man who came and cast his cloth over a dead man's widow was the most entitled to her. On this view Abu Jafar says the sense is that it is not lawful for them to inherit from their fathers and relatives the marriage of those men's wives by compulsion, the address leaving out the fathers, relatives and marriage and turning to the prohibition on inheriting women, since those addressed knew the meaning. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 104} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 105} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 106} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 107} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 108}

**Second view: inheriting the women's estates (p. 109, reports 8882-8883).** Abu Jafar records that others said the sense is that it is not lawful to inherit the women's property by compulsion, because they held their wives back though unwilling until they died, then inherited their wealth. Al-Muthanna (8882, from Abd Allah ibn Salih from Muawiya ibn Salih from Ali ibn Abi Talha) reports from Ibn Abbas that when a man died leaving a girl, his relative cast his cloth over her and kept people from her: if she was beautiful he married her, and if she was plain he kept her until she died and inherited her. Al-Hasan ibn Yahya (8883, from Abd al-Razzaq from Mamar) reports from al-Zuhri that it came down about some of the Ansar, among whom the guardian of a man who died was more entitled to his wife, keeping her until she died and inheriting her. In the editor's note the manuscript has one spelling and the printed text a different word for "plain", and the right word is the one meaning ugly. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 109}

**Al-Tabari's choice (pp. 109-110).** Abu Jafar chooses the first view, that the sense is that it is not lawful for them to inherit the wives of their relatives, because God has already set out the shares of those entitled to inherit, so that belongs to its people whether the male or female deceased disliked or accepted their inheriting from them. It is therefore known that God did not forbid his servants to take women as heirs in what he made their inheritance from them, but forbade that women be compelled as things inherited, that is, forbade inheriting the right to marry them, since the dead man they inherited from held over them the disposal of their marriage as a man holds the use of what he has hired of houses and land and other things of use. God made clear that what a man owns of his wife's marital right differs from what he owns of the uses of other possessions that may be hired out: when the holder of that right dies, what he held over his wife by marriage does not pass to his heirs as the things he owned by purchase, gift or hire do. This is al-Tabari's choice, and the museum gives no verdict. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 109} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 110}

**The editor's notes on pp. 109-110.** In note 2 on p. 109 he says the printed text has a thoroughly corrupt sentence and the manuscript a copyist's slip of the pen, and he gave the right form. In note 3 he says the manuscript has a word the first publisher could not understand, so he altered it and deleted "or accepted"; the editor holds that it should be read as the word meaning "disliked", so that the sentence stands as in the manuscript with no deletion, and says the first publisher did the book grave harm by his changes, as the reader has seen in thousands of notes, and closes with a supplication for forgiveness for them both. On p. 110 he says the manuscript and printed text read the phrase on inheriting women in a form that the context corrects to "in what"; that the manuscript has a wrong form for "other things that have uses" and the printed one is right, meaning all things from which their owner benefits; that on the marital right the printed text has "his wife" where the manuscript has the other form, which he kept, and that the word means the woman's private part, or intercourse, or the marriage contract, all close, the first being best; and that the manuscript and printed text have the singular for "inheritance" where the plural is right. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 109} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 110}

**The sense of the withholding (p. 110).** Abu Jafar says the interpreters differed over the prohibition on withholding women so as to take part of what they had been given. One group said the sense is that the heirs of dead men must not keep their widows from marrying whom they wished, so that they die and the heirs take from their wealth when they have died, and here the wording is cut off at the end of the page and continues on p. 111. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 110}

**The rest of the first view on the withholding (p. 111).** The first view ends by saying that the ones forbidden are the heirs of the dead man, who may not keep his widow back so that she buys herself free with part of the dowry her late husband had paid her. Abu Jafar says a group held this, among them Ibn Abbas, al-Hasan al-Basri and Ikrima. In note 1 the editor points to reports 8877, 8873, 8871 and those after them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 111}

**Second view: the husband who harms his wife (pp. 111-112, reports 8884-8889).** Others said the sense is: do not keep your wives back to hurt them when you have no need of them, so that they pay a ransom from their dowries. Al-Muthanna (8884, from Abdallah ibn Salih, Muawiya ibn Salih, Ali ibn Abi Talha) reports from Ibn Abbas a man with a wife he dislikes, to whom a dowry is owed, who harms her so that she ransoms herself. Al-Hasan ibn Yahya (8885, from Abd al-Razzaq, Mamar) reports from Qatada that a man may not keep his wife to harm her until she ransoms herself; Mamar adds, from Simak ibn al-Fadl, from Ibn al-Baylamani, that the two verses came down, one about a matter of the Jahiliyya and one about Islam. Al-Muthanna gives the Ibn al-Baylamani report again by another chain (8886, from Suwayd ibn Nasr, Ibn al-Mubarak, Mamar), and Abdallah, that is Ibn al-Mubarak, adds that it was not lawful to take women over as inherited property in the Jahiliyya, nor to keep them back in Islam. Al-Muthanna reports from Said that the meaning is to confine them (8887, from al-Himmani, Sharik, Salim); Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (8888, from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal, Asbat) reports from al-Suddi that it means harming them so that they ransom themselves; and Abu Jafar, from al-Husayn ibn al-Faraj (8889, from Abu Muadh, Ubayd ibn Sulayman), reports from al-Dahhak that the withholding is a man's disliking his wife and harming her until she ransoms herself, and that al-Dahhak cites another verse of the same sura, [[passage/quran-4-21|4:21]]. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 111} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 112}

**The editor's notes on these reports (pp. 111-112).** At 8885 the editor calls Simak ibn al-Fadl al-Sanani reliable, quotes al-Thawri that his reports hardly fall for their soundness, says Mamar is Mamar ibn Rashid, and says Ibn al-Baylamani is Abd al-Rahman ibn al-Baylamani, a client of Umar and reliable, whose entry is at reports 4946 and 4947 (numbers read from the scan). At 8886 he says Abdallah is Ibn al-Mubarak and that the printed text dropped the word for "matter", which he restored from the manuscript margin. At 8889 he corrects the name Ubayd ibn Sulayman, which the printed text garbles, and calls the chain a recurring one in the commentary. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 111} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 112}

**Third view: the women's guardians (pp. 112-113, reports 8890-8891).** Muhammad ibn Amr (from Abu Asim, Isa, Ibn Abi Najih) reports from Mujahid that the withholding here is like the withholding in the sura of the Cow, that is, it is the guardians who are addressed; al-Muthanna (from Abu Hudhayfa, Shibl) repeats this from Mujahid. The editor refers to the commentary on 2:232 and notes that the manuscript left out the sura's name, which he added. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 112} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 113}

**Fourth view: the man who has parted from his wife (p. 113, report 8892).** Abu Jafar gives another view, that the withholding was a Jahiliyya act and was forbidden in Islam (the opening words of this view are unreadable in the scan, so nothing is added to them). Yunus ibn Abd al-Ala (from Ibn Wahb) reports from Ibn Zayd that the withholding was practised among Quraysh in Mecca: a man would marry a noble woman who did not suit him, part from her on condition that she not marry without his leave, bring witnesses and have it written against her; when a suitor asked for her, if she gave him something and satisfied him he allowed it, otherwise he withheld her. The editor notes that the printed text altered a phrase and that he kept the manuscript's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 113}

**Al-Tabari's choice and arguments (pp. 113-114).** Abu Jafar says he has already explained the sense and origin of the word for withholding. He chooses the view that God forbade the husband to constrict and harm his wife when he dislikes her company and wants to part, so that she ransoms herself with part of her dowry. His argument: only two people can withhold a woman, her husband, who constricts her and holds her against his own wish in order to take back what he gave, or her guardian, who is in charge of her marriage. The guardian plainly gave her nothing, so he cannot be said to withhold her in order to take part of what he gave; hence it must be the husband. He adds that God gave no one any right over a wife after parting from her and her final separation, so a former husband has no way to withhold her for a ransom, and that God allowed husbands to constrict wives who commit a plainly shown shameful act until they ransom themselves. From this he sees the error of Ibn Zayd's view and of the view that the guardians of unmarried women are meant, and the soundness of his own. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 113} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 114}

**The editor's grammatical notes and the verb's case (p. 114).** The editor explains the structure of Abu Jafar's sentence: one clause is joined to an earlier one, the answer to the conditional is the clause beginning "it was clear", and the phrase on the soundness of his view is joined to the word "error". He says he added the bracketed words to complete Abu Jafar's practice in the earlier commentary. Abu Jafar then says that the last verb of the prohibition is joined in the accusative to the verbal noun before it, so the sense is that it is unlawful to treat women as an inheritance without their consent and unlawful to hold them back, as in Ibn Masud's reading, which has the particle before the verb; and that to take it as a jussive of prohibition would not be wrong. The editor says the manuscript and printed text both dropped the particle, an error that Ibn Masud's reading shows, and refers to al-Farra's Maani al-Quran (1:259). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 114}

**The meaning of the shameful act: first view, adultery (pp. 115-116, reports 8893-8898).** Abu Jafar says the sense is that believers may not constrict their wives to harm them while the wives are obedient, so as to take part of the dowries, unless the wives commit a plainly shown shameful act, in which case harming them so they ransom themselves becomes lawful. The interpreters differed over what that act is. Some said it is adultery, and that when a man's wife commits it, he may withhold her so she ransoms herself with what he gave. Abu Kurayb (8893, from Ibn Idris, Ashath) reports from al-Hasan, about an unmarried girl who commits fornication, that she is flogged a hundred, banished for a year and made to return to her husband what she took from him, and that he read this verse in that sense. Al-Hasan ibn Yahya (8894, from Abd al-Razzaq, Mamar) reports from Ata al-Khurasani that a man whose wife commits it takes back what he paid and sends her away, and that the prescribed punishments later abrogated this. Ahmad ibn Mani (8895, from Ibn al-Mubarak, Mamar, Ayyub) reports from Abu Qilaba that a man who sees such an act from his wife may harm her and press her until she leaves him by khul; Ibn Humayd gives it again (8896). Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (8897, from Asbat) reports from al-Suddi that it is adultery and that, if they do it, he takes back their dowries. Al-Qasim (8898, number unreadable and inferred from the sequence; from al-Husayn, Hajjaj, Ibn Jurayj) reports Abd al-Karim hearing al-Hasan al-Basri say adultery, and al-Hasan and Abu al-Shatha saying that when she does it, her husband may ask her for khul and she ransoms herself. In the notes the editor says the manuscript reads "if the man saw his wife" and that the printed reading is right, and that the manuscript has an unclear word he reads as "herself" while the printed text dropped it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 115} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 116}

**Second view: the act is disobedience (pp. 116-117, reports 8899-8904).** Others said what is meant here is nushuz, a wife's disobedience. Al-Muthanna (8899, from Abdallah ibn Salih, Muawiya ibn Salih, Ali ibn Abi Talha) reports from Ibn Abbas that it is hatred and nushuz, and that when she does it the ransom becomes lawful for him. Ibn Humayd (8900, from Hakkam, Anbasa, Ali ibn Budhayma) reports from Miqsam, in Ibn Masud's reading, which differs in the verb, that when she disobeys you and wrongs you it is lawful for you to take what she took from you. Ibn Humayd (8901, from Jarir, Mutarrif ibn Tarif, Khalid) reports from al-Dahhak ibn Muzahim that here it is nushuz and that when she is guilty of it he may take her khul. Al-Hasan ibn Yahya (8902, from Abd al-Razzaq, Mamar) reports it from Qatada, and al-Qasim (8903, from al-Husayn, Hajjaj, Ibn Jurayj) reports from Ata ibn Abi Rabah that if they do it, one may keep them or let them go as one wishes. Abu Jafar, from al-Husayn ibn al-Faraj (8904, from Abu Muadh, Ubayd ibn Sulayman), reports from al-Dahhak that God was just in judgement and then turned to the women; the act is disobedience and nushuz; when it comes from her God commanded him to strike her and to desert her, and if she does not leave it he may afterwards take the ransom without blame. In the notes the editor refers 8900 to report 4828, says the manuscript has an error mark on one word and that the first publisher took the wording from al-Durr al-Manthur, vouches for Mutarrif ibn Tarif at 8901 and for Khalid (Khalid ibn Abi Nawf al-Sijistani, who relates mursal from Ibn Abbas), and says at 8904 that the printed text often errs on the name Ubayd ibn Sulayman and that he will not point it out again. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 116} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 117}

**Al-Tabari's choice and the report of Jabir (p. 118).** Abu Jafar chooses that every shameful act is meant: abuse by the tongue against the husband, harm to him, and adultery, because God's wording covers every shameful act that is plainly shown. So any husband whose wife commits adultery or nushuz may withhold her as God set out and constrict her until she ransoms herself, by whatever kind of act she came, as long as it is plain, according to the apparent sense of the Book and the sound report from the Prophet. Yunus ibn Sulayman al-Basri (8905, from Hatim ibn Ismail, Jafar ibn Muhammad, his father) reports from Jabir that the Prophet said: fear God about women, for you took them by God's trust and made their persons lawful by God's word; you have it from them that they let no one you dislike tread your bedding, and if they do, strike them without severity, and they are owed their upkeep and clothing by custom. The editor says in his notes on pp. 118-119 that the name stands so here but that he cannot find it among al-Tabari's teachers, and is nearly sure it is corrupted from a similar name (the name he proposes is not clear in the scan); that al-Tabari gave two portions of this report by this chain at 2365 and 2003, that it is part of Jabir's long report on the Farewell Pilgrimage, and that al-Suyuti ascribed this portion to al-Tabari alone, missing that it comes from the long report. On p. 118 he also notes that the printed text has a different word for abuse from the manuscript, with the same meaning; that he printed the word for "plain" as his best reading of a poor hand; that the right word is "so every husband", not "so for every"; and that the manuscript garbles the plural "shameful acts", which the printed text repairs. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 118} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 119}

**Ibn Umar's report and al-Tabari's argument (pp. 119-120, report 8906).** Musa ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Masruqi (from Zayd ibn al-Hubab, Musa ibn Ubayda al-Rabadhi, Sadaqa ibn Yasar) reports from Ibn Umar that the Prophet said: people, women with you are like captives, taken by God's trust and made lawful by God's word; you have a right over them and they over you; among your right is that they let no one tread your bedding and do not disobey you in what is customary, and if they do, they have their upkeep and clothing by custom. The editor calls this chain very weak because of Musa ibn Ubayda al-Rabadhi, says al-Suyuti cites it and ascribes it to al-Tabari alone, that he found it nowhere else, and that its meaning is established by the soundness of Jabir's report before it, by the report of Amr ibn al-Ahwas al-Jushami (Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah; Tirmidhi called it good and sound) and by that of Abu Hurra al-Raqashi from his uncle in Ahmad's Musnad; and he explains that the word for such women is the plural of a captive woman. Abu Jafar then reasons that among a husband's rights is that his wife lets no one else on his bed and does not disobey in what is customary, and that her upkeep and clothing are due from him when she has done what is due from her. So if she gives herself to another he may withhold her upkeep and clothing, as he may when she disobeys, and in doing so he denies her no right due to her. If she then ransoms herself and he takes back what he gave, he has not taken it by a forbidden withholding but by a permitted one, and he falls within the exception God made for the withholders. From this Abu Jafar finds false the view that the exception was abrogated by the prescribed punishments: the punishment is God's right on whoever commits adultery, whereas withholding for a ransom is the husband's right, as is his constricting a disobedient wife, and neither ruling cancels the other. In the note on p. 120 the editor says the manuscript and printed text both slip in one phrase, which he corrected, and refers to report 8894. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 119} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 120}

**The sense of the verse and the two readings (pp. 120-121).** Abu Jafar sums up: it is unlawful for you, believers, to constrict your wives and deny them upkeep and clothing in order to take part of their dowries, unless they come with a manifest act of adultery, abuse or opposition in what they owe you, in which case withholding and constricting them is lawful for you if they ransom themselves. He says the readers differed on the pointing of the descriptive word: some read it with the passive vowel, meaning the act has been made clear and announced; others with the active, meaning it is plain to people that it is a shameful act. Both are widespread readings in the Muslim regions, and whichever one reads, one is right, because an act its doer shows is manifest, and it is not manifest unless it is made plain. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 120} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 121}

**Treating them well (pp. 121-122, report 8907).** Abu Jafar explains the command to live well with wives as: deal well with your wives and keep company with them as I commanded, that is, keep them by giving the rights God made obligatory for them, or let them go with grace. Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (8907, from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal, Asbat) reports from al-Suddi a form meaning "mix with them"; Abu Jafar remarks that Muhammad ibn al-Husayn said so, but it is rather the word meaning "deal with them in character", from the word for companionship. The editor's first note says this distinction, and Abu Jafar's correction, show his eye for differences in meaning (the note is cut off at the end of p. 122 and no continuation shows at the head of p. 123). On p. 121 he refers to the earlier commentary on the word for "custom". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 121} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 122}

**Disliking wives and the abundant good (pp. 122-123, reports 8908-8911).** Abu Jafar says the sense is: do not withhold your wives to take part of their dowries when there has been no suspicion and no disobedience, and treat them well even if you dislike them, for you may dislike them and keep them, and God places in your keeping them against your dislike abundant good, in a child he gives you by them or in your affection for them after dislike. Muhammad ibn Amr (8908, from Abu Asim, Isa, Ibn Abi Najih) reports from Mujahid that God may place abundant good in the dislike; al-Muthanna repeats it (8909) by another chain; Muhammad ibn al-Husayn (8910, from Asbat) reports from al-Suddi that the good is the child; and Muhammad ibn Sad (8911, from his father, his uncle, his father, his father, from Ibn Abbas) reports that the good is that he comes to feel affection for her, so that the man is given her child and God places abundant good in it. Abu Jafar says that, on Mujahid's reading, the pronoun refers to the verbal noun of the disliking, that is, in their being disliked, and that if it referred to the disliked thing itself that too would be allowed and sound. In a note on p. 123 the editor says the manuscript and printed text wrote this sentence in the verse's own wording, but the context needs the form he printed. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 122} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 123}
