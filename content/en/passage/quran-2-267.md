## Summary
Verse 267 of [[passage/quran-2|Surat al-Baqara]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 267 of 286 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

al-Tabari explains the address to the believers as those who affirmed God, His Messenger and the verses of His Book, and spending as zakat and charity, with Ibn Abbas (6120): give charity; the good things as what was earned by dealing, either in trade or a craft, of gold and silver, meaning the good quality and not the poor, so that they give of what they earned lawfully the fine part. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 555} Mujahid: the good earning is trade (6121 to 6124, 6127, 6128), 6124 lawful trade; Abd Allah ibn Ma'qil (6125): there is nothing impure in a believer's wealth, but do not aim at the bad part of it to spend; Ali ibn Abi Talib (6126), in Ubayda's report: gold and silver; Ibn Abbas (6129): from the best of your wealth and the finest of it, the editor noting that a singular pronoun may refer back to a plural in the comparative form, and referring to report 6152; al-Suddi (6130): gold and silver, the editor noting that the printed edition dropped a word here. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 555} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 556} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 557}

Al-Tabari then explains what God brought out of the earth: that they should spend likewise from what God brought out of the earth for them, give charity and zakat from dates, grapes, wheat, barley and whatever plant produce charity is due on. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 557} Ali (6131): grain, fruit and everything on which zakat is due; Mujahid (6132): the date palm, and (6133): from the fruit of the palm. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 557} We stop at report 6133. The sayings are transmitted as they came, al-Tabari's choices and explanations are his, corrections and comments are the editor's, with no museum verdict and no legal ruling.

More on ([[passage/quran-2-267|2:267]]), pp. 557 to 560. Report 6134 from Mujahid: the good earning is trade and what God brought out of the earth is fruit; 6135 from al-Suddi: fruit and grain. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 558}

Al-Tabari then explains the prohibition on aiming at the bad as a prohibition on working at it and intending it. He notes that in the reading of Abd Allah (ibn Mas'ud) the verb is a form of amamtu, while this one is from yammamtu, the meaning being the same though the wording differs; one says ta'ammamtu so-and-so, tayammamtuhu and amamtuhu, all meaning I aimed at him and set out for him, and he cites a line of Maymun ibn Qays al-A'sha for this sense (mentioned, not reproduced). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 558} The editor notes that Abu Hayyan states that al-Tabari relates this reading, so he keeps the manuscript form, corrects the verb's wording, and explains the words for a barren expanse and rough ground in the line; this is the editor's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 558} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 559} Reports 6136 (al-Suddi), 6137 and 6138 (Qatada): it means do not deliberately aim. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 559}

Al-Tabari explains the bad as the poor quality, not the good: the inferior part of one's wealth is not to be chosen for charity, which is to come from the fine part. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 559} He gives the occasion: a man of the Ansar hung a cluster of poor dates in the place where the Muslims hung the charity of their fruit. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 559} In 6139, from al-Bara ibn Azib, the verse came down about the Ansar: at the time of cutting the palms they brought out clusters of unripe dates from their orchards and hung them on a rope between two columns in the Prophet's mosque for the poor among the Emigrants to eat, and a man would take poor dates and slip them among the clusters, thinking that was allowed, and the verse came down about whoever did that. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 559} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 560} In 6140, from al-Bara in similar words: the man slipped in a cluster of poor dates thinking it acceptable among the many clusters put out, and that cluster, had it been given to them as a gift, they would not have accepted. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 560}

In his notes on 6139 the editor says that al-Husayn ibn Amr al-Anqazi is spoken against while his father is reliable; that al-Hakim transmits the report in the Mustadrak, calling it strange and sound on Muslim's condition, with al-Dhahabi agreeing; that Ibn Kathir cites it and ascribes it to al-Hakim with a difference in the wording of the grading; and he explains the terms for poor dates, cluster, unripe dates, cutting and orchard walls. On 6140 he explains the phrase for acceptable as meaning sufficient for him, a rare idiom in his view that the dictionaries do not record. These sources and gradings are the editor's alone. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 559} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 560} We stop at 6140. The reports are transmitted as they came, al-Tabari's explanations are his, the notes are the editor's, with no museum verdict and no legal ruling.

More on ([[passage/quran-2-267|2:267]]), pp. 560 to 563. Report 6141, from al-Bara ibn Azib, concludes: they used to bring in charity their poorest dates and worst food, and the verse came down; the editor refers to al-Bayhaqi's fuller version in the Sunan. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 560} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 561} In 6142, from Ali ibn Abi Talib in Ubayda al-Salmani's report, the verse came down about the obligatory zakat: a man would cut his dates, set the good aside, and when the collector came give him the poor. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 561} In 6143, from Abu Umama ibn Sahl ibn Hunayf, what is meant are the ju'rur and the lawn of hubayq (two inferior kinds of date), and the Prophet forbade taking them as charity; the editor gives the biography of the narrator Abd al-Jalil ibn Humayd al-Yahsubi, citing al-Nasa'i's remark that there is nothing wrong with him and Ibn Hibban's listing of him among the reliable, the report's transmission in al-Nasa'i, Abu Dawud and al-Hakim, and that al-Hakim graded it sound on the condition of the two Shaykhs, with al-Dhahabi agreeing; these sources and gradings are the editor's alone. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 561}

In 6144, Mujahid: they gave dates in charity with their shrivelled and worst, were forbidden, and told to give of the good. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 562} In 6145, Qatada was told that a man who had two orchards in the Prophet's time would take the poorer dates for charity and mix shrivelled ones in, and God faulted them for it and forbade it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 562} In 6146, Qatada: you aim at the worst of your wealth for charity, though you would not take it yourself unless you overlooked its quality; the editor explains the word for dregs and notes a blank in the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 562} In 6147, al-Hasan al-Basri: the man used to give the dregs of his wealth in charity and the verse came down. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 562} In 6148, Mujahid on the hung clusters in which he saw shrivelled dates and said what is this; with Ata, that a person hung poor dates among the clusters hung up in Medina and the Prophet said what is this, how bad is what this one hung, and the verse came down. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 562} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 563}

Others said the bad here means what is forbidden, that they should not aim at the forbidden to spend from it and leave the lawful good; 6149 from Ibn Zayd: the bad is the forbidden, not to be sought out for spending, for God does not accept it; the editor notes a plain error in the manuscript and printed text in one wording. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 563}

Al-Tabari's choice. Abu Jafar says the interpretation of the verse is what he related from the Companions of the Prophet, because its chain is sound and the interpreters agree on it, and not what Ibn Zayd said; the editor notes a gap in the manuscript and print which he supplied in brackets so the sentence makes sense. This is al-Tabari's choice and argument. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 563}

Al-Tabari then begins on the close of the verse: the pronoun returns to the bad, and to overlook means to relax in taking it something of what is due to them of their right, granting themselves leniency. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 563} We stop at the end of p. 563. The sayings are transmitted as they came, al-Tabari's choices and explanations are his, the notes are the editor's, with no museum verdict and no legal ruling.

More on ([[passage/quran-2-267|2:267]]), pp. 564 to 566, on the part of the verse about overlooking when taking the poor. Al-Tabari explains that the Arabs say a man overlooked part of what was owed him for another when he is lenient, and he cites a line of al-Tirimmah ibn Hakim for the sense (mentioned, not reproduced); the editor's note on the poem's lines and words, among them the terms for small weak camels, menders and the steady-tempered, and for overlooking as indulgence and leniency, is his. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 564}

Abu Jafar then says the interpreters differed, in three views.

First view: you would not take the poor from your debtors in what is due to you except by being lenient with them. 6150 from Ali ibn Abi Talib by Ubayda's chain: none of you takes this poor stuff until something is taken off for him; 6151 from al-Bara ibn Azib: if a man was owed by another who gave him that, he would not take it unless he saw he had been shortchanged, the editor noting it completes 6141. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 564} In 6152, Ibn Abbas: if one of you was owed a right and the man brought less, you would not take it at the good rate until it was reduced, then God says how do you accept for Me what you would not accept for yourselves, when My right on you is of the best of your wealth and the finest of it, which is the saying of ([[passage/quran-3-92|3:92]]); the editor notes the difference between the printed text and manuscript in the superlative's form and refers to al-Durr al-manthur. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 565} 6153, Mujahid: you would not take it from your debtors or in your sales except with an addition over the good in measure. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 565} 6154, Ibn Abbas: men gave their zakat of dates as shrivelled ones, and he said that if one of them were owed and were repaid in that, he would not take it except seeing his right overlooked. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 565} 6155, al-Rabi ibn Anas: if you were owed a debt and were repaid worse than your due, would you take it other than reluctantly? {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 565} 6156, al-Dahhak: when they were commanded to pay zakat, a man among the hypocrites came with his worst food of dates and the like, God disliked that, and no one takes less than his right unless he knows it is short, so they should not accept for God what they do not accept for themselves. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 565} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 566}

Second view: you would not take this poor stuff, if you bought it from its owner at the price of the good, unless they were lenient in its price. 6157, al-Hasan al-Basri: if you found it being sold in the market you would not take it until something was cut from its price; 6158, Qatada the same. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 566}

Third view: you would not take this poor stuff if it were given to you as a gift, unless you overlooked it and took it reluctantly out of shame before the giver. 6159, al-Bara ibn Azib: if it were given to them they would not accept it except out of shame before its giver, who had sent what he himself had no need of; the editor notes this completes 6139. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 566} We stop at 6159; al-Tabari's choice among these views is not reached in the pages read. The sayings are transmitted as they came, the notes are the editor's, with no museum verdict and no legal ruling.

More on ([[passage/quran-2-267|2:267]]), pp. 567 to 569. Report 6160, from al-Bara ibn Azib, completes the like of 6140: they would accept it only out of shame before its giver and annoyance that he sent what he had no need of; the editor notes it completes 6140. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 567}

Others said the sense is that you would not take this poor stuff of your due unless you relaxed your claim. 6161, from Ibn Ma'qil as Ata reports: you would not take it from a right of yours unless he let you off some of his due. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 567}

Others said what is meant is the forbidden, that you would not take the forbidden unless you closed your eyes to the sin in taking it. 6162, Ibn Zayd: I am not taking that forbidden thing until you overlook its sin, adding that the Arabs say he took it and shut his eyes to what was in it, knowing it was forbidden and false. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 567}

Al-Tabari's choice and argument. Abu Jafar says the better interpretation, in his view, is that God urged His servants to charity and paying zakat from their wealth and made it obligatory, so what He made obligatory in their wealth became a right of those entitled to the shares of charity, and then He told them to take it from the good, fine part. He holds that those entitled are partners of the owners in their wealth by what charity is due to them in it, that any two partners in property each have a share by their ownership, and that neither may deny his partner his right by giving the equivalent from something poorer; so too the one paying zakat is forbidden to give those entitled from the bad, poor part what is due to them in the good, fine part of his wealth, just as if all his wealth were poor, zakat would be due on it and he would not have to give them fine goods from outside it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 567} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 568} The editor notes, in the margin, a correction of a place in the printed text from the manuscript and the syntax of two sentences and the object of a verb. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 568}

Al-Tabari then says God addressed the owners to pay zakat from the fine part and not aim at the poor to give it to those entitled while withholding the fine part owed; and that they themselves would not take the poor in place of the fine owed them from partners and debtors except by overlooking and reluctance, so they should not do to those with a right in their wealth what they would not accept from others in their own rights. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 568} As for voluntary charity that is not obligatory, he says that though he dislikes giving in it anything but the best and finest of one's wealth, since God is most deserving of being approached with the noblest wealth, he does not hold it forbidden to give less than the best, because what is below the best may be of wider benefit through its quantity or more fitting for the poor person than the best would be through its scarcity; this is al-Tabari's argument and choice, not a ruling by the museum. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 568} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 569} The editor notes the syntax of the last sentence. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 569}

Al-Tabari adds that a group of scholars said as he did. 6163 and 6164, Ibn Sirin asked Ubayda about the verse, who said it concerns zakat, and that a counterfeit dirham was dearer to him than a date; 6165, Ibn Sirin from Ubayda: this is about the obligatory, and there is no harm in a man giving a date voluntarily, and a counterfeit dirham is better than a date. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 569} We stop at 6165. The sayings are transmitted as they came, al-Tabari's choices, argument and example are his, the notes are the editor's, with no museum verdict and no legal ruling.

More on ([[passage/quran-2-267|2:267]]), p. 570. Report 6166, from Ibn Sirin: this concerns the obligatory zakat, but for voluntary giving there is no harm in a man giving a counterfeit dirham, and a counterfeit dirham is better than a date. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 570} Al-Tabari then explains the close of the verse: God is rich beyond their charity and anything else, and He ordered it and made it obligatory in their wealth out of mercy toward them, so that it enriches their poor, strengthens their weak and brings them reward in the next life, not from any need of His for it; and that He is praiseworthy means that He is praised by His creatures for the favours He has given them and the bounty He has spread for them; this is al-Tabari's explanation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 570} Report 6167, from al-Bara ibn Azib: rich beyond your charity, which the editor notes completes 6139. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 570} Here ([[passage/quran-2-267|2:267]]) closes.
