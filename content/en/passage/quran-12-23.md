## Summary
Verse 23 of [[passage/quran-12|Surat Yusuf]], a Meccan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 23 of 111 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**Commentary on verse 23 (pp. 24-31)**

Al-Tabari says: the wife of the Aziz, in whose house Joseph was, tried to draw Joseph into herself, that is, she wanted him to lie with her. Report 18964, from Ibn Ishaq, through Ibn Humayd and Salama: when he reached maturity, the one in whose house he was, the wife of the Aziz, solicited him. Report 18965, from al-Suddi: she loved him; report 18966, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, through Isra'il and Abu Husayn: she said "ta'ala" (come). The editor notes that the added words between brackets are needed for the sentence to stand. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 24}

On "and she closed the doors" he says: the woman shut the doors of the rooms on herself and Joseph when she wanted what she wanted from him, door after door. On "and she said, come" the readers differed. Most of the people of Kufa and Basra read it with fatha on the ha and the ta, meaning "come to you, draw near", and he cites verses of a poet addressing Ali ibn Abi Talib, telling him that Iraq and its people are an "outstretched neck" toward him, that is, inclined to him as though they craned their necks in longing and expectation, with "hayta hayta" meaning come and draw near; the editor did not identify the poet and refers to Abu Ubayda's Majaz al-Qur'an and the Lisan. Report 18967, from Ibn Abbas, through Muhammad ibn Abd Allah al-Kharrami, Abu al-Jawwab, Ammar ibn Ruzayq, al-A'mash and Sa'id ibn Jubayr: "come to you". The editor gives notes on al-Kharrami (al-Tabari's teacher), on Abu al-Jawwab al-Ahwas ibn Jawwab al-Dabbi, trustworthy though he sometimes erred, and on Ammar ibn Ruzayq al-Dabbi, trustworthy. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 25}

Reports 18968 to 18974 on the sense of "hayta lak" as "come to you": 18968, 18969 and 18971 from Ibn Abbas, 18970 from Zirr ibn Hubaysh, who read it with the fatha, 18972 from Ikrima, who said it is in the speech of the Hawran, and 18973 and 18974 from al-Hasan. In a note on report 18972 the editor says that Ahmad ibn Suhayl al-Wasiti was al-Tabari's teacher, that his reports include some objectionable ones, though Ibn Hibban counted him among the trustworthy, that he could not find a "Qurra ibn Isa" of this name, and that the printed text altered the name of al-Nadr ibn Arabi al-Jazari. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 26}

Report 18975, from al-Suddi: come to you, and it is Coptic; report 18976, from al-Hasan: a Syriac word meaning "upon you"; reports 18977 and 18978, from al-Hasan, 18979, from Zirr, and 18980, from al-Thawri: come to you. Report 18981, from Ibn Abbas, who read it so and said: she was calling him to herself. Reports 18982 to 18985, from Mujahid: an Arabic expression with which she called him to herself. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 27}

Report 18986, from al-Hasan, with fatha on the ha and the ta: come to you. Report 18987, from Abu Ubayd: al-Kisa'i used to relate it; it is a dialect of the people of Hawran that reached the Hijaz, meaning "come", and Abu Ubayda said he asked a learned old man of Hawran, who said it was their speech and that he knew it. Reports 18988, from Ibn Ishaq: come, and 18989, from Ibn Zayd: come to me. A group of the early readers read "hi'tu lak" with kasra on the ha, damma on the ta and a hamza, meaning "I have made myself ready for you", from the saying "hi'tu for the matter"; Ibn Abbas and Abu Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami are among those from whom it is reported; report 18990, from Qatada, that Ibn Abbas read it so, and Abu Ubayda said he knew it only with the hamza; report 18991, from Abu Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 28}

Reports 18992 to 18994, from Ikrima: I made myself ready for you. Report 18995, from Abu Wa'il: "hi'tu lak" meaning I made myself ready, and Abu Amr ibn al-Ala and al-Kisa'i rejected this reading. Report 18996, from Abu Ubayda Ma'mar ibn al-Muthanna: he was present when Abu Ahmad, a man learned in the Qur'an, asked Abu Amr about the reading of "hi'tu lak" with kasra on the ha and a hamza in place of the ya; Abu Amr said: false, he made it a form "fa'altu" of "I made ready", but look: here is the trench, go through the Arabs until you reach Yemen, do you know anyone who says "hi'tu lak"? The editor notes that the bracketed addition is from the Majaz al-Qur'an, that the trench is Shapur's trench dug from the town of Hit across the edge of the desert to Kazima near Basra, and that the text is Abu Ubayda's own words in the Majaz. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 29}

Report 18997, from al-Qasim: al-Kisa'i did not relate "hi'tu lak" from the Arabs. Most of the people of Medina read "hiyta lak" with kasra on the ha, a silent ya and fatha on the ta; some Meccans read "haytu lak" with fatha on the ha, a silent ya and damma on the ta; and some Basrans, namely Abd Allah ibn Ishaq, read "hayti lak" with fatha on the ya and kasra on the ta. Some reciters quoted a line of Tarafa ibn al-Abd with "hayt" in the fatha-ha, damma-ta form, in which he says his people are not the farthest away when a caller from the clan says "hayt"; the editor found it nowhere else. Al-Tabari said: the best reading is that of the one who read "hayta lak" with fatha on the ha and the ta and a silent ya, because it is the well-known language of the Arabs and none other, and because, as is reported, it was the reading of the Messenger of God. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 30}

Report 18998, from Ibn Mas'ud, through al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, al-Thawri, al-A'mash and Abu Wa'il: I have heard the readers and found them close to one another, so read as you were taught and beware of affectation and dispute, for it is only like one of you saying "halumma" and "ta'ala"; then he read "hayta lak", and when told that some people read "haytu lak" he said: I read it as I was taught, and that is dearer to me. The editor notes that the chain is sound, that al-Bukhari recorded it in his Sahih in abridged form, and Abu Dawud, and that al-Tabari gave it earlier in abridged form. Reports 18999, 19000 and 19001 repeat it in abridged form by other routes, one through Masruq, and in 19001 he read it with fatha on the ha and the ta and no hamza. Abu Ubayda Ma'mar ibn al-Muthanna mentioned that the Arabs do not make "hayt" dual or plural or feminine but keep one form in every case, the number being shown by what follows: "hayta lak" to one, "hayta lakuma" to two, "hayta lakum" to a group and "hayta lakunna" to women. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 31}

On "God forbid" he says: Joseph said, when the woman called him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 31}

Joseph said, when the woman called him to herself and said "come to me": I seek refuge in God from what you call me to and ask His protection from it. The editor refers to the explanation of "refuge". On "he is my lord, he has made my lodging good" he says: your husband, your companion, is my master. Reports 19002 to 19008, from al-Suddi, Ibn Abi Najih, Mujahid, Ibn Jurayj and Ibn Ishaq, through their chains: "my lord" means my master, that is, the woman's husband, that is, Itfir. The editor refers to the explanation of "lord". On "he made my lodging good" he says: he made my station good, honoured me and trusted me, so I shall not betray him; the editor refers to the earlier explanation of "lodging". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 32}

Report 19009, from Ibn Ishaq: he trusted me with his house and family; report 19010, from al-Suddi: so I shall not betray him with his family; report 19011, from Mujahid: Joseph means his master, the woman's husband. On "the wrongdoers do not prosper" he says: one who does wrong, doing what he has no right to do, does not attain lasting good or succeed, and what you call me to, wickedness, is wrong and a betrayal of my master who trusted me with his home; report 19012, from Ibn Ishaq: what you call me to is wrong, and whoever does it does not prosper. The editor refers to the explanation of "prospering". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 33}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
