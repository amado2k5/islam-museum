## Summary
Verse 35 of [[passage/quran-6|Surat al-An'am]], a Meccan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 35 of 165 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

On "if their turning away is hard on you, then if you can seek a tunnel in the earth or a ladder in the sky and bring them a sign" al-Tabari says: if the turning away of these polytheists from you, Muhammad, and their refusal to trust you in the truth God sent you with weighs on you and is hard for you, and you cannot be patient with the hurt that reaches you from them, "if you can seek a tunnel in the earth" means if you can make a burrow in the earth like the jerboa's exit, one of its holes, and go into it, "or a ladder in the sky" means a way up to climb, like steps and the like, as the poet Tamim ibn Ubayy ibn Muqbil says in a line from a fine poem saying that the regions of the lands do not shield a man and no ladders are built for him in the heavens; "and bring them a sign" from them, that is, a mark and proof of the soundness of your word other than what I gave you, then do so. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 337}

The editor refers for "turning away", "seeking" and "sign" to earlier notes, says the line is in Abu Ubayda's Majaz al-Qur'an, Sharh Shawahid al-Mughni and Lisan al-Arab, gives the lines before it about time's wearing down a young man, explains "ahja' al-bilad" as the lands' regions and edges, also reported as "a'na'", and says "then do so" means: if you can seek a tunnel ... then do it.

A group of interpreters said so: report 13201, from Ibn Abbas: "nafaq" is a burrow into which you go "and bring them a sign", or you make a ladder in the sky to climb and bring them a sign better than what we brought them, so do it. 13202, from Qatada: a burrow, and the ladder is steps. 13203, from al-Suddi: "nafaq" is a burrow and "sullam" a way up. 13204, from Ibn Abbas: a tunnel in the earth is a burrow. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 338}

The answer of the condition is left unstated because the sense shows it and the hearers know it. The Arabs do this where the meaning is understood by those addressed, a man saying to another "if you can rise with us in our need, if you are able to help us" and dropping the answer, meaning "if you can help us, do so"; but when the one addressed does not know the sense without the answer being stated, they do not drop it, so one does not say "if you stand" and fall silent, for the one so addressed does not know the answer until it is stated, "if you stand you will gain good" or "if you stand, good". An example of what is in the verse, an answer dropped though meant because the addressee understands, is the poet Ubayd ibn al-Abras's line, whose sense is: take your share of what we live on and live. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 339}

The editor refers to al-Farra's Ma'ani al-Qur'an, says Ubayd's line came with its source in volume 3, that the manuscript had the line right though undotted while the printed text misread it, and corrected "fa'ishi" for "fatahutt".

Then "had God willed he would have gathered them to guidance, so do not be among the ignorant": those of these unbelievers who deny you, Muhammad, so that their denial grieves you, if I willed to gather them on a straight religion and the right path of Islam, so that the word of you all be one and your religion and theirs one, I would have gathered them to it, and it would not have been hard for me, for I am able to do it by my kindness, but I did not do so by my prior knowledge in my creation and my decree already effective upon them before I created them and shaped their bodies; "so do not be, Muhammad, among the ignorant" means do not be of those who do not know that if God willed to gather all his creatures to guidance by his kindness he could, and that whoever of his creatures disbelieves in him disbelieves by God's prior knowledge of him and his effective decree that he will be among the unbelievers, by choice and not compulsion; for when you know that to be right, the turning away of those polytheists who turn from what you call them to of truth, and the denial of those who deny you, will not weigh on you. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 339}

The interpreters said so: report 13205, from Ibn Abbas: God says: had I willed I would have gathered them all to guidance. Al-Tabari holds this report a clear proof of the error of what the people of delegation among the Qadariyya said, who deny that God has gracious aids for those of his creatures whose success he wills, by which he is kind to them until they are guided to the truth, yield to it, turn to right guidance, submit to it and prefer it to error and unbelief in God. For he reported that had he willed guidance for all who disbelieved in him, so that they were united on guidance, he would have done it; and had he done that, without doubt they would be guided and not astray, and being guided would without doubt be better for them; his leaving them not gathered to guidance is his leaving to do for them in their religion some of what is better for them, which he is able to do, and in his leaving to do it is the clearest proof that he did not give them all the means by which they reach guidance and come by them to faith. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 340}

The editor refers for "ignorant" and "ignorance" to volumes 2 and 8, explains "ahl al-tafwid" as those who say the matter is delegated to man, whose will is enough to bring about his act, obedience or disobedience, he being the creator of his acts with choice in his hand, and "Qadariyya" and "ahl al-qadar" as those who deny the decree, those who affirm it being "ahl al-ithbat", referring to what was earlier in volume 1.

These are al-Tabari's statements on the question of the decree as transmitted, with his reasoning for them, and the museum gives no verdict on them.

Verse 35 closes here at p. 341, and al-Tabari goes on to [[passage/quran-6-36|6:36]].

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
