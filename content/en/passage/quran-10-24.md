**Commentary on verse 24 (pp. 55-59)**

He begins "The likeness of the life of this world is only as water We send down from the sky, and the plants of the earth, from which people and cattle eat, mingle with it, until, when the earth has put on its ornament and adorned itself and its people think they have power over it, Our command comes upon it by night or day and We make it a harvested field as if it had not flourished the day before; thus We detail the signs for people who reflect". The likeness of what you boast of in the world, its adornment and wealth, with the dimming and vexation attached to it and its ending in perishing and death, is like water We sent down from the sky, like rain We sent from the sky to the earth, "and the plants of the earth mingled with it", so that various kinds of plants grew with that rain, mixed with one another. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 55}

Report 17598, from Ibn Abbas: the plants mingled, and by the water every kind grew of what people eat, such as wheat, barley and the other grains of the earth, vegetables and fruit, and of what cattle and beasts eat, grasses and pasture. The editor refers to the gloss on "cattle". "Until the earth had put on its ornament" means its beauty and splendour appeared, "and adorned itself" means it made itself fair; the editor refers to the glosses on "ornament" and "adornment". Then "its people thought" begins, meaning the people of the earth. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 55}

Al-Tabari completes "its people thought they had power over it", that is over what it had grown; the statement speaks of "the earth" though the sense is of the plants, since what is meant is clear from the address. "Our command came upon it by night or day" means Our decree came to the earth to destroy the plants on it, by night or by day; "We made it a harvested field" means We made what was on it cut and uprooted from its roots, the word being "harvested" turned into a different form. The editor refers to Abu Ubayda's Majaz al-Qur'an. "As if it had not flourished yesterday" means as if those crops and plants had not stood growing on the earth before that, yesterday; the verb originally means to dwell in a place. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 56}

He cites a line by al-Nabigha al-Dhubyani from his well-known poem describing the bare-bodied woman, which uses the verb for dwelling in a place with its people (described, not quoted); the editor notes that he kept the manuscript's wording, which agrees with the diwan, in place of the printed text's. So too, he says, will perishing come upon what you boast of in your world and its ornaments, ending and destroying it as Our command and decree destroyed the plants of this earth after its beauty and splendour, until it became as if it had not flourished yesterday. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 56}

On "thus We detail the signs for people who reflect": as We made clear to you, people, the likeness of the world and made known to you its nature and affair, so We make clear Our proofs and evidences to whoever reflects, takes a lesson and looks; He singled out the people of reflection because they are the ones who tell matters apart and examine the realities behind the doubts that arise in the heart. The editor refers to the gloss on "detailing the signs". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 57}

Report 17599, from Qatada: yes, by God, whoever clings to the world and is attached to it, the world will soon spit him out and be finished with him. Report 17600, from Qatada, on "adorned itself": it grew plants and became fair. Report 17601, from Abd al-Rahman ibn Abi Bakr ibn Abd al-Rahman ibn al-Harith ibn Hisham, that he heard Marwan read this verse on the pulpit with an added clause, "and God would not destroy it except for the sins of its people"; Marwan said he had read it and it is not in the mushaf; Abbas ibn Abd Allah ibn al-Abbas said that is how Ibn Abbas reads it, and when they sent to Ibn Abbas he said that is how Ubayy ibn Ka'b had him recite it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 57}

In the note the editor identifies al-Harith ibn Abi Usama (trusted), calls Abd al-Aziz ibn Aban al-Umawi a liar and fabricator, says he found no mention of Abd al-Rahman ibn Abi Bakr among narrators, sets out the differing accounts of Ibn Sa'd, al-Zubayri and Ibn Hajar about the sons of Abu Bakr ibn Abd al-Rahman, and says the report has a ruined chain on every side and that the reading in it, if sound by another route, is an explanatory reading which no reciter may recite as the text of the recitation, being irregular and departing from the written mushaf by an addition without proof. These are the editor's judgements, not the museum's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 57}

Report 17602, from Qatada: "as if it had not flourished" means as if it had not lived, as if it had not been in comfort. Report 17603, from Abu Salama ibn Abd al-Rahman, on Ubayy's reading, which has a different verb and an added clause "and We did not destroy it except for the sins of its people"; the editor says Abu Salama did not hear from Ubayy ibn Ka'b, so the chain is mursal. He notes the readers' difference on "adorned itself": most readers of the Hijaz and Iraq read the contracted form of "tazayyanat", with the ta assimilated into the za because their points of articulation are near, and an alif introduced so that it can be pronounced since the ta had come to rest; Abu al-Aliya, Abu Raja, al-A'raj and a group are reported to have read "azyanat", on the pattern "af'alat". He says the correct reading is the first, by the consensus of the authoritative readers. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 58}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
