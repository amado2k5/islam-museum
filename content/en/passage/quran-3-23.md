## Verse 23 in al-Tabari's commentary (Shakir ed., volume 6, pp. 288-291)

Al-Tabari ([[passage/quran-3-23|3:23]]) says the address at its opening is to Muhammad, and the sense is those who were given a share of the Book and are called to God's Book. He then records the interpreters' difference over which Book is meant, in two views.

The first is that it is the Torah, to whose contents he called them to be content, since the sects that claim books acknowledge it and what is in it, and that these were God's rulings before He abrogated what He abrogated. In 6781, Ibn Abbas (Abu Kurayb, Yunus, Muhammad ibn Ishaq, Muhammad ibn Abi Muhammad the client of Zayd ibn Thabit, Said ibn Jubayr and Ikrima): the Messenger of God went into the House of Study among a group of Jews and called them to God. Nu'aym ibn Amr and al-Harith ibn Zayd said to him, What religion are you on, Muhammad? He said, The creed and religion of Abraham. They said, But Abraham was a Jew! The Messenger said to them, Then come to the Torah; it is between us and you. They refused, and God sent down this verse and what follows in the next ([[passage/quran-3-24|3:24]]). In 6782, Ibn Humayd (Salama, Ibn Ishaq, Muhammad ibn Abi Muhammad the client of the family of Zayd, Said ibn Jubayr or Ikrima, Ibn Abbas) to the same effect, except that he said 'come, you two' in the dual and 'God sent down about them'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 288-289}

The editor notes that the name in the manuscript and print is Nu'aym ibn Amr, as in al-Qurtubi (4: 50) and al-Baghawi (2: 119), but in Ibn Hisham's report from Ibn Ishaq (2: 201) it is Nu'man ibn Amr, as also earlier among the Messenger's opponents (2: 161) and in what al-Suyuti gave in al-Durr al-Manthur (2: 14), ascribing it to Ibn Ishaq, Ibn Jarir, Ibn al-Mundhir and Ibn Abi Hatim; he adds that the differences in the names of the Jews are many and a difficulty. He says 'they two refused' is from the manuscript and the print changed it to the plural; that the dual in 'come, you two' fell out of the printed Sira but is in the European manuscript, and he remarks on al-Tabari's care in keeping a slight difference in the wording. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 288-289}

The second view is that it is God's Book sent down on Muhammad, and that a party of them was called to the Messenger to judge between them by the truth and refused. In 6783, Qatada (Bishr, Yazid, Said): these are God's enemies the Jews, called to God's Book and to His Prophet to judge between them, though they find him written down with them in the Torah and the Gospel, and they turned from him in aversion. In 6784, Qatada (al-Muthanna, Ishaq, Abdullah ibn Abi Ja'far, his father): the Jews, called to God's Book and His Prophet, finding him written with them, then turning away. In 6785, Ibn Jurayj (al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hajjaj): the people of the Book were called to God's Book to be judged between with truth, in the fixed punishments, and the Prophet used to call them to Islam and they turned from it. The editor notes that the wording in the manuscript and print differs from al-Durr and that he thinks the right text drops a conjunction. He cites Ibn al-Kalbi's report from Abu Salih from Ibn Abbas that the verse came down about a Jewish man and woman of Khaybar who committed fornication; the Jews disliked stoning them because of their standing and raised them to the Messenger, who judged stoning; the rabbis said there was no stoning for them; he said, The Torah is between me and you; when they brought it and reached the verse of stoning, Ibn Suriya put his hand over it and read what came after. He says the report is well known. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 289-290}

Al-Tabari's own choice is that the sounder view is that God reported of a party of the Jews who lived around the place of emigration of the Messenger in his time, who had knowledge of the Torah, that they were called to the Book of God which they acknowledged to be from Him, the Torah, over some of what they and the Messenger disputed. Their dispute may have been over Muhammad and his prophethood; or over Abraham the Friend of the Merciful and his religion; or over what they were called to of Islam and acknowledging it; or over a fixed punishment. All of these were things they disputed with the Messenger, and he called them in them to the Torah's ruling, and they refused, some hiding it. The verse gives no evidence which it was, and there is no need to know, for what they were called to judge by was something they were bound to answer in their religion, and they refused; God reported their apostasy, their belying what is in their Book and their denial of the covenants taken on them to uphold and act on it, so that in belying Muhammad and the truth he brought they are as they were in belying Moses and what he brought, though they professed to follow him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 290-291}

He then glosses their turning away as turning their backs on the Book of God to whose judgment they were called, turning away and leaving it, while knowing its truth and proof. The editor notes that the sentence 'the verse gives no evidence which of them it was' was damaged in the manuscript and print and he put the right wording, that this phrase occurs often in al-Tabari (1: 520, 2: 517, 3: 64), and that he kept the wording 'to whose judgment they were called' since the verse indicates it and what comes later shows it right; he refers to the earlier commentary on turning away and aversion. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 291}

We stop at the end of p. 291, after al-Tabari's gloss of the turning away in the twenty-third verse, before p. 292. These are reports as transmitted, al-Tabari's choices and arguments are his, and the editor's notes and references are his, with no museum verdict.
