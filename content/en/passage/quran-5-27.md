**Commentary on verse 27: "and recite to them the account of Adam's two sons in truth", pp. 201-203**

Al-Tabari says God addresses His prophet Muhammad: recite to those Jews who meant to stretch out their hands against him and his companions, and make known to them the bad end of wrongdoing and scheming, the evil outcome of treachery and breaking a pledge, and the reward of the one who breaks it against the one who keeps faith, namely the account of Adam's two sons, Abel and Cain, and what became of the one who obeyed his Lord and kept his pledge and the one who disobeyed and broke it. By it the Jews are to know the harm that follows their betrayal and breaking of the covenant and what they intended, and for the Prophet and his companions it is fair consolation in the good reward God gives for keeping faith. The editor corrects the reading of "treachery", sets out the sentence's structure, and points to earlier notes on "recite" and "account".  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 201}

He then says the scholars differed over why the two sons offered the sacrifice, why God accepted from one, and who they were. Some said it was by God's command, and the accepted one offered the best of his property while the other offered the worst; they were Adam's two sons from his own loins, Abel and Cain.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 202}

Report 11704, from Isma'il ibn Rafi', says it reached him that one was a sheep owner and a lamb born in his flock became so dear to him that he preferred it at night and carried it on his back; when the sacrifice was ordered he offered it to God and God accepted it, and it grazed in Paradise until it was ransomed for Abraham's son. The editor notes the weakness of Isma'il ibn Rafi' and comments on Hisham ibn Sa'd's memory.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 202}

Report 11705, from 'Abdallah ibn 'Amr, says one was a farmer and the other a herder, they were ordered to offer a sacrifice, the herder offered his best and fattest sheep willingly, and the farmer his worst crop, darnel, unwillingly; God accepted the herder's and not the farmer's. He added that the slain one was the stronger of the two but scrupulousness stopped him from raising his hand against his brother. The editor explains the grain word and notes al-Tabari gave the report in his history.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 203}

Others said it was not at God's command. Report 11706, from Ibn 'Abbas, says there was then no poor man to give charity to, and a man simply offered a sacrifice; if God was pleased He sent a fire that consumed it, and if not the fire died down. While Adam's two sons sat together they said: let us offer a sacrifice. One was a herder and the other a farmer; the herder offered his best and fattest sheep, the other some of his crop; the fire came down between them, consumed the sheep and left the crop.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 203}

The rest of report 11706 has Adam's son say to his brother: will you walk among people who know you offered a sacrifice that was accepted from you and was rejected from me? By God, people will not look at me and you while you are better than I; he said: I will kill you, and his brother said: what is my fault, God accepts only from the God-fearing.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 204}

Reports 11707, 11708 and 11709, from Mujahid, say they were Abel and Cain, Adam's sons from his loins; one offered a sheep from the best of his flock and the other herbs or crops, the sheep's offering was accepted, the fire ate the young goat and left the crop, and he said: I will kill you. Report 11710, from a man who heard Mujahid, adds that God bound one of the killer's legs from calf to thigh until the Day of Resurrection and set his face toward the sun wherever it turns, with an enclosure of snow in winter and of fire in summer, and seven angels with him, one coming as another goes.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 204}

Report 11711, from Ibn 'Abbas, says one offered a ram and the other a heap of grain, and the offering of the sheep's owner was accepted. Report 11712, from him, says they were two men of Adam's sons. Report 11713, from 'Atiyya, says they were Cain and Abel, a herder and a farmer, one offering a lamb from the best of his flock and the other of his worst crop, and the fire consumed the lamb. The editor explains the grain word as a heap of food without measure or weight.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 205}

Report 11714, from Ibn Ishaq, from some people of the earlier scripture, says Adam ordered Cain to marry Abel's twin sister and Abel to marry Cain's twin; Abel accepted and Cain refused, disdaining Abel's sister and preferring his own, saying we are born of Paradise and they of the earth and I have more right to my sister; others say his sister was among the most beautiful and he begrudged her to his brother. Their father told him she was not lawful to him and he refused, so he told them each to offer a sacrifice, and whichever God accepted would have more right to her. Cain worked the soil and Abel tended livestock; Cain offered wheat and Abel firstlings of his flock, and God sent a white fire that consumed Abel's offering and left Cain's. The editor keeps the names as in the manuscript and notes that al-Tabari calls Cain "Qayn" in his history.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 205}

Report 11715, from al-Suddi, from several Companions, says every child born to Adam was born with a girl, and a boy of one birth was married to a girl of another, until Cain and Abel were born; Cain was a farmer and Abel a herder, Cain had a sister more beautiful than Abel's, and Abel asked to marry her but Cain refused. Adam was away in Mecca, having offered the trust to the heavens, earth and mountains, which declined, and to Cain, who accepted; when Adam left they made their offerings: Abel a fat young animal, Cain a bundle of ears of grain, from which he took a large ear and rubbed it and ate it. The fire ate Abel's offering and left Cain's; he was angry and said: I will kill you so you do not marry my sister, and Abel said: God accepts only from the God-fearing.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 207}

Reports 11716 and 11717, from Qatada, say they were Abel and Cain: the first a herder who offered his best, and a fire came down and consumed it, since an accepted offering was consumed by fire and a rejected one eaten by birds and beasts; the second a farmer who offered his worst crop, on which no fire came down, so he envied his brother and said: I will kill you. Report 11718, from Mujahid, says the fire left the crop and consumed the young goat.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 208}

Others said the two who offered were men of the Children of Israel, not Adam's sons from his loins. Report 11719, from al-Hasan, says the two were of the Children of Israel, that offering was in the Children of Israel's time, and that Adam was the first to die. The editor gives a note on a narrator and refers to the rebuttal of this view at pp. 219-220.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 208}

Al-Tabari begins his choice: the sounder of the two views is that the two who offered were Adam's sons from his loins and not descendants among the Children of Israel, since God is far above addressing His servants with what has no benefit for them, and those addressed by the verse knew that offering a sacrifice to God belonged only to Adam's children, not to angels or devils.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 208}

Al-Tabari completes his argument: had God not meant the two sons from his loins, mentioning them would have given the addressees nothing they did not already have, and it is not possible that He should address them with what does not benefit them; so it is known that He meant Adam's own sons and not his sons' sons, far removed in lineage, and the people of reports, biography and interpretation agree they were his sons from his loins in his time, which is witness enough. The editor says he added words that make the sentence work.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 209}

Report 11720, from Salim ibn Abi al-Ja'd, says that after Adam's son killed his brother, Adam stayed a hundred years grieving and did not laugh, until he was greeted with a phrase that made him laugh; the editor notes a narrator was judged weak. Report 11721, from 'Ali ibn Abi Talib, says Adam wept at the killing and recited verses saying the lands and those in them had changed, the earth's colour was dusty and ugly, everything with colour and taste had altered and the freshness of fair faces had faded; he was answered in verses that Abel was slain, the living were like the slaughtered dead, and the killer came crying in fear. The editor notes that the chain includes Ghiyath ibn Ibrahim, reported by Yahya ibn Ma'in and others to be a liar who invented reports, and that he restored the name from al-Tabari's history.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 209}

On their offering, al-Tabari says the sound view is that God reported that they offered, and did not say whether this was at His command or not; either is possible, and in either case they offered only to seek nearness to God. He explains "he said: I will kill you" as the one whose offering was not accepted speaking to the one whose was, the two names left out since they had just been mentioned. A similar report is from Ibn 'Abbas; report 11722 gives the brother's reply: what is my fault, God accepts only from the God-fearing.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 210}

Report 11723, from Ibn Zayd, gives the sense: had you feared God in your offering it would have been accepted, but you brought one adulterated with the worst you had and I brought a good one of my best; and he used to say that God accepts from you and not from me. Al-Tabari means by the God-fearing those who fear God by performing His obligations and avoiding disobedience; a group of interpreters said they are those who guard against associating others with God, as report 11724 from al-Dahhak says.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 211}

He adds that the word for sacrifice has the pattern of nearness-making, like the word for criterion, and that earlier nations' sacrifices were like our alms and zakat, except that the accepted ones were known by the fire consuming them and the others left; in our community the sacrifices are good deeds such as prayer, fasting, charity to the poor and the obligatory zakat, with no way to know at once which are accepted or rejected.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 211}

It is told that 'Amir ibn 'Abdallah al-'Anbari wept when death came, and when asked why said it was because he heard God say that He accepts only from the God-fearing, as report 11725 relays. Some said the sacrifice of the God-fearing is prayer, as report 11726 gives from 'Adi ibn Thabit. The editor notes the narrators, and that 'Amir was one of the eight ascetics.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 212}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
