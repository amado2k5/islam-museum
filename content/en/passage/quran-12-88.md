**Commentary on verse 88 (pp. 234-243)**

On "so when they entered upon him they said, O Aziz, hardship has touched us and our family, and we have brought poor merchandise, so give us full measure and be charitable to us; God rewards the charitable", al-Tabari says: something is left out of the speech, spared by what is mentioned, namely: they set out returning to Egypt until they reached it and went in to Joseph; "when they entered upon him they said, O Aziz, hardship has touched us and our family", that is, the severity of drought and dearth, "and we have brought poor merchandise". Report 19740, from Ibn Ishaq, through Ibn Humayd and Salama: they set out for Egypt returning to it "with poor merchandise", that is, little that did not reach what they used to trade with unless it was overlooked for them; they had seen what befell their father and the succession of trials on him in his son and his sight, until they came to Joseph. When they entered upon him they said "O Aziz", hoping he would have mercy on them in the matter of their brother, "hardship has touched us and our family". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 234}

By "and we have brought poor merchandise" he means dirhams or a price that is not acceptable as the price of food except from one who overlooks it. The root meaning of "iza" is driving by pushing, as al-Nabigha al-Dhubyani said in a verse describing a wind that blew from the direction of Dhu Arul, driving along with the night pieces of thin cold cloud; the editor says Dhu Arul is a mountain in the lands of Ghatafan, "al-sirad" is thin cold cloud blown by the wind, and "al-sirm" is the plural of "sirma", pieces of cloud. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 234}

"Tuzji" means drives and pushes, as in a verse of al-A'sha of Banu Tha'laba praising one who gives a hundred noble camels and their slave, and fresh-calved she-camels driving their young behind them, and in a verse of Hatim lamenting Mulhan, saying that a turned-away guest and a widow driving her young ones along in the night should weep for him, meaning she drives them before her out of weakness and inability to walk. So it is said "poor merchandise" because it is not current and is only passed by allowance, at a loss to those who take it. The editor says al-A'sha's verse is from his poem on Qays ibn Ma'dikarib, that "hajan" are noble white camels and "'udh" the plural of "'a'idh", the recently calved she-camel, that Hatim's verse is not in his collection but is recited anonymously by Ibn Barri and apparently is Hatim's since Mulhan was his cousin, and that he corrects the printed and manuscript wording "on a taking". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 235}

The interpreters differed in the way they explained it, though their meanings are close. Report 19741, from Ibn Abbas, through Abu Kurayb, Waki', Ibn Waki', Isra'il, Simak and Ikrima: "poor merchandise", he said, bad, counterfeit, not passing until something is deducted from it. Report 19742, through al-Hasan ibn Muhammad, Amr ibn Muhammad al-Anqazi, Isra'il, Simak and Ikrima: the bad, which does not pass until something is deducted. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 235}

Report 19743, from Ibn Abbas, through Ibn Waki', Ibn Uyayna, Uthman ibn Abi Sulayman and Ibn Abi Mulayka: worn, the sack, the rope and the like. Report 19744, through al-Hasan ibn Yahya, Abd al-Razzaq, Ibn Uyayna, Uthman ibn Abi Sulayman and Ibn Abi Mulayka, Ibn Abbas having been asked about the words: threadbare goods, the rope, the sack and the like. Report 19745, through al-Muthanna, Ishaq and Abd al-Razzaq, is the same. Report 19746, from Ibn Abbas, through Muhammad ibn Sa'd, his father, his uncle and his father from his father: "bida'a" is dirhams and "muzjat" is of little account. Report 19747, from Ibn Abbas, through al-Muthanna, Amr ibn Awn, Hushaym, Ibn Abi Ziyad and a narrator not named: unsellable, of little worth. Report 19748, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr and Ikrima, through Abu Kurayb, Abu Bakr ibn Ayyash and Abu Husayn: Sa'id said deficient, and Ikrima said worthless dirhams. The editor explains "fusul" as the plural of "fasl", the bad and base of anything. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 236}

Reports 19749 and 19750 are the same from Sa'id ibn Jubayr and Ikrima, in the second of which one said deficient and the other bad. Report 19751, from Abd Allah ibn al-Harith, through Sufyan and Yazid ibn Abi Ziyad: it was ghee and wool. Report 19752, through al-Hasan, Ali ibn Asim and Yazid ibn Abi Ziyad: a man asked Abd Allah ibn al-Harith about the words and he said, few; the goods of the bedouin are wool and ghee. Report 19753, from Abu Salih, through Ishaq ibn Ziyad al-Qattan, Abu Ya'qub al-Basri, Muhammad ibn Ishaq al-Balkhi, Marwan ibn Mu'awiya al-Fazari, Marwan ibn Amr al-Udhri and Abu Isma'il: pine nuts and the green seed. The editor says he found no mention of Marwan ibn Amr al-Udhri in the biographical works and fears distortion, and could not tell who Abu Isma'il is, given the obscurity of this chain. Report 19754, from Ibrahim, through Ibn Humayd, Jarir, Mughira and Yazid ibn al-Walid: few; do you not hear his words "so load our beasts", for they read it so, meaning the companions of Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 237}

Report 19755, from Ibrahim, through Ya'qub ibn Ibrahim, Hushaym and Mughira: I think it is only "few", because in Abd Allah's codex it reads "and load our beasts". Report 19756, through Ibn Waki', Jarir and al-Qa'qa' ibn Yazid: few; have you not heard his words "and load our beasts"? Report 19757, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr and al-Hasan, through Ibn Waki', Amr ibn Muhammad and Abu Bakr al-Hudhali: Sa'id said bad, and al-Hasan said few. Report 19758, from Abd Allah ibn al-Harith: the bedouin's goods are ghee and wool. Report 19759, from Atiyya, through Ibn Waki' and Ibn Idris: dirhams of little account. Reports 19760 to 19762, from Mujahid: few. Report 19763, from Abd Allah ibn al-Harith, through Qabisa ibn Uqba, Sufyan and Yazid ibn Abi Ziyad: something of wool and something of ghee. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 238}

Report 19764, from al-Hasan, through Amr ibn Awn, Hushaym and Mansur: few. Reports 19765 and 19766, from Mujahid: few. Report 19767, from Ikrima and Sa'id ibn Jubayr, through Abu Bakr ibn Ayyash and Abu Husayn: Ikrima said deficient and Sa'id said worthless. Report 19768, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr: bad. Reports 19769 to 19772, from al-Dahhak, through Ibn Waki', al-Muharibi and Juwaybir; al-Muthanna, Amr ibn Awn and Hushaym; Ibn Waki' and Abda; and al-Husayn ibn al-Faraj, Abu Mu'adh and Ubayd: unsold, not current. Report 19773, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr and Ikrima, through Ahmad ibn Ishaq, Abu Ahmad al-Zubayri, Isra'il and Abu Husayn: Sa'id said deficient and Ikrima said it has allowance in it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 239}

Report 19774, from Ibn Abbas, through Isra'il, Simak and Ikrima: bad dirhams that pass only with a deduction. Report 19775, from Mujahid, through Isra'il and Abu Yahya: base dirhams that pass only with a deduction; the editor explains that Abu Yahya is al-Qattat al-Kufi who narrates from Mujahid and that the printed text changed the manuscript. Report 19776, from al-Suddi: dirhams that pass with allowance. Reports 19777 and 19778, from Qatada: little. Report 19779, from Ibn Zayd, through Yunus and Ibn Wahb: "muzjat" is few. Report 19780, from Ibn Ishaq, through Ibn Humayd and Salama: few, not reaching what we used to buy from you with, unless you overlook it for us. On "so give us full measure", the editor judges that something has dropped out of al-Tabari's explanation, the sense being plain: complete our rights in the measure with it and give us what you used to give {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 240}

before, for good coin and sound, full dirhams that are not returned. Report 19781, from Ibn Ishaq, through Ibn Humayd and Salama: "give us full measure", that is, give us what you used to give before, though our goods are poor. Report 19782, from al-Suddi: as you used to give us for good dirhams. On "and be charitable to us" he says: be gracious to us by the difference between the price of the good and the bad, and do not lower us from the price of your food because our goods are poor; "God rewards the charitable" means God recompenses those who are generous to people in need with their wealth. Report 19783, from al-Suddi: be gracious by the difference between good and bad. Report 19784, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr: do not cut us from the price because our dirhams are bad. The editor refers to the earlier discussion of "tasaddaq". They differed on whether charity was lawful for the prophets before our Prophet Muhammad or forbidden. Some said it was lawful for none of the prophets: report 19785, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, through al-Qasim, al-Husayn, Hajjaj and Abu Bakr: no prophet ever asked for charity, but they said: the rest follows on the next page. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 241}

Report 19785 concludes: no prophet ever asked for charity, but they said "we have brought poor merchandise, so give us full measure and be charitable to us", meaning do not cut us from the price. Something is also related from Ibn Uyayna: report 19786, through al-Harith and al-Qasim, relating that Sufyan ibn Uyayna was asked whether charity had been forbidden to any prophet before the Prophet; he said: have you not heard "so give us full measure and be charitable to us; God rewards the charitable"? Al-Harith said that al-Qasim said: Ibn Uyayna means that they would not have said that unless charity was lawful for them, being prophets, since charity was forbidden only to Muhammad and to them. The editor explains the printed wording in a note. Others said that "be charitable to us" means be charitable to us by returning our brother to us: report 19787, from Ibn Jurayj, through al-Qasim, al-Husayn and Hajjaj: give our brother back to us. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 242}

Al-Tabari says: this saying from Ibn Jurayj, though it has a face, is not the chosen interpretation of "be charitable to us", because "sadaqa" in the usage of the Arabs is a man's giving a needy person some of his property seeking God's reward for it, even though every kindness is sadaqa; so taking the interpretation of God's speech in the more prevalent sense in the speech of those in whose tongue the Qur'an came down is better and more fitting. The editor notes that a gap occurs in the text and says what he took to fill it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 242}

Mujahid said the same: report 19788, from Uthman ibn al-Aswad, through al-Harith, al-Qasim and Marwan ibn Mu'awiya: I heard Mujahid, who was asked whether it is disliked for a man to say in his prayer "O God, be charitable to me"; he said yes, charity is only for one who seeks reward. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 243}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
