## In al-Tabari's commentary

On "then your hearts hardened", al-Tabari means the disbelievers of the Israelites, whom he says were the dead man's nephews; "hardened" means dried up and grew coarse, as in a verse of rajaz. "After that" means after the slain man was revived and named his killer. The hardness of their hearts was, he reports from Ibn Abbas, that they denied having killed him after he spoke and died a second time; Qatada says it was after what God showed them of reviving the dead. He explains "like stones" as hearts that stiffened against submitting to the truth they had seen. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 233} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 234}

On "or harder in hardness", al-Tabari answers one who asks how "or", which implies doubt, can occur in God's report, saying this is a statement about their hearts as their owners and those who knew them see them, not a doubt on God's part. He reports what Arabic scholars said: that it is to leave the listener unsure, as in "to a hundred thousand or more" and a line of Abu al-Aswad; or like "I fed you only sweet or sour" meaning some hearts like stones and some harder; or that it means "and", or "rather", or "in your view". In his own choice he prefers the first sense and then the view that some are like stones and some harder, since the basic use of "or" is one of two. For the nominative "harder" there are two readings: it joins the sense of the "like", or repeats "it is". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 235} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 236} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 237} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 238}
On "and some stones burst forth with rivers", he says water breaks out from some stones; "splits" means cracks so water comes out as springs and streams; and "falls down for fear of God" means rolls from a mountain top to the foot. He holds that God described the stones so, after making them a likeness for the hard hearts, as an excuse for the stones, unlike those who rejected his messengers after the signs they saw, having sound minds and souls that stones were never given. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 238} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 239}

Al-Tabari reports from Mujahid, Qatada, Ibn Abbas and Ibn Jurayj that every stone from which water gushes, which splits for water, or which tumbles from a mountain does so from fear of God, and that God excused the stones but not the wretched among the children of Adam. They differ on what the stones' falling means: some say the shifting of their shadows; some, the mountain turned to dust when its Lord appeared to it; some, that God gave certain stones knowledge and understanding so they obeyed, citing the weeping of the palm trunk the Prophet leaned on and a stone that used to greet him before his mission, both of which the editor calls sound. Others take it as figurative, like "a wall about to fall", it appears humbled; others that a stone, by pointing to its maker, inspires fear in others. Al-Tabari says these views are not far from the meaning but the reading of the early scholars is otherwise, so he does not turn the verse to any of them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 239} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 240} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 241} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 242} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 243}
On "God is not unaware of what you do", he addresses the Israelites who rejected the signs and the rabbis of the Jews: God is not heedless of their deeds but counts them and will repay them in the next world or punish them in this one; "ghafla" is leaving something through inattention or forgetfulness. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 243} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 244}
