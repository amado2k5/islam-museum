**Commentary on verse 14: the covenant of the Christians and the stirred-up enmity (pp. 135-138)**

On 'and from those who say "we are Christians" We took their covenant, and they forgot a portion of what they were reminded of', al-Tabari says God took a covenant from the Christians to obey Him, perform His duties, follow His messengers and believe them, but in His covenant they took the way of the misguided community of the Jews: they changed and broke their religion, left their share of the covenant to keep faith with Him, and neglected His command. Report 11596, from Qatada, says they forgot God's book among them, His covenant and His command, and report 11597, from al-Suddi, says the Christians said what the Jews said and forgot a portion of what they were reminded of. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 136}

Then 'so We stirred up enmity and hatred among them until the day of resurrection': the verb means to incite among them and cast between them, as one sets one thing on another; that is, when these Christians left their share of what He commanded and forbade, God stirred up enmity and hatred among them. The commentators then differ on how this stirring happened. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 136}

Some said it was through the factions and desires that arose among them. Reports 11598, 11599 and 11600, from Ibrahim al-Nakha'i, call it the differing desires and mutual hatred, and say they were set against one another by quarrels and disputes over religion; Mu'awiya ibn Qurra adds that religious quarrels cancel deeds. Others said it is the enmity and hatred itself: report 11601, from Qatada, says that when they left God's book, disobeyed His messengers, wasted His duties and suspended His limits, God cast enmity and hatred between them until the day of resurrection through their evil deeds, and had they held to God's book and command they would not have divided or hated one another. Al-Tabari prefers the view that He set them against one another through the desires that arose among them, as al-Nakha'i said, because the Christians' enmity arose from their differing statements about the Messiah, which are desires, not revelation from God. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 137}

He then notes the disagreement over whom 'among them' refers to. Some said the Jews and the Christians, so the sense is that God stirred up enmity between Jews and Christians because they forgot a portion of what they were reminded of. Report 11602, from al-Suddi, says that when the Christians did this God cast enmity and hatred between them and the Jews until the day of resurrection; report 11603, from Ibn Zayd, says they are the Jews and Christians, as one incites two animals against each other; reports 11604 and 11605, from Mujahid, and 11606, from Qatada, say the same. Others said it means the Christians alone. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 138}

Report 11607, from al-Rabi' ibn Anas, says God had told the Children of Israel not to sell His signs for a small price and to teach wisdom without wages, and only a few did so; the rest took bribes in judging and went beyond the limits, so He said of the Jews who judged by other than what God commanded what He said, and of the Christians 'so We stirred up'. Al-Tabari prefers al-Rabi's view, that the stirring in this verse is about the Christians in particular and the pronoun refers to them, not the Jews, because the stirring is mentioned in the account of the Christians after the Jews' account had ended and the Christians' begun, so it is likelier to mean them alone than both parties. The editor corrects a name in the chain, 'Abd Allah ibn Abi Ja'far al-Razi, and a damaged sentence in both the printed text and the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 139}

If asked what enmity there is among the Christians, he answers: the enmity of the Nestorians, Jacobites and Melkites toward one another. The view that God stirred up enmity between Jews and Christians is not far off, he adds, but this reading is closer to him and fits the verse better. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 140}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
