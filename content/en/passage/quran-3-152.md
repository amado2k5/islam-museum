## Summary
Verse 152 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 152 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**God kept His promise, spoken through the Messenger (p. 281).** Al-Tabari: God kept faith with you, believers among the Companions of Muhammad at Uhud, in the promise He gave you on His Messenger's tongue, which was his word to the archers: stay in your place and do not leave it, even if you see that we have routed them, for we shall keep prevailing as long as you hold your place. He promised them victory that day if they kept to his command. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 281}

**Al-Suddi (8004, pp. 281-282).** When the Messenger of God went out against the idolaters at Uhud he told the archers to stand at the foot of the mountain facing the idolaters' cavalry and put 'Abd Allah ibn Jubayr, brother of Khawwat ibn Jubayr, over them. Then Talha ibn 'Uthman, the idolaters' standard-bearer, called out: you claim God will send us quickly to the Fire by your swords and you to Paradise by ours; is any of you ready to be sent to Paradise by my sword, or to send me to the Fire by his? 'Ali ibn Abi Talib rose to him and swore he would not leave him until God sent him to the Fire by his sword or 'Ali to Paradise by Talha's. 'Ali struck him, cut off his leg, and he fell with his nakedness uncovered; he adjured 'Ali by God and kinship, and 'Ali left him. The Messenger said the takbir and asked 'Ali what had kept him from finishing him; 'Ali said his cousin had adjured him when he was uncovered and he was ashamed. Then al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam and al-Miqdad ibn al-Aswad charged the idolaters and routed them, and the Prophet and his companions charged and routed Abu Sufyan. When Khalid ibn al-Walid, who was over the idolaters' cavalry, saw this he charged, the archers shot at him and he fell back. But when the archers saw the Messenger and his companions in the midst of the idolaters' camp plundering it, they hurried for the spoils; some said they would not leave the Messenger's order, but most went. When Khalid saw how few archers were left he called to his horse, charged, killed the archers, and charged the Prophet's companions; when the idolaters saw their cavalry fighting they called to one another, pressed the Muslims, routed them and killed them. In a footnote the editor says that at report 7943 he had noted an undotted word in the manuscript that he thought was "karra", but here it is clearly written as "hamala" and he fears this may be the more likely; he refers to 7943 and al-Tabari's History (3: 14-15). He also gives, at the foot of p. 281, a cross-reference to earlier notes (vol. 6, pp. 495-496, note 5; then vol. 7, p. 21 and p. 154), mixed in the machine-read text with stray lines that look like the end of a chain of transmission or a note from the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 281} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 282}

**Al-Bara' ibn 'Azib (8005, 8006, pp. 282-283).** Marwan ibn Ishaq from Mus'ab ibn al-Miqdam from Isra'il from Abu Ishaq from al-Bara': On Uhud day, when we met the idolaters, the Messenger of God seated men facing the archers and put 'Abd Allah ibn Jubayr over them, telling them: do not leave your place; if you see us prevail over them, do not leave, and if you see them prevail over us, do not help us. When the two sides met the idolaters were routed, until I saw the women lifting their skirts and their anklets showing, and they began to say: the booty, the booty. 'Abd Allah said: wait, do you not know what the Messenger of God made you promise? They refused and went, and when they reached the enemy God turned their faces, and seventy Muslims were killed. The same, in near words, from Sufyan ibn Waki' from his father from Isra'il (8006). The editor refers to al-Tabari's History (3: 13-14) and Musnad Ahmad (4: 293-294). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 282} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 283}

**Ibn 'Abbas (8007, p. 283).** By Muhammad ibn Sa'd's chain: Abu Sufyan came three nights into Shawwal and camped at Uhud. The Messenger of God went out and called the people, who gathered; he put al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam over the horse, with al-Miqdad ibn al-Aswad al-Kindi, gave the banner to a man of Quraysh called Mus'ab ibn 'Umayr, and sent Hamza ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib ahead with the unmailed foot soldiers (husr). Khalid ibn al-Walid came on the idolaters' cavalry with 'Ikrima ibn Abi Jahl. The Messenger sent al-Zubayr, telling him to face Khalid and stay opposite until he gave word, and ordered other horse to another flank not to leave until told. Abu Sufyan came carrying al-Lat and al-'Uzza; the Prophet sent to al-Zubayr to charge, and he charged Khalid and routed him and those with him, because God had promised the believers help and that He was with them. The editor notes the printed text wrongly read "the bridge" for "the unmailed foot", plural of the word for one who is bare of arm and leg or has no helmet or mail, and refers to the History (3: 14). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 283}

**Ibn Ishaq, from several scholars of Medina (8008, pp. 283-285).** From al-Zuhri, Muhammad ibn Yahya ibn Hibban, 'Asim ibn 'Umar ibn Qatada, al-Husayn ibn 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Amr ibn Sa'd ibn Mu'adh and others, in an account of Uhud in which their reports are combined: the Messenger of God camped in the ravine of Uhud on the valley side towards the mountain, with his back and army to Uhud, and said no one should fight until ordered. Quraysh let their baggage animals and horses graze in fields at al-Samgha, in the Qanat valley, belonging to the Muslims; an Ansari man, when fighting was forbidden, said: are the crops of Banu Qayla to be grazed before we have even fought? The Messenger drew up for battle with seven hundred men; Quraysh with three thousand and two hundred led horses, Khalid ibn al-Walid on the right wing of the horse and 'Ikrima ibn Abi Jahl on the left. The Messenger put over the archers 'Abd Allah ibn Jubayr of Banu 'Amr ibn 'Awf, marked in white clothes, with fifty archers, telling him to shower the horse with arrows so they could not come from behind, and whether it went for or against them to hold his place so they would not be attacked from his side. When the lines met and the war was hot, Abu Dujana fought until he plunged deep into the people, and Hamza and 'Ali with other Muslims; God sent down His help and made good His promise, and they cut them down with swords until they drove them off, the defeat being beyond doubt. In footnotes the editor corrects three places in the report from Ibn Hisham's Sira (3: 64) and the History (3: 9), where both the printed text and manuscript err; he glosses the words for riding animals and for horses, says al-Samgha is land at one side of Uhud and Qanat a valley coming from al-Ta'if down to the base of the martyrs' graves at Uhud, and that Banu Qayla are the Aws and Khazraj, the Ansar, named for an old mother. He calls this an exceedingly damaging abridgment: al-Tabari stitched Ibn Ishaq's words together while Ibn Hisham gives them in a different order in scattered places (3: 69-72, 77, 82), including the standing of Hind bint 'Utba with the women beating tambourines behind the men to urge them on; he left the commentary as it is because the fault is al-Tabari's own, and says the word for "cut them down" means killed and exterminated, as the next verse's commentary will show. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 283} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 284} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 285}

**Al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam (8009, pp. 285-286).** Ibn Humayd from Salama from Ibn Ishaq from Yahya ibn 'Abbad ibn 'Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr from his father from his grandfather: al-Zubayr said, by God, I saw myself looking at the anklets of Hind bint 'Utba and her companions, women with their skirts tucked up fleeing, with nothing, little or much, between them and us, when the archers turned to the camp for plunder as we cleared the people from it, leaving our backs to the horse, and we were attacked from behind. A caller cried that Muhammad was killed; we turned, and the people turned on us after we had killed the standard-bearers, until none of them came near it. The editor says the manuscript writes two words at the start of the report in a confused way he calls among the oddest slips he has seen, and corrects them from the Sira (3: 82) and the History (3: 16-17); he glosses the word for anklets, "tucked up" and "turned". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 285} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 286}

**Ibn Ishaq and al-Rabi' (8010, 8011, p. 286).** Ibn Ishaq (8010): I made good to you what I promised you of victory over your enemy. Al-Rabi' from 'Ammar (8011): that was Uhud day; he told them: you will prevail, so let me not learn that you took any of their spoils until you have finished; but they left the order of the Prophet of God and disobeyed, fell on the spoils, forgot his charge to them and went against what he had ordered. The editor says the manuscript and printed text differ on some words of this report and he has given them as they appear in report 8025, and that the phrase "let me not learn" is a threat and rebuke, referring to 8158, 8160 and al-Durr al-Manthur (2: 85). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 286}

**The sense of "cutting down" (p. 287).** Al-Tabari: God kept faith with you, believers among the Companions of the Messenger of God, in what He promised of victory over your enemy at Uhud, when you were killing them; one says the verb for cutting down of a man when one kills him. The editor refers to the earlier commentary on the word (vol. 6, p. 443). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 287}

**The reports (8012-8022, pp. 287-289).** 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf (8012): the word means killing. 'Ubayd Allah ibn 'Abd Allah (8013): killing. Mujahid (8014): you kill them. Qatada (8015, 8016): killing by His leave, you kill them. Al-Rabi' (8017): the word is killing. Al-Suddi (8018): you kill them. Ibn Ishaq (8019): with the swords, that is killing. Al-Hasan (8020): killing. Ibn 'Abbas (8021): you kill them. In a footnote on 8012 the editor discusses the narrators of its chain: 'Abd al-'Aziz ibn 'Imran al-Zuhri al-A'raj, known as Ibn Abi Thabit, was said not to be reliable but a man of verse; Yahya is quoted as having seen him in Baghdad abusing people and attacking their lineages, saying his hadith is nothing; of Muhammad ibn 'Abd al-'Aziz ibn 'Umar ibn 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf al-Bukhari is quoted as saying "rejected in hadith", and Abu Hatim that the three brothers are weak in hadith and have no sound report; he refers to al-Bukhari's Tarikh al-Kabir, Ibn Abi Hatim and earlier places (the reference numbers are garbled in the machine-read text). On 8019 he notes that the printed text and manuscript add a needless preposition and the right wording is from Ibn Hisham's Sira (3: 120), a continuation of 8010. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 287} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 288}

**"By His leave" (pp. 288-289).** Al-Tabari: it means by My judgement and decree for you in that and My giving you power over them. Ibn Ishaq (8022): by My leave, My giving your hands power over them and holding their hands from you. The editor refers to earlier commentary on the word for leave and to the Sira (3: 120) as a continuation of 8019. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 288} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 289}

**Losing heart, disputing and disobeying (p. 289).** Al-Tabari: to lose heart means to become cowardly and weak; to dispute in the matter means to differ over God's command, disobey and go against your prophet, leaving his order and the charge he gave you; he means the archers whom the Prophet ordered to keep their post at the mouth of the ravine at Uhud facing Khalid ibn al-Walid and the idolaters' horsemen. The phrase "after He showed you what you love" means after what God showed you, believers, of victory and triumph over the idolaters, namely the rout they inflicted on them away from their women and goods before the archers left their places and before the idolaters' horse came out upon them from behind. He says the reports of the exegetes agree with this. The editor refers to the earlier commentary on losing heart (vol. 7, p. 168). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 289} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 290}

**The reports (8023-8029, pp. 290-292).** Qatada (8023): you differed in the matter and disobeyed; that was Uhud day, when the Prophet gave them a charge and a command and they forgot the charge, turned aside and went against it, so their enemy was thrown upon them after He had shown them what they love. Ibn 'Abbas (8024): the Messenger of God sent some men to their rear on Uhud day, saying: be here, turn back whoever flees from us and be guards for us from behind. When the people were routed, those set behind saw the women going up the mountain and saw the spoils; some said, go to the Messenger and take the spoils before others get them first, and another group said, rather we obey the Messenger and hold our place; the first are those who want this world, the second those who want the next, and the failing was when they disputed among themselves, having seen the conquest and booty. Al-Rabi' (8025): you were cowardly before your enemy, and disputed; on Uhud day he told them: you will prevail, so let me not learn that you took any of their spoils until you have finished; they left the Prophet's order, disobeyed, fell on the spoils, forgot his charge, so their enemy was thrown upon them after showing them in them what they love. Ibn 'Abbas (8026): failing is cowardice. Al-Suddi (8027): what you love, of conquest. Ibn Ishaq (8028): you abandoned one another, you differed over My command, you left your prophet's order and charge, meaning the archers, after the conquest that was beyond doubt and the rout of the people away from their women and goods. Al-Hasan (8029): what you love, of conquest. In footnotes the editor says the printed word "they went past" is weak in sense and that the language books record a verb for a people leaving their post; that the wording "it turned upon them" in the printed text and manuscript, and in al-Durr al-Manthur (2: 85), has no meaning, so he judged the right word to be "was thrown" and adopted it; he corrects places in 8024 from al-Tabari's History and explains that the word for a camel bolting means shying and straying; that "you disputed with one another" in 8028 is an error and the right wording is from Ibn Hisham's Sira (3: 121); and that 8025 came earlier, abridged, as 8011. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 290} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 291} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 292}

**Another view on the order of the words (pp. 292-293).** Al-Tabari: it was also said that the sense is "until you disputed in the matter, you lost heart and disobeyed after He showed you what you love", an instance of putting forward what belongs later, with the conjunction inserted though it carries no meaning, as in [[passage/quran-37-103|37:103]] and [[passage/quran-37-104|37:104]], where it means "We called him", this being said of both "until when" and "when"; and he gives [[passage/quran-21-96|21:96]] and [[passage/quran-21-97|21:97]], where the sense is "drew near". He then cites a line by a poet, in which he says that their clans had multiplied and they saw their sons grown, then turned on his people as a vile deceiver. The editor says the poet is al-Aswad ibn Ya'fur al-Nahshali in some accounts and unattributed in most books, lists where it appears (al-Farra''s Ma'ani al-Qur'an 1: 238 and 107, Lisan al-'Arab, Ta'wil Mushkil al-Qur'an, al-Ma'ani al-Kabir, Majalis Tha'lab, Amali al-Shajari, al-Insaf and al-Khizana), says it is from a satire on Banu Najiyah that he could not find complete, and explains that "their bellies multiplied" means their clans multiplied, "turned the back of the shield" is a saying for one who was friendly and then became hostile, and that the last word means a vile deceiver. In footnotes he says he adopted al-Farra''s own wording for this passage because the printed text and manuscript both vary. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 292} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 293}

**Those who want this world and those who want the next (pp. 293-294).** Al-Tabari: those who want this world are those who left the place the Messenger of God seated them in the ravine at Uhud against the idolaters' cavalry and joined the Muslim camp for plunder when they saw the idolaters routed; those who want the next are the archers who stood firm in the places he seated them, following his order to keep his covenant and seeking what is with God of reward and the abode of the next world. Al-Suddi (8030): those who went wanting spoils are the people of this world; those who stayed saying they would not go against the Messenger's word wanted the next. Ibn 'Abbas (8031): the same. Al-Dahhak (8032): at Uhud the Prophet ordered a group of Muslims: be a guard post for the people, meaning that they should hold and not leave until he gave leave. When he met Abu Sufyan and the idolaters and routed them, and the Muslims saw God had routed the idolaters, some went off calling "the booty, do not let it escape you", while others stayed, saying they would not leave their place until the Prophet gave leave; about this the verse came down. Ibn Mas'ud used to say: I never knew that any of the Prophet's companions wanted this world and its goods until Uhud day. The editor explains the guard post as armed men assigned to watch a frontier and "I never knew" as "I did not know". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 293} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 294}

**The remaining reports on wanting this world and wanting the next (8033-8039, pp. 295-296).** Ibn Jurayj from Ibn 'Abbas (8033): when God routed the idolaters at Uhud the archers said: go after the people, and do not let them reach the spoils before you; others said they would not leave their post until the Prophet gave leave; the verse came down about the two groups, and Ibn Mas'ud said he never knew that any of the Prophet's companions wanted this world and its goods until that day. Al-Hasan (8034): those wanting this world are the ones pulling the spoils to themselves, and those wanting the next are the ones following the enemy and killing them. Ibn Mas'ud by way of al-Suddi from 'Abd Khayr (8035 and 8036, in two wordings): he had not thought any of the Prophet's companions wanted this world until what came down about them at Uhud. Al-Rabi' (8037, continuing on p. 296): when Ibn Mas'ud saw them fall upon the spoils he said he had not reckoned that any of them wanted this world until that day. Ibn Sa'd from Ibn 'Abbas (8038): Ibn Mas'ud said he had not perceived it until then. Ibn Ishaq (8039): the first group are those who wanted plunder out of desire for this world and left what they were ordered, the obedience that carries the next world's reward; the second are those who fought in God's cause and did not disobey what they were forbidden for the sake of worldly goods, desiring God's good reward in the hereafter. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 295} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 296}

**The editor's notes (pp. 295-296).** On 8034 he says the printed edition has a wrong word for 'pull', that the manuscript's word is undotted, and that his reading is the correct one; to pull something is to draw it to oneself. On 8035 he says al-Husayn ibn 'Amr ibn Muhammad al-'Anqazi occurs often and was treated at no. 1625, that the printed edition wrongly has another name, and that 'Abd Khayr ibn Yazid al-Hamdani lived into the Jahiliyya, narrated from Abu Bakr, Ibn Mas'ud, 'Ali, Zayd ibn Arqam and 'A'isha, and is a trustworthy Successor. On 8038 he notes it is the rest of report 8024 and that al-Tabari gives it in his history (the volume and page are garbled in the scanned text). On 8039 he gives Ibn Hisham's Sira (3: 121), the continuation of the reports ending at 8028, and says the printed edition and manuscript both had faulty wording at one point, which he corrected from Ibn Hisham, restoring a conjunction from the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 295} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 296}

**Turning their faces away and the testing (pp. 296-297).** Abu Ja'far on the next clause: God then turned your faces, believers, away from the idolaters after showing you what you love in them and in yourselves, their rout and your victory over them, as a penalty for your disobeying His Messenger's order, going against obeying him and preferring this world to the next; the test is that the hypocrite is told apart from the sincere believer. The editor refers to the earlier commentary on testing (the reference numbers are garbled in the scanned text). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 296} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 297}

**Reports (8040-8042, p. 297).** Al-Suddi (8040): God then mentions when Khalid ibn al-Walid turned on them. Al-Hasan (8041): the enemy was turned away from them; as many Muslims were killed as the number captured at Badr, the Prophet's uncle was killed, his tooth was broken and his face cut; he wiped the blood and said: how can a people prosper who did this to their prophet while he calls them to their Lord? Then [[passage/quran-3-128|3:128]] came down. They said: had the Messenger not promised us victory? And God sent down the verse on the keeping of the promise, including what it says of turning away and pardon. Ibn Ishaq (8042): the turning away was a testing of them, and came through some of their sins. The editor notes this is in Ibn Hisham (3: 121), continuing the preceding report, and that the printed Ibn Hisham lost some words and its text is disordered, which can be corrected from this passage of the commentary. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 297}

**The meaning of the pardon (p. 298).** Abu Ja'far: God pardoned you, those who went against His Messenger's order and abandoned obedience in holding the place he told you to keep; He forgave the penalty of your sin, for something greater than what He punished you with, your enemies' routing you and the turning of your faces from them, since He did not wipe out your company. Al-Hasan (8043) clapped his hands and said: how did He pardon them when seventy of them were killed, the Prophet's uncle was killed, his tooth broken and his face cut? Then, in God's name, he says what amounts to: I pardoned you when you disobeyed Me, in that I did not wipe you out. These men were with the Messenger in God's cause, angry for God and fighting His enemies, were forbidden a thing and did it, and were not left until this grief reached them; whereas the most corrupt of the corrupt today commits every great sin, drags his robes after it, and claims no harm will touch him; he will come to know. Ibn Jurayj (8044): He did not wipe you out. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 298}

**The editor's notes (p. 298).** The manuscript has a different wording for 'and the turning of your faces from them', and he prefers the printed edition's. In al-Hasan's report the printed edition altered the manuscript's verb to a milder one; the editor keeps the manuscript's word, which means taking the greater part of a thing, with the root of a thing being its source and collection. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 298}

**The pardon and the favour continued (8045-8046, p. 299).** Ibn Ishaq (8045): God pardoned that great matter and did not destroy you for the disobedience of your prophet, but bestowed favour on you. Abu Ja'far on the verse's close: God is possessed of bounty to those who believe in Him and His Messenger, pardoning them much of what would earn them punishment, and if He punishes them for some of it He is kind to them with His good gifts. Ibn Ishaq (8046): so too His favour to the believers: if He punishes them for some sins in this world's present as discipline and admonition, He does not wipe out all the right He holds over them for their disobedience, as mercy to them and a return upon them for the faith they hold. The editor refers to the earlier commentary on 'favour' (references garbled), and to Ibn Hisham (3: 121) as continuing the preceding report, saying the printed Ibn Hisham is badly corrupt there and can be corrected from this passage. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 299}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
