## Summary
Verse 180 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 180 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**Verse 180 (pp. 428-430): the readings of the first verb.** Al-Tabari says the reciters differ: a group of the people of the Hijaz and Iraq read it with ta', and another group with ya' (the note: the printed text and the manuscript wrote the first reading with ya' and the second with ta', plainly a mistake, since al-Tabari then says the grammarians differed over the reading with ya' and not ta', so the editor corrected the places of the two readings). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 428}

**The grammarians' difference (pp. 429-430).** One of the grammarians of Kufa (al-Farra') said the meaning is "let not the misers think that miserliness is better for them", the verb of the participle standing in for the noun, as one says "so-and-so came and I was glad of him", meaning of his coming; and "huwa" is a "'imad", which the Basrans call the pronoun of separation, also named "da'ama" and "sifa" (the note: reference to al-Farra', 1: 248-249 and 1: 104, and to earlier passages). A Basran grammarian said he meant "let them not think miserliness is better for them", dropping the noun on which the thinking falls, namely miserliness, since the thinking had been mentioned and what God had given of His bounty had been mentioned, so he implied both. The note says this sentence is extremely disordered; the editor prints the manuscript as it is, suspecting a dropped line, and found no reference to this Basran statement in the books of exegesis and of Qur'anic grammar; he conjectures that the Basran meant that the verse resembles its sister at [[passage/quran-3-188|3:188]], where the prohibition is repeated for emphasis because the speech grew long, and that here the thinking is likewise repeated and then the second dropped, the first that forced the repetition remaining; and he excuses himself, saying he found no other way to make the sentence run, and that if right it is by God's grace, and if wrong he asks forgiveness. The same speaker adds that omission has occurred in stronger form than this, as in [[passage/quran-57-10|57:10]], where one who spent after the conquest is not mentioned since what follows shows that he meant them. One who rejected the Basran view said that "man" there has the sense of a plural, the meaning being "among you those who spent before the conquest are not equal in their ranks and states, so how of those who spent after it?" so the first suffices. Of the verse under discussion he said that something is omitted, but it is omitted only where something in the speech stands in for it: "huwa" returns to miserliness and "better for them" returns to the nouns, so the two pronouns show there are two nouns before them, and the verb suffices for the noun of action. When read with ta', miserliness precedes the relative; when read with ya', miserliness follows it; and the verb has served for the noun, as the poet said (the extracted text breaks off before the line; the note says its author is not known). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 429} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 430}

**The close of the grammarians' discussion and al-Tabari's choice (p. 431).** Al-Tabari completes the citation of the line by an unknown poet, whose sense is that when a fool is egged on he runs to what opposes, as though he had said he ran to foolishness, the word for the fool standing in for foolishness, just as the word for the misers stands in for miserliness. The note refers to al-Farra' (1: 249 and 104), al-Shajari's Amali, the Insaf (p. 63), the Khizana (2: 383) and other grammar books (the other volume and page figures are garbled). He then chooses the reading with ta', understood as "do not think, Muhammad, that the miserliness of those who are miserly with the wealth God gave them is better for them", leaving out the mention of miserliness because "better for them" shows it is meant and the mention of the misers has preceded. His reason is that the verb needs a subject and a predicate: read with ya' it has no subject of which that pronoun could be the predicate, whereas read with ta' the word for the misers is its subject, standing in for the omitted noun of miserliness, and the pronoun with "better for them" is the predicate, which follows well-known eloquent Arab usage. The ya' reading is, in his view, not wrong but not the most eloquent or best known. The meaning on his reading is: do not think, Muhammad, that the miserliness of those who hold back what God gave them in the world of wealth, not giving out of it God's right that He made obligatory on them in zakat, is better for them with God on the Day of Resurrection; rather it is worse for them with Him in the Hereafter. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 431} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 432}

**What the miserliness is (pp. 432-433, reports 8278-8280).** Al-Suddi (8278), through Muhammad ibn al-Husayn from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal from Asbat: they are those to whom God gave of His bounty and who were miserly about spending it in God's way and did not pay its zakat. Others said that the Jews are meant, who were miserly about making plain to people what God sent down in the Torah about Muhammad and his description: Ibn 'Abbas (8279), through Muhammad ibn Sa'd from his father, his uncle, his father and his grandfather: the People of the Book, who were miserly with the Book by not making it plain to people; and Mujahid (8280), through al-Qasim from al-Husayn from Hajjaj from Ibn Jurayj: they are the Jews, his remark running to [[passage/quran-3-184|3:184]]. The note says that in these reports the wording of the verse is left in al-Tabari's reading with ta', whereas the mushaf in use today has ya'. Al-Tabari chooses the first interpretation, that miserliness here means withholding zakat, because of the many reports that the Prophet explained the threat as meaning that the miser who withheld God's right from his wealth becomes a serpent about his neck, and because of what God says next, in [[passage/quran-3-181|3:181]], where He describes the saying of the polytheists among the Jews, who claimed, when God commanded them to give zakat, that God is poor. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 432} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 433}

**The collaring (p. 433, report 8281).** Al-Tabari says the threat means that God will make what the zakat-withholders withheld a collar on their necks, in the shape of the familiar collars. Abu Malik al-'Abdi (8281), through al-Hasan ibn Qaza'a from Maslama ibn 'Alqama from Dawud from Abu Qaza'a: no servant is approached by a kinsman asking for something of his surplus and is miserly with him, but what he was miserly with will be brought out to him as a bald serpent; and he recited this verse. The notes say that the "shuja'" is the male snake, a vicious, stubborn kind, and "aqra'" an attribute of evil snakes, which they claim, when a snake has lived long and its venom grown, gathers it in its head until the skin of the scalp falls off. They then give the chain: al-Hasan ibn Qaza'a ibn 'Ubayd al-Hashimi, al-Tabari's teacher, trustworthy; Maslama ibn 'Alqama al-Mazini, trustworthy according to Ibn Ma'in and others, weakened by Ahmad, with no criticism recorded by al-Bukhari; Dawud, Ibn Abi Hind; Abu Qaza'a, Suwayd ibn Hujayr ibn Bayan al-Bahili al-Basri, a trustworthy Successor praised by Ahmad, Ibn al-Madini and others, and the editor says al-Hafiz slipped in the Isaba (1: 331) in saying he was of Dhuhl when all sources say Bahila, and that the naming of him as "Hajar ibn Bayan" at 8283 is a pure error. Of Abu Malik al-'Abdi the editor does not know who he is nor whether Companion or Successor; he found no one who gave his biography except al-Hafiz in the Isaba, who built his entry on this report in al-Tabari alone, pointing to the next report "from Abu Qaza'a from a man", which he says al-Tha'labi related "from a man of Qays", and to the third "from Abu Qaza'a, mursal"; al-Hafiz added that Abu Qaza'a is a famous Basran Successor who used to report from Companions without naming them, so the matter stays open. The editor answers that this proves neither companionship nor even the man's existence, and that the report stays weak since it is not known that its narrator from the Prophet was a Companion; and that Ibn Kathir (2: 307) pointed to the three reports in al-Tabari, assigning them to no one else, and the editor knows of no one who related them besides al-Tabari save al-Hafiz's reference to al-Tha'labi. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 433} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 434}

**Reports 8282-8284 (pp. 434-436).** Ibn al-Muthanna (8282), through 'Abd al-A'la from Dawud from Abu Qaza'a from a man, from the Prophet: no kinsman comes to a kinsman asking for something of the surplus God put with him and is miserly with him, but a serpent is brought out for him from Hell, licking its lips, until it collars him. The note says 'Abd al-A'la is Ibn 'Abd al-A'la al-Qurashi al-Sami, trustworthy, used by the six, that the report repeats the one before, and that "the snake talammazat" is that it put out its tongue as an eater smacks. Ibn al-Muthanna (8283), through Abu Mu'awiya Muhammad ibn Khazim from Dawud from Abu Qaza'a: the Prophet said the like, that a serpent from the Fire will come on the Day of Resurrection, and then recited the verse. The note says that "Abu Qaza'a Hajar ibn Bayan" in the manuscript and printed text is an error twice over: "Hajar" should be "Hujayr" in the diminutive, the same error occurring in the Isaba in the entry on Abu Malik al-'Abdi, and the word "ibn" has dropped out between the byname and the name, since "Hujayr ibn Bayan" is the father of Abu Qaza'a and not his name; the father is counted among the Companions and has entries in the Isti'ab (no. 543), Usd al-Ghaba (1: 387), the Isaba (1: 330-331) and Ibn Abi Hatim. It adds that Ibn Kathir slipped, trusting this wrongly transmitted report and writing "from Abu Qaza'a, whose name is Hajar ibn Bayan", and that al-Suyuti followed him and added a further error, attributing the hadith (2: 105) to Ibn Abi Shayba in his Musnad and Ibn Jarir "from Hajar ibn Bayan". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 434} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 435}

Bahz ibn Hakim (8284), from his father, from his grandfather Mu'awiya ibn Hayda: I heard the Prophet say that no man comes to his client asking for something of surplus wealth he has and is refused, but on the Day of Resurrection a serpent licking its lips will be summoned for the surplus he withheld. Al-Tabari relates it from three teachers: Ziyad ibn 'Ubaydallah al-Murri from Marwan ibn Mu'awiya, 'Abdallah ibn 'Abdallah al-Kilabi from 'Abdallah ibn Bakr al-Sahmi, and Ya'qub ibn Ibrahim from 'Abd al-Wahid ibn Wasil Abu 'Ubayda al-Haddad, the wording being Ya'qub's. The notes say the editor did not know the first two and found no entry for either; the printed text has "Muhammad ibn 'Abdallah" instead of "'Abdallah ibn 'Abdallah al-Kilabi", still more obscure; the Lisan al-Mizan (2: 495) has "Ziyad ibn 'Abdallah ibn Khuza'i from Marwan ibn Mu'awiya", whom Ibn Hibban in the Thiqat says that "our teachers narrated from, sometimes with oddities", so he may be al-Tabari's teacher with "'Ubaydallah" corrupted to "'Abdallah" in the Lisan print; the third, Ya'qub ibn Ibrahim, is the hafiz al-Dawraqi, mentioned often, last at 3726. The chains are sound despite the first two being unknown, contenting oneself with al-Dawraqi's narration and because the report is established from other teachers through Bahz ibn Hakim; Ahmad relates it in the Musnad from 'Abd al-Razzaq from Ma'mar, from Yazid ibn Harun and from Yahya ibn Sa'id, all three from Bahz by this chain, and al-Nasa'i (1: 358) from Muhammad ibn 'Abd al-A'la from Mu'tamir from Bahz; the editor wonders that Ibn Kathir (2: 307) attributes it only to Ibn Jarir and Ibn Mardawayh though it is in the Musnad and the Sunan of al-Nasa'i, and notes that al-Suyuti (2: 105) adds Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, who called it good, and al-Bayhaqi in the Shu'ab, and that al-Mundhiri in the Targhib (2: 33) attributes it to Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi and al-Nasa'i. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 435} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 436}

**Ibn Mas'ud's reports (8285-8292, pp. 436-438).** Ibn Mas'ud (8285), through Ibn Bashshar from 'Abd al-Rahman from Sufyan from Abu Ishaq from Abu Wa'il: a serpent pecks at the head of one of them, saying "I am your wealth that you were miserly with"; (8286), through Ibn al-Muthanna from Muhammad ibn Ja'far from Shu'ba: a shuja' coiling about the head of one of them; (8287), through Ibn al-Muthanna from Ibn Abi 'Adi from Shu'ba, and through Khallad ibn Aslam from al-Nadr ibn Shumayl from Shu'ba, the same except that both said a black shuja'; (8288), through al-Hasan ibn Yahya from 'Abd al-Razzaq from al-Thawri: his wealth will come on the Day of Resurrection as a serpent that pecks at his head, saying "I am your wealth you were miserly with", and winds about his neck. The notes say 'Abd al-Rahman is Ibn Mahdi, Sufyan al-Thawri, Abu Ishaq al-Sabi'i; that the wording is stopped at Ibn Mas'ud and abridged, though in meaning raised to the Prophet, and that al-Tabari relates it so by the chains of 8292 and 8288-8285 and then relates it raised in a longer wording (8289); that al-Hakim (2: 298-299) relates it through Abu Bakr ibn 'Ayyash from Abu Ishaq and through al-Thawri, stopped at Ibn Mas'ud, to like effect, calling it sound on the conditions of the two shaykhs, who did not extract it, with al-Dhahabi agreeing; and that Ibn Kathir (2: 306) was lax in pointing to al-Hakim's report after quoting the raised report from Ahmad's Musnad in a way that suggests al-Hakim's is the same and raised, and added to the confusion by saying Ibn Jarir related it by more than one route from Ibn Mas'ud, stopped, which is not so for al-Hakim's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 436} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 437}

Ibn Mas'ud (8289), in a raised report through Sufyan ibn 'Uyayna from Jami' ibn Abi Rashid and 'Abd al-Malik ibn A'yan from Abu Wa'il: the Prophet said there is no one who does not pay the zakat of his wealth but a bald serpent is made to stand before him to collar him, and the Prophet then recited the verse to them. The notes say that "mathala lahu" means it stood before him upright; that al-Tabari left his teacher unnamed in this chain, but the report is established through trustworthy narrators from Ibn 'Uyayna; that Jami' ibn Abi Rashid al-Kahili al-Sayrafi is trustworthy and used by the six, whereas the copies of al-Tabari read "Jami' ibn Shaddad", an error, since Ibn Shaddad died early and Ibn 'Uyayna, born in 107, neither met him nor narrated from him, Ibn Shaddad having died in 107 or 108, and the figures for his death given in the Tahdhib (118, 127, 128) being wrong, partly from al-Mizzi and partly from copies; that 'Abd al-Malik ibn A'yan al-Kufi is a trustworthy Successor, criticised as a Shi'i though no one denied his truthfulness, and the six used him, with al-Bukhari mentioning him in the Du'afa' (p. 222) as a Shi'i from whom Ibn 'Uyayna and Isma'il ibn Sumay' narrated, tolerable in hadith, without impugning his truthfulness, which is why he admitted him to his Sahih; and that Ahmad relates it in the Musnad (3577) from Sufyan from Jami' from Abu Wa'il to like effect, raised, as do al-Tirmidhi (4: 85) and Ibn Majah (1784), both from Ibn Abi 'Umar, and al-Nasa'i (1: 333-334) from Mujahid ibn Musa, all from Ibn 'Uyayna; al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah add the two names as al-Tabari does, and al-Tirmidhi called it good and sound. Ibn Kathir (2: 306) cites it from the Musnad and credits al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah; al-Suyuti (2: 105) adds 'Abd ibn Humayd, Ibn Khuzayma and Ibn al-Mundhir. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 437}

Al-Suddi (8290), through Muhammad ibn al-Husayn from Ahmad ibn al-Mufaddal from Asbat: his wealth will be made on the Day of Resurrection a bald serpent that collars him, seizes his neck and follows him until it throws him into the Fire. Abu Wa'il (8291), through al-Qasim from al-Husayn from Khalaf ibn Khalifa from Abu Hashim: it is the man God gave wealth and who withheld from his kin the right God put in his wealth; it is made a snake and collars him, and he says "what have I to do with you?" and it says "I am your wealth". Masruq (8292), through al-Muthanna from Abu Ghassan from Isra'il from Hakim ibn Jubayr from Salim ibn Abi al-Ja'd: I asked Ibn Mas'ud, and he said they will be collared with a bald serpent biting at the head. The notes say Abu Ghassan is the hafiz Malik ibn Isma'il ibn Dirham al-Nahdi, Isra'il is Ibn Yunus ibn Abi Ishaq al-Sabi'i, and Hakim ibn Jubayr al-Asadi is weak; this wording is stopped at Ibn Mas'ud and the weakness of its chain does no harm, since it has come stopped by sound chains (8285-8288) and raised (8289). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 438}

**The second view: a collar of fire (8293-8296, pp. 438-439).** Others said the meaning is that a collar of fire will be put on their necks: Ibrahim (8293), through Ibn Bashshar from 'Abd al-Rahman from Sufyan from Mansur, then (8294) through Ibn al-Muthanna from Muhammad ibn Ja'far from Shu'ba, (8295) through al-Hasan from 'Abd al-Razzaq from al-Thawri, and (8296) through Ibn Humayd from Jarir, all from Mansur from Ibrahim: a collar of fire; the note says the printed text has the word without the accusative ending and the editor adopts the manuscript's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 438} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 439}

**The third view: the Jewish rabbis' concealment (8297, p. 439).** Others said the meaning is that the rabbis of the Jews who hid Muhammad's prophethood will be made to carry what they hid: Ibn 'Abbas (8297), through Muhammad ibn Sa'd from his father, his uncle, his father and his grandfather: have you not heard that He said that they are miserly and order people to miserliness ([[passage/quran-4-37|4:37]] and [[passage/quran-57-24|57:24]]), meaning the People of the Book: they conceal and order people to conceal. The note says it is in the verse of Surat al-Nisa' because its completion mentions concealing what God gave them of His bounty. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 439}

**The fourth view: being charged to bring it (8298, p. 439).** Others said the meaning is that on the Day of Resurrection they will be charged with bringing what they were miserly with in the world of their wealth: Mujahid (8298), through Muhammad ibn 'Amr from Abu 'Asim from 'Isa from Ibn Abi Najih: they will be charged to bring what they withheld, his remark running to [[passage/quran-3-184|3:184]]. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 439}

**The close of the interpreters' views (p. 440, report 8299).** Mujahid (8299), through Ibn al-Muthanna from Abu Hudhayfa from Shibl from Ibn Abi Najih: they will be charged to bring the like of what they withheld of their wealth at the Resurrection. Al-Tabari then says that the best of the views is the interpretation he gave at the opening of the verse, because of the reports he narrated from the Prophet, and no one knows better than the Prophet what God meant by what He sent down. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 440}

**The inheritance of the heavens and the earth (pp. 440-441).** Al-Tabari says the meaning is God's description of Himself as the Living who does not die and who remains after the perishing of His creation. He raises an objection: inheritance, as people know it, is what passes from an owner's possession to his heir at his death, and the world belongs to God before His creatures perish and after, so what does inheritance mean here? He answers that the meaning is what he described, God's description of Himself as enduring and His informing creation that perishing is written for them: an owner's property becomes an inheritance only after his death, so God is telling His servants that the possessions of all His creatures pass from them with their deaths, that there is no one but perishes except Himself, and that when He has destroyed all creatures and their possessions have gone, no one is left to have what they owned but Him. He takes the sense of the preceding verse to be that those who withhold what God gave them will be collared with what they withheld in the next world, after they have perished and their possessions have gone, at the moment when they own nothing and God has His inheritance and that of others among His creatures. Then, he says, God is informed and knowing about what these withholders and others do, encompassing all of it, so that He repays each according to what he deserves, the doer of good with good and the doer of wrong as He sees. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 441}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v07 p. 441}
