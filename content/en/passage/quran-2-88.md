## In al-Tabari's commentary

On "is it that whenever a messenger came to you with what your souls did not desire, you grew arrogant, denying some and killing others?" al-Tabari, citing Mujahid, says those addressed are the Jews of the Children of Israel: God sent them Moses with the Torah, followed him with messengers, gave Jesus the clear proofs and strengthened him, yet whenever a messenger came with what they did not wish they were arrogant toward him, denying some and killing others. He holds that the question form is a rebuke in address but has the sense of a statement. On "they said: our hearts are ghulf", there are two readings: with a silent lam, the reading of the common people of the regions, meaning hearts are in coverings and sheaths (ghulf as plural of aghlaf, as an uncircumcised man is called), and with a vowelled, doubled lam. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 323} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 324}

On "ghulf" al-Tabari continues. Those who read it with a silent lam take it as the plural of aghlaf, "in coverings and sheaths", pointing to the uncircumcised man being called aghlaf and to the rule that such plurals take a silent middle letter, which is doubled only by poetic necessity, as in a verse of Tarafa. He reports from Hudhayfa (stopped at him) that the uncircumcised, bound heart is the disbeliever's heart (the editor shows the chain is broken and notes a raised version of similar meaning in Ahmad's Musnad through Abu Sa'id), from Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, al-A'mash and al-Suddi that the hearts are in coverings and veiling, from Qatada and Abu al-'Aliya that they do not understand, and from Ibn Zayd that the heart is in a sheath so nothing reaches it, citing Fussilat 41:5. Those who read it with a vowelled lam take it as the plural of ghilaf, "containers of knowledge"; he reports from 'Atiyya that they are vessels of remembrance and knowledge and from Ibn Abbas that they are full of knowledge and need neither Muhammad nor anyone else. Al-Tabari chooses the silent-lam reading, since the readers of the regions and the interpreters agree on it and the dissenters are isolated, following his principle that what the agreed authority transmits is not to be contested by a lone reading. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 324} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 325} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 326} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 327} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 328}

On "rather, God has cursed them for their disbelief" he says la'n is expulsion, distancing and banishment, so God put them far from His mercy for rejecting His signs and denying His prophets, citing a verse of al-Shammakh. He holds that "bal" (rather) refutes their claim "our hearts are ghulf": it is not as they claimed; God distanced them because of their disbelief. On "so little do they believe" there are two views: that only a few of them believe, which is Qatada's, who says more of the polytheists returned than of the People of the Book, only a small band of whom believed, or that they believe only in a little of what they hold. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 328} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 329}

Al-Tabari then gives his own judgement on "so little do they believe": God reports that they have little belief in what He sent down to His prophet, and "qalilan" is in the accusative as the description of an omitted verbal noun, "a little belief do they believe". This, he says, refutes Qatada's report: if the sense were "only a few of them believe", "few" would be in the nominative and "ma" would be left without a governing word, which no Arab speech allows. On "ma" he gives the view that it is a redundant particle, as in "fa-bima rahmatin", citing a verse of Muhalhil, and then prefers the view of those who reject this, that "ma" embraces all things and what follows narrows it, since a redundant word with no meaning should not be ascribed to God. To the question what belief they had at all, he answers that the Jews affirmed God's oneness, the resurrection, reward and punishment, while rejecting Muhammad and his prophethood though it was in their books; what they affirmed was the little, and what they denied the much. He also mentions another view, that they disbelieved in everything and "little" is an idiom of negation, as in the Arab saying "qalla ma ra'aytu mithla hadha". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 329} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 330} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 331}
