## Summary
Verse 1 of [[passage/quran-2|Surat al-Baqara]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 1 of 286 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

## In al-Tabari's commentary
The commentary on Surat al-Baqara, headed in the edition "the account of the commentary on the surah in which the cow is mentioned", opens with "the interpretation of the words of God, Alif Lam Mim". Al-Tabari says the interpreters of the Qur'an differ over it. Some say it is one of the names of the Qur'an, reported from Qatada, Mujahid and Ibn Jurayj. Some say it is an opening with which God begins the Qur'an, from Mujahid by several routes. Others say it is the name of the surah, from Abd al-Rahman ibn Zayd ibn Aslam from his father. Some say it is the greatest name of God, from al-Suddi from Ibn Abbas and Abdullah ibn Mas'ud, and from al-Sha'bi that the openings of the surahs are among God's names. Some say it is an oath by which God swore and also one of His names, from Ibn Abbas through Ali ibn Abi Talha and from Ikrima. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 205} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 206} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 207}
Some say it is separate letters from nouns and verbs, each letter for a meaning different from the other, with Ibn Abbas giving "I am God, I know best", and Sa'id ibn Jubayr the same; al-Suddi that it is a letter drawn from the letters spelling God's names; Ibn Abbas that it is a name broken into parts. Some say they are letters of the alphabet laid down, from Mujahid. Some say each letter holds many different meanings; here he gives a report from al-Rabi' ibn Anas that these are among the twenty-nine letters on which all tongues revolve, each is the key of one of God's names and is found in His favours and trials and in the life-spans of peoples, with a saying he attributes to Jesus, son of Mary, and that the alif is the key of "Allah", the lam of "the Gentle" and the mim of "the Glorious". He then mentions a view that they are letters of the numerical value system and says he disliked naming whom it is reported from, since the narrator is one whose reports are not relied on. Some say every book has a secret, and the secret of the Qur'an is its openings. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 207} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 208} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 209}
The grammarians differed too. Some say they are letters of the alphabet, and naming those at the head of the surahs is enough without naming the rest, as someone who says "a b t th" need not name the other twenty-eight; so "that Book" in [[passage/quran-2-2|2:2]] is in the nominative, for the sense is "Alif, Lam, Mim of the separate letters: that is the Book I sent down to you, collected, with no doubt in it". When it is said that "a b t th" has become like a name for the alphabet as "al-hamd" became the name of the Opening of the Book, the answer is that since a man can say "my son is in 't z'" and be understood to mean he is among the separate letters, "a b t th" is not a name. They cite a rajaz line of a poet of Banu Asad from which one understands that a woman is in the abjad. Others say the surahs were begun with them to open the hearing of the idolaters, who had agreed to turn away from the Qur'an, so that when they listened, the composed text could be recited to them. Some say they are letters with which God opens His speech. If asked whether there can be anything in the Qur'an with no meaning, the answer is that He opened with them so it would be known that the previous surah had finished and another had begun, a sign of the break, as the Arabs reciting verse say "bal" and break off one speech to begin another. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 209} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 210}
Al-Tabari then says that each of these views has a known ground. For those who say "Alif Lam Mim" is a name of the Qur'an there are two: that it is a name of the Qur'an as "al-Furqan" is, so that "Alif Lam Mim, that Book" has the sense of an oath, "by the Qur'an, this is the Book with no doubt in it"; or that it is a name of the surah by which it is known, as things are known by their names, so that a listener hearing someone say "I read 'Alif Lam Mim Sad' today" understands which surah. If it is objected that "Alif Lam Mim" and "Alif Lam Ra" have likes among many surahs, he replies that names, though several share them and they need other words to separate them, were first laid down to tell things apart. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 211}
Al-Tabari completes his answer to the objection that when many share a name something is needed to tell the bearers apart: the speaker says he read "Alif Lam Mim of al-Baqara" or "Alif Lam Mim of Al Imran", as one reporting on two men both called Amr, one of Tamim and one of Azd, would say "I met Amr the Tamimi and Amr the Azdi"; so it is with those who take the disjoined letters as names of the surahs. Those who say they are openings with which God begins His speech take them as signs that one surah has ended and another begun, a mark of the break, as the Arabs put "bal" at the start of a poem. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 212}
Those who say the disjoined letters are partly from God's names and partly from His attributes, each for a meaning other than the other's, follow a line in which the poet says that when they asked her to stop, she said "qaf", meaning "I stopped", showing by the letter qaf what the whole word was; so one said the alif is the alif of "ana" (I), the lam of "Allah", the mim of "a'lam" (know best), each letter a full word, "I am God, I know best". They cite the Arabs' habit of cutting letters from a word when what remains shows what was dropped, as in calling "Haritha" "Hari" and "Malik" "Mal" in address; a rajaz line where the poet uses only the "ya" of a verb and another using the first letters of two words; and a report from Muhammad ibn Sirin that Ubayda told him, when Yazid ibn Mu'awiya died, that a trial was coming and he should lie down, and Ayyub and Ibn Awn showed this with their hands under the right cheek, using only a letter of the word for "lie down". The editor says the name in the chain is a corruption of Ubayda al-Salmani. They also cite additions of letters that are not in the word, in a line with an extra letter on the word for breast and another adding a "d" to a word, so what was cut from the letters of each of these words is like what was cut from speech. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 212} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 213} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 214}
Those who say each letter of "Alif Lam Mim" and its likes points to many meanings, as reported from al-Rabi' ibn Anas, take each letter as part of a whole word whose completion is understood, but differ from the first group about which word. The alif is from many words indicating all those meanings: among them the completion of the name "Allah", of the name of God's favours, "alaa'", and the indication that a people's term is one year, since alif is one in the numerical value system; the lam is the completion of "latif" and of "lutf", and indicates a term of thirty years; the mim is the completion of "majid" and of "majd", and indicates forty years. Each letter is kept separate and short of the full word because the full word would show only one meaning and He meant many. On this view God opened His speech by describing Himself as the Knower from whom nothing is hidden, and made this a way for His servants at the start of their sermons and letters, and a trial for them, as He opened with praise in some surahs, with glorification in [[passage/quran-17-1|17:1]], and with exaltation in others; so the openings that are letters of the alphabet are praises of Himself at times by knowledge, at times by justice, at times by favour. Then "that Book" is a fresh statement cut off from "Alif Lam Mim". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 214} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 215} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 216}
Those who say they are letters of the numerical value system alone say: we know of no meaning of the disjoined letters that can be understood except the numerical value and spelling out "Alif Lam Mim"; God does not address His servants except with what they understand; since the meaning of spelling cannot be right, it must be the numerical value, because "that Book" cannot follow "Alif Lam Mim" without making the meaning impossible. They cite a report al-Tabari gives from Jabir ibn Abdullah ibn Ri'ab, through a chain including al-Kalbi: Abu Yasir ibn Akhtab passed by the Prophet as he recited the opening of al-Baqara and told his brother Huyay ibn Akhtab and a group of Jews; they came and asked him about what they had heard, saying that God had sent earlier prophets and they knew of none to whom He had told the length of his rule and the span of his community except him; Huyay counted alif one, lam thirty, mim forty, seventy-one years, asked whether there was more, and when the Prophet named Alif Lam Mim Sad counted 161, Alif Lam Ra 231, Alif Lam Mim Ra 271, and said "your matter has been made confusing to us, we do not know whether you were given little or much"; Abu Yasir said perhaps he had been given all these, 734 years, and they said his matter had become obscure to them. They say [[passage/quran-3-7|3:7]] came down about them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 216} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 217} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 218}
In his note the editor judges the chain weak: it is from Muhammad ibn Ishaq by a weak chain, Ibn Hisham gives it in the Sira with a hedging form through Ibn Abbas and Jabir together, and al-Bukhari gives it in the Great History with three chains, which the editor sets out, one of them having an unknown client of Zayd ibn Thabit. This report is what those who hold the numerical-value reading cite; the museum gives it as transmitted and holds no verdict. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 218}
The editor continues his analysis of the chains of the Abu Yasir report: the second chain in al-Bukhari goes through Salama ibn al-Fadl from Ibn Ishaq from Muhammad ibn Abi Muhammad, a client of Zayd ibn Thabit, from Ikrima or Sa'id from Ibn Abbas, with doubt, and the third is the chain of al-Kalbi from Abu Salih from Ibn Abbas that al-Tabari gives. He says al-Bukhari notes the unsteadiness of the account on Salama and that Ibn al-Madini said they threw out his report before leaving al-Rayy; the editor leans to trusting Salama and sees the unsteadiness as Ibn Ishaq's, and says the version here is the weakest. He quotes Ibn Kathir that whoever claims the letters indicate reckoning of periods and the extraction of the times of events has claimed what is not his, and that the hadith that came on this is weak and turns on al-Kalbi, whose lone reports are not used as proof. The editor says al-Kalbi is very weak and accused of lying, that al-Tabari himself called him weak earlier, and that it is surprising he cites these reports when he then chooses that the letters hold all the meanings including the numerical value. This is the editor's criticism, not a museum judgement. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 219} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 220}
Al-Tabari's own view is that the sound interpretation of the openings that are letters of the alphabet is that God made them separate letters and did not join them, because He meant each letter to point to many meanings and not one, as al-Rabi' ibn Anas said, although al-Rabi' limited it to three; and that each letter holds what al-Rabi' said and what the other interpreters said, except what is reported of some grammarians, that they are letters of the alphabet, naming those given in place of the twenty-eight, so that "that Book" is a statement about them. That, he says, is a mistaken and unsound view because it departs from the sayings of all the Companions, Followers and later interpreters, and its author contradicted himself when he came to the case of "that Book" in the nominative, saying once that each raises the other, once that it is raised by the pronoun in "no doubt in it", and once by "a guidance for the God-fearing". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 220} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 221}
To the question how one letter can hold many different meanings, he answers as one word can: "umma" is a community, a span of time, a devout obedient man, and a religion or creed; "din" is requital and retaliation, authority and obedience, lowliness, and reckoning. So "Alif Lam Mim", "Alif Lam Ra" and "Alif Lam Mim Sad" and their likes are each letter pointing to different meanings, covering everything the commentators said of God's names and attributes, and also openings for the surahs; their being letters of God's names and attributes does not stop them being openings, for God began many surahs with praise of Himself and exaltation, so beginning some with an oath by them is not impossible. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 221} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 222}
So the surahs begun with letters of the alphabet are, among their meanings, openings of the surahs, and they are also oaths, since one of their meanings is that they are letters of God's names and attributes and an oath by God, His names and attributes is sound; they are letters of the numerical value system; and they are signs and names of the surahs begun with them. This takes in the meanings of all he described. He argues that if God had meant one meaning of these alone, the Prophet would have explained it plainly, and that his leaving it unexplained is the plainest proof that all the meanings it can bear are meant, since none is impossible to reason, as many meanings can belong to one word; and he asks whoever refuses this to explain the difference between it and "umma" and "din", and asks anyone who takes one single sense for his proof and sets it against his opponent's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 222} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 223}
As for the grammarian who said it is like "bal" in the reciter's verse, an addition with the sense of being dropped, he corrects him in three respects: first, he describes God as addressing the Arabs in what is not their language, since the Arabs, though they began their recital with "bal", did not begin any speech with "Alif Lam Mim", "Alif Lam Ra" or "Alif Lam Mim Sad" as they did with "bal", and God addressed them in the language they know, as [[passage/quran-26-193|26:193-195]] describes the Qur'an as in clear Arabic speech, so how could be clear what no one understands; second, he ascribes to God speech with no benefit, which all affirmers of God's oneness deny; third, "bal" has a known sense in Arabic, a turning back from speech that has ended, as in "your brother did not come, but your father", and no scholar of Arabic is known to have said that it comes as an opening with the sense of an addition to be dropped, save the one he names; he cites a poem of al-A'sha, so the likeness is far off. This ends the commentary on the words "Alif Lam Mim". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 223} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 224}
