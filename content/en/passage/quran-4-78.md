## Summary
Verse 78 of [[passage/quran-4|Surat an-Nisa]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 78 of 176 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**Start of the commentary (p. 551).** Al-Tabari says the sense is: wherever you are, death reaches you and you die, even in high-built strongholds; then he says the point is not to be alarmed at death, nor flee from fighting or weaken in meeting your enemy out of care for your lives against being killed and dying, for death... (the sentence breaks off at the foot of p. 551). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 551}

**End of the sentence and the dispute over the towers (p. 552, note 1).** Al-Tabari completes the sentence: death comes down on them wherever they are and reaches them wherever they are, even if they guard against it behind impregnable strongholds. He then says the interpreters differed over the meaning of the towers; some said they are fortified palaces. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 552}

**Reports 9957-9958 (pp. 552-553, notes 1-2).** Bishr b. Mu'adh (9957, from Yazid, from Sa'id) reports from Qatada that they are fortified palaces. Ali b. Sahl (9958, from Mu'ammal b. Isma'il, from Abu Hammam, from Kathir Abu al-Fadl) reports from Mujahid an account of people before us: a woman with a hired man bore a girl, and sent him to fetch fire; at the door he met a man who told him that the girl would practise prostitution with a hundred men, then be married by her hired man, and die by a spider. The hired man slit the child's belly with a blade; she was treated, recovered, grew up and practised prostitution on a seashore. The man returned with great wealth and asked for a beautiful woman to marry; a woman pointed her out and he married her. He told her his story, and she said she was that girl and showed him the scar on her belly; she had practised prostitution without knowing whether with a hundred, fewer or more. He then told her what had been said about her death by a spider, so he built her a tower in the desert and made it strong. While they were in it she saw a spider on the ceiling and said no one but she would kill it; she brought it down and crushed it with her big toe, its poison ran between nail and flesh, her foot turned black and she died. The narrator says the verse came down about this. This is an account told as an occasion for the verse as Mujahid transmitted it, and the museum gives no verdict on it. The editor explains the verb for her practice as promiscuity and fornication; says the print reads the insect's name with a different letter and he kept the manuscript; that Kathir Abu al-Fadl is Kathir b. Yasar al-Tufawi al-Basri, who reported from al-Hasan al-Basri, Thabit al-Bunani and others, and has an entry in al-Tahdhib; and that Ibn Kathir gave the report in his tafsir from the tafsir of Ibn Abi Hatim and al-Suyuti mentioned it in al-Durr al-Manthur, attributing it to Ibn Abi Hatim and Abu Nu'aym in al-Hilya (the name of Abu Nu'aym is garbled in the scan). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 553}

**Reports 9959-9961 (p. 553, note 3).** Al-Qasim (9959, from al-Husayn, from Hajjaj) reports from Ibn Jurayj that they are high-built palaces (the report number is read from the sequence). Al-Tabari then states another view, that they are specific palaces in the sky. Muhammad b. al-Husayn (9960, from Ahmad b. Mufaddal, from Asbat) reports from al-Suddi that they are white palaces built in the lowest heaven. Al-Muthanna (9961, from Ishaq, from Abd al-Rahman b. Sa'd, from Abu Ja'far) reports from al-Rabi' that they are palaces in the sky. The editor says the name and chain of Abd al-Rahman b. Sa'd came in earlier places (report numbers as printed), that manuscript and print write him here as "b. Sa'id", and that the correct form comes in two later places. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 553}

**The grammarians on the passive participle (pp. 554-555, notes 1-3).** Al-Tabari reports that one Basran said the doubled form means tall and the light form means adorned (the editor traces it to Abu Ubayda's Majaz al-Qur'an). Another of them said much the same but made the light form mean something done with plaster, the gloss for the plaster word being garbled in the scan. The editor says manuscript and print read "others of them" where the context requires the singular. Then he gives the view of some Kufans (al-Farra's, in his Ma'ani al-Qur'an, as the editor says): the two forms share a root; the doubled one is doubled because the action recurs across a plural, as with "dyed robes" and "slaughtered sheep", and so with palaces, many and built repeatedly, like "he bolted the doors" or "I broke the stick into pieces"; the light form is also allowed; and when a single thing is meant and the action in it recurs, both are allowed, as with a torn garment and a cut hide, but if the action does not recur they allow only the light form, as in "a slaughtered ram", since slaughter does not recur as tearing does in a garment. They say for this reason a single palace is called by the light form, though the doubled form is allowed for the repetition of building. The editor says the print's wording here differs from the manuscript and he kept the manuscript for its correct sense. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 555}

**The second part of the verse: attributing good and ill (p. 555, notes 2-4).** Al-Tabari says the sense is: if ease, victory, conquest and booty reach them, they ascribe it to God and his decree; if hardship in living, defeat by an enemy, wounds and pain reach them, they say to you, Muhammad, that it comes of your mistaken management. He says this is a report from God about those he described to his Prophet earlier among those told to restrain themselves from fighting. The editor refers to earlier interpretations of the words for befalling, good, nearness and ill (page numbers garbled in the scan). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 555}

**Reports 9962-9964 (pp. 555-556, note 1).** Al-Muthanna (9962, from Ishaq, from Abd al-Rahman b. Sa'd and Ibn Abi Ja'far, from Abu Ja'far) reports from al-Rabi', from Abu al-Aliya, that this concerns the good and ill that reach them in ease and hardship; the editor refers to his note on the previous report. Al-Qasim (9963, from al-Husayn, from Hajjaj, from Abu Ja'far, from al-Rabi') reports the same from Abu al-Aliya. Yunus (9964, from Ibn Wahb) reports from Ibn Zayd that these verses came down about the war; he recited from the verse on precaution ([[passage/quran-4-71|4:71]]) to the close of [[passage/quran-4-79|4:79]], and said that when ill reaches them they say the Prophet managed badly and judged badly. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 556}

**The reply: both come from God (p. 556, note 2).** Al-Tabari says the sense is: say, Muhammad, to those who ascribe good to God and ill to you: both come from God, not from me or anyone else; from him come ease and hardship, victory and success, and the rout and the defeat. The editor says the print has "killing", the manuscript is unpointed and could be read "wealth", and he preferred the word for a rout, from the saying that someone routed and broke a people (this preference is his). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 556}

**Reports 9965-9967 (pp. 556-557).** Al-Muthanna (9965, from Abd al-Razzaq, from Ma'mar) reports from Qatada that it means blessings and calamities. Yunus (9966, from Ibn Wahb) reports from Ibn Zayd that it is victory and defeat. Al-Muthanna (9967, from Abdullah b. Salih, from Mu'awiya b. Salih, from Ali b. Abi Talha) reports from Ibn Abbas that good and ill are both from God: the good he blessed you with, and the ill he tried you with. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 557}

**The last sentence (p. 557, note 1).** Al-Tabari says the sense is: what is the matter with these people, who ascribe good to God and ill to you, that they barely know the reality of what you tell them, that everything that befalls them of good or ill, harm or ease is from God, none else being able to bring it, no ill touching anyone except by his decree and no ease or favour coming except by his will. He says this is God informing his servants that the keys to all things are in his hand and no one else owns any of them. The editor gives al-Farra's statement in Ma'ani al-Qur'an that this particle became so frequent in speech that people supposed the lam was attached to what follows. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 557}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 557}
