**Commentary on verse 160 (pp. 174-177)**

Al-Tabari says of "and We divided them into twelve tribes, nations" that God split the people of Moses among the Children of Israel into several clans, twelve in number, and he refers back to his earlier explanation of "tribes" (asbat). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 174}

He then sets out the dispute of the Arabic grammarians over why the number "twelve" is feminine when "asbat" is a masculine plural. Some Basran grammarians held that the sense is "twelve groups", and that the groups were then said to be tribes, so the number was not made to agree with "asbat". Others found this explanation weak and argued that a number cannot take a form other than that of the noun that follows it; a feminine noun such as "groups" would have to come first so that the feminine number is correct. Some Kufan grammarians said the number is feminine because the sentence has "nations" in mind and the feminine prevailed, and they compare a line by a poet who treats "belly" in the sense of clan and so gives it a feminine plural. Other Kufans said it is feminine because of the mention of "nations"; the editor ascribes this to al-Farra' and lists where the verse is cited in the grammatical and lexical works. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 174} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 175}

Al-Tabari's own choice is that "twelve" is feminine because of the feminine "piece": the sense is "We cut them into twelve pieces", and "tribes" then explains what the pieces are. "Tribes" cannot be the specifying noun of the number, because after the numbers eleven to nineteen the specifier is a singular, not a plural, as Arabic says the equivalent of "twelve woman" with a singular noun and does not put the plural noun after the number. "Nations" means groups, and the "tribe" among the Children of Israel is like a "generation". It is also said that they were divided into tribes because they differed in their religion. The editor adds notes on the wording of al-Tabari's sentences between the manuscript and the printed text and on the grammatical sense of "tafsir" as the specifier. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 175} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 176}

He then turns to "and We inspired Moses when his people asked him for water: Strike the rock with your staff". When God had divided the Children of Israel into twelve groups and made them wander in the wilderness, they asked Moses for water out of thirst and because water had sunk away, and he was inspired to strike the rock with his staff. Al-Tabari refers back to his earlier account of why they asked and of the meaning of "inspiration". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 176}

"So there gushed from it twelve springs" of water, and "each people knew its drinking place", that is, each people among the twelve tribes, so that no tribe entered another's place to drink. "We shaded them with the cloud" sheltered them from the heat of the sun and its harm, and "We sent down on them manna and quails" as their food. "Eat of the good things We provided" means from the lawful things God provided and made good for them. On "and they did not wrong Us, but they wronged themselves", al-Tabari sees an omitted clause, left out because what is stated makes it clear: they grew tired of the food and said they could not endure a single food, so they exchanged the lower for the better. They did not wrong God in the sense of lessening His dominion by what they asked and did; they diminished their own share by exchanging the better for the lower. The editor explains that "ajima" of food means to dislike and tire of it through long continuance, and he refers back to the earlier commentary on the cloud, manna and quails. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v13 p. 177}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
