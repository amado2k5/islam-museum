## Summary
Verse 19 of [[passage/quran-3|Surat Al 'Imran]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 19 of 200 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

## Verse 19 in al-Tabari's commentary (Shakir ed., volume 6, pp. 273-275)

Al-Tabari ([[passage/quran-3-19|3:19]]) says that the word for religion here means obedience and humility. He cites a line by a poet whose name the editor does not know, describing a day on which the people, except the poet's own, were obedient in humility; a line of al-Qutami about a woman who did to him what she would and humbled him; and a line of al-A'sha Maymun ibn Qays praising one who subdued the Rabab when they disliked obedience in a raid and attack; he glosses the verb in both as 'humbled' and the noun as obedience. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 273-274}

In his notes the editor says that the first line appears in the commentary (26: 115, Bulaq) with a following line; that its second half is in Lisan al-'Arab and other dictionaries; that he doubts its soundness and does not know what 'the Day of al-Hazn' means; that he thinks a verb in it may be 'gathered' rather than 'mustered'; and that the following line describes a raid on al-Jurf, a place at al-Hira where al-Mundhir's dwellings were, from al-Farra's Ma'ani, the printed text of which makes no sense. On al-Qutami's line he says it is in his Diwan (p. 15), among fine lines in which he describes his beloved Umayma, who is called Janub in the line al-Tabari gave, Nawar in another report and Zalum in another, and that the verb means she did things to him, enslaved him, tormented him or repaid him. On al-A'sha's line he refers to its explanation in part three (p. 571). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 273-274}

Al-Tabari then says that submission, in the sense of Islam, is yielding by humbling oneself and showing humility, and that the verb means to enter into peace, as one says that people entered a drought or entered spring; so they submitted when they entered into peace, which is yielding with humility and giving up resistance. The editor refers to the earlier commentary on Islam and on peace. The sense of the sentence, on his reading, is that the obedience which is obedience in God's sight is obedience to Him, with the tongues and hearts acknowledging servitude and humility to Him, yielding to Him in obeying what He commanded and forbade and humbling themselves before Him without arrogance or turning away, and without associating any of His creation with Him in worship and divinity. The editor notes that the print has different forms of the nouns for worship and divinity and that he kept the manuscript's, and that the words mean worship. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 274-275}

He adds that a group of the interpreters said the same. In 6763, Qatada (Bishr, Yazid, Said): Islam is the testimony to oneness and the acknowledgement of what the Messenger brought from God; it is God's religion, which He legislated for Himself, sent His messengers with and guided His friends to; He accepts none other and rewards only by it. The editor says the pronoun in 'what he brought' goes back to the Messenger of God, as if Qatada had said the testimony to oneness and that Muhammad is God's messenger, since the testimony is not complete without him, and that al-Suyuti gave it in al-Durr al-Manthur (2: 12) and ascribed it also to Abd ibn Humayd in this wording. In 6764, Abu al-Aliyah (al-Muthanna, Ishaq, Ibn Abi Ja'far, his father, al-Rabi): Islam is sincerity to God alone and worship of Him with no partner. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 275}

Report 6764 from Abu al-Aliyah closes: Islam is sincerity to God alone and worship of Him with no partner; establishing prayer, paying zakat and the other obligations follow from this. In 6765, Ibn Zayd (Yunus, Ibn Wahb), on the word in al-Hujurat ([[passage/quran-49-14|49:14]]): we entered peace and left war. The editor says it comes in the commentary on al-Hujurat (26: 90, Bulaq) in a longer wording with the same chain. In 6766, Muhammad ibn Ja'far ibn al-Zubayr (Ibn Humayd, Salama, Ibn Ishaq): that is, what you are on, Muhammad, of oneness for the Lord and belief in the messengers. The editor says Ibn Hisham gave it in his Sira (2: 227), dropping a letter from the wording, and that it belongs to the remaining reports that ended at 6761. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 276}

Al-Tabari then explains the second part of the verse. He says those meant are the people given the Gospel, which is the Book named there, over the matter of Jesus and their lying against God in what they said about him, sayings that multiplied their disagreement, scattered their word and set them against one another until some made the blood of others lawful; they differed only after they knew the truth in what they differed over and were sure that in the great lie they told they were in the wrong. God told His servants that they did what they did of falsehood and said what is unbelief while knowing it was wrong; they did not say it from ignorance but said it and differed over it out of aggression by some against others and the search for leadership, kingship and authority. The editor refers to the earlier commentary on aggression. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 276-277}

In 6767, al-Rabi (al-Muthanna, Ishaq, Ibn Abi Ja'far, his father), from Abu al-Aliyah: only after the Book and knowledge came to them, out of aggression over the world and the search for its kingdom and authority, so that some killed others over the world after having been the scholars of the people. In 6768, Ibn Umar (through al-Rabi): he used to recite this verse often and say it was aggression over the world and the search for its kingship and authority, and then: by God, we were struck from its side; it would not have harmed us who ruled over us, once he upheld among us the Book of God and the practice of His Prophet, but we were struck from its side. The editor notes that the printed text dropped a word and so confused the sentence, and the right one is from the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 277}

In 6769, al-Rabi: when death came to Moses he called seventy rabbis of the Banu Israel, entrusted the Torah to them and made them guardians of it, each rabbi a portion, and appointed Joshua son of Nun as his successor. When the first generation passed, and the second and third, division fell among them, the sons of those seventy who had been given knowledge, until they shed one another's blood and evil and disagreement arose; all of it came from those given knowledge, out of aggression among themselves over the world, seeking its authority, kingdom, treasures and ornament, so God set their tyrants over them. Al-Rabi then said that God sent down what He sent down in this place, from this verse to the end of the next. The editor notes that the wording 'each rabbi a portion' stands so in the manuscript and in al-Durr al-Manthur (2: 13), as if it meant he entrusted each rabbi with a portion, and that the wording has its difficulties. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 277-278}

Al-Tabari remarks that this saying of al-Rabi ibn Anas shows he held that those meant are the Jews of the Banu Israel, not the Christians or others. The editor says the printed text and the manuscript have 'al-Rabi ibn Anas says: this shows', which is faulty since the remark is al-Tabari's comment on al-Rabi's report, so he put the right wording; 'not the Christians' means not the Christians among those given knowledge, and 'or others' points to Ibn Umar's report 6768. Others, al-Tabari says, directed it to the Christians who were given the Gospel. In 6770, Muhammad ibn Ja'far ibn al-Zubayr (Ibn Humayd, Salama, Ibn Ishaq): only after the knowledge came to you, that is, that God is One with no partner, and the aggression among them, meaning the Christians. The editor notes that Ibn Hisham gave it (2: 227) among the remaining reports that ended at 6766, and that 'meaning the Christians' is not in Ibn Hisham and seems al-Tabari's own gloss. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 278}

Of the verse's close, al-Tabari says that whoever denies God's proofs and signs, which He set up as a reminder for the one who reasons and as evidence for the one who takes heed, God will count his deeds in this world and repay him in the next, and the swiftness of His reckoning is a swiftness of counting; its sense is that He keeps every doer's deed, needs no tying of knots as His creatures tie them with their hands or hold them in their hearts, and keeps it with no strain, effort or toil such as others have in reckoning. Mujahid said the like. In 6771, Mujahid (Muhammad ibn Amr, Abu Asim, Isa, Ibn Abi Najih): His counting against them. In 6772, Mujahid (al-Muthanna, Abu Hudhayfa, Shibl, Ibn Abi Najih): His counting. The editor refers to the language indexes for 'unbelief' and 'signs' and to the earlier commentary on the swiftness of reckoning. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 279}
