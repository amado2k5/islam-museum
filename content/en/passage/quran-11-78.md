**Commentary on verse 78 (pp. 411-416)**

On [[passage/quran-11-78|11:78]] al-Tabari says: his people came to Lot rushing to him, 'yuhra'un', trembling as they hurried from their eagerness for the shameful act. One says 'ahra'a' of a man who shivers from cold, anger or fever, and 'muhra'' of one hurried and eager; he cites a rajaz line and a line of Muhalhil about captives coming in haste. Reports 18361 to 18371, from Mujahid, al-Dahhak, Qatada, al-Suddi, Sufyan b. 'Uyayna, Shamir b. 'Atiyya and Ibn Abbas, agree closely: they ran, hurried, made speed; Ibn 'Uyayna said as if they were being pushed, and Shamir said a gait between a run and a trot. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 412}

As for 'and before that they used to do evil deeds', it means before they came to Lot they used to come to men from behind (report 18372, Ibn Jurayj). As for 'my people, these are my daughters', Lot said to his people when they came asking for his guest: these are my daughters, meaning the women of his community; marry them, they are purer for you. Report 18373, from Qatada: Lot commanded them to marry women; 18374: Ma'mar has the same from Mujahid; 18375, from Mujahid: they were not his daughters but women of his community, and every prophet is father of his community; 18376: he told them to marry women and offered them no fornication; 18377, from Ibn Abi Najih, in the same sense, where the editor thinks something has dropped from the wording. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 413}

Report 18378, from Qatada: he commanded them to marry women, and God's prophet wished to shield his guests with his daughters; 18379, from al-Rabi': he meant marriage; 18380, from Sa'id b. Jubayr: he meant their women, who are his daughters because he is their prophet, and Sa'id cited a reading in [[passage/quran-33-6|33:6]] that the Prophet is a father to them; the editor notes one narrator in the chain is trustworthy. Report 18381, from al-Suddi: they said, did we not forbid you to host the worlds? He said, these are my daughters, purer for you, if you will act; is there not among you a right-minded man? {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 414}

Report 18382, from Ibn Ishaq: when the messengers came to Lot his people came to them as soon as they were told, rushing, and they claim, and God knows best, that Lot's wife told them where they were, saying that Lot had guests she had never seen the like of for beauty; they used to come to men out of desire rather than women, a shameful act none of the worlds had done before them; when they came they said, did we not forbid you the worlds, that is, did we not tell you that no one should come near you? He said 'my people, these are my daughters, purer for you', ransoming my guests from you with them, and he called them only to what is lawful in marriage. Report 18383, from Mujahid: 'my daughters' means the women. Al-Tabari then gives the variants on 'purer': most readers read it with the nominative, taking 'they' as subject and 'purer' as predicate; Isa b. Umar al-Basri is reported to have read it in the accusative. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 415}

Al-Tabari sets out the grammarians: one of the Basran grammarians said the accusative cannot be, since only the predicate of a verb takes it; one of the Kufans held that whoever puts it in the accusative treats it as an indefinite emerging from a definite, with 'they' as a support for the verb; another said the Arabs are heard to say 'this is Zayd himself' and treats it as a predicate, adding that a verb cannot occur here because 'taqrib' answers back a statement; the editor explains the Kufan term. Al-Tabari concludes: the reading I cannot allow to be contradicted is the nominative, for the consensus of the authorities among the readers of the cities and its soundness in Arabic, and the accusative is far from sound. As for 'fear God and do not shame me before my guest', he says: fear God, people, and beware His punishment for the shameful act you commit and seek, and do not humiliate me by doing to my guest what they hate to have done. 'Guest' here is one word with the sense of a plural, and the Arabs call one and many 'daif', as they say a just man and a just people. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v15 p. 416}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
