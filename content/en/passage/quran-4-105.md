## Summary
Verse 105 of [[passage/quran-4|Surat an-Nisa]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 105 of 176 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**Beginning of the commentary on verses 105-106: the book, judging, and the treacherous (pp. 175-176, note 1 on p. 176).** Al-Tabari treats verses 105 and 106 of sura 4 together, and they are filed here under 105 with verse [[passage/quran-4-106|4:106]] linked. He says that God is telling Muhammad that He has sent down to him the Book, meaning the Qur'an, with the truth, so that he may judge between people by what God has shown him, that is by what God sent down to him of His Book. As for not being an advocate for the treacherous, he says it means: do not be, for whoever betrayed a Muslim or a covenanted person in his person or property, one who argues for him and defends him against the person claiming his right which he betrayed. The command to seek forgiveness means asking God to pardon him the penalty for his fault in arguing for the one who betrayed another's property, and God has ever been forgiving to His believing servants when they seek forgiveness, and merciful to them; so let the Prophet do so and God will forgive what went before of arguing for this traitor. Al-Tabari adds that it has been said that the Prophet did not argue for the traitor but intended to, and was ordered to seek forgiveness for what he intended. It is also reported that the traitors with whom God rebuked His Prophet over arguing for them were Banu Ubayriq. The interpreters differed over the treachery for which God described him, and some said it was a theft he committed. The editor refers to the language indexes for the explanation of the words for seeking forgiveness, forgiving and merciful. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 176} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 176}

**Reports 10409 and 10410: Ibn Ubayriq and the stolen coat of mail (pp. 176-177, notes 2-3 on p. 176 and note 1 on p. 177).** Report 10409 (Muhammad ibn Amr, from Abu Asim, from Isa, from Ibn Abi Najih, from Mujahid) says that the passage from this verse to the verse about seeking God's approval, [[passage/quran-4-114|4:114]], concerns Ibn Ubayriq and the iron coat of mail he stole from a Jew; his companions among the believers told the Prophet to excuse him before the people with his tongue, and they accused an innocent Jewish man of the theft. Report 10410 (al-Muthanna, from Abu Hudhayfa, from Shibl, from Ibn Abi Najih, from Mujahid) is the same in sense. The editor notes that the printed text has "Tu'ma ibn Ubayriq", a name that appears in report 10412, while the manuscript has "Ibn Ubayriq" here, and that the reports differ over these Banu Ubayriq; that the words "of the Jews" are restored from the manuscript; and that report 10410 is not established in the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 177} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 177}

**Report 10411: Banu Ubayriq and the theft of flour and arms (pp. 177-179, notes 1-3 on p. 177, notes 1-8 on p. 178 and notes 1-4 on p. 179).** Report 10411 (al-Hasan ibn Ahmad ibn Abi Shu'ayb Abu Muslim al-Harrani, from Muhammad ibn Salama, from Muhammad ibn Ishaq, from Asim ibn Umar ibn Qatada, from his father, from his grandfather Qatada ibn al-Nu'man) is the long account. Qatada says there was a household among them called Banu Ubayriq, Bishr, Bushayr and Mubashshir, and Bushayr was a hypocrite who composed verse lampooning the Prophet's companions and then ascribed it to some other Arab, saying so-and-so said this; when the companions heard it they said that none but the vile one would say it, and he answered with a line, saying that whenever men compose a poem they are angry and say Ibn al-Ubayriq made it. They were a household of need in the age of ignorance and in Islam, and the food of the people of Medina was dates and barley; if a man had means and a caravan of Syrian traders brought fine flour, he bought some for himself alone, while the family ate dates and barley. When such a caravan came, Qatada's uncle Rifa'a ibn Zayd bought a load of fine flour and put it in an upper room of his in which he also kept arms, two coats of mail with their swords and fittings. In the night the room was broken into and the food and arms taken. At dawn his uncle told him they had been robbed, and they asked around the settlement and were told that Banu Ubayriq had lit a fire that night, which they took to be over some of their food. Banu Ubayriq had said, while the inquiry went on, that they thought the culprit was Labid ibn Sahl, a man of theirs known for righteousness and Islam; when Labid heard this he drew his sword and came to them and said that the sword would mix with them unless they made the theft plain, and they told him to go away, since he was not the one. Qatada says they went on asking until they had no doubt that Banu Ubayriq were the thieves, and his uncle told him to go to the Prophet. He told the Prophet that a rough household had broken into his uncle's room and taken his arms, and asked that they return the arms, since the food was not wanted. The Prophet said he would look into it. When Banu Ubayriq heard, they went to one of their own, Usayr ibn Urwa, and a group of the settlement gathered and came to the Prophet, saying that Qatada ibn al-Nu'man and his uncle were accusing a household of Islam and righteousness of theft without proof. Qatada went to the Prophet again, who said he was accusing a household reported to be Muslim and righteous without proof. Qatada went back wishing he had lost some of his own property and had not spoken to the Prophet, and his uncle Rifa'a said that God is the one whose help is sought. The editor's notes on this part: the word for the traders names people, originally Nabataeans, who carried flour, oil and the like to Medina, and the word for fine flour is for pure white flour; the printed text has "from them" where the manuscript has "from us", and he kept what the other sources give; the upper room is a loft or chamber; the other sources have "from under the house" for the night raid, but al-Tabari's wording seems the right form of the report; he explains that the verb for searching the settlement is better with the letter used in the other sources, as in the story of Joseph, and that the word for the settlement means the quarter a tribe or clan lives in, as in the saying that the best quarters of the Ansar are those of Banu al-Najjar and then Banu Abd al-Ashhal; that drawing a sword means unsheathing it; and that the printed text has a nominative for a word the manuscript has in the accusative, which is plainly right. On p. 179 he notes that the word for a rough household means people of coarseness, that the manuscript has a variant in the clause about the food, both being right, that the printed text and the manuscript differ in the words of the Prophet's promise to look into it, and that the word for proof means evidence and demonstration. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 179} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 179}

**Report 10411 continued: the revelation, the return of the arms and what became of Bushayr (pp. 179-181, notes 1-3 on p. 180 and notes 1-3 on p. 181).** In the report Qatada returned to his uncle and told him what the Prophet had said, and the uncle again said that God is the one whose help is sought. Soon after, the Qur'an came down with the verses of this passage, which the report explains clause by clause: the one who must not be defended is Banu Ubayriq, the forgiveness sought is for what he had said to Qatada, those who hide from people are Banu Ubayriq, the slander and manifest sin laid on an innocent is what they said of Labid, and the group who wished to lead the Prophet astray is Usayr and his companions. The passage runs to the verse promising a great reward to the believers and, in the report, to the verse about the Book and wisdom sent down to the Prophet. When it came down, the Prophet brought the arms and returned them to Rifa'a. Qatada says that when he brought his uncle the arms, the old man, who had grown old in the age of ignorance and whose Islam Qatada used to think flawed, said that the arms were for the cause of God, so Qatada knew that his Islam was sound. After the Qur'an came down, Bushayr joined the polytheists and lodged with Sulafa bint Sa'd ibn Shuhayd, and God revealed concerning him the verses on opposing the Messenger after guidance has become clear, ending with the verse that whoever associates others with God has gone far astray, [[passage/quran-4-115|4:115]] and [[passage/quran-4-116|4:116]]. When Bushayr lodged with Sulafa, Hassan ibn Thabit lampooned her in some lines of verse; she took his saddle and baggage, put it on her head, went out and threw it in the Abtah of Mecca, and said that he had brought her the poetry of Hassan and would bring her no good. The editor's notes: the word for aged in the age of ignorance means old and hard, as a dried stick is hard; the word for "flawed" means marred, with fault and corruption, that is that his faith had hypocrisy in it; the name of the woman is differently given in the printed text, the manuscript, al-Tirmidhi, Ibn Kathir and al-Hakim's Mustadrak, which has her as a sister of Banu Amr ibn Awf who was with Talha ibn Abi Talha in Mecca, and he says the right form, which is also in al-Suyuti's Durr and in Hassan's diwan, is Sulafa bint Sa'd ibn Shuhayd; he gives her lineage from the Aws through Banu Awf, citing Ibn Hazm's book on the genealogies of the Arabs (p. 314), which mentions her kinsman Uwaym ibn Sa'd ibn Shuhayd, who had companionship with the Prophet and was made governor of Palestine by Umar; he adds that this is the Sulafa who was the wife of Talha ibn Abi Talha and the mother of Musafi', al-Julas and Kilab, who were killed at Uhud with their father, Musafi' and al-Julas by Asim ibn Thabit ibn Abi al-Aflah, the one protected by bees, and that she then vowed to drink wine from Asim's skull, but the bees (Ibn Hisham 3:66 and 3:180) kept Hudhayl from taking his head to sell to her. Of Hassan's lines (his diwan, p. 271) he says that they speak ill of Sulafa; the word for the Abtah is the wide gravel bed of Mecca's valley. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 181} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 181}

**The editor on the chain of report 10411 (pp. 181-182).** Shakir says that al-Hasan ibn Ahmad ibn Abi Shu'ayb Abu Muslim al-Harrani was from Harran and lived in Baghdad; that al-Khatib al-Baghdadi called him trustworthy and reliable; that Ibn Hibban listed him among the reliable narrators but added that he reports unusual things; that he narrated from Muhammad ibn Salama; and that he is in Tahdhib al-Tahdhib, Ibn Abi Hatim and Tarikh Baghdad. The report is in al-Tirmidhi's Sunan on the commentary on this verse, with the same chain; in al-Hakim's Mustadrak (4:385), with a different chain through Yunus ibn Bukayr and a wording that differs from al-Tabari's; in Ibn Kathir's commentary (2:574-576) and in al-Suyuti's Durr (2:214-215), who adds Ibn Abi Hatim and Abu al-Shaykh. Al-Hakim called it sound on the condition of Muslim; al-Tirmidhi called it strange, saying none connected it except Muhammad ibn Salama al-Harrani, while Yunus ibn Bukayr and others narrated it from Ibn Ishaq, from Asim ibn Umar ibn Qatada, as a report with the Successor at the end without "from his father, from his grandfather"; but al-Hakim, as the editor points out, has it through Yunus raised to Qatada ibn al-Nu'man. He adds that Ibn Hajar says Abu Muslim narrated from his father and grandfather, and fears that this is a slip arising from this chain: the words "my grandfather and my father both told me" were said by Abd Allah ibn al-Hasan ibn Ahmad, not by al-Hasan ibn Ahmad, and that al-Khatib quotes Abu Shu'ayb as saying that his father (al-Hasan) said that Yahya ibn Ma'in, Ahmad ibn Hanbal, Ali ibn al-Madini and Ishaq ibn Abi Isra'il heard it from him in the mosque in Baghdad. In Ibn Kathir's version the speaker of that line is Muhammad ibn Salama, which he says is right. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 182} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 182}
