## Summary
Verse 36 of [[passage/quran-4|Surat an-Nisa]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 36 of 176 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**The opening of the verse: worship of God and no associates (pp. 333-334, note 1).** Al-Tabari says the sense is: abase yourselves to God in obedience and submit to Him by it, single Him out in lordship, devote to Him humility and lowliness by halting at His command and abstaining from what He forbade, and do not make for Him in lordship and worship a partner whom you revere as you revere Him. Note 1 refers to the earlier treatment of worship. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 334}

**Parents, relatives, the fatherless and the poor (p. 334, notes 2-5).** Al-Tabari says God commanded kindness toward parents, meaning dutiful treatment of them; the word for kindness takes its case because it is a command to keep to kindness toward parents in the manner of urging (note 2 refers to the treatment in volume 2, pp. 290-292); and some said the sense is 'take good counsel about parents with kindness', which is close to what he said. By relatives he means those who are kin through the father or the mother, whose nearness is by a womb tie on either side, and kindness to them is joining the tie (note 3: an earlier cross-reference). Orphans are the plural of the word for a child whose father has died (note 4), and the poor the plural of a word for one overtaken by the humiliation of want and need, who is thereby abased (note 5). God then charges them to take good counsel for these, with kindness and sympathy toward them, and to keep His charge of kindness to them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 334}

**The neighbour who is a relative (pp. 335-336, reports 9437-9445).** Al-Tabari says the interpreters differed over the neighbour described as having kinship. Some said it is the neighbour with whom you have kinship and a womb tie. Al-Muthanna (9437, from Abd Allah b. Salih, from Mu'awiya, from Ali b. Abi Talha) from Ibn Abbas: the one between whom and you there is kinship; Muhammad b. Sa'd (9438, from his father, uncle, father, grandfather) from Ibn Abbas: the one with a womb tie; al-Hasan b. Yahya (9439, from Abd al-Razzaq, from Ma'mar, from Qatada and Ibn Abi Najih) from Mujahid: your neighbour who is your kin; Ibn Waki (9440, from his father, from Isra'il, from Jabir) from Ikrima and Mujahid: kinship; al-Muthanna (9441, from Amr b. Awn, from Hushaym, from Juwaybir) from al-Dahhak: your neighbour with whom there is kinship; al-Muthanna (9442, from Abu Hudhayfa, from Shibl, from Ibn Abi Najih) from Mujahid likewise; Bishr b. Mu'adh (9443, from Yazid, from Sa'id) from Qatada: if he has a neighbour with a womb tie he has two rights, the right of kinship and the right of neighbour; Yunus (9444, from Ibn Wahb) from Ibn Zayd: the neighbour who is your kin. Others said rather the neighbour of one who is your kinsman; Abd al-Rahman (9445, from Jarir, from Layth) reports from Maymun b. Mihran that it is the man who approaches you by virtue of being neighbour to your relative. Al-Tabari says this view runs against what is known in Arab speech, because the one described as having kinship in the verse is the neighbour himself; had it meant what Maymun said, the wording would have joined the neighbour to the kinsman in a different construction, making the charge about the neighbour of a kinsman, whereas with the definite article the kinship can only be a description of the neighbour, so the charge is about the neighbour who is kin; he thinks Maymun's error plain. In the notes (p. 336) the editor says the manuscript and the printed edition both garble two phrases here and he corrected them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 336}

**Nearness by Islam (p. 337, report 9446).** Others said the sense is the neighbour near to you by Islam. Muhammad b. Umara al-Asadi (9446, from Ubayd Allah b. Musa, from Sufyan, from Abu Ishaq) reports from Nawf al-Shami that it is the Muslim (note 1: the editor says Nawf is the son of Fadala al-Himyari al-Bikali, whose biography came earlier at no. 3965 as printed, with a second reference whose number is unclear). Al-Tabari says this too has no sense, because the interpretation of God's book may not be turned except to the commoner usage of the Arabs in whose tongue the Qur'an came down, the known and not the unfamiliar, unless a proof that must be accepted stands against it; and what is known is that when one says 'so-and-so is a kinsman' it means near by blood, not near by religion, so turning it to blood kinship is better. Note 2 says the word for 'known' is in the genitive, qualifying the preceding word, and that the printed edition erred in it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 337}

**The neighbour described as distant (pp. 337-339, reports 9447-9456).** Al-Tabari says the interpreters differed over the neighbour so described. Some said the distant neighbour with whom you have no kinship. Al-Muthanna (9447, from Abu Salih, from Mu'awiya, from Ali b. Abi Talha) from Ibn Abbas: the one with whom you have no kinship; Muhammad b. Sa'd (9448, by the same chain to Ibn Abbas): the neighbour from a distant people; Bishr b. Mu'adh (9449, from Yazid, from Sa'id; its number is misprinted but fits the sequence) from Qatada: one with whom there is no kinship, yet he is a neighbour and has the right of neighbourhood; Muhammad b. al-Husayn (9450, from Ahmad b. al-Mufaddal, from Asbat) from al-Suddi: the stranger neighbour among the people; al-Hasan b. Yahya (9451, from Abd al-Razzaq, from Ma'mar, from Qatada and Ibn Abi Najih) from Mujahid: your neighbour from other people; al-Muthanna (9452, from Abu Hudhayfa, from Shibl, from Ibn Abi Najih) from Mujahid: your neighbour with no kinship, distant in lineage yet a neighbour; Ibn Waki (9453, from his father, from Isra'il, from Jabir) from Ikrima and Mujahid: the one who keeps aloof; Yunus (9454, from Ibn Wahb) from Ibn Zayd: the one with whom you have no womb tie or kinship (p. 338, note 1: the printed edition has a meaningless word, the manuscript's reading is right); Yahya b. Abi Talib (9455, from Yazid, from Juwaybir) from al-Dahhak: from other people. Others said it is the polytheist neighbour; Muhammad b. Umara al-Asadi (9456, from Ubayd Allah b. Musa, from Sufyan, from Abu Ishaq) reports from Nawf al-Shami that it is the Jew and the Christian (p. 339, note 1: Ubayd Allah b. Musa b. Abi al-Mukhtar al-Absi's biography came earlier at no. 5796 as printed; he narrates from Sufyan al-Thawri and from Shayban al-Tamimi; the chain in the printed edition and manuscript has Shayban from Abu Ishaq but written doubtfully in the manuscript, and since 9446 clearly has Sufyan from Abu Ishaq in the manuscript the editor preferred and printed Sufyan). Al-Tabari says the better of the two views is that of those who said the word here means the distant stranger, Muslim or polytheist, Jew or Christian, given what he showed, that the neighbour who is kin is the neighbour with blood ties, so the other must be the distant neighbour, making the charge cover every kind of neighbour, near and far. He adds that the word means 'far' in Arab speech and cites a line of al-A'sha of the Banu Qays, in which the poet says he came to a man called Hurayth as a visitor from afar and found Hurayth tight-fisted in giving (described, not reproduced). The editor's note on the line gives its place in the poet's Diwan (p. 49), Abu Ubayda's Majaz al-Qur'an (p. 126) and al-Kamil (2:26), says it recurs later in the commentary, and says it comes from a poem lampooning al-Harith b. Wa'la b. Mujalid b. Zabban al-Raqashi, who came to ask him for something and was told 'no honour! are you not the one who said' (the note continues on the next page). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 339}

**More on the sense of distance (p. 340, notes 1-2).** Al-Tabari says the word for the distant one here means remoteness and estrangement; from it people say someone kept away from another when he was distant from him, or that he shunned him, or withheld his good from him; the ritually impure person is likewise so named for withdrawing from prayer until he washes. So the sense is the neighbour who keeps clear of kinship. Note 1 says the printed edition dropped a word between two phrases and spoiled the sense, the right reading being from the manuscript. The editor's note on the al-A'sha line, continued from p. 339: after the first line in which the poet complains of what the man did to him (slandering, belittling, then asking), al-A'sha said what wounded him, and in further lines he blamed the man for resembling neither Wa'la in generosity nor his father al-Mujalid, and for a house in which a visiting friend sees what frightens (described, not reproduced), among much poetry; Hurayth is a shortened diminutive of al-Harith; a man is called tight of hand when he is miserly, his qualities unyielding; the printed edition erred over one word here and in another place of the commentary, and the variant the commentary gives at 26:20 agrees with all the earlier references and does no harm. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 340}

**The travelling companion (pp. 340-342, reports 9457-9470).** The interpreters gave three views of the companion at one's side. Some said he is the man's travelling companion. Al-Muthanna (9457, from Abd Allah b. Salih, from Mu'awiya, from Ali b. Abi Talha; the chain starts at the foot of p. 340 and ends on p. 341) from Ibn Abbas: the travelling companion; Ibn Bashshar (9458, from Yahya and Abd al-Rahman, from Sufyan, from Abu Bukayr) from Sa'id b. Jubayr: the travelling companion on a journey; al-Hasan b. Yahya (9459, from Abd al-Razzaq, from Ma'mar, from Qatada and Ibn Abi Najih) from Mujahid: your companion on a journey; Bishr b. Mu'adh (9460, from Yazid, from Sa'id) from Qatada, likewise; al-Muthanna (9461, from Abu Hudhayfa, from Shibl, from Ibn Abi Najih) from Mujahid: his lodging is yours, his food yours, his travel yours; Sufyan (9462, from his father, from Isra'il, from Jabir) from Ikrima and Mujahid: the travelling companion (Ikrima's name is misprinted); al-Muthanna (9463, from al-Himmani, from Sharik, from Jabir, from Amir) from Ali and Abd Allah: the righteous companion; al-Qasim (9464, number misprinted and inferred from the sequence; from al-Husayn, from Hajjaj, from Ibn Jurayj, from Salim) from Mujahid: your travelling companion who comes to you with his hand in yours; al-Muthanna (9465, from Suwayd b. Nasr, from Ibn al-Mubarak reading to Ibn Jurayj, from Salim) from Mujahid, likewise; Muhammad b. al-Husayn (9466, from Ahmad b. al-Mufaddal, from Asbat) from al-Suddi: the companion on a journey; al-Muthanna (9467, from Abu Dukayn, from Sufyan, from Abu Bukayr) from Sa'id b. The editor's note 1 on 9458: Abu Bukayr is al-Taymi, the muezzin of Taym, named Marzuq, who narrated from Sa'id b. Jubayr, Ikrima and Mujahid and from whom Layth b. **Second view: the man's wife (pp. 342-343, reports 9471-9479).** Others said she is the wife who is beside him. Ibn Waki (9471, from his father, from Sufyan, from Jabir, from Amir or al-Qasim) from Ali and Abd Allah: the woman (the note says the blessing formula after their names is an addition from the manuscript; it is not repeated here); al-Muthanna (9472, from Amr b. Sa'd (9473, from his father, his uncle, his father, his grandfather) from Ibn Abbas: the one with you in your house; Muhammad b. al-Muthanna (9474, from Muhammad b. Ja'far, from Shu'ba, from Hilal) from Abd al-Rahman b. Abi Layla: the woman; Ibn Bashshar (9475, from Abd al-Rahman, from Sufyan, from Abu al-Hashim) from Ibrahim al-Nakha'i: the woman; al-Hasan b. The note on 9479: the editor found the shaykh's name with a dotted letter in the manuscript, knew no biography of him, and found him in the Tarikh al-Sahaba wa-l-Tabi'in appended to the History (p. 86) with an undotted letter; he thinks the first more likely right. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 343}

**Third view: the one who attaches himself hoping for benefit (pp. 343-344, reports 9480-9481).** Others said he is the one who keeps close to you and accompanies you hoping for your benefit. Al-Qasim (9480, from al-Husayn, from Hajjaj, from Ibn Jurayj) from Ibn Abbas: the constant companion, and also the fellow who goes with you; Yunus (9481, from Ibn Wahb) from Ibn Zayd: the one who clings to you, at your side, hoping for your good and benefit. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 344}

**Al-Tabari's choice and the two reports (pp. 344-346, reports 9482-9483).** Al-Tabari says the right sense, in his view, is the one who accompanies a person at his side, as one says a man is at another's side, as with leading horses side by side; it includes the travelling companion, the wife and the one attached hoping for benefit, since all are at the side of the one they are with, and God charged kindness to all of them because the companion has a right over the one he accompanies (his choice). Note 1 on p. 344 says this linguistic explanation is rarely found in the lexica. He then gives 9482 (Sahl b. Musa al-Razi, from Ibn Abi Fudayk, from 'so-and-so son of Abd Allah', from the one he trusted): the Prophet and a companion were on two mounts; he entered a tamarisk thicket and cut two shoots, one crooked and one straight, came out and gave his companion the straight one and kept the crooked; the man said he was more entitled to the straight one; he said every companion who accompanies another is asked about his companionship, even for an hour of the day. Notes 2-3 explain words (the thicket and the tamarisk; the right reading of a word printed with the wrong letter, meaning cut green crop used as fodder, on which the printed edition, the manuscript and al-Durr al-Manthur differ). The editor's note on p. 345 sends the reader to his earlier notes on Sahl and Ibn Abi Fudayk (nos. 4319 and 180), grades the report weak and mursal because of the unnamed narrator, and says he found it only in al-Durr al-Manthur (2:159), attributed to no one but Ibn Jarir. Then 9483 (al-Muthanna, from Suwayd b. Nasr, from Ibn al-Mubarak, from Haywa, from Shurahbil b. Sharik, from Abu Abd al-Rahman al-Hubuli) from Abd Allah b. Amr, from the Prophet: the best companions with God are best to their companion, and the best neighbours with God are best to their neighbour. Note 2: Ahmad has it in the Musnad (6566), al-Hakim in the Mustadrak (4:164) and al-Tirmidhi (3:129); the editor quotes his brother Ahmad Shakir that its chain is sound, and says Abu Abd al-Rahman al-Hubuli is Abd Allah b. Yazid al-Ma'afiri, whose biography is at no. 6657 as printed. Al-Tabari then says that since the phrase can bear what was mentioned, covering whoever accompanies another on a journey, in marriage or by attachment, and God did not single any out, the right view is that all are meant and kindness to each was enjoined (his choice). Notes 3-5 on p. 345 and the two on p. 346 are textual: the printed edition dropped a word because it was written confusedly in the manuscript and erred over two others, and the editor says the right readings are those he printed from the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 346}

**The passing traveller and the guest (pp. 346-347, reports 9484-9489).** Al-Tabari says the interpreters differed over the clause on the traveller of the road. Some said he is the traveller who passes by. Al-Hasan b. Yahya (9484, from Abd al-Razzaq, from Ma'mar, from Qatada and Ibn Abi Najih) from Mujahid: the one who passes you travelling; al-Muthanna (printed as 9484 with a further letter, that is a second report under the same number; from Suwayd b. Nasr, from Ibn al-Mubarak, from Ma'mar) from Mujahid and Qatada, likewise; al-Muthanna (9485, from Ishaq, from Ibn Abi Ja'far, from his father) from al-Rabi: the one passing you, even if rich at home. Others said he is the guest. Al-Muthanna (9486, from Abu Hudhayfa, from Shibl, from Ibn Abi Najih) from Mujahid: the guest, who has a right on the road and at home; Bishr b. Mu'adh (9487, from Yazid, from Sa'id) from Qatada: the guest; al-Muthanna (9488, from Amr b. Awn, from Hushaym, from Juwaybir) from al-Dahhak; Yahya b. Abi Talib (9489, from Yazid, from Juwaybir) from al-Dahhak, likewise. Al-Tabari says the right view is that he is the one of the road, the road being the way and its son the one who travels it; he has a claim on whoever he passes in need and cut off, if his journey is not in disobedience of God: help if he needs help, hospitality if he needs it, a mount if he needs one (his choice). The two notes on p. 347 refer to earlier places (the page numbers print unclearly) and explain that the word for beasts of burden means animals on which one is carried. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 347}

**Those owned, and why ownership is tied to the right hand (pp. 347-348, report 9490).** Al-Tabari says the meaning is those whom the addressees own among their slaves, and that tying ownership to the right hand is like saying 'your mouth spoke', 'your foot walked', 'your hand struck', meaning you spoke, walked, struck: the attribute is tied to the limb commonly known for that act among people. So the slaves are under our hands: we feed them from what our right hands pass them, clothe them and direct them with it, and ownership is tied to the right hands. This is al-Tabari's account of the phrasing. Al-Muthanna (9490, from Abu Hudhayfa, from Shibl, from Ibn Abi Najih) from Mujahid: whatever God has entrusted to you; all this God charged. Al-Tabari says Mujahid means the parents, the kin, the orphans, the poor, the neighbour near and far, the companion at one's side and the traveller; God charged all these as kindness to them and commanded His creatures to keep His charge, so keeping God's charge, then His Messenger's, is a duty on them. The notes on p. 348 are textual corrections from the manuscript at three places; the editor says the publisher of the printed edition misread an unpointed sentence and spoiled its sense. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 348}

**The opening of the gloss on the proud one (p. 349, notes 1-4).** Al-Tabari says the sense is that God does not love one who is full of conceit, the word being of the form meaning one who acts the proud man, from a verb for being conceited, and he cites a line of a poet (the editor says Anas b. Musahiq al-Abdi of Abd al-Qays, as printed) and a line of al-Ajjaj: the first speaks of leadership and tells the hearer to go if he is for conceit (described, not reproduced); the second calls conceit a garment of the ignorant. Note 1 says the verb has another form with a different root letter, which some preferred since it is closer to the noun for conceit. Notes 3-4 trace the lines: the first to Abu Tammam's Hamasa (1:133), Abu Ubayda's Majaz al-Qur'an (1:127) and al-Lisan, with lines before it that the editor gives and his vowelling of the imperative in it either way; the second to al-Ajjaj's Diwan (p. 86), the Majaz and al-Lisan, from the additions to the Diwan, with lines after it, and the printed edition's mistake over one word corrected from the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v08 p. 349}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
