## Summary
Verse 282 of [[passage/quran-2|Surat al-Baqara]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 282 of 286 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

([[passage/quran-2-282|2:282]]), pp. 43 to 49. Al-Tabari explains that the address is to those who believe God and His Messenger: when you trade on credit, buy on it, or deal in it to a known time that you fixed among yourselves. This includes the loan and the advance sale (salam), and whatever may be sold in advance becomes a debt on the seller, and also cash sale of property for deferred prices; all are deferred debts when their terms are known. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 43} The editor says the manuscript and print are unintelligible here from copying errors, that he repaired the sentence by conjecture, and explains salam as a payment now for a described good at a known term, giving some jurists' definition of it as a sale of something known and described, owed in the obligation, for a present price. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 43}

Ibn Abbas used to say the verse came down about advance sale specifically. 6317, Ibn Abi Najih from him: advance sale in wheat, in a known measure to a known term. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 43} The editor gives the life of one transmitter (Yahya ibn Isa al-Ramli, d. 201) and the critics' differing words: Abu Dawud heard that Ahmad praised him, Ibn Ma'in said "nothing", al-'Ijli called him reliable, and Ibn 'Adi said most of what he narrates has no corroboration. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 43} 6318 gives the same from Ibn Abbas; the editor could not identify "Yahya ibn al-Samit" in the chain, suggests the pious Yahya ibn Ayyub al-Maqabiri, a teacher of Muslim, without finding text for it, and notes that Ibn Abi Najih's reports from Ibn Abbas are cut since he narrates from the Followers. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 44} 6319 is the same; the editor corrects a name and calls the chain weak because the man from whom Abu Hayyan narrates is unnamed. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 44} 6320, an unnamed man from Ibn Abbas: it came down about salaf in wheat; the editor vouches for Muhammad ibn Muhabbab (Abu Hammam), says al-Mundhiri erred in saying his reports are not used as proof, and weakens the chain for the unnamed man. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 45}

6321, Ibn Abbas (through Qatada from Abu Hassan al-A'raj): I bear witness that guaranteed salaf to a named term is something God made lawful and permitted, and he recited the verse. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 45} The editor documents it in the works of Abd al-Razzaq, al-Shafi'i (al-Umm), al-Hakim and al-Bayhaqi, corrects "Abu Hayyan" to "Abu Hassan", answers al-Dhahabi's remark on Ibrahim ibn Bishar al-Ramadi by citing Ibn Hibban's praise of him and noting that Hisham al-Dastuwa'i, Ma'mar and Shu'ba also narrate it from Qatada, and refers to Ibn Kathir and al-Suyuti; these are the editor's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 45}

Al-Tabari then puts an objection: why does the verse say "with a debt" when the word for contracting debt already implies it, and can there be dealing in debt without a debt? He answers that the Arabs use the verb also for repaying one another and for exchanging taking and giving, so God made clear that the ruling meant is that of debt and not of recompense. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 46} He mentions that some claimed it is emphasis, like the angels all prostrating together ([[passage/quran-15-30|15:30]], [[passage/quran-38-73|38:73]]), and says that has no sense here, which is al-Tabari's view. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 46} The editor says he mended the manuscript by adding one word, points to al-Nahhas's abridged quotation of al-Tabari, and to Abu Hayyan and al-Qurtubi for the saying's fame, adding that he does not know who first said it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 46}

Next, the command to write it down: write the debt contracted to a fixed term, whether a sale or a loan. Scholars differed on writing it for the debtor, whether obligatory or recommended. Some said it is a binding duty. 6322, al-Dahhak: whoever sells to a fixed term is ordered to write, small or large; 6323, Ibn Jurayj: whoever takes a debt should write and whoever sells should call witnesses; 6324, al-Rabi': it was obligatory; 6325, al-Rabi' the same, adding that then the concession and latitude came, citing the clause on trusting one another ([[passage/quran-2-283|2:283]]). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 47} 6326, Qatada: it was mentioned to him that Abu Sulayman al-Mar'ashi, a companion of Ka'b, asked his companions whether they knew a wronged man who prayed and was not answered; he said it is a man who sold something without writing or witness, and when it fell due his counterpart denied it, so he prayed and was not answered, since he had disobeyed his Lord; the editor notes the transmitter's name in the manuscript, Ibn Kathir and al-Bukhari's Kuna. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 48}

Others said writing the debt was obligatory and was abrogated by the ruling that the trusted one return his trust in the next verse ([[passage/quran-2-283|2:283]]). 6327, al-Sha'bi: no harm, if you trust him, in not writing or witnessing; 6328, 'Amir: a concession, so whoever wishes to trust his partner may; 6329, al-Sha'bi: if he trusts him he does not call a witness or write. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 48} 6330, al-Sha'bi: they held that the trust clause abrogated the writing and witnessing before it, a concession and mercy from God; 6331, Ibn Jurayj: someone other than 'Ata' said it abrogated the writing and testimony; 6332, Ibn Zayd: without that statement no one would be allowed to take a debt except by document, witnesses or pledge, and when it came it abrogated all that and the matter returned to trust; 6333, Sulayman al-Taymi asked al-Hasan whether everyone who sells should call witnesses and was pointed to God's word that the trusted one must return his trust. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 49} The editor remarks that no earlier report mentions 'Ata', so a report may have dropped out, and explains that "harf" is used figuratively for a statement. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 49} These reports are given as transmitted, al-Tabari's choices are his, and the editor's notes, documentation and gradings are his, with no museum verdict and no legal ruling.

6334, 'Amir (al-Sha'bi): he recited the passage up to the clause on trusting one another ([[passage/quran-2-283|2:283]]) and said a concession was given, so whoever wishes to trust his companion may. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 50} 6335, al-Sha'bi: if you call witnesses it is prudence, and if you do not you are in latitude and permission. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 50} 6336: Isma'il ibn Abi Khalid asked al-Sha'bi about a man who borrows something from another, must he call a witness; he recited up to the trust clause and said it abrogated what came before. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 50} 6337, Abu Sa'id al-Khudri recited the passage up to the trust clause and said it abrogated what preceded. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 50} The editor sets out the manuscript and print differences in this report, takes its wording from al-Nahhas's book on the abrogating and abrogated, restores "from his father", which the copyists dropped, and gives the transmitters' biographies: Muhammad ibn Marwan al-'Uqayli (Ahmad said he deliberately left his reports, Ibn Ma'in said "no harm", Abu Dawud "truthful"), Abd al-Malik ibn Abi Nadra (Ibn Hibban lists him among the reliable and says he sometimes erred; al-Daraqutni "no harm") and his father al-Mundhir ibn Malik (Ahmad "reliable"; Ibn Sa'd "reliable, much hadith, though not everyone takes him as proof"); all of this is the editor's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 51}

Al-Tabari then explains the clause that a scribe should write between creditor and debtor with truth and fairness in the document he writes, not cutting the right-holder's right, nor establishing a false claim against the debtor, nor imposing on him what he does not owe. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 51} 6338, Qatada: the scribe should fear God in his writing, leaving out no right and adding no falsehood. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 51} As for the prohibition on the scribe refusing, he takes it to mean that one asked to write the debt document must not refuse to write it as God taught him the skill, singling him out with that knowledge over many of His creatures. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 51} The editor has a note on the manuscript and print wording and on the verb "haf" in the lexicons. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 51}

The scholars differed on whether writing is binding on the scribe when asked, like their difference over the creditor. 6339, Mujahid: it is binding on the scribe to write; 6340: Ibn Jurayj asked 'Ata' whether it is binding that he not refuse, and 'Ata' said yes, and Mujahid said it is binding on the scribe; 6341, Mujahid the same; 6342, 'Amir and 'Ata': if there is no scribe and you are called, do not refuse to write for them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 52} Among those who said it is abrogated: 6343, al-Dahhak, that it was a firm duty that the clause forbidding harm to scribe or witness abrogated. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 53} 6344, al-Rabi': this was binding on scribes. Others said it is binding but on the scribe while he is free; 6345, al-Suddi: the scribe does not refuse to write if he is free. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 53}

Abu Ja'far's view is that God ordered those who contract debt for a term to write the debt documents between them and ordered the scribe to write them with justice; God's command is a binding duty unless proof shows it to be guidance and recommendation, and nothing here shows that, so it is an obligation they may not neglect, and whoever neglects it is "harij", that is sinful; the editor refers to earlier notes on that word. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 53} He sees no ground for the plea that the command to write is abrogated by the trust clause, since that was allowed only where there is no way to a document or a scribe; where both exist, the duty is what God ordered. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 53} A ruling abrogates another only when the two cannot hold together in one situation, as he has explained; if neither cancels the other it is not abrogation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 54} He then turns the argument on its head: if the clause on travel without a scribe, the pledge and trust abrogated the command to write, then the verse on dry ablution when water is absent ([[passage/quran-5-6|5:6]]) would abrogate washing with water at home and on the road when water is present, and the fasting for the one who finds no slave in the expiation of zihar ([[passage/quran-58-4|58:4]]) would abrogate freeing a slave ([[passage/quran-58-3|58:3]]); he asks the one who claims abrogation what separates him from someone saying that about dry ablution and the rest. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 54} He anticipates the answer that the trust clause is cut off from the travel clause, whose ruling ended with the pledge, and refers to the term debt, and replies: what proof from a source or an analogy, when the ruling on the debt in which a scribe and document are available has ended with God's word that He teaches you? {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 55} Those who say the commands to write and not to refuse are only guidance and recommendation are asked for proof, confronted with God's other commands, and asked the difference between what they claim here and deny elsewhere, for they can say nothing in one case without having the same imposed on them in the other; this is al-Tabari's choice and argument. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 55} The editor notes the print's distortion of a sentence here and the places where al-Nahhas and al-Qurtubi quote al-Tabari's view in abridged form, and calls this the argument of a godly scholar who knows the meanings of speech; that is the editor's praise. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 55}

Next he begins listing those who said "justice" here means the truth, but the page that follows (p. 56) is visibly blank at this point, and the editor says the copyist dropped al-Tabari's reports with their chains on what the interpreters said "justice" means, and that it cannot be completed until a copy less given to omission is found. This stretch is a stated gap, not a missing page. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 56} Then he explains the next clause: the scribe writes and the debtor takes charge of dictating to the scribe what he owes the owner of the money, and the dictator should fear God and beware of His punishment for lessening his creditor's right wrongfully or by transgression, being taken for it where he can pay only from his good deeds, or by bearing his creditor's wrongs. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 56} 6346, al-Rabi': this too was binding, and he must not wrong his creditor of anything; 6347, Ibn Zayd: he does not reduce anything of the man's right when he dictates. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 56} The editor restores a word dropped by the copyist at the start of the clause. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 56} These reports are as transmitted, al-Tabari's choices and argument are his, and the editor's notes, documentation and gradings are his, with no museum verdict and no legal ruling.

Al-Tabari now explains the clause on a debtor who is foolish, weak or unable to dictate. The foolish person (safih) is for him one who is ignorant of what is right in what he must dictate to the scribe. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 57} 6348, Mujahid: the foolish person is the one ignorant of dictation and of affairs. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 57} Others said the foolish person here is the small child: 6349, al-Suddi: the small one; 6350, al-Dahhak: the small boy, whose guardian dictates with justice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 57} Abu Ja'far holds that the better of the two interpretations is the first, one ignorant of dictation and of where right lies against error, since folly in Arabic speech means ignorance, as he showed earlier; the editor refers to the earlier commentary on the word. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 57}

He takes this to cover any person ignorant of what is right to dictate, young or old, male or female, but says what fits the apparent sense best is every adult, men not under guardianship and women, who does not know where error and right lie in dictating; for the verse opens by addressing those who contract debt, and a child or a person under guardianship may not be contracted with, and because God distinguishes three classes (the foolish, the weak and the one unable to dictate), each with its own description. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 58} So the foolish is one able to dictate who is excused because he does not know what is right; the weak is the one unable to dictate even if sound, because of a stammering tongue or muteness; and the one unable is prevented, either by imprisonment that stops him attending the scribe or by absence from the place of dictating. God lifted the duty of dictating from them for those reasons and commanded the guardian of the right to dictate; so by "his guardian" al-Tabari understands the guardian of the right. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 58} The editor has two notes, on the muddled manuscript wording and on the print's correct repair. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 58}

He rejects the view that the foolish person here is the child and the weak person the dull adult, because it would make "unable to dictate" mean sane men with control of their property who cannot dictate through some defect of the tongue or absence, and that would void the guardian's dictating, since a sane, sound man is not placed under guardianship over his property even if mute or absent, and no one's judgement over his property is valid without his command. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 59} 6351, al-Rabi': the guardian is the guardian of the right; 6352, Ibn Abbas: if he is unable, the owner of the debt dictates with justice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 59} Of those who said the weak is the dull-witted and the guardian is the guardian of the foolish and the weak: 6353, al-Dahhak, that the guardian of the foolish or the weak is ordered to dictate with justice; 6354, al-Suddi, that the weak is the dull-witted; 6355, Mujahid, the same; 6356, Ibn Zayd: one who does not know, so as to confirm his right for the other, and is ignorant of it; his guardian stands in his place until he gives this one his right. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 60} Al-Tabari says he has shown the better interpretation, and that "with justice" here means with the truth. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 60}

Then the command to call witnesses: seek two witnesses for your rights, and one says "so-and-so is my witness (shahid / shahid) over this property"; the editor refers to the earlier commentary on the root. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 60} "Of your men" means for him your free Muslims, not your slaves and not your free unbelievers; 6357 and 6358, Mujahid: the free. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 61} If two men are not found, then a man and two women. Al-Tabari gives the grammar: the man and the two women are in the nominative by return to the verb of being, or on the sense "let a man and two women witness", or "it is a man and two women", and the accusative is also allowed on the sense "call a man and two women"; all are permissible. The editor says most of this wording is from al-Farra's Ma'ani al-Qur'an and that the manuscript and print both gave the accusative, and he chose the better form. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 61} As for the witnesses approved, they are the upright whose religion and rectitude are accepted; 6359, al-Rabi': this is in debt, and they are upright; 6360, al-Dahhak: God ordered them to call men of justice from among their men. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 62}

He then comes to the clause on one of the two women erring and the other reminding her, on which the readers differed. Most of the people of the Hijaz and Medina and some Iraqis read it with a fatha on the particle and both verbs in the subjunctive, meaning the man and two women stand as witnesses so that one may remind the other if she errs, which they treat as an advanced phrase whose sense is delayed; and they explained the subjunctive of the second verb by saying the conditional, being first, attaches to what precedes it and its answer is turned back to it, as one says "it pleases me that the asker asks and is given", meaning it pleases me that the asker be given if he asks, since what pleases is the giving and not the asking. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 62} Others read the same but with a sukun on the dhal and a lightened kaf, and they differ among themselves on what that reading means. Some took it to mean that she makes the other a male by their joining: her testimony joined to her companion's is accepted like the testimony of one male in debts, since each alone is not accepted, so their joint testimony stands as one male's. They relied on the Arab saying that someone's mother "bore him a male" and that a woman who bears sons is "mudhkir". This is reported from Sufyan ibn 'Uyayna. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 63} The editor notes the comparison with al-Farra's Ma'ani al-Qur'an and corrects the manuscript and print wording. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 63} These reports are given as transmitted, al-Tabari's choices and arguments are his, and the editor's notes are his, with no museum verdict and no legal ruling.

6361, Abu Ubayd al-Qasim ibn Sallam, reporting from Sufyan ibn 'Uyayna: the sense of reminding is not remembrance after forgetting but comes from "male", so that when she testifies with the other their testimony is like a male's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 64} Others among those who read it that way took it as remembrance after forgetting. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 64} Others read it with a kasra on the particle and the second verb raised and doubled, as a fresh statement of what the two women do: if one forgets her testimony the other reminds her, cut off from what precedes; on this reader's meaning, seek two witnesses, and if two men are not found, a man and two women of the approved, and if one of the two errs the other reminds her. This was the reading of al-A'mash and those who took it from him; he put the first verb in the jussive after the conditional particle, moving the vowel to the lightest when one lam was absorbed into the other, and raised the second verb with fa as the answer to the condition. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 64} The editor has notes on manuscript and print differences in these passages. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 65}

Abu Ja'far's choice is the reading of those who open the particle, double the kaf and put the last letter in the subjunctive, meaning a man and two women are to witness so that if one errs the other reminds her; the second verb is subjunctive by conjunction with the first, and the particle is opened because it takes the place of "so that", which suffices, while the second verb is joined on to show that what stands in for the governing word has indicated its sense and its effect. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 65} He chose it because the proof of the early and later readers agree on it and al-A'mash and his followers stood alone, and one may not leave a reading that Muslims have transmitted widely for another. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 65} He prefers the doubled form because it means a repeated reminding from one to the other, informing her that she forgot so that she may remember; this is al-Tabari's choice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 65} The editor says the Bulaq print is meaningless at this point and the manuscript unpointed, so he repaired the reading and added the word "she forgot" in brackets. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 65}

Al-Tabari judges the interpretation reported from Ibn 'Uyayna to be a mistaken one without sense, for several reasons: it contradicts all the interpreters; it is known that one woman's erring in testimony is her losing it and forgetting it, like a man's erring in his religion when he is perplexed and turns from the truth, so how can the other become a male with her when she has forgotten and strayed, and the straying one needs reminding more than making male. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 66} He then allows that Ibn 'Uyayna may have meant that the remembering woman, when her companion is too weak to recall, sharpens and strengthens her until she is like a man in the strength to recall what she was too weak to; as one calls a strong thing in its work "male", a sword that cuts well "a male sword" and a man "male" for being effective, strong of grip and sound of resolve. If this is his meaning, it is one approach to the interpretation and returns to al-Tabari's own, though the reading differs from the chosen one. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 66} He says he knows no one who interpreted it so and preferred the reading with the lightened form, so the right reading is what he chose. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 67} All of this is al-Tabari's judgement and choice; the editor notes the differences of manuscript and print and the origin of the verb for sharpening in the lexicons. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 66}

Then he lists those who explained the clause as he did. 6362, Qatada: God knew rights would arise, so He took reliability from some of them from others; take God's reliability, for it is more obedient to your Lord and better protects your property; if the debtor is God-fearing, the writing only adds good, and if wicked, he is likelier to pay when he knows witnesses are against him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 67} 6363, al-Rabi': one forgets and the other reminds; 6364, al-Suddi: one forgets the testimony and the other reminds her; 6365, al-Dahhak, the same. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 68} 6366, Ibn Zayd: both are dialects and are the same, and we read with the doubled form. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 68}

Next he sets out the interpreters' disagreement on the situation in which God forbade the witnesses to refuse when called. One group: it means they must not refuse to respond when called to witness a document and rights; 6367, Qatada: a man would go round a large settlement with people in it and call them to witness and none would follow him, and Qatada took the verse to mean witnessing for one man against another; 6368, al-Rabi' the same, and that God sent the verse down for that; 6369, Qatada: do not refuse to witness when called to a testimony. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 68} The editor explains the word for "settlement" as houses of people gathered at water. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 68} Others said the same but that it is binding on the one called when no other can be found, while if another is found he is free to answer or not; 6370, al-Sha'bi: if he wishes he witnesses, and if no other is found he witnesses. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 69} Others: it means when they are called to witness against the person the caller intends and to give the testimony they have; 6371, al-Hasan: giving it and witnessing; 6372, al-Hasan: it combines two matters, do not refuse to witness when you hold testimony, and do not refuse when called to a testimony. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 69} For the transmitter Abu Amir in 6371 (Salih ibn Rustum al-Muzani) the editor gives Ibn Ma'in's "weak", Ahmad's "fair in hadith", Abu Hatim's "a shaykh whose hadith is written" and Abu Dawud's "reliable", which are the editor's quotations. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 69} 6373, Ibn Abbas: a Muslim who is needed gives the testimony he has and may not refuse when called; 6374, al-Hasan: it is for giving it, not for beginning it, whether he called him to witness or to give it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 70} Others: it means when they are called to give the testimony they hold for the caller; 6375, Mujahid: once he has witnessed; 6376: if they had witnessed before; 6377: if they have been made witnesses; 6378: if you hold testimony and are called. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v06 p. 70} We stop at 6378; report 6379 begins at the foot of p. 70. These reports are given as transmitted, al-Tabari's choices and arguments are his, and the editor's notes and biographies are his, with no museum verdict and no legal ruling.
