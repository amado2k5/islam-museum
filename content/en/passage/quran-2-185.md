## In al-Tabari's commentary

Al-Tabari says that "shahr" (month) is, it is said, from "ishtihar", being made public: one says "so-and-so unsheathed (shahara) his sword" when he drew it from its scabbard, and "the month appeared" when its new moon rose. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 444} Some people with knowledge of Arabic claimed that "Ramadan" was so called for the intensity of the heat in it, when the young camels' feet would burn on the sand; Mujahid disliked saying "Ramadan" alone, saying perhaps it is one of the names of God, and so it is said "the month of Ramadan" as in the verse. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 444} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 445} He explains that "month" is in the nominative as the predicate of "a limited number of days", or in the sense "that is the month of Ramadan" or "the month of Ramadan has been prescribed for you", and gives the grammatical ways of the accusative for those who read it so. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 445}

On "in which the Qur'an was sent down", al-Tabari reports that it came down on the Night of Qadr from the Preserved Tablet to the lowest heaven and was then sent to Muhammad as God willed. He relates from Ibn 'Abbas that the Qur'an was sent down all at once from the Remembrance on the twenty-fourth night of Ramadan and placed in the House of Might, and from Sa'id ibn Jubayr that it came down at once on the Night of Qadr in Ramadan and was placed in the lowest heaven. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 445} He relates a report from Wathila ibn al-Asqa' from the Prophet that the scrolls of Abraham came down on the first night of Ramadan, the Torah after six nights had passed, the Gospel after thirteen, and the Qur'an on the twenty-fourth; the editor notes in a footnote that its chain is sound and traces it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 446} From al-Suddi on Ibn 'Abbas, the blessed night is the Night of Qadr and the Qur'an came down all at once to the Frequented House in the lowest heaven, and then came down on Muhammad in pieces, in commands and prohibitions and wars. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 446} The same is reported from 'Ikrima on Ibn 'Abbas, with an addition that from its first to its last was twenty years, and from al-Sha'bi "it has reached us that it came down all at once to the lowest heaven". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 446} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 447} From Ibn Jurayj on Ibn 'Abbas, the Qur'an was sent down all at once to Gabriel on the Night of Qadr, and he sent down only what he was commanded. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 447}

In a report from Miqsam on Ibn 'Abbas, a man told him doubt had entered his heart over "the month of Ramadan in which the Qur'an was sent down" and over its being sent down, in two other places, on a blessed night and on the Night of Qadr, when it was also sent down in Shawwal, Dhu al-Qa'da and other months; he replied that it was sent down in Ramadan on the Night of Qadr, the blessed night, all at once, and then sent down in stages through the months and days. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 448} On "guidance for the people", al-Tabari says it is direction for the people to the path of truth and the straight way; "clear proofs of guidance" are clear explanations pointing to God's limits, obligations and what is lawful and forbidden; and "the Criterion" is the separation of truth from falsehood; from al-Suddi, "clear proofs of the lawful and forbidden". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 448}

On "whoever of you witnesses the month, let him fast it", he reports the commentators' differing views of what "witnessing the month" means. Some said it is the residence of one who is settled at home: whoever is a resident when Ramadan begins must fast the whole month, even if he later travels. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 449} This is related from Ibn 'Abbas in al-Dahhak's report ("its new moon appearing while he is at home"); in another report from him, whoever witnesses it as a resident must fast whether he stays or travels, and whoever witnesses it while travelling may fast or break the fast as he wishes. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 449} From 'Ubayda al-Salmani: if you witnessed its start, fast its end; from al-Suddi the same; from 'Ali (as Hammad supposes), that whoever reaches Ramadan as a resident is bound to fast; and from Ibrahim: if Ramadan reaches you do not travel in it, and if you fasted a day or two and then travelled, do not break the fast. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 449} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 450} In a report from Abu al-Bakhtari on 'Ubayda, whoever fasted part of it in the town should fast the rest when he goes out, and Ibn 'Abbas used to say he may fast or break the fast as he wishes. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 450} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 451} From Umm Dharra, 'A'isha told her to tell her brother, who was bidding farewell to travel, to stay in Ramadan, saying that if Ramadan caught her on the road she would stay for it; and from Ibrahim ibn Talha, she told him, when he wanted to make the 'umra, "sit until you have broken the fast, then go". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 451} The editor in footnotes gives the circumstances of some narrators and traces the reports. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 450} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 451}

Others said the sense is: whoever witnesses the month, let him fast what he witnessed of it. From Abu Maysara, he set out in Ramadan and, on reaching the bridge, asked for water, drank and broke the fast; in another report he drank a handful from the Euphrates and broke the fast; and in Murthid's report he went out with Abu Maysara and he broke the fast at the bridge. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 452} The editor in a footnote disputes al-Tabari's remark that Hannad erred in the narrator's name and judges otherwise. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 452}

More reports on the traveller breaking the fast: from al-Hasan ibn Sa'd on his father, who was with 'Ali ibn Abi Talib on his land; 'Ali fasted and told the narrator, who was on foot while 'Ali rode, to break the fast; from al-Sha'bi, that he travelled in Ramadan and broke the fast at the bridge gate; from Sufyan, "I prefer that you complete it"; from al-Hakam and Hammad, who told Shu'ba to go out; from Ibrahim, "when it is the last ten, I prefer that he stay"; and from al-Hasan and Sa'id ibn al-Musayyab, on one whom Ramadan reached as a resident and who then travelled: if he wishes he breaks the fast. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 453}

Others said "whoever of you witnesses the month, let him fast it" means whoever witnesses it of sound mind, adult and charged with the duty. Al-Tabari names Abu Hanifa and his companions among those who said this: they held that whoever Ramadan reaches healthy, sane and adult owes its fast; if he goes mad after it begins and recovers after it has ended, he must make up the days he lost his mind in, and likewise if it reaches him while mad and he recovers a day or more before it ends, he makes up the month except the day he fasted after recovering; and if he does not recover until the whole month has passed, he owes no making up, since he did not witness it as one charged with the fast. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 454} Al-Tabari rejects this as an interpretation that has no sense: if madness removed the duty because of the loss of reason for the whole month, then that would have to be the way for anyone who lost his reason for the whole month, and he states that all agree that one who loses his reason for the whole month through fainting or delirium and recovers after it ends must make up the whole month; he concludes that the verse's meaning is not what the holders of this view said. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 454}

Al-Tabari also holds that the view that whoever witnesses the start of the month as a resident must fast all of it is yet more invalid, because of the accumulated reports that the Prophet left Medina in Ramadan in the year of the conquest after fasting part of it, broke the fast and told his companions to break it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 454} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 455} He relates from Ibn 'Abbas that the Prophet travelled from Medina to Mecca in Ramadan and, reaching 'Usfan, called for a vessel, put it on his hand for the people to see, and drank; and from Ibn 'Abbas that he set out when ten nights of Ramadan had passed, fasted with the people fasting with him, until he reached al-Kadid and broke the fast. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 455} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 456} From Abu Sa'id al-Khudri: "we went out with the Prophet when eighteen nights had passed, and among us were those fasting and those breaking the fast, and the one breaking did not find fault with the one fasting nor the one fasting with the one breaking". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 456} The editor traces these reports in the footnotes. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 455} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 456} Al-Tabari concludes that the first two interpretations are invalid and that the sound one is the third: whoever of you witnesses the month, let him fast all of it that he witnesses as a resident, and whoever is ill or travelling, a number of other days. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 456}

On "whoever is ill or on a journey, a number of other days", he says: whoever is ill or travelling in the month and breaks the fast owes the number of days he broke from days other than those of Ramadan. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 457} He then reports the scholars' differences on the illness for which God allowed breaking the fast. Some said it is the illness in which the sufferer cannot stand to pray: from al-Hasan and Ibrahim, a sick person who cannot pray standing may break the fast, and from al-Hasan that he breaks it when the fast strains him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v03 p. 457}
