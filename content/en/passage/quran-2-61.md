## In al-Tabari's commentary
On "and when you said: O Moses, we will not endure one kind of food" al-Tabari explains that "sabr" is holding the soul back from something, so the sense is: we cannot bring ourselves to keep to one food, which was what God gave them in the wilderness, the quail, so ask your Lord to bring forth for us of what the earth grows. On why they asked, he reports from Qatada that in the wilderness they had been shaded by the cloud and given manna and quail, tired of it and recalled their life in Egypt, so asked Moses and were told to go down to a town; from Abu al-Aliya that their food was the quail and their drink the manna; from Ibn Zayd that their drink was honey from the sky and their food a bird, and they knew no bread or anything else; and from Mujahid and Ibn Abi Najih that they wanted to exchange the manna and quail for herbs and what was named with them. He adds that "min" in "of what the earth grows" is partitive. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 124} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 125} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 126}

Al-Tabari notes that some took "min" in "of what the earth grows" as redundant, citing the Arab saying "I saw not from anyone", while a group of grammarians denied that and said its presence shows that part of what is named is meant; so for him the sense is: ask your Lord to bring forth for us some of what the earth grows of its herbs and cucumbers. Herbs, cucumbers, lentils and onions are well known. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 126} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 127}
On "fum" the commentators differ: some say wheat and bread, from Ata, Mujahid, Qatada, al-Hasan, Abu Malik, al-Suddi, Ibn Zayd and Ibn Abbas, and from Ibn Abbas that in the tongue of Banu Hashim it is wheat, citing a line of Uhayha ibn al-Julah, with the editor remarking on the weakness of the chain of some of these reports. Others say it is garlic, from Mujahid and al-Rabi', and in some readings it is "thumiha", attributed by some to Ibn Mas'ud. Al-Tabari holds that calling wheat and bread "fum" belongs to an old dialect, and that if the reading with "tha" is sound it is one of the interchanged letters, since "fa" and "tha" are close in articulation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 127} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 128} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 129} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 130}
On "would you exchange what is lower for what is better" he says Moses asked them whether they would take the baser in worth and value of life in place of what is higher; "istibdal" is leaving one thing for another in its place, and "adna" means baser, lower and smaller in worth, though some took it as "nearer". He reports the sense of baser from Qatada and others, and notes the dialect form with hamza in "dani'". For him there is no doubt that whoever exchanges manna and quail for herbs, cucumbers, lentils, onions and garlic exchanges a humble life for a higher one. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 130} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 131}

On "go down to a town, for you shall have what you asked" al-Tabari holds that the sentence omits "so Moses prayed and We answered him and said", and that "hubut" means descending to a place and settling there; he reports from Mujahid that "adna" means worse. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 131} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 132}
He then records the reciters' differences: most read "misran" with tanwin, meaning a town among towns, since they were in the desert and what they asked for is found only in villages and towns, though some who gave it tanwin may have meant the Egypt they had left, following the script of the codex; some read it without tanwin and plainly meant Egypt itself. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 132} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 133}
The commentators also differ on what was meant: Qatada, al-Suddi, Mujahid and Ibn Zayd say a town among towns, Ibn Zayd adding that it was the Holy Land, and Mujahid that they never went back to Egypt; Abu al-Aliya and al-Rabi' say it was Pharaoh's Egypt. The first group argue that God made Syria the Israelites' dwelling after they left Egypt, that the wilderness came from their refusing to fight the giants, and that He never said He returned them to Egypt; the others cite the verses in al-Shu'ara and al-Dukhan that God made them inherit gardens and springs, and the readings attributed to Ubayy ibn Ka'b and Ibn Mas'ud without the alif. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 133} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 134} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 135}
Al-Tabari's own choice is that neither the Book of God nor any report from the Messenger shows which view is right, and the better course is to say that Moses asked his Lord for what they asked of the earth's produce and was answered and told to take his people down to a settled land that would grow it, which could be Egypt or Syria. As to the reading, it is with alif and tanwin, because the scripts of the Muslims' codices agree and the reciters concur. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 135} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 136}

On "and humiliation and poverty were set upon them" al-Tabari explains that "duribat" means imposed, laid and made binding upon them, as one says the ruler imposed the poll tax on the protected communities; "dhilla" is lowness and abasement, the same abasement mentioned in the verse of surah al-Tawba about paying the tax by hand in humility, which he reports from al-Hasan and Qatada. "Maskana" is the poverty of want and need, which Abu al-Aliya calls want and al-Suddi poverty; Ibn Zayd says those meant are the Jews of the Children of Israel, not the Copts of Egypt. He says God reports that He replaced their honour with humiliation, their favour with misery and His pleasure with anger as recompense for their rejecting His signs and killing His prophets without right. This is al-Tabari's reading of what the verse says of the generation it addresses, given here as his interpretation and not as a judgement of the museum on anyone. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 136} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 137}
On "and they returned with anger from God" he says "ba'u" means they went back, bearing it; the word is used only with a good or ill attached, as in the verse in surah al-Ma'ida "that you bear my sin and yours"; so the sense is they returned bearing God's anger, and he reports from al-Rabi' and al-Dahhak that they deserved the anger. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 138}
On "that was because they disbelieved in God's signs and killed the prophets without right" he says "that" points to the imposing of humiliation and poverty and the coming of anger, and the "ba'" gives the cause, citing al-A'sha; "kufr" means covering and concealing a thing, and God's signs are His proofs and marks of His oneness and the truth of His messengers. He then discusses the word "nabi", saying its root is hamza from "anba'a", the plural being "anbiya'" or "nuba'a", and records the view that it comes from "nabwa", a raised place, with a line of al-Qutami. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 139} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 140} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 141}

Al-Tabari adds that "killing the prophets without right" means killing God's messengers without His leave, denying their mission. In "that was because they disobeyed and were transgressors" the second "that" refers back to the first: what befell them was for their disbelief and killing of prophets and for their disobedience and transgression, "i'tida'" being to exceed the limit God set for His servants and pass to another. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 142}
