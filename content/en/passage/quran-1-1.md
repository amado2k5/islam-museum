## Summary
Verse 1 of [[passage/quran-1|Surat al-Fatiha]], a Meccan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 1 of 7 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

## In al-Tabari's commentary
Al-Tabari opens the commentary on al-Fatiha, headed in the edition "the account of the interpretation of the names of the Opening of the Book" though the running page head still reads "Introduction", with a report he says is sound, through Abu Hurayra: the surah is the mother of the Qur'an, the Opening of the Book and the seven oft-repeated verses. The editor traces the hadith to the Musnad, al-Bukhari, Ibn Kathir and al-Suyuti. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 107}
He explains the first name: the surah is called the Opening of the Book because the copies of the Qur'an are begun by writing it and the prayers are begun by reciting it, so it opens what follows both in writing and in recitation. It is called "mother of the Qur'an" because it comes before the other surahs, and the Arabs call anything that gathers or leads a matter a "mother": the skin that holds the brain is the "mother of the head", and the banner an army gathers under is a "mother"; he cites a line of Dhu al-Rumma about a banner tied to a spear that his companions rally to, notes that Mecca was called "mother of the towns" because it came first or because the earth was spread out from it, and gives a line in which fifty is called a "mother" because it holds the numbers below it. The editor notes that this last line belongs to Abu Muhammad al-Taymi, not to Humayd ibn Thawr, that the wording "your mother" is found only in al-Tabari, and that the manuscript and the printed text differ in places. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 107} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 108}
On "the Seven": all readers and scholars agree it has seven verses; they differ on how the seven are made up. Most of the people of Kufa count the basmala as a verse, which is reported from a group of Companions and Followers; most of the careful readers of Medina do not, and count "You have favoured them" as the seventh. He says he has stated the position he holds in his short book on the rulings of Islamic law and will set out the different views at length in his larger one, and so does not decide the question here. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 109}
On "the oft-repeated" in [[passage/quran-15-87|15:87]]: it is so called because its recitation is repeated in every prayer, obligatory or voluntary, which is how al-Hasan al-Basri took it. Al-Tabari gives a report in which al-Hasan was asked about the verse, said it was the Fatiha, recited the whole surah and said it is repeated in every reading or every prayer (the narrator doubts the wording). He cites lines by Abu al-Najm al-Ijli and another rajaz poet that pair "the oft-repeated" with the Qur'an and the mother of the Book. He adds that naming the Fatiha "the seven oft-repeated" does not rule out naming the whole Qur'an, or the surahs that follow the long ones, "oft-repeated", since each name has its own sense, and promises to show the sense of the whole Qur'an being so called at Surat al-Zumar. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 109} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 110}
He then turns to "the account of the interpretation of seeking refuge". Seeking refuge means asking protection, so "I seek refuge in God from the accursed Satan" means: I ask God alone, and none of His creation, to protect me from Satan, lest he harm me in my religion or turn me from a right I owe my Lord. In Arabic usage a shaytan is any rebel among jinn, humans, animals or anything else; he cites [[passage/quran-6-112|6:112]], where human devils are set beside jinn, and the saying of Umar ibn al-Khattab, who got off a horse that strutted under him, remarking that they had set him only on a devil until he disliked himself. The editor adds that Ibn Kathir quotes the report and calls its chain sound. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 111}
Al-Tabari says a rebel is called shaytan because his character and acts differ from those of his kind and he is far from good. He also gives a view that the word comes from "my house is distant from yours", citing a line of al-Nabigha and one of Umayya ibn Abi al-Salt, and argues from Umayya's use of "shatin" instead of "sha'it" that the root is "shatana", not "shata". "Rajim" is a form with the sense of a passive, like "a dyed hand" or "an oiled beard": the cursed and reviled one; the root means throwing, in speech or in deed, and he cites the threat to throw stones in [[passage/quran-19-46|19:46]] by Abraham's father. It may also be that Satan is so called because God drove him from His heavens and struck at him with shooting stars. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 111} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 112}
He reports from Ibn Abbas that when Gabriel first came to the Prophet he taught him to seek refuge, with the words seeking refuge in the All-Hearing, All-Knowing from the accursed Satan, then the basmala, then the opening of [[passage/quran-96-1|96:1]], and that this was the first surah God sent down in Gabriel's speech; so he was told to seek refuge in God, not in creatures. The editor records Ibn Kathir's judgement that the report is strange and weak and cut in its chain, and sets out the criticisms of Bishr ibn Umara and the dispute over whether al-Dahhak heard from Ibn Abbas. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 113}
The section "the interpretation of In the name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful" opens with the word "in the name". Al-Tabari says God taught His Prophet to put the mention of His beautiful names before all his acts and made this a practice for His creatures, who begin their speech, letters, books and needs with it, so that what is said ("in the name of God") stands for what is left unsaid. The letter "bi" calls for a verb that has no visible form, and the listener's understanding makes it unnecessary, as when someone asked "What did you eat today?" answers "food" without repeating "I ate". So a person who recites a surah after the basmala means "I recite in the name of God", and a person who stands or sits means "I stand, I sit, in His name", and likewise for other acts. He gives the report from Ibn Abbas that Gabriel taught the Prophet to say it, meaning "recite with the mention of your Lord, and stand and sit with His mention". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 114} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 115}
He then raises an objection: if every reader and every person who stands acts by God's help, why not "by God, the Compassionate, the Merciful", since "in the name of God" might suggest that the act is done by something other than God? His answer is that the sense is not what the objector imagines: it means "I begin with naming God and mentioning Him before everything", or "I recite while naming God", not "I recite by God". To the further question why "name" (ism) is used when the naming (tasmiya) is the verbal noun, he replies that the Arabs often give verbal nouns the forms of other nouns ("I honoured him, karama", "I spoke to him, kalam", "I humiliated him, hawan"), citing lines by al-Qutami and others, one of which the editor could not trace, and one by al-Harith ibn Khalid al-Makhzumi, with the point that such nouns take the same object as the verb. The editor points to the story behind the last line. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 115} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 116}
So, on al-Tabari's account, "name" is put in the place of "naming", as speech is put for speaking and giving for the act of giving, and "in the name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful" at the start of a recitation means "I recite, beginning with the naming of God". He repeats Ibn Abbas's report with its chain as support and holds that it also refutes the view that the sense is "by God, the Compassionate, the Merciful, before everything". The editor adds a long note that in "in the name of God" the "name" is a noun of action standing for the naming. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 117} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 118}
His second argument is that people are commanded to begin by naming God, not by reporting His greatness and attributes, as in the naming required over a slaughtered animal, game, and food and drink. There is no disagreement among the community's scholars that a man who says "by God" at the slaughter, without "in the name of God", has not done what was laid down for him; this shows that "in the name of God" does not mean "by God", and refutes the view that the name of God is God Himself. He says this is not the place to lengthen the discussion of whether the name is the thing named, other than it, or an attribute of it; it is only the place to settle whether the name here is a noun or a verbal noun meaning naming. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 118}
He then answers an objection from a line by Labid ibn Rabi'a, said to his two daughters about mourning him for a year, which an early authority on Arabic (the editor identifies him as Abu Ubayda Ma'mar ibn al-Muthanna, in his book on figurative language in the Qur'an) read as "then the name of peace" meaning simply "peace". Al-Tabari answers that on that logic one could say "I saw the name of Zayd" or "I ate the name of the food", which all Arabs rule out, and asks the holders of that view whether they would allow "I ate the name of honey" for "I ate honey"; if yes, they leave the Arabic tongue, and if no, they can give no difference. He then offers two readings of Labid's line that differ from theirs. The first is that "peace" is one of God's names, so that the sense is "then hold to the name of God and the mention of Him, and leave the mention of me and the weeping for me", an urging, with the noun raised because the urging word comes after, and he cites a line of rajaz as a parallel. The editor notes that here al-Tabari is severe on Abu Ubayda. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 119} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 120}
Al-Tabari gives the second reading of Labid's line: "then my naming of God over you", like someone who sees something he admires and says "the name of God be on you", protecting it from harm. In his note the editor judges the first reading the likelier, since Labid had told his daughters to mourn him for a year and after the year a farewell greeting would have no sense, so the context supports al-Tabari. He then asks those who follow Abu Ubayda's reading whether they hold these two readings possible; if not, they show how little they know of the ways of Arabic speech, and if so, what proof makes their reading right rather than these? They have none. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 121}
He then mentions a report, raised to the Prophet through Ibn Mas'ud and Abu Sa'id, in which Jesus, handed by his mother to the teacher, was told to write "bism" and explained its letters as attributes of God. The editor states that this report is fabricated and has no basis: it passes through Isma'il ibn Yahya, whom the critics abandoned as a forger, an unnamed narrator, and the weak Atiyya al-Awfi. Al-Tabari himself fears the narrator slipped, meaning the letters as children are taught them in the school, and says the interpretation makes no sense applied to the basmala as it is recited in the Book of God. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 121} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 122}
Under "the interpretation of the word Allah" he says its sense, as reported from Ibn Abbas, is the One whom everything worships and every creature serves: the possessor of godhead and of being worshipped by all His creation. Asked whether the word has a root in a verb of the pattern "fa'ila yaf'alu", he answers that this comes not from what was heard from the Arabs but from inference: all Arabs agree that one may say of a man devoted to worship and to seeking what is with God that he "ta'allaha", and he cites a line of Ru'ba ibn al-Ajjaj in which the poet speaks of his own devotion. So "ta'alluh" is the reflexive form of "alaha", meaning to worship. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 122} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 123}
He supports this with two reports from Ibn Abbas and Mujahid on a reading of [[passage/quran-7-127|7:127]] with "ilahataka", meaning "your worship", and the remark that Pharaoh was worshipped and did not worship; they show that "alaha" means "worshipped" and "ilaha" is its verbal noun. The editor notes that both chains are weak because of Sufyan ibn Waki', that the known reading is "alihataka" (your gods), and that the reading al-Tabari gives is recorded in books of unusual readings from Ibn Mas'ud, Ibn Abbas and others. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 123} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 124}
Asked what one should say when reporting that God has made this due from His servant, he says he has no report on it, but by analogy with the same raised report (the one in which Jesus says Allah is the God of the gods, which the editor again calls fabricated) one may say that God "alaha" the servant and the servant "alaha" through Him, and that "Allah" in Arabic derives from "al-ilah". To the objection that the two forms differ, he answers that [[passage/quran-18-38|18:38]] likewise derives from "but I, He is God my Lord", where the hamza of "ana" is dropped and the two nuns merge, and gives a line whose poet is unknown to the editor; in the same way the hamza of "al-ilah" falls and the two lams merge. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 125} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 126}
In "the interpretation of the Compassionate, the Merciful" he says "rahman" has the pattern of "fa'lan" and "rahim" that of "fa'il", both from "rahima"; the Arabs build "fa'lan" names such as angry, drunk, thirsty, and use "fa'il" where praise or blame is meant, as in "knowing" and "able". To the objection that if both come from mercy the repetition is pointless, he replies that each word has a sense the other does not carry; in grammar, scholars agree that "rahman" departs further from the verb's pattern than "rahim", and whatever departs further shows the bearer's greater excellence in praise or blame. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 126} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 127}
From the reports there is disagreement among interpreters: from al-Arzami that "rahman" is for all creatures and "rahim" for the believers (the editor says al-Arzami is very weak), and from the report attributed to Jesus that "rahman" is of this world and the next and "rahim" of the next, which comes through the weak chains named above. Al-Tabari holds that both reports show a difference between the two names, though they differ on the nature of the difference, one placing it in this world and the other in the next, and then poses the question of which interpretation is sounder. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 127}

We stop here at the foot of p. 127, at the question which of the two interpretations of "the Compassionate, the Merciful" is sounder; the next batch begins at p. 128. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 127}
