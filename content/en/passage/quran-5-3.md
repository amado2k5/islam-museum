## Summary
Verse 3 of [[passage/quran-5|Surat al-Ma'ida]], a Medinan surah. Scholars connect its revelation to [[event/farewell-sermon-at-arafat|Farewell Sermon at Arafat]]. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 3 of 120 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

Al-Tabari then turns to the third verse, the one naming what God forbade: the carrion, blood, pig's flesh and what was dedicated to other than God. He says: God forbade you, believers, the carrion; 'carrion' is every creature with flowing blood, of land beasts and birds, that God permitted to be eaten, tame or wild, whose life left it without slaughter. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 492}

On 'and the flesh of swine', the sense is that God forbade you the flesh of swine, the tame and the wild. Carrion and blood are general in outward form but particular in meaning; the flesh of swine is wholly forbidden inside and out, with nothing excepted. On 'what was dedicated to other than God', the sense is what was named on with other than God's name; the word comes from the cry of a newborn at birth, and from the call of the pilgrim who utters the talbiya; it means what was slaughtered for the gods and idols. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 493}

On 'the strangled', the commentators differ on how it happens: some say the animal puts its head between two branches of a tree and is strangled; report 11002, from al-Suddi: the one that puts its head between two branches of a tree and is strangled and dies; report 11003, from Qatada: the one that dies in its strangling. Others say it is tied and strangled by its tether; report 11004, from al-Dahhak: the sheep is tied and killed by its tether. Others say it is a beast of cattle the idolaters strangled until it died; report 11005, from Ibn Abbas, and report 11006, from Qatada, the same. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 494}

Al-Tabari prefers the view that the strangled is the one that is choked, whether in its tether or by putting its head where it cannot free itself and is choked to death; since the word describes it as strangled, not as strangled by another, which would be the strangled-one form. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 495}

On 'the beaten to death', from the verb meaning to strike until one is near death: report 11007, from Ibn Abbas: the one beaten with wood until it dies; report 11008, from Qatada: the idolaters used to strike it with sticks, and when it died they ate it; report 11009, from Qatada through Shu'ba: they struck it until it died and then ate it; report 11010, from Ma'mar through Qatada: the one beaten until it dies; report 11011, from al-Dahhak: the one beaten until it dies; report 11012, from al-Suddi: the one struck until it dies. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 496}

Report 11013, from al-Dahhak: the animal of cattle or other, struck with wood for their gods until they killed it and ate it; report 11014, from Abu Abd Allah al-Sunabihi: the beaten is only in the case of livestock, and there is no beaten in game. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 497}

On 'the fallen', it means the one that falls from a high place, from a mountain or into a well or otherwise; falling is throwing oneself from high to low. Report 11015, from Ibn Abbas: the one that falls from the mountain; report 11016, from Qatada: the one that falls into the well and they eat it; report 11017, from Qatada: the one that falls into the well; report 11018, from al-Suddi: the one that falls from the mountain or into a well and dies; report 11019, from al-Dahhak: the one that falls into a well or from the top of a mountain and dies. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 498}

On 'the gored', it is the sheep gored by another so that it dies from the goring without slaughter; God forbade it to the believers unless they reach its slaughter before it dies. The word is a form of 'the gored one' changed from the passive to the form fa'ila. Some Basran grammarians keep the feminine marker because the word became like a noun, like tariqa and tawila, as if the gored were taken to mean the goring one. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 499}

Al-Tabari explains the feminine ending of 'the gored': when the Arabs drop the noun modified by a fa'ila formed from a passive, they keep the feminine marker so it is known to be an attribute of a feminine; hence the marker is kept in 'the gored'; without it one could not tell if it were masculine or feminine. He holds this to be the soundest view, since the commentators widely say it means 'the struck'. Report 11021, from Ibn Abbas: a sheep that gores a sheep; report 11022, from Abu Maysara, who read 'the gored' with the passive; report 11023, from al-Dahhak: two sheep butt each other and both die; report 11024, from al-Suddi: the one gored by sheep or cattle and dying. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 500}

Report 11025, from Qatada: two rams butted, one died, and they ate it; report 11026, the same; report 11027, from al-Dahhak: a sheep gores a sheep and it dies. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 501}

On 'what the beast of prey has eaten', the sense is the forbidding of what a beast of prey has taken, other than a trained hunting beast. Report 11028, from Ibn Abbas: what the beast of prey seized; report 11029, from al-Dahhak: the same; report 11030, from Qatada: in the Age of Ignorance, when a beast of prey killed something or ate from it, they ate what was left; report 11031, from Ibn Abbas through Ata ibn al-Sa'ib: he read 'the eaten by a beast of prey' in the form of a noun. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 502}

On 'except what you have slaughtered', the sense is except what you have purified by the slaughter that God made a purifier. Commentators differ on what it excepts. Some say it is an exception from all that God named as forbidden, from what was dedicated to other than God, the strangled, the beaten, the fallen, the gored and what the beast of prey ate. Report 11032, from Ibn Abbas: whatever of these is caught alive so that its tail moves or its eye blinks, slaughter it and name God's name, and it is lawful. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 502}

Report 11033, from al-Hasan: whichever of these you catch in time to slaughter, slaughter and eat; asked how one knows, he said: when it blinks its eye or strikes its tail. Report 11034, from Qatada: all of this, except swine's flesh, if you catch from it an eye that blinks, a tail that moves, or a leg that kicks, and you slaughter it, God has made it lawful for you. Report 11035, from Qatada through Ma'mar: the same. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 503}

Report 11036, from Ali: if the slaughter of the beaten, the fallen or the gored is caught while it moves a hand or foot, eat it; report 11037, from Ibrahim: when a beast of prey has eaten from the game, or the beaten, the gored or the fallen, and you catch its slaughter, eat it; report 11038, from Ja'far al-Sadiq through his father Muhammad al-Baqir from Ali: if it kicks with its leg, blinks its eye or moves its tail, that suffices. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 503}

Report 11039, from Ibn Tawus through his father: if you slaughter it and it wags its tail or moves, it is lawful for you; report 11040, from al-Hasan: if the beaten blinks its eye, kicks with its leg or wags its tail, slaughter and eat; report 11042, from Ubayd ibn Umayr: if it blinks its eye, wags its tail or moves, it is lawful for you; report 11043, from al-Dahhak: the people of the Age of Ignorance ate these; God forbade them in Islam except what is slaughtered, so whatever moves a man, tail or eye, and is slaughtered, is lawful. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 504}

Report 11044, from Ibn Zayd: all of this is forbidden, except what is slaughtered from it. Al-Tabari says that on this view the beaten and the fallen are forbidden if they die from the fall, the beating, the goring or the beast's attack, unless you catch their slaughter before they die, and then eating is lawful. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 504}

Others say that 'except what you have slaughtered' is an exception from the prohibition, not from the list of forbidden things, since carrion has no slaughter; the sense is: carrion, blood and all we named are forbidden, except what you have slaughtered by the slaughter God made lawful; this view is held by a group of the people of Medina. Report 11045, from Malik, asked about a sheep whose belly was torn by a beast until its intestines came out: he said he did not think it could be slaughtered or eaten; report 11046, from Malik through Ashhab: if the beast reached the chest, he did not think it should be eaten; if it only reached the limbs, there is no harm. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 505}

Al-Tabari prefers the first view, that 'except what you have slaughtered' is an exception from what precedes: what was dedicated to other than God, the strangled, the beaten, the fallen, the gored, and what the beast of prey ate, since each is described by the state in which it is before its death; whatever is caught alive before its soul leaves its body, and which God permitted, is lawful. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 505}

He raises an objection: why list again what was forbidden, when the verse began with carrion, which includes all carrion? He answers that the listing is for clarity, since these things, though carrion in fact, are named by their names so it is known that their ruling is prohibition even if they are not yet dead. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 506}

Al-Tabari answers: the reason for repeating it, though the ruling for what dies of the causes by which it is described was already given with carrion, is that those addressed by this verse counted as carrion only what died of an incidental illness other than strangling, falling, goring or a beast's attack; so God told them the ruling for these is that of what dies of incidental illnesses, and that the cause making carrion forbidden is not death by sickness or harm before it perished, but that it was not slaughtered by the slaughter that made it lawful. Report 11047, from al-Suddi through Asbat: this is forbidden because some Arabs ate it and did not count it as carrion, counting as dead only what died of sickness; so God forbade it to them except what had God's name said over it and whose slaughter was caught with life still in it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 507}

On 'and what was slaughtered on the stone altars', it means God also forbade what was slaughtered on the nusub; 'what' is nominative, joined to the 'what' of 'what the beast of prey ate'. The nusub are stone idols, plural ansab, set up in a place where the idolaters offered to them, and they are not images. Ibn Jurayj described them (report 11048): the nusub are not images, which are shaped and carved, but stones set up, three hundred and sixty, some say three hundred of them belonging to Khuza'a; when they slaughtered they sprinkled the blood on what faced the House and cut the meat in slices and laid it on the stones. The Muslims said: the people of the Age of Ignorance honoured the House with blood, so we have more right to honour it; and God revealed that neither their flesh nor their blood reaches Him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 508}

Report 11049, from Mujahid through Ibn Uyayna and Ibn Abi Najih: stones on which the people of the Age of Ignorance slaughtered; report 11050: stones around the Ka'ba on which they slaughtered, replacing them whenever they liked with stones they preferred; report 11051 gives the same through Shibl. Report 11052, from Qatada: the nusub are stones the people of the Age of Ignorance worshipped and slaughtered for, which God forbade; report 11053, from Qatada through Ma'mar: the stone altars of the Age of Ignorance. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 509}

Report 11054, from Ibn Abbas through Ali ibn Abi Talha: stone altars on which they slaughtered and pronounced the call; report 11055, from Mujahid, in the sense given; report 11056, from al-Dahhak: stones for which they pronounced and on which they slaughtered; report 11057, from Ibn Zayd: what is slaughtered on the nusub and what is dedicated to other than God are one. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 509}

On 'and to seek to divide by the arrows', it means to seek knowledge of what is apportioned to you or not by arrows; it is formed from 'qism', the apportioning of provision and needs. In the Age of Ignorance, when one of them wished to travel or raid, he cast the arrows, which are the azlam, and some were written 'my Lord has forbidden me' and others 'my Lord has commanded me'; if the arrow marked with the command came out he went on, and if the one marked with the prohibition came out he held back. The azlam are the plural of zalam, also zulam, the divining arrows described. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 510}

Report 11058, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr through Sufyan and Abu Husayn: the arrows; when they wished to set out they made arrows for sitting and for setting out, and if setting out came up they went, and if sitting came up they sat. Report 11059, from him: white pebbles they cast; al-Tabari reports Sufyan ibn Wakī' saying it is chess, which the editor calls a very odd claim. Report 11060, from al-Hasan: they took three arrows, one marked 'command me', another 'forbid me', and left the third blank; then cast them, and if the blank one came out they cast again. Report 11061, from Mujahid: stones they wrote on, called the arrows. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 511}

Reports 11062 and 11063, from Mujahid: the arrows cast for every journey, raid and trade; reports 11064 and 11065: the Persians' dice with which they gambled, and the Arabs' arrows. Report 11066, from Qatada: a man wishing to travel wrote on one arrow 'this orders me to stay' and on another 'this orders me to go', and put with them a blank one, then cast them: if the one ordering to stay came up he stayed, if the one ordering to go came up he went, and if the other came up he cast again. Report 11067 gives the same. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 512}

Report 11068, from al-Dahhak: they divided by the arrows in their affairs. Report 11069, from Ibn Zayd: the azlam were their arrows; one who wanted something wrote what he wanted on the arrows and cast them, and whichever came out he followed, even if it was what he liked least. Report 11070, from al-Suddi: the arrows kept in the Age of Ignorance by the soothsayers; a man who wished to travel or marry gave the soothsayer something and he cast the arrows for him; if what he liked came out he told him to do it, and if what he disliked came out he forbade it, as Abd al-Muttalib cast the arrows at Zamzam and over Abd Allah and the camels. Report 11071, from Abd Allah ibn Kathir: we heard that the people of the Age of Ignorance cast the arrows over departing and staying, and the arrow of departing came out and they departed, or staying and they stayed. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 513}

Report 11072, from Ibn Ishaq and found in Ibn Hisham's Sira, describes Hubal: Hubal was the greatest idol of Quraysh at Mecca, set over a well inside the Ka'ba where the gifts to the Ka'ba were gathered, and beside it were seven arrows, each bearing a writing. One was for the blood-price: when they disputed who should bear it they cast the arrows, and whoever it fell on bore it. One said 'yes' for a matter they intended, and if it came out they acted; one said 'no', and if it came out they did not do the thing. Others said 'from you', 'attached', 'from others' and 'waters', the last used when they wanted to dig for water. When they wished to circumcise a boy, arrange a marriage, bury a dead man or doubted the descent of one of them, they took him to Hubal with a hundred dirhams and a slaughter camel, gave these to the keeper of the arrows, brought forward their man and said: this is so-and-so son of so-and-so, we intend such and such for him, so bring out the truth. Then they told the keeper to cast. If 'from you' came out, he was of pure descent among them; 'from others' made him an ally; 'attached' left him where he was, with neither lineage nor pact; 'yes' meant they went ahead, and 'no' meant they put it off to the next year. The editor notes in the footnotes that some words are supplied from Ibn Hisham where the sentence needs them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 514}

Report 11073, from Ibn Abbas through Ali: the arrows with which they divided in their affairs. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 515}

Al-Tabari then takes 'that is transgression'. He says it means these matters he mentioned, eating carrion, blood, swine's flesh and the rest forbidden in the verse, together with divining by arrows; and transgression (fisq) is departure from God's command and obedience to what He forbade and warned against, His disobedience. Report 11074, from Ibn Abbas: whoever eats any of that has committed transgression. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 515}

Next, 'today those who disbelieved have despaired of your religion'. Al-Tabari says it means: now the hope of the confederates and the people of disbelief and denial is cut off that you might leave your religion and revert to idolatry. Report 11075, from Ibn Abbas: that you would return to their religion, ever; report 11076, from al-Suddi: they despaired that you would turn back from your religion, though the editor doubts the word 'I think' in it, since it is undotted in the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 516}

If someone asks which day this was, the answer is that it was reported to be the day of Arafa in the year of the Farewell Pilgrimage, after the Arabs had entered Islam. Report 11077, from Ibn Jurayj: Mujahid said this is when it happened; Ibn Jurayj added that others said it was the day of Arafa, on a Friday, when the Prophet looked and saw none but monotheists and no idolater, praised God, and Gabriel came down with the revelation that they would not return as they were. Report 11078, from Ibn Zayd: this is the day of Arafa. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 516}

Then 'so do not fear them, but fear Me'. Al-Tabari says: do not fear, believers, those who have despaired of your turning back, nor fear that they will overcome and force you away from your religion; fear Me instead, if you go against My command, dare to disobey Me and overstep My limits, lest I bring down My punishment on you. Report 11079, from Ibn Jurayj: do not fear that they will overcome you. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 517}

Next, 'today I have perfected your religion for you'. Al-Tabari notes that the interpreters differed. Some said it means: today, believers, I have perfected My obligations, limits, commands and prohibitions, what I made lawful and unlawful, what I sent down in My Book, what I made clear through revelation on My messenger's tongue, and the proofs set up for everything you need of your religion, so there is nothing to add after this day. They say it was the day of Arafa in the Farewell Pilgrimage, that no obligation, permission or prohibition came down on the Prophet after this verse, and that he lived only eighty-one nights after it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 517}

Report 11080, from Ibn Abbas: God told His Prophet and the believers He had completed faith for them, so they need no addition ever, He has perfected it so it will never be lessened, and approved it so He will never be displeased with it. Report 11081, from al-Suddi: it came down on the day of Arafa and no lawful or unlawful matter came after; the Messenger returned and died; and Asma bint Umays said: I performed that pilgrimage with him, and as we travelled Gabriel appeared to him on the mount, which could not bear the weight of the Qur'an upon it and knelt, so I came and covered him with a cloak I wore. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 518}

Report 11082, from Ibn Jurayj: the Prophet stayed eighty-one nights after it came down. Report 11083, from Harun ibn Antara on his father: when it came down on the day of the greater pilgrimage Umar wept, and the Prophet asked what made him weep; he said: we were increasing in our religion, but once it is complete nothing completes but it then falls short; the Prophet said: you speak truth. The editor remarks that what falls short is the people of the religion, whose hearts harden when time lengthens, and cites the saying that Islam began as a stranger and will return a stranger. Report 11084, from Waki' on his father, is similar. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 519}

Others said it means: I have perfected your pilgrimage, so the Sacred Land was set apart for you to make pilgrimage to, believers, without idolaters mixing with you in it. Report 11085, from al-Hakam: He completed their religion in that they made pilgrimage and no idolater did so with them; report 11086, from Qatada: God made their religion pure for them and drove the idolaters from the House; report 11087, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr: the completion of the pilgrimage and the removal of the idolaters from the House. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 519}

Al-Tabari then weighs these, saying the soundest view is that God told His Prophet and the believers that on the day He sent down this verse He had perfected their religion by setting the Sacred Land apart for them and driving the idolaters from it, so that the Muslims made pilgrimage without them. As to the obligations and rulings, there is disagreement whether they were completed that day or not; what we mentioned was reported from Ibn Abbas and al-Suddi, and it was reported from al-Bara' ibn Azib that the last verse revealed in the Qur'an was the verse on kalala in sura al-Nisa', and no scholar denies that revelation did not cease for the Prophet until his death. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 520}

Al-Tabari continues his argument: revelation was more continuous than ever in the last period before the Prophet's death. Since the verse on kalala was the last to come down, and it concerns rulings and obligations, it is known that the meaning of 'today I have perfected your religion' is not what those who read it as the completion of acts of worship, rulings and obligations took it to be. If someone asks why the view that an obligation came down after it is preferred to the view that none did, the answer is that the one who says none came down only reports that he knows of none, and a denial is not testimony; testimony is the word of the one who says one came down, and the report of a truthful person may not be rejected where he could be telling the truth. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 521}

Then 'and I have completed My favour upon you'. Al-Tabari says it means: I have completed My favour, believers, by giving you victory over My enemy and yours among the idolaters, driving them from your lands and cutting their hope that you would go back to the idolatry you were in. Report 11088, from Ibn Abbas: the idolaters and the Muslims used to make the pilgrimage together, and when sura Bara'a came down the idolaters were driven from the House and the Muslims made the pilgrimage with no idolater sharing the Sacred House with them; that was part of the completion of the favour. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 521}

Report 11089, from Qatada: we were told this verse came down on the day of Arafa, a Friday, when God drove the idolaters from the Sacred Mosque and made the Muslims' pilgrimage pure for them. Report 11090, from al-Sha'bi: it came down at Arafat, where the landmarks of the Age of Ignorance had been torn down and idolatry had faded, and no idolater made the pilgrimage with them that year. Report 11091, from Amir, who is al-Sha'bi: it came down while the Prophet was standing at Arafat, with the people gathered round him, the landmarks and rites of the Age of Ignorance in ruins, idolatry faded and no one circling the House naked; report 11092 is similar. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 522}

Then 'and I have approved Islam as your religion'. Al-Tabari says it means: I have approved for you submission to My command and compliance with obedience to Me, within the limits, obligations and landmarks I laid down, and 'religion' here means obedience to Me from you. If someone asks whether God was not pleased with Islam for His servants until the day He sent down this verse, the answer is that He was always pleased with Islam as the religion of His creation, but He kept moving His Prophet and his companions through the degrees and stations of Islam, stage after stage, until He completed its laws and landmarks for them and brought them to its highest degree; then, when He sent down this verse, He spoke of it as it stands today, so hold to it and do not part from it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 523}

Qatada said of it, in report 11093: we were told that on the Day of Resurrection each religion will be shown to its people; faith will bring glad tidings to its people and promise them good, until Islam comes and says: Lord, You are Peace and I am Islam; and He will say: it is you I accept today and by you I reward today. The editor cites a close report from the Musnad of Abu Dawud al-Tayalisi on Abu Hurayra. Al-Tabari thinks Qatada took 'faith' in this report as affirmation and declaration by the tongue, since that is its meaning among the Arabs, and took 'Islam' as the heart's submission to God in affirming His oneness and the body's compliance with what He commanded and forbade. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 523}

Al-Tabari then gives those who said the verse came down at Arafa in the Farewell Pilgrimage. Report 11094, from Tariq ibn Shihab: the Jews said to Umar: you recite a verse which, had it come down on us, we would have taken as a festival; Umar said: I know when it came down, where, and where the Messenger of God was when it came down: it came down on the day of Arafa while he was standing at Arafa; Sufyan added: and I doubt whether it was a Friday. The editor lists who recorded it: Ahmad in the Musnad, al-Bukhari and Muslim, and Ibn Kathir, who thinks Sufyan's doubt is scruple in transmission, since that the standing at Arafa in the Farewell Pilgrimage fell on a Friday is certain. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 524}

Report 11095, from Tariq ibn Shihab: a Jew said to Umar that if the Jews had known the day this verse came down they would have made it a festival; Umar said: I know the day it came down, the hour, and where the Messenger was: it came down on the night of Friday (or of jam'), while we were with the Messenger at Arafat. In a footnote the editor gives al-Nawawi's explanation of 'night of jam'' and 'night of Friday', and that Umar meant they had made that day a festival twice over, being both the day of Arafa and a Friday, each a festival for the people of Islam. Report 11096, from Tariq on Umar, is similar; the editor notes that Ibn Hajar showed the Jewish man was Ka'b al-Ahbar, and raises the possibility that his question came before he became Muslim. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 525}

Report 11097, from Ammar, a client of Banu Hashim: Ibn Abbas recited the verse with a man of the People of the Book beside him, who said: if we knew which day it came down we would take it as a festival; Ibn Abbas said: it came down on the day of Arafa, a Friday. Report 11098, from Ammar: Ibn Abbas told the Jew it came down on the day of two festivals, a festival day and a Friday; the editor traces it to al-Tirmidhi, who calls it good and unusual. Report 11099, from Ibn Abbas, is similar. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 526}

Report 11100, from Qubaysa: Ka'b said: if any other community had had this verse sent down on it, they would have looked to the day it came down and made it a festival to gather on; Umar said: which verse, Ka'b? He said: 'today I have perfected your religion for you'. Umar said: I know the day it came down and the place: a Friday and the day of Arafa, and both, thanks to God, are festivals for us. In a long footnote the editor examines the chain, and judges that al-Tabari erred in identifying the narrator Ishaq as ibn Kharasha; he thinks it is Ishaq ibn Qubaysa ibn Dhu'ayb, narrating from his father. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 526}

Report 11101, from Isa ibn Jariya al-Ansari: we were sitting in the office when a Christian said to us: people of Islam, a verse has come down on you which, had it come down on us, we would have kept that day and hour as a festival as long as two of us remained; none of us answered him; I met Muhammad ibn Ka'b al-Qurazi and asked him, and he said: did you not answer him? Umar ibn al-Khattab said: it came down on the Prophet while he was standing on the mountain on the day... {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 527}

The rest of report 11101: it came down on the Prophet while he was standing on the mountain on the day of Arafa, so that day will remain a festival for the Muslims while any of them remain. The editor notes that Isa ibn Jariya was criticised by Ibn Ma'in and Abu Dawud. Report 11102, from Amir: it came down on the Prophet on the eve of Arafa while he was at the standing place. Report 11103: Dawud said to Amir that the Jews ask how the Arabs failed to preserve the day on which God perfected their religion; Amir said: did they not preserve it? It is the day of Arafa. Report 11104, from Qatada: we heard it came down on the day of Arafa, which fell on a Friday. Report 11105, from Ikrima: Umar said sura al-Ma'ida came down on the day of Arafa, a Friday. Report 11106, from Shahr ibn Hawshab: it came down on the Prophet while he stood at Arafa on his mount, which knelt for fear its foreleg would break. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 528}

Report 11107, from Asma bint Yazid: the whole of sura al-Ma'ida came down while I held the halter of the Messenger's she-camel al-Adba, and from its weight the camel's upper foreleg almost broke; the editor gives its record in Ahmad and al-Haythami, and notes that Shahr in its chain is weak though he has been declared reliable. Report 11108: Amr ibn Qays al-Sakuni heard Mu'awiya on the pulpit reciting this verse to its end and saying that it came down on the day of Arafa, a Friday. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 529}

Others said: this verse came down on a Monday, and that the sura was revealed in Medina. Report 11109, from Ibn Abbas: your Prophet was born on a Monday, left Mecca on a Monday, entered Medina on a Monday, sura al-Ma'ida was revealed on a Monday, and the mention was raised on a Monday, meaning the Prophet's death and the end of revelation. The editor notes that Ahmad recorded it without the revelation of al-Ma'ida, and that Ibn Kathir called this report strange with a weak chain and thought Ibn Abbas meant it came down on the day of two festivals, which the narrator confused. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 530}

Report 11110, from Qatada: al-Ma'ida is Medinan. Others said it came down on the Prophet during his journey in the Farewell Pilgrimage; report 11112, from al-Rabi' ibn Anas: sura al-Ma'ida came down on the Prophet during the journey of the Farewell Pilgrimage as he rode his mount, which knelt under him from its weight. Others said it is not a day known to people, and that the meaning is the day that I know and My creation does not; report 11113, from Ibn Abbas: it is not a day known to the people. Al-Tabari then weighs these: the soundest view on the time of revelation is the report from Umar ibn al-Khattab that it came down on the day of Arafa, a Friday, for the soundness of its chain and the weakness of the chains of the others. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 531}

Al-Tabari moves to 'but whoever is driven by necessity in hunger', saying: it means whoever is afflicted with harm in a makhmasa, that is, in famine. Makhmasa follows the pattern mif'ala like 'majbana' and 'mabkhala', from khamas al-batn, the belly's being drawn in; I think what is meant here is gauntness from hunger and extreme want, though elsewhere it can be a gauntness of build rather than hunger, as the Dhubyani poet Nabigha described a woman with a slender belly. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 532}

It is clear that there he did not mean wasting and hardship from hunger, but the fineness of what lies over a woman's hips and thighs, which is praised in women; what does carry the sense of gauntness and wasting through hardship is the line of al-A'sha of Banu Tha'laba, in which you spend the winter nights with full bellies while your neighbour women, hungry, spend them with shrunken bellies; and from this sense comes the phrase 'in makhmasa'. Some Basran grammarians said makhmasa is a verbal noun from 'hunger gaunted him'; others of the Arabic scholars held that it is a noun standing for the verbal noun and not itself one, which is why the mif'ala form occurs as a noun for verbal nouns, feminine and masculine. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 533}

The interpreters spoke in the same sense. Report 11114, from Ibn Abbas: in famine; report 11115, from Qatada: that is, in famine; report 11116 gives the same through Ma'mar. Report 11117, from al-Suddi: He mentioned carrion and what it includes and made it lawful in necessity, 'in makhmasa', meaning in famine; report 11118, from Ibn Zayd: makhmasa is hunger. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 534}

Then 'not inclining to sin'. Al-Tabari says it means: whoever is driven by hunger to eat what I forbade you, believers, of carrion, blood, swine's flesh and the rest forbidden in this verse, not inclined to sin. 'Ghayr' is accusative as a circumstantial clause on the pronoun in 'whoever is driven', and it has the sense of 'la'; it takes the case that 'inclining' would take had the sentence read 'not inclining'. The editor notes that the printed text wrongly gave 'illa' here, which spoils the sense, and that the manuscript has the right 'la'. The one inclining to sin is the one who leans to it and swerves toward it, here meaning the one who does it deliberately and aims at it, from 'the people leaned (janafa) toward it'; al-Tabari had explained janaf in the verse in sura al-Baqara about the one making a bequest. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 535}

As for the carrion eater's inclining to sin in eating it or anything else this verse forbade believers, it is his intending to eat it not to ward off the necessity that has come upon him but to disobey God and oppose His command to leave it. The interpreters spoke in the same sense. Report 11119, from Ibn Abbas: of what is forbidden among what was named at the start of this verse, not intending sin. Report 11120, from Mujahid: not intending sin; it is allowed to the one driven by necessity, if not intending sin, to eat it out of hardship, but whoever transgresses, exceeds or sets out in disobedience of God is forbidden to eat it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 536}

Report 11121, from Qatada: that is, not exposing himself to disobedience; report 11122, from him through Ma'mar: not intending sin, not exposing himself; report 11123, from al-Suddi: not exposing himself to sin, meaning seeking pleasure in it or exceeding in his eating; report 11124, from Ibn Zayd: he does not eat it to seek sin or out of boldness against it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 536}

Then 'then God is forgiving, merciful'. Al-Tabari says something is left unsaid here, covered by what is said: the meaning is that whoever is driven by hunger to what I forbade among what I mentioned in this verse and eats it, not inclining to sin, God is forgiving and merciful to him; 'and he ate' is omitted and 'to him' stated, since the rest of the sentence shows both. 'Forgiving' means that He covers over his eating of what he ate by pardoning him and overlooking the punishment; 'merciful' means He is gentle with him; and of His mercy and gentleness He allowed him to eat what He allowed of carrion and the rest named with it, when he fears for his life from the ravenous pang of hunger and the harm of a need that has come on his body. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 537}

If someone asks what amount of eating God promised forgiveness to the one driven to carrion and the other forbidden things, the answer is what report 11125 gives, from Abu Waqid al-Laythi: we said, Messenger of God, we are in a land where hunger afflicts us, so what of carrion is lawful for us? He said: if you have no morning drink, no evening drink, and cannot gather vegetables, then it is yours. In a long footnote the editor sets out the standing of al-Tabari's chain, which is weak because of Muhammad ibn al-Qasim al-Asadi, accused of lying, then notes that it is reported by a sound route in Ahmad and recorded by al-Haythami, al-Hakim and al-Bayhaqi, with al-Dhahabi pointing to a break since Hassan ibn Atiyya did not hear from Abu Waqid. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 538}

Report 11126, from al-Hasan: a man asked the Messenger of God: until when is what is forbidden lawful for me? He said: until your family are satisfied with milk or their provisions arrive. The editor explains in a footnote the morning drink, the milk drawn at dawn and drunk then, and the evening drink, drawn at evening and drunk then. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 539}

Report 11127, from al-Hasan, is the same but with 'or their provisions are collected'. Report 11128, from Urwa ibn al-Zubayr on someone who told him: a Bedouin came to the Prophet asking what God had forbidden him and what He had made lawful, and the Prophet said: good things are lawful to you and foul things forbidden, unless you are poor and in need of food not lawful to you, then eat of it until you no longer need it. The man said: what is my poverty that makes it lawful, and what is my wealth that frees me from it? The Prophet said: if you hope for offspring from your flock, get by on the meat of your animals until they bear; or if you hope for a gain you are seeking, get by on something of it; feed your family what you see fit until you no longer need it. The Bedouin said: what is my wealth that I should leave it when I have it? {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 540}

The Prophet said: if you give your family an evening drink at night, avoid what God has forbidden you of food, but your own property is all open to you with nothing forbidden in it. The editor notes al-Haythami recorded it as from Samura ibn Jundub, and that Ibn Kathir took it from this place. Report 11129, from Ibn Awn: I found Samura's book with al-Hasan and read it to him, and in it was: an evening or morning drink suffices from necessity; report 11130, from Ibn Awn: I read in the book of Samura ibn Jundub: an evening or morning drink is enough for necessity, and the editor notes this book is Samura's letter to his sons, which holds much knowledge. Report 11131, from al-Hasan: when a man is driven to carrion he eats his sustenance of it, meaning what holds the body up of food and drink. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 541}

We stop here at the foot of p. 541, after report 11131, in the commentary on verse 3 of sura al-Ma'ida, at how much the one in necessity may eat, and will continue from p. 542. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v09 p. 541}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
