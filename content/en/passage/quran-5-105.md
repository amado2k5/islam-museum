## Summary
Verse 105 of [[passage/quran-5|Surat al-Ma'ida]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 105 of 120 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

Al-Tabari explains "O you who believe, you are responsible for your own souls; whoever goes astray cannot harm you if you are guided": believers, take care of your own souls, put them right, work for their deliverance from God's punishment and look to what brings them near their Lord, for "whoever goes astray", whoever disbelieves and takes a way other than the way of truth, cannot harm you if you are guided, believe in your Lord and obey Him in what He commanded and forbade, forbidding what He forbade and permitting what He permitted. The accusative of "your souls" is the accusative of urging, and the Arabs urge with prepositions and adverbs such as "alayka", "indaka", "dunaka" and "ilayka". The interpreters differ on its meaning. Some said it means: take care of your own souls when you have commanded the right and forbidden the wrong and it has not been accepted from you. Report 12848, from al-Hasan: this verse was read to Ibn Mas'ud, who said: this is not its time; say it as long as it is accepted from you, and when it is rejected by you, take care of your own souls (the editor gives notes on Sawwar ibn Abd Allah and Abu al-Ashhab). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 138}

Reports 12849 and 12850, from al-Hasan, are similar: a man said to Ibn Mas'ud, has God not said "O you who believe, you are responsible for your own souls"? He said: this is not its time; say it as long as it is accepted from you, and when it is rejected by you, take care of your own souls (the editor traces the report in al-Haythami and notes that al-Hasan al-Basri did not hear from Ibn Mas'ud). Report 12851, from Sufyan ibn Uqal: Ibn Umar was told, if only you would sit out these days and neither command nor forbid, for God says "you are responsible for your own souls; whoever goes astray cannot harm you if you are guided"! Ibn Umar said: it is not meant for me or my companions, because the messenger of God said, let the one present convey it to the one absent, and we were the present and you are the absent; this verse is for people who will come after us, who if they speak will not be heeded. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 139}

Report 12852, from Abu Mazin: I went to Medina in the time of Uthman, and some Muslims were sitting; one of them read "you are responsible for your own souls", and most of them said: the interpretation of this verse has not come today. Report 12853 is similar. Report 12854, from Sawwar ibn Shubayb: I was with Ibn Umar when a sharp-tongued man came and said: we are six, all of us have read the Qur'an, all strive, and all hate to do anything base, yet we testify of one another that we are polytheists! A man answered him, but the questioner said that he was asking the elder, and repeated his case to Abd Allah. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 140}

The editor identifies Abu Mazin al-Azdi al-Hudani as one of the pious men of the Azd who came to Medina in Uthman's time, and thinks Ibn Abi Hatim is mistaken to place a man between Qatada and Abu Mazin, since Qatada states in report 12857 that he heard from him directly; he traces the report in al-Suyuti's al-Durr al-Manthur, keeps the manuscript reading "he read" in the singular, and notes an addition in Ibn Kathir that is not in the manuscript.

The rest of 12854: Ibn Umar said to the questioner: perhaps you think I will order you to go and kill them! Admonish them and forbid them, and if they disobey you, look to yourself, for God says "you are responsible for your own souls". Report 12855, from al-Hasan: a man asked Ibn Mas'ud about the verse and he said: this is not its time; today it is accepted, but a time is near when you will command the right and such and such will be done to you, or it will not be accepted from you, and then "you are responsible for your own souls". Report 12856, from Qatada, from a man: I was in Medina in the caliphate of Uthman, in a circle that included companions of the Prophet and an elder to whom they referred; a man read the verse and the elder said: its interpretation belongs to the end of time. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 141}

The editor gives notes on Sawwar ibn Shubayb al-Sa'di al-A'raji, a trustworthy Kufan who narrated from Ibn Umar, and points to Ibn Kathir and to al-Suyuti, who ascribes the report only to Ibn Mardawayh. He explains "today it is accepted" as the word of truth in commanding right and forbidding wrong, and explains "they refer to him" as turning to him for knowledge and understanding, citing a line of al-Farazdaq and remarking that the usage should be recorded in the dictionaries.

Report 12857, from Qatada: Abu Mazin, a pious man of the Azd from the Banu al-Hiddan, said: I went to Medina in the lifetime of Uthman and sat in a circle of the messenger's companions; a man read the verse, and one of the oldest of the company said: leave this verse, its interpretation is at the end of time. Report 12858, from Jubayr ibn Nufayr: I was in a circle with the messenger's companions, the youngest of them, and they were discussing commanding right and forbidding wrong; I said: does God not say "you are responsible for your own souls"? They turned on me with one voice and said: do you pull out a verse whose interpretation you do not know? until I wished I had not spoken. When it was time for them to rise they said: you are a young man. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 142}

The editor corrects the spelling of "Banu al-Hiddan" and keeps the manuscript wording for the circle, and explains that "to pull out with a verse" means to cite it or draw something from it.

The rest of 12858: you pulled out a verse you do not know, and perhaps you will live to that time, when you see greed obeyed, desire followed and every man pleased with his own opinion; then look to yourself, and whoever goes astray will not harm you if you are guided. Report 12859, from Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud: they were sitting with him when two men quarrelled as people do, until each stood up against the other; a man among the sitters said: shall I not go and command and forbid them? Another said: look to yourself, for God says "you are responsible for your own souls". Ibn Mas'ud heard and said: hush, the interpretation of this has not yet come! The Qur'an was revealed where it was revealed, and some verses had their interpretation fulfilled before they descended. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 143}

On 12858 the editor identifies Ibn Fadala as Mubarak ibn Fadala of Basra and Mu'awiya ibn Salih al-Hadrami as a leading scholar, calls Jubayr ibn Nufayr one who lived through both eras, and judges the chain broken because no narration of Mu'awiya from Jubayr is recorded; Ibn Kathir transmits it and al-Suyuti ascribes it to Ibn Jarir alone. He explains "hush" as a word of rebuke and thinks the manuscript's "gently" would have been right if written correctly.

The rest of 12859, from Ibn Mas'ud: some had their interpretation fulfilled in the Prophet's time, some shortly after, some will be fulfilled after today, some at the Hour, and some on the Day of Reckoning, concerning Paradise and the Fire. So as long as your hearts are one and your desires are one, you are not split into factions and none of you tastes the violence of another, command and forbid; but when hearts and desires differ, you are split into factions and taste one another's violence, then each man looks to himself, and then the interpretation of this verse has come. Report 12860, from Ibn Mas'ud, is similar. Report 12861, from al-Hasan: some of the Prophet's companions interpreted the verse, and some of his companions said: leave this verse, it is not for you. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 144}

The editor says he found no biography of Layth ibn Harun, identifies Ishaq ibn Sulayman al-Razi, keeps the manuscript wording against additions in the printed text, and lists who transmitted and traced the report: Ibn Kathir and al-Suyuti, who adds Abd ibn Humayd, Nu'aym ibn Hammad in al-Fitan, Ibn Abi Hatim, Abu al-Shaykh, Ibn Mardawayh and al-Bayhaqi in al-Shu'ab.

Report 12862, from Abu Umayya al-Sha'bani: I asked Abu Tha'laba al-Khushani about the verse and he said: you asked one who knows; I asked the messenger of God, who said: command one another to do right and forbid wrong, and when you see a world preferred, greed obeyed and each man pleased with his own opinion, then look to yourself; after you come days of patience, in which the one who holds to what you now hold will have the reward of fifty workers. They said: fifty of them? He said: rather fifty workers of you. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 145}

The editor judges the chain of 12861 incomplete, since Harami ibn Umara died in 201 and could not have heard al-Hasan al-Basri, who died about 110, so he marked the gap with dots. He gives notes on the men of the chain of 12862 (Isma'il ibn Isra'il, Ayyub ibn Suwayd, Utba ibn Abi Hakim, Amr ibn Jariya), corrects the name Amr from the manuscript, and records the disagreement over the name of Abu Umayya al-Sha'bani.

Report 12863 is another chain from Abu Umayya al-Sha'bani with nearly the same wording: command the right and forbid the wrong until you see greed obeyed, desire followed and every man pleased with his own opinion; then look to your own small concern and leave the common people, for ahead of you are days in which the worker's reward is that of fifty of you. Al-Tabari then begins a second view: the meaning is that when a servant acts in obedience to God, those who go astray after him and perish do not harm him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v11 p. 146}

The editor says this is al-Tirmidhi's own chain; al-Tirmidhi recorded it with Ibn al-Mubarak's addition and called it good and unusual, and Ibn Majah and Abu Dawud also recorded it; Ibn Kathir transmits it and al-Suyuti traces it. He places in brackets a link missing from the chain in both the manuscript and the printed text.

We stop here at the foot of p. 146, in the middle of the commentary on verse 105, after report 12863 and the start of the second view, that when a servant obeys God, those who go astray after him cannot harm him.

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
