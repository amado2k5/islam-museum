## Summary
Verse 48 of [[passage/quran-5|Surat al-Ma'ida]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 48 of 120 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

**Commentary on verse 48: "We sent down to you the Book in truth, confirming the Book before it and as a guardian over it", pp. 377-380**

Al-Tabari says this addresses His prophet Muhammad: We sent down to you, Muhammad, the Book, the Qur'an, "in truth", that is in veracity with no lie or doubt that it is from God, "confirming what was before it of the Book", sent to confirm God's earlier books to His prophets, "and a guardian over it", confirming the earlier books and witness over them that they are true from God, trusted over them and preserving them. The root of the word is guarding and watching: one says so-and-so was guardian over it when he watched, protected and witnessed it.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 377}

The interpreters said likewise though their wording differed. Some said it means a witness, as reports 12103m and 12104, from Ibn 'Abbas and al-Suddi, say; report 12105, from Qatada, says trusted and a witness over the earlier books; report 12106, from Mujahid, says entrusted with the Qur'an, a witness and confirmer, and Ibn Jurayj reports others as saying the Qur'an is trusted over the books: when the People of the Book tell us something of their book, if it is in the Qur'an confirm it and if not, deny it.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 378}

Others said it means trusted over it. Reports 12107 to 12118, from Ibn 'Abbas with chains through al-Tamimi, say entrusted with it; report 12114 says the guardian is the trustee and the Qur'an is trustee over every book before it; report 12115 says it is a witness over the Torah and Gospel, confirming them, trusted and ruling over the earlier books. The editor says al-Tamimi is Arbada al-Tamimi, the reliable Successor.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 379}

Report 12119, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, says entrusted over the books before it; report 12120, from al-Hasan and 'Ikrima, says confirming these books and trusted over them. Others said guardian means confirmer; report 12121, from Ibn Zayd, says confirming it: everything God sent down of Torah, Gospel or Psalms, the Qur'an confirms. Others said what is meant by "confirming what was before it and a guardian over it" is the Prophet of God.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 380}

**Commentary on verse 48, continued: "judge between them by what God sent down" and "for each of you We made a law and a way", pp. 381-387**

Reports 12122 and 12123, from Mujahid, say that "a guardian over it" means Muhammad, entrusted with the Qur'an. Al-Tabari spells out what that reading requires: We sent down to you the Book confirming what was before it, as a guardian over it, so that "guardian" would be a circumstantial of the "you" in "to you", that is the Prophet, and the pronoun in "over it" would refer to the Book. He then rejects it as far from what is understood in Arabic and indeed an error, because "guardian" is joined to "confirming" and so can only describe what "confirming" describes, the Book; had Mujahid's meaning been intended, no conjunction would have been needed. The editor's notes record differences between the printed text and the manuscript over the conjunction and two words, and say which reading he kept because it makes the sense run.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 381}

Al-Tabari adds that anyone who supposed that "confirming", on Mujahid's reading, describes the "you" is answered by "what was before it": the pronoun there refers to someone other than the one addressed, and if "confirming" described the Prophet the text would say "what was before you". The editor again notes manuscript variants in a few words.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 382}

He then turns to "so judge between them by what God sent down, and do not follow their desires away from the truth", which he calls a command from God to His prophet to judge between those of the People of the Book and other creeds who come to him for judgment, by the Qur'an: hudud, wounds, retaliation and lives. He stones the married adulterer, takes a life for a life, an eye for an eye and a nose for a nose, since the Qur'an came confirming the earlier books and guarding over them. He is forbidden to follow the desires of the Jews, who said: if Muhammad gives you flogging for the married adulterer instead of stoning, and the killing of the lowly for the noble but not the noble for the lowly, take it; if not, beware. So he is to act on God's book when they bring him a case and he chooses to rule, and not abandon it in preference to their wishes over the truth.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 382}

Report 12124, from Ibn 'Abbas, says "by what God sent down" means by God's limits. Report 12125 says Masruq used to have a Jew and a Christian swear by God, and then recited the verse. The editor notes that the printed text reads "choose the ruling" and that the manuscript is right, since the Prophet was free either to rule between them or to leave it, as at verse 42, and that the proof for taking oaths from them is the verse of 5:49, not that one.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 383}

On "for each of you We made a law and a way", al-Tabari says God made a path for each people. "Shir'a" is the shari'a itself, its plural being "shira'", though "shara'i'" is also right since the meaning is the same; whatever one enters into is a shari'a, hence the watering-place, and the "laws of Islam" because its people enter into it, and a people equal in something are said to be "shara'". "Minhaj" is originally the clear, plain road, said of a road that is "nahj" or "manhaj"; he cites a line by a rajaz poet about a clear road and sweet water, and then it is used of anything plain and easy. The sense: for each people among you We made a road to the truth that it makes for, and a clear way it acts on. The notes discuss the plural form and the place Falj in the line, a water of Banu Tamim that their poets praise, with verses the editor quotes in its praise.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 384}

The interpreters then differed over who "for each of you" means. Some said the different religious communities: God made a law and a way for each. Report 12126, from Qatada, says a path and a custom, and the customs differ: the Torah has a law, the Gospel has a law, the Qur'an has a law, in which God permits what He wills and forbids what He wills as a trial, to know who obeys and who disobeys; but the one religion that He accepts from no one else is oneness and sincerity to God that the messengers brought. Report 12127 has him say the religion is one and the laws differ. Report 12128, from 'Ali, says faith since God sent Adam is the testimony that there is no god but God and the acknowledgment of what came from God, and each people has the law or way that came to them. The editor says he does not know the 'Abd Allah ibn Hashim in the chain and that Sayf ibn 'Umar al-Tamimi is discarded as a transmitter.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 385}

Others said that the community of Muhammad is meant, so that the sense is: We made the Book We sent down to Muhammad a law and a way for all of you, for everyone who enters Islam. Report 12129, from Mujahid, says the law is the custom and the way is the path, and that for everyone who enters Muhammad's religion God made the Qur'an a law and a way.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 386}

Al-Tabari prefers the first view, that for each community of the nations We made a law and a way, because of the words that follow, "had God willed He would have made you one community": if Muhammad's community, which is already one, were meant, that would have no sensible meaning. He explains that the address recalls what was written for the Children of Israel in the Torah, then Jesus and the Gospel, then Muhammad and the Book confirming what came before; that God gave him and his community a law other than the laws of the earlier peoples, even though the religion is one in oneness, acknowledgment of what they brought and keeping to His commands and prohibitions, so they differ in what was made lawful and unlawful for each.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 386}

Interpreters said as he does about law and way. Reports 12130 to 12135, from Ibn 'Abbas with chains through al-Tamimi and through Yahya ibn Wattab, say the law is a custom and the way a path. The notes identify the transmitters: Abu Yahya al-Razi, Ishaq ibn Sulayman the reliable, Abu Sinan Sa'id ibn Sinan, and Yahya ibn Wattab al-Asadi, the reciter, reliable and of few reports.  {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v10 p. 387}

We stop here at the foot of p. 387, after report 12135 from Ibn 'Abbas that the law is a custom and the way a path; the rest of verse 48 follows in the next batch, from p. 388.

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
