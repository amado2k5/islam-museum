## Summary
Verse 19 of [[passage/quran-2|Surat al-Baqara]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 19 of 286 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

## In al-Tabari's commentary: "or like a rainstorm from the sky"
Al-Tabari derives "sayyib" from "saba al-matar yasubu", the rain coming down, its original form being "sayyub" with the waw turned to ya and doubled, and cites lines by Alqama and others; he gives Ibn Abbas and Ata that the sayyib is rain. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 333} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 334}

Al-Tabari reports from Ibn Abbas, al-Suddi, Qatada, Mujahid, al-Rabi' ibn Anas, al-Dahhak, Ibn Zayd, Ata and Sufyan that the sayyib is rain. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 334} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 335} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 336}
He reads the two parables together: the likeness of the hypocrites' seeking light by the light of their profession, while hiding disbelief, is that of one who kindles a fire, or of a dark rain falling from the sky on a dark night, which is the darkness in the verse. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 336}
To the objection that "aw" ("or") expresses doubt and doubt cannot be ascribed to God, he replies that "aw" can stand for "and" in sense, citing lines by Tawba ibn al-Humayyir, Jarir and Mutammim ibn Nuwayra, so both parables are for the hypocrites. He takes the omission of "likeness" before "or like a rainstorm" as brevity, since what precedes shows it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 336} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 337} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 338}
He then begins on "in it are darkness, thunder and lightning", recording that the exegetes differed over thunder, some saying it is an angel who drives the clouds, which he gives from Mujahid and Abu Salih. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 338}

## In al-Tabari's commentary: thunder
Al-Tabari gives two views on thunder. The first is that it is an angel who drives the clouds with glorification. He relates it from Mujahid, Abu Salih and Shahr ibn Hawshab (who drives them as a camel-driver drives camels, the lightning bolts being fire from the angel's mouth when his anger grows), from Ibn Abbas through the al-Dahhak and al-Suddi chains (the angel is named al-Ra'd and his voice is his glorification), from Ikrima (he gathers the clouds as a herdsman gathers camels), from Qatada (a creature of God, obedient and listening), and from Ali ibn Abi Talib that it is an angel. He also gives Ikrima's report that when Ibn Abbas heard thunder he said: glory to Him whom you glorify. The edition's editor notes the weakness of some of these chains. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 338} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 339} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 340} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 341}
The second view is that thunder is wind choked under the clouds that rises and makes the sound, which he relates from Abu al-Jald, in the reply to a letter from Ibn Abbas. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 341} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 342}
Al-Tabari holds that if thunder is what Ibn Abbas and Mujahid said, the verse means "in it are darkness and the sound of thunder", the mention of thunder standing for its sound; if it is what Abu al-Jald said, nothing is omitted. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 342}

## In al-Tabari's commentary: lightning
Al-Tabari records that the scholars differed over lightning. Some said it is the "makhariq" (twisted cloths or whips) of the angels, with which they drive the clouds, which he relates from Ali ibn Abi Talib and from Ibn Abbas through al-Suddi. Others said it is a whip of light with which an angel drives the clouds, from Ibn Abbas through al-Dahhak. Others said it is water, from Abu al-Jald's answer to Ibn Abbas's letter. Others said it is the "mas'" (lashing) of an angel, from Mujahid, with a report from Muhammad ibn Muslim al-Ta'ifi of an angel with four faces and one from Shu'ayb al-Jaba'i that it is the beating of the wings of the bearers of the Throne. The edition's editor notes that some of these chains are weak or of unknown narrators. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 342} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 343} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 344} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 345}
Al-Tabari suggests that what Ali, Ibn Abbas and Mujahid said may come to one meaning: the "makhariq" are the whips of light with which the angel drives the clouds, and his driving them is his lashing them, "mis'a" originally meaning striking with swords in contest. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 345} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 346}

## In al-Tabari's commentary: the meaning of the parable of the rainstorm
Al-Tabari records that the exegetes differed over the verse, and gives four views from Ibn Abbas. In the first, from Ibn Ishaq's chain, the hypocrites in the darkness of disbelief and fear of being killed are like someone in the dark of a rainstorm putting his fingers in his ears against the thunderbolts; the lightning almost snatches away their sight, meaning the brilliance of the truth; when it lights for them they walk in it, meaning they recognise the truth and speak it, and when they relapse into disbelief they stand bewildered. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 346}
In the second, from al-Suddi, two hypocrites of Medina fled from the Prophet to the polytheists and were caught in rain with thunder, thunderbolts and lightning, putting their fingers in their ears in fear, walking when it lit and standing when it grew dark; by morning they came to the Prophet and became Muslims, and God made their case a parable for the hypocrites of Medina, who say that Muhammad's religion is true when their wealth grows and, when hardship strikes, blame it on his religion and turn back. Al-Tabari adds later that he does not know this report to be sound since he doubts its chain, and the edition's editor comments that al-Suddi's chain gathers scattered Companion interpretations into one narrative, so accepting it is open to question when it rises to a report of a particular event. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 347} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 348}
In the third, from the al-Awfi chain, it is the parable of the hypocrite in the light of what he speaks of God's book and does for show, who does otherwise when alone; the darkness is error, the lightning belief, and those meant are the People of the Book. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 348} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 349}
In the fourth, from Ali ibn Abi Talha, the rainstorm stands for the Qur'an, the darkness for trial, the thunder for warning, and the Qur'an's clear verses almost expose the hypocrites' weak points; when they gain honour through Islam they are at ease, and when it suffers a blow they return to disbelief. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 349}
He also gives Mujahid that the lighting and darkening of the lightning is in the same sense, and Qatada that the hypocrite, seeing ease in Islam, says "I am with you", and when hardship comes he cannot bear it or seek its reward. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 349} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 350}

Al-Tabari also gives al-Rabi' ibn Anas: their likeness is that of people travelling a dark night with rain, thunder and lightning on a road, who see the road when it flashes and are bewildered when it fades; so the hypocrite, each time he speaks the word of sincerity, is lit, and when he doubts he is bewildered. Al-Dahhak: the darkness is error and the lightning belief. Ibn Zayd: a parable of hypocrites who took light from Islam. Ibn Jurayj: the hypocrite hears nothing without thinking it is death aimed at him. Ata: a parable struck for the disbeliever. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 350} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 351}
Al-Tabari says these reports are close in meaning: all take the rainstorm as a parable of the outward belief of the hypocrite, the darkness his error, the brightness of the lightning the light of his belief, his putting fingers in his ears his faint-heartedness before God's punishment, his walking in the light his steadiness in the light of his belief, and his standing in the dark his bewilderment. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 352}
His own reading is that the likeness of the hypocrites' seeking light by the profession they voiced, whereby they gained the rulings of believers while denying inwardly and not knowing which of the two roads holds guidance, is that of people on a dark night with a driving thunder, fierce lightning flashing across the sky and bolts descending. The rainstorm stands for their outward profession, the darkness for their doubt, denial and the sickness of their hearts, and the thunder and bolts for their dread of God's warning through His messenger, a warning they still doubt. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 352} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 353}
Their fingers in their ears are a likeness of their guarding themselves from the Messenger and the believers with the words they show, as one fearing thunderbolts guards his ears, and "fear of death" a likeness of their fear that swift punishment may fall on them. As for the report from Ibn Mas'ud and Ibn Abbas that they put their fingers in their ears in the Prophet's gathering, al-Tabari says he does not know it to be sound because he doubts its chain, and if it is not sound his own reading is the better one for the verse. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 353} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 354}
