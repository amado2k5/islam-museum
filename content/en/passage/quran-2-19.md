## In al-Tabari's commentary: "or like a rainstorm from the sky"
Al-Tabari derives "sayyib" from "saba al-matar yasubu", the rain coming down, its original form being "sayyub" with the waw turned to ya and doubled, and cites lines by Alqama and others; he gives Ibn Abbas and Ata that the sayyib is rain. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 333} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 334}

Al-Tabari reports from Ibn Abbas, al-Suddi, Qatada, Mujahid, al-Rabi' ibn Anas, al-Dahhak, Ibn Zayd, Ata and Sufyan that the sayyib is rain. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 334} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 335} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 336}
He reads the two parables together: the likeness of the hypocrites' seeking light by the light of their profession, while hiding disbelief, is that of one who kindles a fire, or of a dark rain falling from the sky on a dark night, which is the darkness in the verse. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 336}
To the objection that "aw" ("or") expresses doubt and doubt cannot be ascribed to God, he replies that "aw" can stand for "and" in sense, citing lines by Tawba ibn al-Humayyir, Jarir and Mutammim ibn Nuwayra, so both parables are for the hypocrites. He takes the omission of "likeness" before "or like a rainstorm" as brevity, since what precedes shows it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 336} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 337} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 338}
He then begins on "in it are darkness, thunder and lightning", recording that the exegetes differed over thunder, some saying it is an angel who drives the clouds, which he gives from Mujahid and Abu Salih. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 338}

## In al-Tabari's commentary: thunder
Al-Tabari gives two views on thunder. The first is that it is an angel who drives the clouds with glorification. He relates it from Mujahid, Abu Salih and Shahr ibn Hawshab (who drives them as a camel-driver drives camels, the lightning bolts being fire from the angel's mouth when his anger grows), from Ibn Abbas through the al-Dahhak and al-Suddi chains (the angel is named al-Ra'd and his voice is his glorification), from Ikrima (he gathers the clouds as a herdsman gathers camels), from Qatada (a creature of God, obedient and listening), and from Ali ibn Abi Talib that it is an angel. He also gives Ikrima's report that when Ibn Abbas heard thunder he said: glory to Him whom you glorify. The edition's editor notes the weakness of some of these chains. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 338} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 339} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 340} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 341}
The second view is that thunder is wind choked under the clouds that rises and makes the sound, which he relates from Abu al-Jald, in the reply to a letter from Ibn Abbas. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 341} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 342}
Al-Tabari holds that if thunder is what Ibn Abbas and Mujahid said, the verse means "in it are darkness and the sound of thunder", the mention of thunder standing for its sound; if it is what Abu al-Jald said, nothing is omitted. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 342}

## In al-Tabari's commentary: lightning
Al-Tabari records that the scholars differed over lightning. Some said it is the "makhariq" (twisted cloths or whips) of the angels, with which they drive the clouds, which he relates from Ali ibn Abi Talib and from Ibn Abbas through al-Suddi. Others said it is a whip of light with which an angel drives the clouds, from Ibn Abbas through al-Dahhak. Others said it is water, from Abu al-Jald's answer to Ibn Abbas's letter. Others said it is the "mas'" (lashing) of an angel, from Mujahid, with a report from Muhammad ibn Muslim al-Ta'ifi of an angel with four faces and one from Shu'ayb al-Jaba'i that it is the beating of the wings of the bearers of the Throne. The edition's editor notes that some of these chains are weak or of unknown narrators. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 342} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 343} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 344} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 345}
Al-Tabari suggests that what Ali, Ibn Abbas and Mujahid said may come to one meaning: the "makhariq" are the whips of light with which the angel drives the clouds, and his driving them is his lashing them, "mis'a" originally meaning striking with swords in contest. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 345} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 346}
