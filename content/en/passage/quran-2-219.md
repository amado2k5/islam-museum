## Summary
Verse 219 of [[passage/quran-2|Surat al-Baqara]], a Medinan surah. Scholars connect its revelation to [[concept/maysar|gambling (maysir)]]. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 219 of 286 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

## In al-Tabari's commentary

On "they ask you about wine and gambling; say, in both is great sin and benefits for people, but their sin is greater than their benefit": it means, your companions ask you, Muhammad, about wine and drinking it. "Khamr" is every drink that "khammara" the mind, covering and veiling it, from "I covered (khamartu) the vessel" when covered it, and "a woman's khimar" because she covers her head with it; one says "he walks to you in khamar", meaning in concealment, as al-'Ajjaj says, and whatever mixes with and overwhelms the mind, of sickness or intoxication, is "khamr". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 320} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 321} "Maysir" is of the form "maf'il" from "this matter was 'yasara' for me" when it became due to me, "yasir" being the one who is owed by arrows, a share or other, and then the gambler was called "yasir" or "yasar", as in two cited lines; the editor remarks on the sense in the dictionaries and on the poets. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 321} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 322} Mujahid said much the same: maysir is gambling, so called from their saying "aysiru wa-jzuru", "put down such and such" (4106); and from him: all gambling is of maysir, even children playing with walnuts (4107); from 'Abdallah ibn Mas'ud: beware of these marked dice that you throw with a throw, for they are of maysir (4108-4110). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 322}

From Muhammad ibn Sirin: gambling is maysir (4111); everything with a stake is of maysir (4112); all gambling is maysir, even playing backgammon on standing, shouting or the feather a man puts on his head (4113); every game with gambling in it, of drinking, shouting or standing, is of maysir (4114). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 323} From al-Hasan: maysir is gambling (4115); from Tawus and 'Ata': all gambling is of maysir, even children playing with dice and walnuts (4116); from Sa'id (4117); from 'Ubaydallah, that is Ibn Mas'ud: beware of these two dice thrown with a throw, for they are of maysir (4118); from Qatada: maysir is all gambling (4119). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 323} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 324} From 'Ubaydallah ibn 'Umar, that he heard 'Umar ibn 'Ubaydallah say to al-Qasim ibn Muhammad: backgammon is maysir, what of chess, is it maysir? and al-Qasim said, whatever distracts from remembrance of God and from prayer is maysir (4120). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 324} From Ibn 'Abbas: maysir is gambling; in the Jahiliyya a man would wager his family and property, and whichever beat his companion went off with the other's family and property (4121); from al-Suddi (4122) and Qatada (4123); from Mujahid and Sa'id ibn Jubayr: all gambling, even the walnuts children play with (4124); from al-Dahhak (4125), Qatada (4126) and Ibn 'Umar: gambling is of maysir (4127); from Mujahid: maysir is the arrows of the Arabs and the dice of the Persians, and 'Ata' ibn Maysara claimed it is all gambling (4128); from Makhul (4129) and Ibn 'Umar (4130). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 324} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 325}

On "say, in both is great sin and benefits for people": it means, say to them, Muhammad, in both, wine and gambling, is great sin. From al-Suddi: the sin of wine is that a man drinks and becomes drunk and harms people, and the sin of gambling is that a man gambles, withholds what is due and wrongs (4131); from Mujahid: this is the first fault found with wine (4132); from Ibn 'Abbas: it means what is lost of the religion in one who drinks it (4133). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 325} Al-Tabari says the better view for wine is what al-Suddi said, the drinker's loss of reason when drunk until knowledge of his Lord leaves him, which is the gravest of sins, and this is, God willing, the meaning of Ibn 'Abbas's words; and for gambling it is being occupied by it from remembrance of God and from prayer and the arising of enmity and hatred between the gamblers, as God described it in "Satan only wants to cause enmity and hatred between you through wine and gambling and to turn you from the remembrance of God and from prayer" (5:91). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 326}

On "and benefits for people": the benefits of wine were its price before it was forbidden and the pleasure they reached by drinking it, and he cites two lines of al-A'sha describing it and a line of Hassan, with the editor's explanation and corrections to the printed wording. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 326} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 327} The benefits of gambling are the shares of the slaughtered camel they get; they would cast arrows over a camel, and when one beat his companion he slaughtered it and they divided it into tenths by the number of arrows; he cites a line of al-A'sha, and the editor notes that gambling was counted among them as a mark of generosity. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 327} The interpreters said likewise: from Mujahid, the benefits here are what they get of the camel (4134); from al-Suddi, the benefit of wine is in its pleasure and price and that of gambling in what is won (4135); from Mujahid, their benefits before they were forbidden (4136); from Ibn 'Abbas, the pleasure and delight they got when they drank it (4137). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 328}

The readers differed on "in both is great (kabir) sin": most of the people of Medina and some of Kufa and Basra read "kabir" with ba', meaning that in drinking this and gambling this is a great measure of sin; others from the two cities read "kathir" with tha', meaning a multitude of sins, as though they saw "ithm" as meaning "sins" though singular in form. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 328}

Al-Tabari says the sounder of the two readings is that of those who read "kabir" with ba', because all agree on reading "and their sin is greater (akbar) than their benefit" with ba'; this is clear evidence that the description of the first sin is greatness and magnitude, not number, for had number been meant it would have said "more (akthar) than their benefit". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 329}

On "and their sin is greater than their benefit", he takes it to mean that the sin of drinking wine and of this gambling is a greater harm to them than the benefit they gain by them, because when drunk they leapt on one another and fought, and when they gambled evil arose between them because of it, which led them to the things for which they sin. The verse was revealed about wine before its prohibition was made explicit, so God ascribed the sin to the two, though the sin lies in their causes, since it arises from them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 329} A number of interpreters said instead that it means their sin after prohibition is greater than their benefit before it: from Ibn 'Abbas, their benefits were before prohibition and their sin after they were forbidden (4138); from al-Rabi', the same (4139); from al-Dahhak, their sin after prohibition is greater than their benefit before it (4140); from Ibn 'Abbas by 'Ali ibn Abi Talha, what goes from religion, and the sin in it, is greater than what they get of delight when they drink it (4141). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 329} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 330}

Al-Tabari says he chose his reading because of the abundant, mutually supporting reports that this verse was revealed before the prohibition of wine and gambling, so the sin God ascribed to them is the sin arising from their causes, not the sin after prohibition. He then gives the reports for revelation before the prohibition. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 330} From Sa'id ibn Jubayr: when the verse on wine and gambling came down, some disliked it because of "in both is great sin" and some drank because of "and benefits for people", until the verse in al-Nisa' (4:43) came down; they then left it at prayer times and drank it at other times, until the verse in al-Ma'ida (5:90) came down (4142). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 330} In the printed text the report breaks off after the verse of al-Ma'ida and is followed by a remark attributed to 'Umar that is garbled in print and cannot be read, so it is not given here.

From 'Abdallah ibn 'Umar: God sent down three things on wine; first the verse of al-Baqara, and they said, we benefit from it and drink it as God said; then the verse of al-Nisa' came down, and they said, we will not drink it near prayer; then the verse of al-Ma'ida came down, and the Prophet said, wine is forbidden (4143). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 331} The editor's note on this chain: Abu 'Amir is al-'Aqadi, trustworthy; Muhammad ibn Abi Humayd al-Ansari is weak, munkar al-hadith, with agreement on his weakness; "Abu Tawba al-Misri" is no known narrator and comes from Muhammad ibn Abi Humayd's confusion, the right name being Abu Tu'ma al-Umawi, mawla of 'Umar ibn 'Abd al-'Aziz, a trustworthy tabi'i and reciter in Egypt; al-Tayalisi relates it in his Musnad with an added story of the wineskins being slit; Ibn Kathir corrected the name in his tafsir by writing "that is Abu Tu'ma"; and al-Suyuti cites it in al-Durr al-manthur, attributing it to al-Tayalisi, al-Tabari, Ibn Abi Hatim, Ibn Mardawayh and al-Bayhaqi. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 331}

From 'Ikrima and al-Hasan: God said not to approach prayer when drunk (4:43) and said they ask about wine and gambling (2:219), and the verse in al-Ma'ida abrogated them (4144). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 332} From Abu al-Qumus Zayd ibn 'Ali: God sent down three times on wine; first the verse of al-Baqara, and those Muslims whom God willed drank on that, until two men drank, entered prayer and began to talk incoherently, and the verse of al-Nisa' came down, so they avoided it at prayer, until a man drank it and began to lament those killed at Badr in verse; word reached the Prophet, who came in alarm dragging his cloak, and when the man saw him he said, I seek refuge in God from the anger of God and His messenger, by God I shall never taste it again; then God sent down its prohibition in the two verses of al-Ma'ida, and 'Umar ibn al-Khattab said, we desist, we desist (4145). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 332} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 333} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 334} The editor notes that the transmitters of this report attribute the verses to Abu Bakr al-Siddiq, that 'A'isha denied it, and that the lines are from Abu Bakr ibn Shu'ub mixed with those of Buhayr ibn 'Abdallah al-Qushayri; that Abu al-Qumus's report is mursal and no proof; and that al-Hafiz (Ibn Hajar) weakened it because it conflicts with al-Fakihi's sound report from 'A'isha that Abu Bakr never said a line of poetry in the Jahiliyya or Islam and that he and 'Uthman had left wine in the Jahiliyya. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 332} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 333} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 334}

From al-Sha'bi: four verses came down on wine: the verse of al-Baqara, which they left; then the verse of al-Nahl (16:67), and they drank; then the two verses of al-Ma'ida (4146). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 334}

From al-Suddi: after the verse of al-Baqara they kept drinking until 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf made a meal and invited some of the Prophet's Companions, among them 'Ali ibn Abi Talib, who recited al-Kafirun and did not grasp it, so God sent down the stricter verse of al-Nisa' (4:43). Then it was lawful for them; they drank from the dawn prayer until the day rose or reached midday and rose for the noon prayer sober, then did not drink until they had prayed 'isha, then drank until midnight, slept, and rose for dawn sober; until Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas made a meal and invited some of the Prophet's Companions, among them a man of the Ansar, and roasted a camel's head for them; when they had eaten and drunk they became drunk and fell to talking, Sa'd said something and the Ansari grew angry, lifted the camel's jawbone and broke Sa'd's nose, and God sent down the abrogation and prohibition of wine in the two verses of al-Ma'ida (4147). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 334} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 335} From Qatada and Mujahid: when the verse came down some people drank and some left it, until its prohibition came down in al-Ma'ida (4148). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 335} From Mujahid: "in both is great sin" was the first fault found with wine (4149), and the editor refers to the same text at no. 4332. From Qatada: God censured the two but did not forbid them, then sent down in al-Nisa' something stricter, so they drank until prayer was due and abstained for it, so drunkenness was forbidden to them at those times; then He sent down al-Ma'ida after the battle of the Confederates and the prohibition came, of little or much, intoxicating or not, and the Arabs had then no enjoyment (`aysh) they liked better (4150). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 335} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 336} The editor remarks that "`aysh" is a fine figure of speech, meaning enjoyment and pleasure. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 336}

From al-Rabi': when the verse came down the Prophet said, your Lord is advancing toward the prohibition of wine; he said the same when the verse of al-Nisa' came down; then the verse of al-Ma'ida came down and wine was forbidden at that point (4151). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 336} From Ibn Zayd: it was abrogated three times, in al-Ma'ida, by the penalty the Prophet set, and by his flogging; the Prophet flogged them as a penalty but acted on his own judgement, there was no named penalty, yet it was a penalty (4152). The editor explains: the verse of al-Baqara was first abrogated by the verse of al-Ma'ida; God then made its penalty flogging without a stated number, a second abrogation; then the Prophet exercised his judgement on the number and manner of the flogging, and that was the third. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 336}

On "and they ask you what they should spend; say, the surplus (al-`afw)": he means the Prophet's Companions ask what they should spend of their wealth in giving, and he tells them to spend the `afw from it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 337} The interpreters differed on the meaning of "al-`afw" here:

Some said, the surplus: from Ibn 'Abbas, what is left over beyond your family (4153); from Qatada, the surplus (4154, 4155); from 'Ata' (4156); from al-Suddi (4157); from Ibn Zayd, the people worked each day for what the day brought, and if anything remained that day beyond their dependants they gave it, not leaving their dependants hungry, and gave it in charity to the people (4158); from al-Hasan, the surplus of wealth (4159). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 337} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 338}

Others said, what is `afw, not noticeable in the one who spends or gives: from Ibn 'Abbas, what does not show in your wealth (4160); from Tawus, a small amount of everything (4161). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 338}

Others said, the middle of spending, neither extravagance nor stinginess: from al-Hasan, do not exhaust your wealth until it is spent on people (4162); from 'Ata', `afw in spending is not to exhaust your wealth until it is gone and you ask people (4163), and that it is what they spend without extravagance or stinginess in what is right, and Mujahid said, `afw is charity out of sufficiency (4164); and from al-Hasan again, that you do not exhaust your wealth (4165). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 338} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 339}

Others said it means, take from them whatever they bring, little or much; from Ibn 'Abbas, accept from them whatever they bring, little or much (4166). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 339}

Others said, what is good of your wealth: from al-Rabi', the good of it, the best and finest of your wealth (4167); from Qatada, `afw is the surplus, meaning the best of your wealth (4168). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 339}

Others said that "al-`afw" is the obligatory charity: from Mujahid (Abu 'Asim doubting the chain), the obligatory charity (4169). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 340}

Al-Tabari says the soundest of these views is that of those who said `afw is the surplus of a man's wealth beyond his own and his family's necessary support, the surplus for which the reports from the Prophet are many in permitting charity in good causes. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 340} He gives among them: from Abu Hurayra, a man said, I have a dinar; he said, spend it on yourself; he said, I have another; spend it on your family; I have another; spend it on your child; I have another; then you know best (4170). The editor notes that 'Ali ibn Muslim al-Tusi is trustworthy and that the report is in Ahmad, Abu Dawud, al-Nasa'i, al-Hakim and Ibn Hibban, and that Ibn Kathir ascribed it to Sahih Muslim but the editor denies it is there after long search. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 340} From Jabir ibn 'Abdallah: when one of you is poor let him begin with himself; if he has surplus let him begin with himself and his dependants; if he then finds more let him give charity to others (4171), reported in similar form by Muslim and Ahmad. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 341} From Jabir: a man brought the Prophet a gold nugget he had found in a mine and said, take this from me as charity, by God I own nothing else this morning; the Prophet turned away several times, then took it angrily and threw it at him so that it would have wounded him had it hit, and said, one of you brings all his wealth to give as charity and sits begging from people; charity is from sufficiency (4172). Al-Hakim graded it sound on Muslim's conditions and al-Dhahabi agreed; the note corrects the printed name to 'Asim ibn 'Umar ibn Qatada. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 341} From 'Abdallah ibn Mas'ud: give a little from the surplus, begin with your dependants, and you are not blamed for bare sufficiency (4173). The note says "Ibrahim al-Makhrami" in the chain is unknown and is probably Ibrahim al-Hajari, who is weak (weakened by Ibn 'Uyayna, al-Bukhari, Abu Zur'a and Abu Hatim); that it is part of a longer report cited by al-Suyuti and al-Mundhiri from Abu Ya'la and al-Hakim; that the editor finds no grading by al-Hakim in the Mustadrak; and that al-Haythami says its men are reliable. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 342}

Al-Tabari then says that since the Prophet permitted his community to give charity from what exceeds the giver's need, that surplus is the `afw of a man's wealth, because in Arab speech `afw, of wealth or anything, is increase and abundance; so one says of a man "take what is `afw for you from so-and-so", meaning what is left over and clear for you without his strain, and he cites a line of Labid on fat camels, "full of fat". So what God permitted in "say, the `afw" is what the Prophet explained: the best charity is what is given out of sufficiency. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 343}

If one asks, why is `afw not the obligatory charity? He answers that he rejects it because proof stands that one whose zakat has fallen due, and whose wealth then perishes except the amount owed to the recipients, must hand it over if the loss came after neglecting to pay, and that is his strain, not his `afw. So the view that `afw is whatever the owner delivers to his imam, little or much, and the view that it is the obligatory charity, are invalid. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 344} Likewise there is no basis for the view that it means "what does not show in your wealth", because when Abu Lubaba said to the Prophet that part of his repentance was to strip himself of his wealth as charity to God and His messenger, the Prophet said a third is enough for you, and similarly it was reported to Ka'b ibn Malik; and a third plainly shows in a man's wealth. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 344} The right course, in his view, is as God described His servants who neither overspend nor stint but are between the two (25:67), and as in the prohibition of a tied hand and of opening it fully (17:29), which the Prophet limited in less than that according to the wealth and what it bears. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 344} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 345}

The scholars then differed whether this verse is abrogated or stands. Some said it is abrogated by the obligatory zakat: from Ibn 'Abbas, this was before charity was made obligatory (4174); from him also, no fixed obligation was set for it, then the command to take the `afw (7:199), then the obligations came down specified (4175); from al-Suddi, zakat abrogated it (4176). Others said it stands, unabrogated: from Mujahid (Abu 'Asim doubting the chain), concerning its being named the obligatory charity, the wording of the report being garbled in the printed text (4177). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 345} Al-Tabari begins his choice, that the right view is what Ibn 'Abbas said as 'Atiyya relates it: "say, the `afw" is not the imposition of an obligation as God's right in a man's wealth but a notification of what pleases Him in spending as against what angers Him, an answer to one who asked His Prophet; the sentence continues on the next page. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v04 p. 345}

Al-Tabari goes on: this is a discipline from God for all His creatures in voluntary charity; its ruling stands, neither abrogating an earlier ruling nor abrogated by a later one. A person of scruple and religion should not go beyond, in voluntary charity, what the Prophet taught in saying that when one of you has a surplus he begins with himself, then his family, then his child, and then follows with the remainder the ways God is pleased with; that is the middle course between extravagance and stinginess. {c(346)} To one who claims abrogation he says: what is the proof, when all agree that a man may spend a third of his wealth in charity, gift and bequest? If he means that giving the `afw is not obligatory and that any obligation fell away with zakat, what proof is there that it was ever obligatory, when the verse carries no command but is an answer to what people asked in order to learn which charity pleases God? {c(346)}

On the readings, the readers differed on "al-`afw": most readers of the Hijaz and the two Sanctuaries and most of Kufa read it in the accusative, and some of Basra in the nominative. The one who reads the accusative takes "madha" as a single word governed by "they spend", so the sense is, what do they spend? The one who reads the nominative takes "ma" as the interrogative and "dha" as "that which", so the sense is, what is it that they spend? Say, what they spend is the surplus. Al-Tabari holds both readings correct in Arabic, given how close the meanings are and how widespread each reading is, but prefers the accusative as read by more readers and better known. {c(346,347)}

On "thus God makes clear the signs to you that you may reflect": he says, as I have made clear My signs and proofs in this surah, shown you what saves you from My punishments, set out My bounds and obligations, pointed to the proofs of My oneness and then to the proofs of My messenger, so I make clear to you in the rest of My book My signs and proofs, that you may reflect on My promise and warning, reward and punishment, and prefer obedience to Me over the little pleasures of the passing world. {c(347,348)} (Museum note: Tanzil numbering begins verse 220 with "in this world and the hereafter", but al-Tabari comments on it with the preceding clause, so it is given here.) From Ibn 'Abbas: it means reflecting on the passing and perishing of this world and the coming and lasting of the hereafter (4178); from Qatada: so that you know the hereafter's superiority over this world (4179); from Ibn Juraij: this world is a house of trial then perishing, the hereafter a house of recompense then lasting, so you reflect and work for the one that lasts (4180); from Qatada: whoever reflects on them knows the superiority of one over the other, so be among those who forgo the world's want for the hereafter's (4181). {c(348,349)}
