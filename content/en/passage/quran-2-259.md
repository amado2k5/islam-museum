## Summary
Verse 259 of [[passage/quran-2|Surat al-Baqara]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 259 of 286 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

Al-Tabari opens ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]) on p. 438 saying that it matches what came before in wonder expressed to Muhammad, and is joined to the preceding verse even though the wording differs, because the two are alike in sense, as the Arabs join speech to a likeness already given though the wording differs. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 438} He mentions that some Basran grammarians claimed the particle of likeness in it is superfluous, so that the sense is: have you not seen the one who disputed with Abraham, or the one who went by a village; al-Tabari objects that nothing in God's Book may be without meaning, as he showed earlier; this is his objection and view. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 438}

He then says the interpreters differed on who passed by the ruined village. Some said Uzayr: Najiya ibn Ka'b (5882), Sulayman ibn Buraydah (5883), Qatada (5884), al-Rabi', Ikrima, al-Suddi (5888), al-Dahhak (5889), and Ibn Abbas according to Salm al-Khawwas (5890); the editor gives biographies of Najiya and Salm al-Khawwas and corrects a reading. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 439} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 440} Others said it was Urmiya ibn Hilqiya, and it was reported from Muhammad ibn Ishaq that Urmiya is al-Khidr, that the name of al-Khidr, as Wahb ibn Munabbih claimed from the Children of Israel, is Urmiya son of Hilqiya of the tribe of Aaron son of Amram (5891); from Wahb ibn Munabbih (5892, 5893, 5894) that when Jerusalem was destroyed and the books burned Urmiya stood at the side of the mountain and put his question about the village's revival; from Abdullah ibn Ubayd ibn Umayr (5895, 5896) that he was a prophet named Urmiya; and from Bakr ibn Mudar (5897) that they say, and God knows best, it was Urmiya. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 440} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 441} In the notes the editor says the name in their books is Jeremiah, that al-Tabari rejected this view in his History, and comments on points in the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 440} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 441} These are reports from Successors, Wahb ibn Munabbih and People of the Book sources, and the museum does not rule on them.

Al-Tabari then says that the soundest view is that God made His prophet wonder at the one who, seeing a village fallen to ruins, asked how God would revive it once it had perished, though he knew God had begun its creation from nothing, so that knowledge of the power to begin it did not satisfy him; he has no sound clear evidence for the speaker's name, which could be Uzayr or Urmiya, and there is no need to know it, since the aim was not to tell people the name of the speaker; this is his choice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 441} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 442} He adds that the aim was to inform those who denied God's power to revive His creation after their death, from Quraysh and the other Arabs who denied it, and to establish the proof against the Jews around the Prophet's place of emigration by showing him what removes their doubt, since these reports were not known to Muhammad and his people, who were unlettered with no scripture, so the People of the Book knew he could not have known them save by revelation; had the aim been the speaker's name, the proof would have been set out in a way that removes excuse, but the aim was to censure his saying; this is his reasoning. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 442} In the notes the editor explains the sentence structure and the word for unlettered. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 442}

On the village itself the interpreters differed. Some said it is Jerusalem: from Wahb ibn Munabbih (5898 to 5900), that when Urmiya saw Jerusalem's ruin like a great mountain he said what he said; from Qatada (5901), that they were told it was Jerusalem, past which Uzayr went after Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon had destroyed it; from al-Dahhak (5902), that he passed the holy land; from Ikrima (5903) and al-Rabi' (5904), that the village is Jerusalem, past which Uzayr went after Nebuchadnezzar had destroyed it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 442} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 443} In the note the editor says the manuscript writes Nebuchadnezzar's name as two words. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 443} These reports concern Israelite history and sources as transmitted, and the museum does not rule on them. Al-Tabari then mentions that others said it is the village where God destroyed those who left their homes by the thousand in fear of death, in ([[passage/quran-2-243|2:243]]); their argument begins on the next page. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 443}

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]) from p. 444: from Ibn Zayd (5905), the village the man passed was the village struck by plague, then he recounts the story of those who left their homes by the thousand in fear of death as in ([[passage/quran-2-243|2:243]]), whom God caused to die in the very place they went seeking life and then revived, and a man passed by it when it was bleached bones, stood looking and asked how it could be revived, so God took him in death for a full century and then raised him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 444} Al-Tabari says the sound view on that is the same as on the name of the speaker, the two being alike, meaning that the village need not be identified; this is his choice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 444}

Al-Tabari then explains the word for desolate as emptied of its people and inhabitants and notes its linguistic uses; the roofs or structures are buildings and houses, the singular meaning any building, and one says a man built a house; he cites the use in ([[passage/quran-7-137|7:137]]) for building, and the booths of Mecca meaning its tents and structures. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 444} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 445} In the notes the editor sets out the plural of paucity, corrects the printed text by the manuscript and quotes the Lisan al-Arab and Ibn al-Athir on the booths of Mecca. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 444} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 445} From Ibn Abbas (5906), via Ibn Jurayj: desolate means ruined; Ibn Jurayj adds that they were told that Uzayr went out, stood by Jerusalem which Nebuchadnezzar had destroyed, and said, after all that you had of holiness, fighters and wealth, and he grieved. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 445} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 446} From al-Dahhak (5907): it is ruined; from al-Rabi' (5908): Uzayr passed by it after Nebuchadnezzar had destroyed it; from al-Suddi (5909): fallen upon its roofs. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 446}

Al-Tabari then explains the part about the speaker being made to die for a full century. The meaning, as we are told, is that when the speaker passed Jerusalem, or the place God said he passed, in ruins after he had known it inhabited, he said, by what means God would bring it back after its ruin; the editor notes that death can mean the ruin of land. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 446} Some said his saying was doubt about God's power to revive him, so God showed him His power by making a parable in himself, then showed him the place whose rebuilding he had doubted, more alive and better built than it had been before its ruin. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 446} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 447} He says the speaker had known it inhabited, then saw it desolate, its people perished by killing and captivity, with nothing left but traces; so he said in astonishment, by what means God would bring it back, according to some interpreters, and God showed him the way of reviving through what He made as a parable in himself and in his waterskin and food, and then made known His power over that and other things by letting him see with his own eyes the revival of what he had thought marvellous, so that he said what he said of his knowledge of God's power. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 447} This is his explanation. In the notes the editor explains that the two forms are comparatives, not verbs, and corrects a reading of the waterskin and other wording. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 446} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 447} Al-Tabari notes that the occasion of his saying so is given in a report of Wahb ibn Munabbih, which begins at the top of p. 447 and runs on; we stop before it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 447}
