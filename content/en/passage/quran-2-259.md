## Summary
Verse 259 of [[passage/quran-2|Surat al-Baqara]], a Medinan surah. {cite:tanzil-quran}

## Place in the museum
Its place: verse 259 of 286 verses in the surah. The text is kept in the frozen Tanzil mushaf and is not retyped here.

Al-Tabari opens ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]) on p. 438 saying that it matches what came before in wonder expressed to Muhammad, and is joined to the preceding verse even though the wording differs, because the two are alike in sense, as the Arabs join speech to a likeness already given though the wording differs. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 438} He mentions that some Basran grammarians claimed the particle of likeness in it is superfluous, so that the sense is: have you not seen the one who disputed with Abraham, or the one who went by a village; al-Tabari objects that nothing in God's Book may be without meaning, as he showed earlier; this is his objection and view. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 438}

He then says the interpreters differed on who passed by the ruined village. Some said Uzayr: Najiya ibn Ka'b (5882), Sulayman ibn Buraydah (5883), Qatada (5884), al-Rabi', Ikrima, al-Suddi (5888), al-Dahhak (5889), and Ibn Abbas according to Salm al-Khawwas (5890); the editor gives biographies of Najiya and Salm al-Khawwas and corrects a reading. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 439} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 440} Others said it was Urmiya ibn Hilqiya, and it was reported from Muhammad ibn Ishaq that Urmiya is al-Khidr, that the name of al-Khidr, as Wahb ibn Munabbih claimed from the Children of Israel, is Urmiya son of Hilqiya of the tribe of Aaron son of Amram (5891); from Wahb ibn Munabbih (5892, 5893, 5894) that when Jerusalem was destroyed and the books burned Urmiya stood at the side of the mountain and put his question about the village's revival; from Abdullah ibn Ubayd ibn Umayr (5895, 5896) that he was a prophet named Urmiya; and from Bakr ibn Mudar (5897) that they say, and God knows best, it was Urmiya. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 440} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 441} In the notes the editor says the name in their books is Jeremiah, that al-Tabari rejected this view in his History, and comments on points in the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 440} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 441} These are reports from Successors, Wahb ibn Munabbih and People of the Book sources, and the museum does not rule on them.

Al-Tabari then says that the soundest view is that God made His prophet wonder at the one who, seeing a village fallen to ruins, asked how God would revive it once it had perished, though he knew God had begun its creation from nothing, so that knowledge of the power to begin it did not satisfy him; he has no sound clear evidence for the speaker's name, which could be Uzayr or Urmiya, and there is no need to know it, since the aim was not to tell people the name of the speaker; this is his choice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 441} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 442} He adds that the aim was to inform those who denied God's power to revive His creation after their death, from Quraysh and the other Arabs who denied it, and to establish the proof against the Jews around the Prophet's place of emigration by showing him what removes their doubt, since these reports were not known to Muhammad and his people, who were unlettered with no scripture, so the People of the Book knew he could not have known them save by revelation; had the aim been the speaker's name, the proof would have been set out in a way that removes excuse, but the aim was to censure his saying; this is his reasoning. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 442} In the notes the editor explains the sentence structure and the word for unlettered. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 442}

On the village itself the interpreters differed. Some said it is Jerusalem: from Wahb ibn Munabbih (5898 to 5900), that when Urmiya saw Jerusalem's ruin like a great mountain he said what he said; from Qatada (5901), that they were told it was Jerusalem, past which Uzayr went after Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon had destroyed it; from al-Dahhak (5902), that he passed the holy land; from Ikrima (5903) and al-Rabi' (5904), that the village is Jerusalem, past which Uzayr went after Nebuchadnezzar had destroyed it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 442} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 443} In the note the editor says the manuscript writes Nebuchadnezzar's name as two words. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 443} These reports concern Israelite history and sources as transmitted, and the museum does not rule on them. Al-Tabari then mentions that others said it is the village where God destroyed those who left their homes by the thousand in fear of death, in ([[passage/quran-2-243|2:243]]); their argument begins on the next page. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 443}

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]) from p. 444: from Ibn Zayd (5905), the village the man passed was the village struck by plague, then he recounts the story of those who left their homes by the thousand in fear of death as in ([[passage/quran-2-243|2:243]]), whom God caused to die in the very place they went seeking life and then revived, and a man passed by it when it was bleached bones, stood looking and asked how it could be revived, so God took him in death for a full century and then raised him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 444} Al-Tabari says the sound view on that is the same as on the name of the speaker, the two being alike, meaning that the village need not be identified; this is his choice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 444}

Al-Tabari then explains the word for desolate as emptied of its people and inhabitants and notes its linguistic uses; the roofs or structures are buildings and houses, the singular meaning any building, and one says a man built a house; he cites the use in ([[passage/quran-7-137|7:137]]) for building, and the booths of Mecca meaning its tents and structures. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 444} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 445} In the notes the editor sets out the plural of paucity, corrects the printed text by the manuscript and quotes the Lisan al-Arab and Ibn al-Athir on the booths of Mecca. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 444} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 445} From Ibn Abbas (5906), via Ibn Jurayj: desolate means ruined; Ibn Jurayj adds that they were told that Uzayr went out, stood by Jerusalem which Nebuchadnezzar had destroyed, and said, after all that you had of holiness, fighters and wealth, and he grieved. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 445} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 446} From al-Dahhak (5907): it is ruined; from al-Rabi' (5908): Uzayr passed by it after Nebuchadnezzar had destroyed it; from al-Suddi (5909): fallen upon its roofs. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 446}

Al-Tabari then explains the part about the speaker being made to die for a full century. The meaning, as we are told, is that when the speaker passed Jerusalem, or the place God said he passed, in ruins after he had known it inhabited, he said, by what means God would bring it back after its ruin; the editor notes that death can mean the ruin of land. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 446} Some said his saying was doubt about God's power to revive him, so God showed him His power by making a parable in himself, then showed him the place whose rebuilding he had doubted, more alive and better built than it had been before its ruin. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 446} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 447} He says the speaker had known it inhabited, then saw it desolate, its people perished by killing and captivity, with nothing left but traces; so he said in astonishment, by what means God would bring it back, according to some interpreters, and God showed him the way of reviving through what He made as a parable in himself and in his waterskin and food, and then made known His power over that and other things by letting him see with his own eyes the revival of what he had thought marvellous, so that he said what he said of his knowledge of God's power. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 447} This is his explanation. In the notes the editor explains that the two forms are comparatives, not verbs, and corrects a reading of the waterskin and other wording. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 446} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 447} Al-Tabari notes that the occasion of his saying so is given in a report of Wahb ibn Munabbih, which begins at the top of p. 447 and runs on; we stop before it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 447}

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]) from p. 447: report 5910 is long, transmitted by Ibn Humayd from Salama from Ibn Ishaq from one not accused, from Wahb ibn Munabbih al-Yamani; it belongs to the reports of the Children of Israel and their sources, and I give its outline as it stands, without a museum ruling on its soundness. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 447} Wahb says God told Urmiya, when sending him as a prophet to the Children of Israel, that He chose him before creating him, sanctified him before shaping him in the womb and made him a prophet before he reached maturity; He sent him to the king of Israel to guide him. Then wrongdoing grew among the Israelites, they committed sins and forgot what God had done for them, including saving them from Sennacherib; God told Urmiya to recount to them what he was commanded and remind them of His favours, then told him He would destroy the Israelites by Japheth, meaning the people of Babylon; Urmiya cried out and wept, tore his clothes and put ashes on his head. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 448} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 449} God called him, asking if what He had revealed troubled him, and he said yes, destroy me before I see in Israel what I cannot bear; God said He would not destroy Jerusalem and the Israelites until the matter came from him; Urmiya was glad, and said he would never bid his Lord to destroy them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 449} Then they stayed three years only increasing in disobedience, and revelation lessened; their king warned them of God's might and they refused. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 449} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 450} God cast into the heart of Nebuchadnezzar son of Nabuzaradan, grandson of Sennacherib, to march on Jerusalem and do what his grandfather had wanted; he set out with six hundred thousand banners, and when the king heard he asked Urmiya about God's promise, who said that his Lord does not break a promise and he was confident of Him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 450}

God then sent an angel in the form of an Israelite man, who sought Urmiya's ruling about his kin, to whom he did good and whom his kindness only angered; Urmiya told him to do good between himself and God and keep the ties God commanded; the man came back some days later, by which time Nebuchadnezzar's armies had camped around Jerusalem more numerous than locusts and the Israelites were terrified. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 450} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 451} The man came a third time while Urmiya sat on Jerusalem's wall rejoicing in the help promised, and said he had seen them that day in a great act of God's displeasure and asked him to pray to his Lord to destroy them; Urmiya said to the Lord of heaven and earth: if they are right, spare them, and if they are in Your displeasure, destroy them. God sent a thunderbolt from heaven in Jerusalem, the place of offering burned and seven of its gates sank; Urmiya cried out and asked of his promise, and a voice said that what befell them came only through the ruling he gave our messenger; he was certain it was his own ruling given three times and that the visitor was his Lord's messenger, and he flew off until he joined the wild beasts. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 451} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 452}

Nebuchadnezzar and his army entered Jerusalem, killed the Israelites until he had destroyed them and ruined Jerusalem, and ordered each of his men to fill his shield with earth and throw it in, so they filled it; he returned to Babylon with captives, choosing seventy thousand boys among them, including Daniel, Azariah, Mishael and Hananiah; he divided the captives in three, a third left in Syria, a third taken captive and a third killed, and took the vessels of Jerusalem to Babylon. This was the first blow God sent upon the Israelites for their misdeeds and wrongdoing. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 452} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 453}

Then, according to the report, Urmiya came on a donkey with grape juice in a skin and a basket of figs to Ilya; seeing the ruin he was seized by doubt and wondered about its revival, so God caused him to die for a century, with his donkey, juice and figs beside him, and blinded all eyes to him; then God raised him and asked the length of his stay; he reckoned about a day or less and was told a century, and was told to look at his food and drink, which had not changed, and at his donkey, whose bones he saw joined by veins and sinew, clothed with flesh, then life came in it and it rose braying; the juice and figs were as he had left them; having seen God's power he said what showed his knowledge of it. God then gave Urmiya long life, and he is the one seen in the wilds and towns. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 453} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 454}

In the notes the editor compares the printed text, the manuscript and al-Tabari's History, notes that Urmiya is al-Khidr according to Wahb, that the phrase about not bidding his Lord is a figure meaning not asking or praying, gives the names of Nebuchadnezzar and his forebears as in their books and the boys' names as in the book of Daniel, explains the skin, and comments on a verb form; these are his. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 449} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 450} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 453} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 454}

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]) from p. 454: from Wahb ibn Munabbih (5911), via Abd al-Samad ibn Ma'qil: God revealed to Urmiya, while in Egypt, to go to the land of Ilya, since this was not a land to stay in; he rode his donkey with a basket of grapes and figs and a new waterskin full of water; when the shape of Jerusalem and the villages and mosques around it came into view he saw indescribable ruin, and seeing the destruction like a great mountain he wondered about its revival; he went on until he settled, tied his donkey with a new rope, hung up his skin, and God cast sleep on him and took his soul for a full century. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 454} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 455} When seventy years of the century had passed God sent an angel to a great king of Persia called Yusak, bidding him march with his people and rebuild Jerusalem, Ilya and its land until it was more prosperous than before; he asked for three days to prepare, then appointed three hundred stewards each with a thousand workers and their tools, and they set out with three hundred thousand workers. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 455} Once they were at work God returned the spirit of life to Urmiya's eye while the rest of his body was dead, and he saw Ilya and the villages, mosques and rivers around it, with the ploughmen working and renewal until it was as it had been; after thirty more years, completing the century, the spirit was returned to him; he saw his food and drink unchanged, his donkey standing as on the day he tied it, the new rope on its neck still fresh though a century of wind, cold and heat had passed over it and none of it had worn; his body had wasted away and God grew him new flesh and set his bones in place as he watched; he was told to look at his food, drink, donkey and the bones as they are raised and clothed with flesh, and when it was clear to him he said that he knew God's power over everything. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 455} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 456}

Also from Wahb (5912): when Jerusalem was destroyed and the books burned Urmiya stood on the mountainside and wondered about its revival; God caused him to die for a century, then restored those of the Israelites who returned after seventy years, who rebuilt it over thirty years to complete the century; when the century was done God returned his spirit, and it had been rebuilt as before; he looked at the bones joining and being clothed with sinew and flesh, and said what he said of God's power; his food was figs in a basket and his drink a jar with water. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 456} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 457} In the notes the editor gives the biographies of Muhammad ibn Askar and the Ibn Zanjawayh men, explains words and compares the printed text, the manuscript and al-Tabari's History; these are his. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 455} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 456} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 457}

From al-Suddi (5913): Uzayr was coming from Syria on a donkey with juice, grapes and figs; passing the village he stopped, turned his hand over and wondered how it could be revived, not out of denial or doubt; God caused him and his donkey to die, a hundred years passed, then God revived Uzayr and he was asked the length of his stay; he answered, and was told it was a century, and was told to look at his food of figs and grapes and his drink of juice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 457}

Al-Tabari then explains the part on raising and the question of the length of stay. To raise him means to stir him living after his death; the interrogative asks the amount of a number; the sense is that God asked him how long he had remained dead before being raised, and he answered no more than a day; it is said the one raised is Urmiya, or Uzayr, or whoever it was. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 457} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 458} Al-Tabari says he answered so because God took his soul at the start of the day and restored it at the end of the day after the century; he thought the sun had set and estimated a day, then saw part of it remaining and revised to part of a day; he cites the sense of 'rather' in the place of 'or' in ([[passage/quran-37-147|37:147]]), so that the second was a retraction of the first; this is his explanation. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 458} From Qatada (5914, 5915): they were told he died in mid-morning and was raised before sunset, so estimated a day, then saw part of the sun and said part of a day; from al-Rabi' (5916), that God made him die for a century, then raised him; from Ibn Jurayj (5917), that he stood at Jerusalem after Nebuchadnezzar had ruined it and wondered how God would restore it; they were told he died in mid-morning and was raised before sunset after the century. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 458} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 459} In the note the editor explains 'or' in the sense of 'rather' and gives a cross-reference. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 458} These are reports from Wahb ibn Munabbih and other sources on the Children of Israel, given as transmitted without a museum ruling on their soundness.

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]) from p. 459: al-Tabari explains the command to look at the food and drink as meaning the passing years did not change them; he mentions that, according to some, his food was a basket of figs and grapes and his drink a jar of water; others say a basket of grapes and a basket of figs with a skin of juice; others a basket of figs and a jar of wine or a small wine skin; he refers to earlier discussions and promises more later. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 459} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 460} He then gives two readings of the word in question. The first drops the final letter on running on and keeps it when pausing; those who read so take that letter as an added support, as in ([[passage/quran-6-90|6:90]]), and the verb as derived from tasannaytu, arguing that the word for year is pluralised with an ending that makes this regular; this is the reading of most readers of Kufa, and part of the explanation is from al-Farra' in his Ma'ani al-Qur'an. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 460} The second keeps the letter both running on and pausing, making it the verb's final root letter, from asnahtu 'I stayed a year among the people'; this is the reading of most readers of Medina and the Hijaz. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 461} Al-Tabari says the correct reading in his view is to keep the letter in both running on and pausing, since it is written in the Muslims' codex and has a sound basis in both cases; this is his choice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 461} The word means for him that the years did not pass over it so that it changed, in the usage of those who say 'I stayed a year among you', and he cites a verse of Suwayd ibn al-Samit al-Ansari, or Abu Uhayha ibn al-Julah, about palms that are not alternate-bearing. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 461} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 462} He says no letter of God's Book may be dropped, whether running on or pausing, when it has a recognised basis in Arabic; if someone objects that the codex has added letters for pausing, as in ([[passage/quran-6-90|6:90]]) and ([[passage/quran-69-25|69:25]]), those are beyond doubt added letters, whereas what could be a root letter may not be turned into an added one; and the Arabs sometimes join speech with an added letter and pronounce it as in a pause. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 462} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 463} In the notes the editor gives the verses with vocabulary and readings, quotes al-Farra', explains the support letter and corrects the printed text by the manuscript; these are his. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 460} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 461} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 462} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 463}

In support, al-Tabari relays (5918) from Hani, freedman of Uthman: I was the messenger between Uthman and Zayd ibn Thabit; Zayd said to ask him whether it is with the letter or without, and Uthman said, put the letter in it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 463} And from Hani al-Barbari (5919), that he was with Uthman while the codices were being checked and was sent with a sheep's shoulder blade to Ubayy ibn Ka'b bearing the word in its form and words from ([[passage/quran-86-17|86:17]]) and ([[passage/quran-30-30|30:30]]); Ubayy called for the ink-pot and erased one of the two lam letters, wrote the word in ([[passage/quran-30-30|30:30]]) and wrote the word in question adding the letter to it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 463} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 464} Al-Tabari says, on what can be read of the page, that if it came from another verb Ubayy would not have added the letter, nor would Uthman have ordered it, and that the like was reported from Zayd ibn Thabit; this is his argument. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 464} The editor gives biographies of some transmitters of the two reports and doubts that their chains are intact; these are his. The text of p. 464 is damaged in the scan in several lines, and we rely only on what is legible. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 463} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 464} The reports about the codices and readings are given as transmitted.

Al-Tabari then says the interpreters differed on the word's meaning. Some said it did not change: Wahb ibn Munabbih (5920), Qatada (5921, 5922) and al-Suddi (5923) give the meaning as unchanged. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 464} We stop here, since the next page carries what follows.

The rest of ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]) from p. 464: from al-Suddi (5923), completing the earlier report: look at your food of figs and grapes and your drink of juice, which had not changed, so the figs and grapes had not turned sour or the juice fermented, both remaining sweet as they were; for he had come from Syria on a donkey with juice, grapes and figs, and God caused him and his donkey to die, and a century passed over them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 465} From al-Dahhak (5924, 5925): it did not change though a century had passed; from Ibn Abbas (5926), Ikrima (5927) and Ibn Zayd (5928): it did not change in a hundred years. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 465} From Bakr ibn Mudar (5929): they claim in some books that Urmiya was in Ilya when Nebuchadnezzar destroyed it and went to Egypt, then God told him to leave there for Jerusalem; he came and found it in ruins and wondered about its revival, so God caused him to die for a century and raised him, and his donkey was alive and standing at its tether, his food a basket of grapes and a basket of figs unchanged; and Yunus said that Salm al-Khawwas told them his food and drink was a basket of grapes, a basket of figs and a skin of juice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 465} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 466} In the note the editor explains the tether and the basket and corrects the name of Salm al-Khawwas. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 466}

Others said the word means it did not rot: Mujahid (5930, 5931), and from him via Ibn Jurayj (5932), that the food was a basket of figs and the drink a jar of wine, and the word means it did not rot. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 466} Al-Tabari then says he supposes that Mujahid, al-Rabi' and those who spoke as they did took the word to belong with the clay of dark mud in ([[passage/quran-15-26|15:26]]), meaning altered in smell by rotting, and he has already shown that it is not so. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 466} The editor notes that al-Tabari mentioned no report from al-Rabi' before this, so one may have dropped from the copyist's text, and that al-Rabi''s view appeared in report 5916. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 466} He adds that if someone thought it came from the root for stale water, as in the description of Paradise's rivers whose water does not turn in ([[passage/quran-47-15|47:15]]), the word would be built differently; and if the glottal stop were dropped the consonant would be pronounced without doubling and without the final letter, which makes it clear that it cannot come from that root; this is his argument. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 466} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 467}

Al-Tabari then turns to the command to look at the donkey. The interpreters differed on it. Some said the sense is: look at My reviving of your donkey and at its bones, how He restores them and covers them again. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 467} Those who take this view then differed. Some said God said this to him after reviving him in a sound form, then wished to revive his donkey, to show him how He revives the village he saw in ruins and wondered about; report 5933 begins at the foot of the page and we stop before it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 467} The reports from Mujahid, al-Rabi' and others are given as transmitted, and al-Tabari's argument against the derivation is attributed to him.

More on the donkey in ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]). Report 5933 is from Wahb ibn Munabbih via Ibn Ishaq, from one not accused: God raised him and asked how long he had stayed, and he answered a day or part of one; then he looked at his donkey, whose bones were joining one to another with sinews and nerves, then flesh clothed it until it stood whole and the spirit entered it and it stood braying; and he looked at his juice and figs and they were as he had left them, unchanged, so when he saw God's power he said he knew the reach of God's power. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 467} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 468} In a note the editor comments on a reading of a phrase in the printed text and the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 468}

From al-Suddi (5934): God revived Uzayr and questioned him; he said a day or part of one, and was told he had stayed a hundred years and was to look at his food and drink and at his donkey that had perished and whose bones had decayed. God sent a wind that brought the donkey's bones from plain and mountain, where birds and beasts had carried them, and they gathered and were fitted together while he watched; then they were clothed with flesh and blood and it stood a donkey without spirit; then an angel came walking, took hold of the donkey's nostril and blew into it, and it brayed; and he said he knew the reach of God's power. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 468} Al-Tabari explains the meaning on this reading: look at Our reviving your donkey and at its bones, how they are put together again and clothed, and We make you a sign for them. On his view something is omitted from the speech, as its plain wording indicates it, and the definite article in the mention of the bones stands in for the pronoun meant, that is, the donkey's bones; this is al-Tabari's construction. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 468}

Others of this group said: rather God said this to him after He breathed the spirit into his eyes, the first part of him to receive it, after fashioning him whole and before reviving his donkey. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 469} Mujahid (5935, 5936): he was a man of the Children of Israel with the spirit breathed into his eyes, so he saw his whole frame as God revived it, and his donkey as God revived it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 469} Ibn Jurayj (5937): he began with the eyes, breathed the spirit into them, then the bones were put together and joined, then sinews, veins and flesh; then he looked at his donkey, decayed with whitened bones where he had tied it, and the bones were called to gather as God would send down a spirit upon them, so they joined, then sinews, veins, flesh, skin and hair; his donkey had been young and God revived it grown old and shrivelled, with only skin left from the long time; and his food was a basket of grapes and his drink a jar of wine. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 469} The editor adds corrections to the manuscript readings in the notes. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 469} These reports are given as transmitted, without a verdict from the museum.

In ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]) a third strand of this reading: God put the spirit in his head and sight while his body was dead, and he saw his donkey standing as on the day he tied it and his food and drink as on the day he stopped at the place; then God said to him to look at the bones of his own self, how they are put together again. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 470} From Wahb ibn Munabbih (5938): God returned the spirit of life to Urmiya's eye while the rest of his body was dead, and he looked at his food and drink unchanged, and at his donkey standing as on the day he tied it, having neither eaten nor drunk, and at the rope on the donkey's neck, unchanged and new. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 470} In the notes the editor explains the phrase "the rest of him", refers to report 5911, and says the number of report 5939 does not show on the printed page, the next being numbered 5940. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 470}

From al-Dahhak (5940, 5941): he looked at his donkey standing after a hundred years and at his food unchanged; the first part God revived was his head, and he watched the rest of his frame being made and joined, and when it became clear to him he said he knew the reach of God's power. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 470} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 471} From Qatada (5942): we were told that the first part made was his head, then his eyes were fitted, then he was told to look, and his bones began to join one to another as he watched, and he said he knew the reach of God's power. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 471} From al-Rabi' (5943): his donkey stood by him as it was; and we were told, God knows best, that his eyes were the first part made, then he was told to look, and he watched the bones join through his eyes. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 471} The editor's notes correct manuscript readings and identify the transmitter al-Husayn ibn al-Faraj in the chain of 5940. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 470} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 471} We stop before report 5944 (Ibn Zayd), which runs onto the next page. These reports are given as transmitted, without a verdict from the museum.

From Ibn Zayd (5944): God put the spirit into his sight and tongue and told him to call with his tongue and look with his sight; he looked at the skull and called for each bone to join its fellow, and each came to its own until they were joined while he watched, even a bone fragment coming to the place it had broken from and sticking there; then God bound them with sinews and veins, ran flesh and skin over them and breathed the spirit in; when it was clear to him he said he knew the reach of God's power; then he was told to call the bones of the one whose revival he had questioned, as he had called his own, and God revived them as He revived him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 471} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 472} From Bakr ibn Mudar (5945): they claim in some books that God caused Urmiya to die for a hundred years and raised him, and his donkey was alive and standing at its tether; God returned his sight and placed the spirit in him thirty years before he was raised, and he looked at Jerusalem and how it was rebuilt with what surrounded it; and they say, God knows best, that he is the one mentioned in ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 472} On al-Tabari's construction the sense for these interpreters is: look at your donkey, and We make you a sign for them, and look at your bones, how God puts them together again after their decay and clothes them with flesh, reviving them with your life, so that you know how God revives villages and their people after their death. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 472} In a note the editor says that here a part of the old division from which the copy was taken ends, giving the copyist's closing and opening formulas, and that the spelling of Urmiya's name differs between the printed text and the manuscript. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 472}

Al-Tabari's choice. Abu Jafar says the soundest reading is that of those who said God raised the questioner from his death and then showed him plainly, in himself, his food and his donkey, the like of what he had thought strange about the village's revival; what he was shown of reviving himself and his donkey was made a likeness for what he wondered at in the revival of the village's people, and the lesson in his food and drink was made a proof against him of how God revives the village's dwellings and settlements; this is the meaning of Mujahid's words given earlier. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 473} His reasoning, which is his own: the command to look at the bones means the bones one sees with one's eyes; and the donkey's bones had decayed, by every interpreter's account, just as the addressee's had; so the command cannot be confined to the donkey's bones or to his own, and it is better to take it as a command to look at everything within his sight that decay had reached, since God made all of it a proof for him, a lesson and an admonition. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 473}

Al-Tabari then explains the next part of ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]): the clause on making him a sign for them means We caused you to die for a hundred years and then raised you. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 473} He explains that the conjunction with the purposive particle shows it is the purpose of an action after it, as if to say We did that to make you so and so; without the conjunction the particle would be the purpose of the action before it, giving: look at your donkey so that We make you a sign for them. The editor refers to al-Farra' in a note. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 473} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 474} He says the meaning is that God makes him a proof against whoever is ignorant of His power or doubts His greatness, for He is able to bring death and life, annihilation and creation, and no one else has power over these. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 474}

Some interpreters said he was a sign for them in that he came after a hundred years to his son and grandson as a young man while they were old men; from al-A'mash (5946): young, with his children old. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 474} Others said the sense is that he came when those who knew him had died, so he was a sign to those of his people who came to him; report 5947 from al-Suddi begins at the foot of the page and we stop before it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 474} These reports are given as transmitted, without a verdict from the museum.

In ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]), report 5947 from al-Suddi: he returned to his family and found his house sold and rebuilt and those who knew him dead; he told the people in it to leave his house, and when they asked who he was he said he was Uzayr, and they said had Uzayr not died long ago, and he said he was that one and this was his case; when they recognised it they left the house and gave it to him. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 474} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 475} Al-Tabari then says that the better reading of the verse is that God reports that He made the one described a proof for people: a proof against those who knew him among his children and people, who knew of his death and God's reviving him after it, and against those to whom he was sent from among them; this is his choice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 475}

He then moves to the last part of the command to look at the bones, noting that he has already shown that the bones in question are those of his own body and his donkey's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 475} The reciters differed on the verb for raising them. Most Kufan reciters read it with a z, with the first letter vowelled with damma, meaning We fit them one upon another and move them to places in the body; the root sense of that verb is rising, from which one says a boy has risen when he has grown up, and the wife's rebellion against her husband, and a raised piece of ground is called by related nouns; so on this reading it means how We raise them from their places in the ground and return them to their places in the body. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 475} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 476} The editor notes that he favours the verbal noun in the printed text and that the manuscript is poor and corrupt here. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 475} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 476} A group of interpreters took it this way: from Ibn Abbas (5948), how We bring them out, and from al-Suddi (5949), We move them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 476}

Others read it with an r, which is the reading of most Medina reciters, from the verb meaning God revived the dead, so it means how We revive them then clothe them with flesh: Mujahid (5950, 5951), look at them as God revives them; Qatada (5952), the same; Ibn Zayd (5953), how We revive them. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 476} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 477} Some who read it with an r argued from ([[passage/quran-80-22|80:22]]), holding that this clause should be joined to it, and the editor notes the report of al-Farra' from Ibn Abbas. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 477} Some read it with an r and a fathah on the first letter, in the sense of spreading out a thing and folding it, and the editor notes al-Farra''s report from al-Hasan. Al-Tabari says that reading is not praiseworthy, because the Arabs do not say the dead are spread out in this sense but say God revived the dead and they came alive; he argues from ([[passage/quran-80-22|80:22]]) and ([[passage/quran-21-21|21:21]]), adds that one who lives after death is said to have come alive, and cites a line of al-A'sha about a dead man come alive at whom people wonder, whose source in his Diwan the editor gives. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 477} We stop here, before the rest of the discussion. These readings and reports are given as transmitted, al-Tabari's arguments are attributed to him, and the museum gives no verdict.

The reading dispute over the verb in ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]) continues. Al-Tabari reports hearing from the Arabs that one who had mange and recovered was said to have come alive again in this sense; the editor refers to al-Farra' and gives in a note the lines of al-A'sha that precede the cited line, describing his beloved, with explanations of some words. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 477} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 478} Al-Tabari then says: in my view the two verbs, one meaning to set up and one meaning to revive, are close in sense, for the first is fitting together, fixing and returning bone to bone, the second is restoring life to bones, and restoring them is undoubtedly returning them to their places in the body after they left; so though they differ in wording they are close in meaning, and the community has come with both readings in a way that removes any excuse and establishes proof, so whichever one reads is right, since the two meanings agree, and no proof requires judging one correct over the other; this is his choice. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 478} The editor notes that the copy is badly corrupt here, that he worked to correct it, and explains the phrase on the agreement of the two meanings. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 478}

He adds that if someone thinks the reading meaning to revive is the better, because the one told to look was told so that he would see plainly what he denied, he is mistaken, for the reviving of the bones here means returning them to the places in the body of the one looked at so that the spirit that left at death could be restored, as the mention of clothing them with flesh shows, and the spirit was undoubtedly breathed into the bones only after they were fitted together and clothed with flesh. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 479} Since the meaning of the first verb is fitting the bones together and returning them to their places, and that is also the meaning of the second, he holds their meanings equal and in agreement, and in this he finds support for his view. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 479} The third reading, with a fathah on the first letter and an r, he judges impermissible, because it departs from the reading of the Muslims and from sound, eloquent Arabic; this judgement is his, not the museum's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 479} The editor adds notes correcting and pointing the copy's wording. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 479}

Al-Tabari then explains the last part of the verse, the clothing of the bones with flesh: the pronoun refers to the bones, and the sense of clothing is dressing them in flesh and covering them with it as a person's garment covers his body; the Arabs call anything that covers and hides something its dress and clothing. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 480} He cites a line of al-Nabigha al-Ja'di in which the poet thanks God that his term did not come until he had put on a garment of Islam, making Islam a clothing because it covered what he had been and hid it. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 480} In a note the editor reports that the line is also ascribed to Labid ibn Rabi'a and to Qurra ibn Nufatha al-Saluli, gives Ibn Abd al-Barr's view on its attribution and what Abu al-Faraj and Abu Ubayda said, and refers to further sources; these are the editor's remarks, not al-Tabari's. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 480} These statements are given as transmitted, without a verdict from the museum.

The close of ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]). Al-Tabari explains the clause on the matter becoming clear to him and what he said then: the sense is that when what he had thought strange of God's power and greatness became plain to him by sight, he said, after seeing and clarification, what the verse ends with on the reach of God's power over all things. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 481} The reciters then differed on the form of the first verb in that closing statement. Some read it as a command, joining the first letter and without a vowel on the last, which is the reading of most Kufan reciters, and they say it is in the reading of Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud with a verb meaning it was said, so that God commanded the one He had revived after his death; it is also reported from Ibn Abbas. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 481} Harun (5954): it is in Abd Allah's reading as a command; Ibn Tawus from his father (5955), who heard Ibn Abbas read it as a command and say it was said to him; al-Tabari is unsure of the attribution to the father and says I think so; and the editor gives in a note an account of Ahmad ibn Yusuf al-Taghlibi in the chain of 5954 and corrects a reading of the name. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 481} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 482} Al-Rabi' (5956): we were told, God knows best, that he was told to look and watched the bones join one to another with his eyes, and then it was said to him to know. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 482} On this reading al-Tabari gives the sense: when what became clear to him of God's matter and power was clear, God said to him to know now that His power is all-embracing; and he adds that if an interpreter took the command as coming from the narrator of the story, it would also be sound, like someone saying know that such and such, as a command addressed to another while meaning himself. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 482}

Others read it with a cutting alif and a damma on the last letter, as a first-person statement, meaning that the one to whom it became clear said I know now; this is the reading of most Medina reciters and some Iraqi reciters, and a group of interpreters took it so. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 482} Wahb ibn Munabbih via Ibn Ishaq from one not accused (5957) and via Abd al-Samad ibn Ma'qil (5958): when he saw God's power he said it; Qatada (5959): it was in the eye of a prophet, meaning the fitting together of the bones; al-Suddi (5960): Uzayr said it on seeing the reviving of his donkey; al-Dahhak (5961): he watched everything of him being joined, and when it became clear to him said it; Ibn Zayd (5962): the like. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 482} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 483} The editor adds notes on the printed text and the manuscript and on a word the copyist dropped. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 482} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 483}

Al-Tabari's choice. Abu Jafar says the sounder of the two readings is that with the joined alif and unvowelled last letter, as a command from God to the one He revived after his death, that he should know that the God who showed him with his own eyes His great power in reviving him and his donkey after a hundred years of death and decay, until they were as on the day their spirits were taken, and in keeping his food and drink unchanged for a century, is likewise able over everything. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 484} His ground, which is his own, is that what precedes is God's command and address to the one He revived, in telling him to look at the food, drink, donkey and bones; so when this became clear to him as an answer to his question to his Lord, God said to him to know that the One who did these things is able over other things as over what he saw, just as He said to His friend Abraham, after answering his request to see how He gives life to the dead, that he should know God is mighty and wise; so the questioner was likewise told, after being shown how, to know. He points to the verse that follows in ([[passage/quran-2-260|2:260]]). {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 484} The editor's notes explain the sentence's grammar and refer to al-Farra'. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v05 p. 484} This ends the book's commentary on ([[passage/quran-2-259|2:259]]) at p. 484.
