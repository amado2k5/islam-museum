**Commentary on verse 30 (pp. 62-63)**

Al-Tabari says: the women talked about Joseph's affair and the Aziz's wife's in the city of Egypt, and what happened between them spread and was not concealed, and they said "the Aziz's wife is soliciting her young man", that is, her slave, "against himself". Report 19137, from Ibn Ishaq, through Ibn Humayd and Salama: the talk spread in the town and the women talked of his affair and hers. As for "the Aziz", in Arab speech it means "the king", and from it is a line of Abu Du'ad about what was displayed to an "aziz" on a day of dew, and it is from "izza" (might); the editor notes that this explanation is from the rare vocabulary, is not in the dictionaries, and that he found the line nowhere else. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 62}

On "he has smitten her with love" he says: love of Joseph reached the shaghaf of her heart and entered beneath it until it overcame her heart. The shaghaf of the heart is its veil and the covering in which it lies, and al-Nabigha al-Dhubyani meant it in a line describing a care that entered as the shaghaf is entered, sought by the fingers; the editor explains that the fingers are the physicians' fingers, that the lexicographers derive it from "shughaf" with damma on the shin, an ailment that takes below the ribs on the right side and kills if it reaches the spleen, and that this is the better of the two accounts. Reports 19138 to 19140, from Ikrima and Mujahid: his love entered beneath the shaghaf, or into her shaghaf. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 63}

Reports 19141 to 19144 on "he has smitten her with love": from Mujahid, his love was in her shaghaf; from Ibn Abbas, through Muhammad ibn Sa'd, his father, his uncle and his father from his father: it clung to her with love, and through al-Muthanna, Abd Allah ibn Salih, Mu'awiya and Ali: it overcame her. Report 19145, from al-Sha'bi, through Abu Kurayb, Waki', his father and Ayyub ibn A'idh al-Ta'i: "mashghuf" is the lover and "mash'uf" is the madman; the editor notes that al-Sha'bi meant to distinguish them, that the second has the plain ayn, and that Ayyub ibn A'idh is trustworthy. Reports 19146 to 19152, from Abu Raja', al-Hasan and Qatada: love took her inwardly, and some related "love made her sincere"; Abu Bishr says the people of Medina say "it took her inwardly"; from Qatada: her love for him took hold of her within. Reports 19153 and 19154, from Qatada and Mujahid: it clung to her. Report 19155, from al-Dahhak: it is the love clinging to the heart. Report 19156, from him: she was undone with love for him, and the shaghaf is the shaghaf of the heart. Report 19157, from al-Suddi: the shaghaf is a skin over the heart called the tongue of the heart, meaning love entered the skin until it reached the heart; the editor suggests in a footnote that the right reading is "the covering of the heart", because they explained the word as the heart's wrapper and a skin below it like a veil. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 64}

The readers differed in reading it. Most readers of the cities read "shaghafaha" with ghayn, in the sense he described; Abu Raja' read "sha'afaha" with ayn. Reports 19158 to 19161, from Abu Raja', Awf and al-A'raj, with ayn; al-A'raj said, "sha'afaha" when he loves her. These took the sense to be that love had covered her entirely. Some Kufan scholars of the Arabs' speech said it comes from the saying "she was made love-struck by it", as if it took her in every direction, from the "sha'af" of the mountains, their peaks. It is reported from Ibrahim al-Nakha'i that he said: "shaghaf" is the shaghaf of love and "sha'af" is the sha'af of a beast when startled. Report 19162, from al-Qasim, through al-Harith, Abu Awana and Mughira: Ibrahim holds that the root of "sha'af" is fright, which is right in the root, but the Arabs sometimes borrowed the word and put it elsewhere; he cites a line of Imru' al-Qays, in which he mentions his beloved and her husband and says he has struck her heart with passion as the man smearing a mangy camel with tar strikes it; the woman's passion is from love and the camel's is from fright, so he likened the torment and burning of love to that. The editor gives the surrounding lines and refers to the divan and the Lisan, and thinks the manuscript wording needs a passive form. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 66}

Ibn Zayd said in this report 19163, through Yunus and Ibn Wahb: "shaghaf" and "sha'af" are different, "sha'af" in hatred and "shaghaf" in love. This statement of Ibn Zayd has no sense, for "sha'af" in Arab speech meaning general love is too well known for one who knows their speech to be unaware of it. Al-Tabari said: the right reading in our view is "shaghafaha" with ghayn, by the consensus of the authoritative readers. On "we see her in manifest error" they said: we see the Aziz's wife, in soliciting her young man and the overpowering of her heart by love of him, to be in a mistaken deed and a swerving from the right course, manifest to whoever considers it and knows it is error and mistake, neither right nor sound. What they said, and their talk about her affair and Joseph's, was, it is mentioned, a scheme of theirs so that she would show them Joseph. The editor refers to the explanations of "error" and "manifest". {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v16 p. 68}

These statements are given as transmitted; the choices and arguments are al-Tabari's, the notes and source tracings are the editor's, and the museum gives no verdict.
