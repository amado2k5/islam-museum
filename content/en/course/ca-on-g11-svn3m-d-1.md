## Brief
This lesson asks you to describe the basic requirements for plant growth and the soil components that various plants need. The museum offers an early handbook that sorts land and water for a gardener; the modern measures, such as pH, nutrients and porosity, are for your class and your laboratory. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## What did a gardener need to know before planting?
List what the handbook asks about the land and the water. Then compare your list with the requirements your class has named for plant growth.

## Go deeper
Read the land chapter of [[invention/ibn-bassal-soil-water-matching|Ibn Bassal]] closely. He does not treat garden land as a uniform substance needing the same water and work in every season: in Al-Qasd wa-l-bayan he distinguishes four water sources and ten practical land types, relating observed properties to season, plants and cultivation {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha chs.1–2}. His soil chapter warns that the surface can differ from what lies beneath, and that judgment requires examination and repeated practical experience {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha ch.2}. Soft land, for example, is described through open pores admitting air and water; heavy land through changing behaviour in heat and rain {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha ch.2}. "Heavy" does not simply mean useless, and colour alone is insufficient: assessment varies with the subsurface, season, work and the land's history {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha ch.2}.

## Why they did it
The records quote no passage in which Ibn Bassal says why he wrote his book, and they supply no motive for him. What they show is the setting he worked in. Ozel connects [[person/ibn-bassal|Ibn Bassal]] with Toledo, al-Mamun's service, Ibn Wafid's milieu and the ruler's garden by the Tagus {cite:tdv-ibn-bassal biography} {cite:ibn-bassal-filaha-profile biography}. Through the Anonymous Botanist of Seville, the project reports his pilgrimage to the Hijaz, connecting travel with agricultural experience in Sicily, Egypt and Syria {cite:ibn-bassal-filaha-profile biography}. His explanation employs natures and temperaments to understand plants and land {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha chs.1–2}. The Filaha Texts Project notes that his book does not cite earlier agronomic writers and reads this as evidence of personal experience's prominence {cite:ibn-bassal-filaha-profile biography}.

## Two views
Does the absence of earlier authors show purely personal experience? The Filaha Texts Project reads it as evidence of personal experience's prominence; the record adds that failure to name an earlier book does not prove absence of inherited knowledge or the contribution of surrounding workers {cite:ibn-bassal-filaha-profile biography}. The sources also differ about his move: Ozel places it after Toledo's 1085 fall, whereas the project says before it {cite:tdv-ibn-bassal biography} {cite:ibn-bassal-filaha-profile biography}. The record retains the discrepancy rather than assemble a dated itinerary from accounts disagreeing about the same event {cite:tdv-ibn-bassal biography} {cite:ibn-bassal-filaha-profile biography}.

## Often misunderstood
Ibn Bassal's categories can be read as a modern laboratory table. The contribution is this network of relationships between land, water, season and plant, not invention of farming or the first recognition that soils differ {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha chs.1–2}. The explanation employs natures and temperaments to understand plants and land: calling soil hot is not automatically a thermometer reading, and calling it rich or fatty is not a specific analysis of organic matter or a nutrient {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha chs.1–2}. The modern measures, such as pH, nutrients and porosity, stay with your class and your laboratory, as the lesson's Brief says.

## How do we know?
The direct witness is the Filaha project's Arabic text, revised from Millas Vallicrosa and Muhammad Aziman's Tetuan 1955 edition; it is not a manuscript image or our own collation of every witness {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha contents-chs.1–2}. Our reading covers the contents, two complete chapters and the opening of chapter 10, not every chapter {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha contents-chs.1–2}. Ozel reports 273 references to Ibn Bassal in Ibn al-Awwam, the reference's count and not our own manuscript tally, and some quotations are absent from the available abridgement {cite:tdv-ibn-bassal reception}. The project also identifies a transmission gap: part of the Castilian text is missing {cite:ibn-bassal-filaha-profile biography-and-chs.10–12-synopsis}.

## Think about it
Ibn Bassal says that the surface can differ from what lies beneath and that judgment needs repeated examination, and the record of [[person/ibn-bassal|Ibn Bassal]] says his hot and cold descriptions are not modern measurements {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha chs.1–2}. Take a position: is repeated examination enough to make a grower's judgment of land reliable, or does it need measurement? Answer the strongest objection to your position: why does your position still stand?
