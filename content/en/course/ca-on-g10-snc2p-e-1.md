## Brief
This lesson asks you to investigate and explain the laws of reflection, to say how light reflects from different mirrors, and to draw ray diagrams. The museum offers one geometric problem from a medieval book of optics, about where light reflects on a circular mirror; the experiments, the plane, concave and convex mirrors and the ray diagrams are for your class. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## How do you find where the light bounces?
The expectations ask you to use the law of reflection. Decide what you must measure at the point where the ray meets the mirror, and from which line the angles are measured.

## Go deeper
Read the problem as the record sets it. The givens are a circle representing the mirror in a plane, its center, and two points representing an object and an eye. The task is to construct a rim point satisfying equal incidence and reflection angles measured against the local normal {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.64–65}. A circle's radius to that point is perpendicular to its tangent, so the reference is not invariably a fixed horizontal line {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.64–65}. This differs from drawing a chord or checking a ray already supplied, because the meeting point itself is unknown {cite:mathworld-alhazen-problem problem statement}. The record of [[invention/ibn-al-haytham-circular-reflection|the circular-mirror method]] sets out the rest.

## Why they did it
The record gives no reason in [[person/ibn-al-haytham|Ibn al-Haytham]]'s own words. What it shows is the problem the lemmas serve: the lemmas occur within an optical investigation but form linked geometrical problems, one assisting another, and then the results return to locating reflection points {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.59–65}. The historian places the Optics in eleventh-century Egypt {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 p.60}. The record adds that Egypt is the author's setting, not a workshop address verified there {cite:tdv-ibnul-heysem pp.82–87}.

## Often misunderstood
The problem carries his name, but the record limits what that means. His contribution is geometrical lemmas and applications in Book V of the [[work/kitab-al-manazir|Book of Optics]], not invention of mirrors or discovery of reflection's law from nothing {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.59,64–65}. MathWorld attributes an earlier formulation to Ptolemy {cite:mathworld-alhazen-problem history}. The historian's note states a modern equivalence to a fourth-degree equation and expressly says medieval Arabic mathematicians were unaware of that equivalence {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 p.70 note7}.

## How do we know?
The record is open about what was read. It says it read pages 59–75 of Hogendijk's 1996 study from the author's hosted copy, not an Optics manuscript {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.59–75}. The study translates al-Mu'taman's lemmas, so his adaptations must not become quotations from Ibn al-Haytham {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.59–75}. The museum's Arabic Optics source covers Books 1–3, not Book V {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 reliability note}. So what we know of Book V comes through a modern historian, who finds the discussion of certain concave configurations unsatisfactory and says multiple reflection points are possible {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 p.70}.

## Think about it
In the record's modern teaching example, a unit circle centred at (0,0) has the object at (2,1) and the eye at (2,−1), and the point (1,0) gives two 45° angles to the horizontal normal {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.64–65 reconstructed special case}. The record says this checks an easy symmetric case and does not prove that guessing an arc midpoint always suffices {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.64–65 reconstructed special case}. Move the object to a different height and the eye to a different distance: what would you have to check before trusting a guessed point, and why?
