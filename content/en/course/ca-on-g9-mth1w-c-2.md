## Brief
This lesson asks you to simplify algebraic expressions using the properties of operations, and to create and solve equations and verify the solutions. The museum offers al-Khwarizmi's six types of equation and al-Karaji's way of multiplying composite expressions; your own equations and their contexts are for your class to bring. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-mathematics}

## How do you know your answer is right?
Put the answer back into the original problem and compare both sides. Then ask: is testing one value enough to prove a general rule? Read how the record separates doing an operation, checking an example and justifying a rule.

## Go deeper
Read al-Khwarizmi's proof closely. He says that roots are not halved in the first three types, and that for each of the remaining three he made “a figure from which the cause is shown” {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.35}. For a square and ten roots he draws the square as a figure of unknown side, divides the ten roots into four rectangles placed on its sides, then completes the four corners with small squares totalling twenty-five, so that the large square is sixty-four with side eight {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.36}. As [[invention/algebra|the record of his algebra]] reports, the TDV biography describes the squares and rectangles as a geometrical representation and verification of the analytical solution, distinguishing the calculation that finds a number from the drawing that makes the relation behind it visible {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227}. He ends [[concept/al-durub-al-sitta|the theory of the six types]] with the rule that every problem must bring you to one of them {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.43}.

## Why they did it
For the figures, al-Khwarizmi's own stated purpose is that they show the cause {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.35}. For expressions in [[work/al-jabr-wal-muqabala|his book]] that have no figure, because they combine three different kinds, he shows their necessity in words {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.50}. For [[person/al-karaji|al-Karaji]] the records preserve no stated purpose; what they show is that his al-Fakhri kept geometrical demonstrations {cite:tdv-kereci pp. 277–278}.

## Two views
Did al-Karaji's arithmetic of expressions free algebra from geometry? As MacTutor reports, Woepcke and Rashed emphasize algebraic operations becoming less dependent on geometrical representation, an important direction toward autonomous algebraic arithmetic {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. The account it reports from Crossley stresses numerical examples, limits of demonstration and retained Euclidean theorems in equation solving {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. TDV likewise notes geometrical proof in al-Fakhri {cite:tdv-kereci pp. 277–278}. The record presents these interpretations without resolving them.

## How do we know?
What we read of al-Khwarizmi is the Cairo edition, whose editors write [[concept/al-durub-al-sitta|the six types]] in modern symbols and explain that he worked only with positive numbers {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.30} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.32}. For al-Karaji the witnesses are textual: TDV discusses al-Fakhri, al-Badi and other works and their editions and scholarship {cite:tdv-kereci pp. 277–278}. Woepcke's introduction describes damaged manuscript material and restoration, reminding readers that an edition has editorial mediation {cite:karaji-woepcke-1853 p. 3}. [[invention/karaji-polynomial-arithmetic|The record of al-Karaji's arithmetic]] says its explanatory numbers and symbols are the museum's own, not an example claimed to have been copied from the historical book {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}.

## Think about it
The record says that substitution can uncover an arithmetic mistake, but that agreement at one value does not prove a rule for every value {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. Take a position and answer the strongest objection: does a drawing like al-Khwarizmi's squares justify a rule better than a check with one number?
