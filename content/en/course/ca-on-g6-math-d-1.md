## Brief
This lesson asks you to collect and organise data to answer a question about a population, and to analyse data by asking questions, challenging first guesses and drawing conclusions. The museum offers one method of counting letters to read a hidden message, and its warning about small samples. {cite:ontario-curriculum-elementary-mathematics}

## When is a count enough to draw a conclusion?
Look at the expectation about challenging preconceived notions. Count the letters in a short text and in a longer one, and compare how steady the results are before you decide what they show.

## Go deeper
The method starts from a language, not from a message. [[invention/frequency-analysis-al-kindi|Al-Kindi's treatise]] asks for a sample in the same language as the concealed message, about a skin's worth, in which each kind of letter is counted and the kinds are ranked from the most frequent downward {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 216}. Next the shapes of the concealed message are sorted and counted, and the commonest shape is matched with the top letter of the sample, the next with the second, and so on {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 216}. Then the author slows the rush at short messages: they may not contain every letter, and a letter unusually common in one passage may be balanced by another only in a longer text {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 216}.

## Why they did it
In his introduction, as the record reports it, al-Kindi says that earlier philosophers and thinkers used unfamiliar signs to restrict access to their books, and that recovery can help readers understand them {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 213–214}. The record says his introduction places the usefulness in recovering meanings hidden by unfamiliar signs {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 213–214}. That is the statement of purpose the record gives.

## Often misunderstood
A tally can look like a machine that solves everything, but al-Kindi does not make the numbers his final verdict: a short message may depart from ordinary usage, and a guess needs checking against letter combinations and context {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 216–218}. If two letters are guessed, he asks whether they can combine in that language, and contradictions prevent fixing a letter's identity merely because its frequency rank seemed suitable {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 217}. The record adds that the method's textual conditions include language, cipher type, enough material and cross-checks {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 216–219, 225–226}.

## Think about it
Al-Kindi warns that a short message may not contain every letter. You counted the letters in a short paragraph and in a long one. Which of your two tables do you trust more to match the usual pattern of the language, and why? What would you need to see before you trusted the short one?
