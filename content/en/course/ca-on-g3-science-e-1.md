## Brief
This lesson asks you to examine the types of soil found in Ontario and the crops each suits. The museum offers one medieval farming handbook that sorted land into types. It has nothing to offer on substances added to soil, which your class must study elsewhere. Ontario's own soils your class must study in Ontario. {cite:ontario-science-tech-2022}

## How do you know the soil is healthy?
Compare what you see on the surface of the soil with what you find beneath, and what you find in the soil with what grows in it. Which evidence do you trust most, and why?

## Go deeper
[[invention/ibn-bassal-soil-water-matching|Ibn Bassal]] sorted land into ten kinds {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha chs.1–2}. Soft land, for example, is described through open pores admitting air and water; heavy land through changing behaviour in heat and rain {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha ch.2}. Colour alone is not enough, the record says: assessment varies with the subsurface, season, work and the land's history {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha ch.2}. His soil chapter warns that the surface can differ from what lies beneath {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha ch.2}. The record adds that his ten kinds are not a modern laboratory standard {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha chs.1–2}.

## Think about it
Ibn Bassal warns that the surface of the soil can differ from what lies beneath. If you could check only one thing before planting, would you trust what you see on top or what you find below, and why?

## Try this
Take a spoonful of soil from the schoolyard and one from a plant pot, and compare them by sight and touch. Then read the record [[invention/ibn-bassal-soil-water-matching|Ibn Bassal's linked soil, water and crop assessment]] and see which of his descriptions is closest to what you found.
