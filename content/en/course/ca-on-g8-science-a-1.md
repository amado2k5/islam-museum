## Brief
This lesson asks you to describe how science and technology are used in various occupations to address real problems, and to analyse what different communities have contributed to science and technology. The museum offers two people who worked in medieval Muslim societies, an engineer and a physician; the class brings the occupations of today, including the skilled trades, and the contributions of other communities, which the museum does not hold. {cite:ontario-science-tech-2022}

## What problem was this person asked to solve?
The expectations link a skill to a problem and a contribution to a community. For each person you read about, note who needed the work, what problem it answered and what we know of how it was done.

## Go deeper
The engineer [[person/al-jazari-engineer|al-Jazari]] served the Artuqids at Amid in 1181–1206, and TDV says his book was commissioned by Nasir al-Din Mahmud {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. TDV describes six divisions covering clocks, vessels for drinking and banquets, fountains and automatic musical instruments, and water-raising machines {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. It describes coloured drawings and lettered parts, with instructions that move from the general form to making the components, assembly and operation {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. TDV adds that little is known of his life beyond his book's introduction {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}.

## Why they did it
The sources used for this lesson contain no sentence in which al-Jazari or Ibn al-Nafis says why he chose his work. For al-Jazari they show a patron: TDV says the book was commissioned by Nasir al-Din Mahmud {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. For [[person/ibn-al-nafis|Ibn al-Nafis]] they show a physician whom the chronicle calls the shaykh of medicine in Egypt, who dictated his works from memory and endowed his properties and books to the Mansuri hospital {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 702-703} {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 703}. These are facts about their work, not stated motives.

## Often misunderstood
A book of many devices can make an engineer look like the inventor of everything in it. TDV is more careful: it places al-Jazari in a tradition extending from Hellenistic engineering to the Banu Musa, and describes an engineer improving earlier work, not the inventor of every underlying principle in his book {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}.

## Think about it
Al-Jazari's book was commissioned by a ruler, while Ibn al-Nafis left his properties and books to a hospital. For which of the two do the records tell you more about who needed the work and what problem it answered, and what is missing for the other?

- [[person/ibn-al-nafis|Ibn al-Nafis]]: the physician and his books.
