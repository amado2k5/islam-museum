## Brief
This lesson asks you to research one number concept, tell the story of how it was developed and used in a specific culture, and say why it still matters. The museum offers one such story, from the Islamic world of Baghdad and Damascus; the stories of other cultures and the current context are for your class to bring. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-mathematics}

## Who passed the idea on, and what did each person add?
Read the two stories side by side. Note what each says was inherited, and what the record says its maker did not invent. A research story names its sources.

## Go deeper
Read how each story reaches us. Al-Khwarizmi's Arabic book of arithmetic is lost; the oldest witness that survives is a twelfth-century Latin translation in the Cambridge library {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227}. B. Boncompagni published it in Rome in 1857, Kurt Vogel then published a facsimile of the manuscript in 1963, and André Allard a critical edition with a French translation in 1992 {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227}. The book of [[person/al-uqlidisi-ahmad-ibn-ibrahim|al-Uqlidisi]] is a different case: the sources connect it with Damascus in 341/952–953 and distinguish its composition from the surviving copy dated 1157 {cite:uqlidisi-mactutor Biography} {cite:tdv-oklidisi pp. 25–26}. Each story rests on a witness that is not the author's own page, a Latin translation in one case and a later copy in the other. Ask of each how far it stands from the author.

## Why they did it
The record on [[invention/algorism|algorism]] preserves no sentence in which al-Khwarizmi says why he wrote his book of arithmetic; its Arabic original is lost {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227}. For his algebra it reports a stated purpose, the reckoning people require in their inheritances, bequests, trade and the measuring of land {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 30}. For al-Uqlidisi, the biography reports that the author's introduction describes travel, reading and learning from arithmeticians he met, and it stresses the practical value the author attributes to Indian arithmetic {cite:uqlidisi-mactutor Biography}. The record calls his account of his own travel and learning transmitted testimony, not an independently established itinerary {cite:uqlidisi-mactutor Biography}.

## Often misunderstood
Two dates stand beside al-Uqlidisi's book, 341/952–953 and 1157, and they can look like rival answers to the question "when was it invented?". The record says they date different acts, composition and copying, not competing attempts to identify an invention day {cite:uqlidisi-mactutor Biography} {cite:tdv-oklidisi pp. 25–26}. The same care applies to the claim that matters: the record's narrow claim is an early surviving direct treatment of decimal fractions and operations with a units sign, improving older arithmetic {cite:uqlidisi-saidan-dsb decimal-fraction discussion}.

## How do we know?
For algorism the record keeps its witnesses apart: the algebraic introduction describes decimal structure, whereas the Latin translation witnesses a different book, the Indian reckoning; a gap in one cannot automatically be filled from the other {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227}. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, as the record of [[person/al-khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]] reports it, says the connections among the various mediaeval arithmetic works and al-Khwarizmi's own are not well established {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111}. For al-Uqlidisi the museum read Saidan's study in a print of an Encyclopedia.com page, and some arrays and formulae are actually missing from it; they are not recreated {cite:uqlidisi-saidan-dsb decimal-fraction discussion} {cite:tdv-oklidisi pp. 25–26}.

## Think about it
Take a position and answer the strongest objection to it: which witness do you trust more for what its author wrote, a twelfth-century Latin translation of a lost Arabic book, or a surviving copy, dated 1157, of a book composed at Damascus in 341/952–953?
