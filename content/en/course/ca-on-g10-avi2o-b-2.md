## Go deeper
A calligraphy lesson was called *meşk*, "practice". The master wrote a model line, and the pupil copied it and showed it to him. If the master found a fault, he wrote the corrected letter or word beneath the line. If he approved the line, he set beneath it a mark of effort made from the letters sîn, ayn and yâ, or he wrote *âferin*, "well done" {cite:tdv-hattat p.493-499}. Training had two stages: the letters one by one, then joined words. The first ended with a formula saying the letters were complete, the second with lines of verse {cite:tdv-hattat p.493-499}. Imitation was the way in. A calligrapher entered his master's manner by copying an old master's piece exactly and saying so in his signature. Once he was secure, he wrote in his own manner {cite:tdv-hat p.427-437}.

## How do we know?
The account of the lesson comes from the entry on calligraphers (*Hattat*) in the TDV Encyclopaedia of Islam, written by M. Uğur Derman, and the point about imitation from his entry on the art (*Hat*) {cite:tdv-hattat p.493-499} {cite:tdv-hat p.427-437}. The record says the two entries read for it name no guild for calligraphers, so the guild part of the record waits for a source that names one {cite:tdv-hattat p.493-499}. For who practises the art now, it turns to UNESCO, which listed Arabic calligraphy in 2021. UNESCO describes the practice as passed on through formal and informal education and apprenticeship, and practised by professional calligraphers, artists, designers, teachers and artisans, men and women alike {cite:unesco-ich-arabic-calligraphy decision 16.COM 8.b.35}.

## Think about it
