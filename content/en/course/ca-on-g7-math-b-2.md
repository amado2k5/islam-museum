## Brief
This lesson asks you to identify and represent perfect squares and determine their square roots, and to evaluate and express repeated multiplication of whole numbers using exponential notation. The museum offers two glimpses: al-Karaji's naming of the successive powers of an unknown with a rule for multiplying them, and al-Kashi's extraction of roots; perfect squares, exponential notation for numbers and the contexts of everyday life are for your class to bring. {cite:ontario-curriculum-elementary-mathematics}

## How is a power related to a root?
Write 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 as a power. Then ask which number multiplied by itself gives 49, and how you would check your answer.

## Go deeper
Look at how [[person/al-karaji|al-Karaji]] handled the powers of one unknown quantity. The record starts from distinguishing its successive powers: the unknown, its square, its cube and further powers, and also their reciprocals {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. Multiplying two of them needs a rule that names the resulting power, rather than a new geometrical diagram for each operation {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. In modern teaching notation x² times x³ gives x⁵; the record says this is its own illustration, not a manuscript transcription {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. MacTutor stresses that he approached this law without defining x⁰=1, so the complete modern formulation is not credited to him {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}.

## Why they did it
The records read here preserve no sentence in which al-Karaji says why he worked on these operations. They show his setting: his principal mathematical books are placed in Baghdad, and TDV connects al-Fakhri with Fakhr al-Mulk's patronage in the Buyid setting {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography} {cite:tdv-kereci pp. 277–278}. For al-Kashi, the record says his [[work/miftah-al-hisab-kashi|Key to Arithmetic]] addresses calculation useful for astronomy, surveying, architecture, accounting and trade; these are purposes reported for the book, not evidence that a particular workshop used a particular example {cite:kashi-mactutor Key}.

## Two views
As reported by MacTutor, Woepcke's and Rashed's interpretations emphasize algebraic operations becoming less dependent on geometrical representation, an important direction toward autonomous algebraic arithmetic {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. The account it reports from Crossley stresses numerical examples, limits of demonstration and retained Euclidean theorems in equation solving {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. TDV likewise notes geometrical proof in al-Fakhri {cite:tdv-kereci pp. 277–278}. The record presents these readings without resolving them: new operational organization and older forms of justification can coexist in one body of work.

## Think about it
One reading sees algebra becoming less dependent on drawings; another stresses that Euclid's theorems stayed in the proofs. Which would you give more weight to as evidence of a new kind of mathematics, a new rule for naming powers or a proof that still draws a figure, and why?
