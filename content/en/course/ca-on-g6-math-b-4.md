## Brief
This lesson asks you to understand the divisibility rules and use them with 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 and 10, and to write composite numbers as products of prime factors, for example with factor trees. The museum offers one record about numbers and their divisors; the rules and the trees are for your class. {cite:ontario-curriculum-elementary-mathematics}

## How can you tell, without dividing, what divides a number?
Look at the expectations: they ask for rules, not for long division. Test each rule on numbers you can check, and use a factor tree to see which prime factors you cannot split further.

## Go deeper
Write the divisors of 220 that are smaller than 220: 1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 11, 20, 22, 44, 55 and 110. Their sum is 284. Now do the same for 284, whose divisors are 1, 2, 4, 71 and 142: they add up to 220 {cite:weisstein-amicable-pair divisors220284}. Two different numbers that lead to each other like this are called amicable {cite:weisstein-amicable-pair definition}. [[person/kamal-al-din-al-farisi|Kamal al-Din al-Farisi]] wrote the [[work/farisi-tadhkirat-al-ahbab|Memorandum for friends on amicability]], which gives a new approach to proving an older rule {cite:farisi-mactutor number-theory}. The record calls this exercise modern arithmetic, not an instruction from a manuscript.

## Why they did it
The records read here preserve no sentence in which al-Farisi says why he wrote about amicable numbers. They show what the work did: MacTutor treats it as more than a minor change to the older proof of Thabit ibn Qurra, a new approach employing factorization and combinatorial ideas {cite:farisi-mactutor new-approach}. They also show that the same man worked on light: the biography itself distinguishes these two major contributions, the numbers and the water-sphere rainbow model {cite:farisi-mactutor introduction-and-number-theory}.

## Often misunderstood
Al-Farisi did not invent the example of 220 and 284, or Thabit ibn Qurra's older rule {cite:weisstein-amicable-pair definition} {cite:farisi-mactutor number-theory}. MacTutor also reports Hogendijk's argument that Thabit may already have known the larger pair, 17296 and 18416, so the record awards al-Farisi no first discovery {cite:farisi-mactutor large-pair-history}. And calling the numbers friends is a verbal analogy, not a mechanism governing their behavior {cite:weisstein-amicable-pair definition}.

## Think about it
The number 6 has smaller divisors 1, 2 and 3, and they add up to 6. Why does the record not call 6 an amicable pair with itself, and what must the second number do for two numbers to be amicable?
