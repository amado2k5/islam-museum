## Brief
This lesson asks you to read and represent whole numbers up to one million, to describe how they are used every day, and to use estimation and written algorithms with whole numbers and decimal tenths. The museum offers the story of one book of written arithmetic; the numbers and the practice are yours. {cite:ontario-curriculum-elementary-mathematics}

## Why do the places matter?
Write a number such as 305 000 and say what each digit is worth. Then estimate before you calculate, and check whether your written answer is close to your estimate.

## Go deeper
What is known about the man behind the book? The Cairo editors quote Ibn al-Nadim's Fihrist: he came from Khwarazm, devoted himself to al-Ma'mun's treasury of wisdom, was one of the astronomers, and people relied on his two tables known as the Sindhind {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 22}. From the Fihrist and the preface of the algebra they show that he was al-Ma'mun's contemporary, though the years of his birth and death cannot be fixed {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 23}. TDV adds that he died after 232 AH (847 CE) {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227}. These are the facts behind the [[invention/algorism|algorism]] record's picture of [[person/al-khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]].

## Why they did it
The record preserves no sentence in which al-Khwarizmi says why he wrote his book of arithmetic, and its Arabic original is lost {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227}. It does show where he worked: devoted to al-Ma'mun's treasury of wisdom {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 22}. Ibn Khaldun says the craft of arithmetic arose from the need of reckoning in dealings, and that it was passed on in the cities by teaching it to children {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun p. 495}. The preface of his algebra names practical needs of reckoning as the reason for that other book {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 30}.

## Often misunderstood
"Al-Khwarizmi's book" can mean more than one book. TDV places the algebra before the Indian-reckoning book, and names another work of his on addition and subtraction that, from al-Baghdadi's quotations, appears concerned with finger reckoning; shared operations do not make these works one text {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227}. The Cairo editors list the Book of Arithmetic among four books of his that Ibn al-Nadim does not mention {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 23}.

## Think about it
The word "algorithm" comes from al-Khwarizmi's name, but he wrote more than one book about calculating. Why is it a mistake to say that whatever one of his books says must also be in the others, and what would you check before saying so?
