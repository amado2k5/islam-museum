## Brief
This lesson asks you to read and interpret style in art works, and to describe some of the ways art forms and styles reflect the beliefs and traditions of the cultures that made them. The museum offers two Arabic scripts, Kufic and nasta'liq, with what the records say of where each was used. Works from other cultures your class must bring. {cite:ontario-curriculum-elementary-arts}

## How can you tell a style at a glance?
Look first for the features you can see: the angles, the spacing, the way letters stretch along the line. Then ask what the writing was for and who read it. A style often carries both its look and its purpose.

## Go deeper
The Met holds two pages from the Shah Jahan Album, which Jahangir began and Shah Jahan continued. One is by [[person/sultan-ali-mashhadi|Sultan Ali Mashhadi]], with love verses rich in Sufi metaphor written in diagonal lines. The other is a quatrain by Ibn Yamin, signed by Mir Ali Haravi. Both were reset in seventeenth-century illuminated margins, and the second has birds and animals painted by Nanha {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 53} {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 54} {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 56}. Maryam Ekhtiar calls [[concept/nastaliq-script|nasta'liq]] the favoured script of Persian poetry {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. [[concept/kufic-script|Kufic]], by contrast, is marked by clarity, generous spacing and the horizontal extension of letters along the baseline {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 23}.

## Why they did it
The records print no sentence in which Sultan Ali Mashhadi says why he wrote. They show where he worked and what he left. Sheila Blair says he spent most of his long career in Herat working for the great bibliophiles of the Timurid court, Sultan Husayn Bayqara and Ali Shir Nava'i. In 1514, after retiring to Mashhad, he wrote a verse treatise on calligraphy. Blair says it contains practical and autobiographical information and shows the close association between religious discipline and the practice of calligraphy {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 320}.

## Two views
How did nasta'liq begin? Ekhtiar reports a legend that Mir Ali Tabrizi invented it after Ali ibn Abi Talib appeared to him in a dream and told him to make letters like the wings of flying geese. Scholars, she says, hold that it developed in the second half of the fourteenth century in Shiraz and Tabriz, from a gradual merging of naskh and ta'liq {cite:ekhtiar-how-to-read-islamic-calligraphy-2018 p. 27}. The Encyclopaedia of Islam says tradition credits [[person/mir-ali-tabrizi|Mir Ali Tabrizi]], but that surviving manuscripts show nasta'liq existed by the seventh century AH {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1168}.

## Think about it
Kufic is marked by clarity, generous spacing and letters stretched along the line. Nasta'liq joins the evenness of naskh to the sweeping strokes of ta'liq. Using only those features, which script would you choose for a poem on a page and which for writing seen from far away, and why?
