## Brief
This lesson asks you to research a geometric concept, tell the story of its development and use in a specific culture, and describe its relevance to careers and other disciplines. The museum offers one story, the approximation of the ratio of circumference to diameter in the Islamic world; its link to careers and to other cultures is for your class to bring. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-mathematics}

## When is a number an approximation and not an exact value?
Compare the three values al-Khwarizmi mentions and then al-Kashi's, and see how many places agree. What changes as the precision grows, and what stays unknown?

## Go deeper
[[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]] says in his chapter on [[concept/ilm-al-misaha|mensuration]] that taking the circumference as three and a seventh times the diameter is "a convention among people, not a necessity". He gives two other views of the geometers: multiply the diameter by itself and by ten and take the root, and the astronomers' rule, multiply the diameter by 62,832 and divide by 20,000; all of them, he says, are close to one another {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.72}. In the problem of a circle of diameter seven cubits he takes the circumference as twenty-two cubits {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.80}. Centuries later, MacTutor dates al-Kashi's [[work/risala-muhitiyya-kashi|Treatise on the Circumference]] to 1424 and describes expressing twice the circumference-to-diameter ratio in sexagesimal and decimal forms with high precision {cite:kashi-mactutor Circumference treatise}.

## Why they did it
The museum's records preserve no sentence in which [[person/al-kashi|al-Kashi]] says why he sought this precision. Iranica, as the record of [[invention/al-kashi-pi-calculation|his computation]] reports, describes concern about large instruments warping, and the record says geometric, numerical and material accuracy are related without being identical {cite:iranica-kashi instruments and practical precision}. The biographies emphasise his combination of geometry and computation {cite:kashi-mactutor mathematical contributions}. Al-Khwarizmi, by contrast, lists the measurement of land among the needs he wrote his book for {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.30}, and calls the usual value a convention, not a necessity {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.72}.

## Two views
Al-Khwarizmi and the editors of the Cairo edition do not weigh the three values in the same way. He says all of them are close to one another {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.72}. The editors comment that the astronomers' value is the closest to the truth and the root of ten the furthest, and quote the manuscript's gloss that this is "an approximation, not an exact determination", because the circumference is not a straight line {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.71}. They also note that twenty-two cubits, in the problem of a circle of diameter seven cubits, is approximate {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.80}.

## Often misunderstood
Al-Kashi is sometimes credited with inventing decimal fractions. The record reports that MacTutor places decimals and root extraction within earlier work, including al-Karaji's school and al-Samawal, so "used fractions skilfully" must not become "invented all decimal fractions". A chronological comparison with later European results does not prove their authors read him: earlier occurrence and documented textual transmission are different claims {cite:kashi-mactutor decimals and root methods}.

## How do we know?
Okten prints 6;16,59,28,1,34,51,46,15,50 in sexagesimal and 6.2831853071795865 in decimal. Checking the literal printed sequence, however, reveals a discrepancy: it converts to approximately 6.2831853071795924, not the accompanying decimal. The record flags the mismatch without guessing a correction to a historical digit, and since the original was not read, it cannot locate the error in transmission, typography or elsewhere {cite:tdv-kashi-okten mathematics}. Okten names Luckey's edition and German translation, and the museum has not read the original treatise or the German edition {cite:tdv-kashi-okten works6–8}.

## Think about it
Al-Khwarizmi calls the usual value a convention and says the three values are close to one another, while the editors rank them by closeness to the truth. Take a position: does the difference among the three values matter when a builder measures a circle of diameter seven cubits? What is the strongest objection to your position, and how do you answer it?
