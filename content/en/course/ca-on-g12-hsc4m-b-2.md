## Brief
This lesson asks you to explain the major ideas about culture of a leading figure in the humanities and social sciences. The curriculum names many figures; the museum holds a record on one of them, Edward Said, so your class will add the others. {cite:ontario-curriculum-social-sciences-humanities}

## What did he argue, and how would you test it?
State his main idea in one sentence of your own, then ask what evidence a reader would need to accept or question it.

## Go deeper
Read one sentence closely. The museum's record of [[person/edward-said|Edward Said]] reports that Britannica sums up his achievement in a single sentence: in Orientalism he "showed how the Western world had historically constructed distorted images of 'the Orient' to justify colonialism and other forms of political subjugation" {cite:britannica-edward-said}. The record itself gives the argument of the 1978 book with another verb: Orientalism argued that Western study of the Arab and Islamic world proceeded from a fabricated image of the Orient, a body of Eurocentric prejudice, and that this scholarship sprang from a political agenda serving colonial ambition {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en} {cite:britannica-edward-said}. Notice the two verbs, showed and argued. Ask what a reader would need to find in the book itself before agreeing with either, and what the record says came after: later books carried the argument outward, and Culture and Imperialism extended the analysis to the resistance cultures of the colonised {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en} {cite:britannica-edward-said}.

## Two views
The record reports a famous polemic between Said and the historian Bernard Lewis: Said criticised Lewis for Orientalist bias, and Lewis replied by questioning Said's own scholarship {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}. It also reports a third voice. Terry Eagleton, quoted by the English Wikipedia article, gives a more sympathetic verdict and calls Said's work "one of the most fertile and most charitable" elucidations of a partial picture {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}. The record gives no more of Lewis's reply than that one clause, so the strength of his objection cannot be judged from it. A student who wants to weigh the exchange has to find both authors' own words.

## How do we know?
What the record says about Orientalism comes from encyclopaedic summaries, Britannica and the English Wikipedia article, read together with the Arabic and Urdu articles for dates {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en} {cite:britannica-edward-said}. The record does not print the book, nor Lewis's reply. The sources also disagree on small facts, and the record keeps the disagreement: the death date is 25 September in the Arabic article, Britannica and the gravestone the English article reports, but 24 September in the English article's own lead; the Harvard PhD is 1964 in the English article and Britannica, but 1963 in the Arabic article {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}. Summaries that differ on dates invite a careful reader to check what else they pass on.

## Think about it
Lewis questioned Said's scholarship, while Eagleton called his work one of the most fertile and most charitable elucidations of a partial picture. Which of the two judgements would you trust more after reading only this record, and what would you need to read before you could decide, and why?
