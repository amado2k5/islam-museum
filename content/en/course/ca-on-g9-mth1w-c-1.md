## Brief
This lesson asks you to research an algebraic concept, tell the story of its development and use in a specific culture, and describe its relevance today. The museum offers the story of algebra in al-Khwarizmi's book as the Cairo editors tell it; other cultures and today's uses are for your class to bring. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-mathematics}

## What did algebra inherit, and what did the book add?
Gather from the page what the editors say about algebra before al-Khwarizmi, then what they say about him. Place both on a timeline and ask where the word al-jabr falls on it.

## Go deeper
The Cairo editors of al-Khwarizmi's book, Ali Mustafa Mosharafa and Muhammad Mursi Ahmad, open with a survey of [[concept/algebra|algebra]] before him {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.13}. They name the Ahmes (Rhind) papyrus of about 1700 BCE, with a first-degree equation in one unknown and a special sign for the unknown {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.14}; Babylonian tables of squares and cubes {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.16}; Greek geometric solutions of second-degree equations, and a first analytic solution that can be affirmed with Heron of Alexandria {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.16}; Diophantus, who did not study the types systematically as al-Khwarizmi did {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.18}; and Indian rules from Aryabhata to Brahmagupta and Mahavira {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.19} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.20}. Their conclusion is that all these developments led towards algebra in its proper sense, each country being influenced by its neighbours {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.20} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.21}.

## Why they did it
Al-Khwarizmi gives his reason in the preface of [[work/al-jabr-wal-muqabala|his book]]. It opens with praise of God and a blessing on the Prophet, then reflects on those who write learning for later generations: one who reaches what no one had drawn out before him, one who explains what the ancients left obscure, or one who finds a flaw in a book, repairs it and thinks well of its author {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.29}. He says that the caliph al-Ma'mun's love of learning and closeness to scholars encouraged him {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.29}, and that he wrote for what people need in their dealings, such as inheritances and trade {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.30}.

## Two views
Who was first? The Cairo editors report that Ibn Khaldun names al-Khwarizmi the first to write on algebra, with Abu Kamil after him, and that al-Qazwini says he was the first to translate algebra for the Muslims {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.25}. The TDV encyclopaedia reports that historians generally agree that algebra became an independent science with him {cite:tdv-cebir pp. 195–201}. It also sets out why some modern historians doubt that his book was the first: it calls itself a summary, al-Khwarizmi does not claim to be first, its terms are already mature, and there was a book by Ibn Turk and a lost book by Sanad ibn Ali on the subject {cite:tdv-cebir pp. 195–201}. The museum's record of [[invention/algebra|algebra]] gives both views.

## How do we know?
The Cairo edition is based on a manuscript in the Bodleian Library, Oxford, written in Cairo and finished on 19 Muharram 743 AH, about five hundred years after the author's death; to the editors' knowledge it is the only surviving copy {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.26} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.122}. They say the Latin translation by Robert of Chester differs from the Arabic in many places {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.26}. The margins carry glosses that the editors take to be explanations added when the book was taught {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.11}, and they corrected obvious copying errors without notice {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.12}. Ask how much a single late copy can tell us about the earlier history the editors survey.

## Think about it
The Cairo editors call al-Khwarizmi the founder of algebra, yet the TDV encyclopaedia records why some historians doubt that his was the first book. Take a position and answer the strongest objection to it: is “founder of algebra” the right title for him?
