## Brief
This lesson asks you to describe the contributions of scientists to the fields you are studying, including Canadian scientists. The museum offers one scientist of the Islamic world who worked in astronomy, mathematics and the measurement of the Earth. The Canadian scientists, and the fields of geology the museum does not cover, are for your class to bring. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## What counts as a contribution?
Look at the expectation's verb: it asks you to describe, not to rank. For each scientist, separate what the sources document from what later tradition adds, and note who came before.

## How would you compare scientists of different countries?
Choose two or three points to compare, such as the field, the method and the kind of evidence, and use the same points for every scientist, whether Canadian or not.

## Go deeper
Read one passage slowly. [[person/al-biruni-muhammad-ibn-ahmad|Al-Biruni]] writes in the first person about living at Nandana fort in the land of India {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation pp. 188–189}. A nearby mountain stood west of the fort, with a broad plain south of the mountain; he thought it suitable for testing the method {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation pp. 188–189}. From its summit he observed the apparent meeting of earth and sky and surveyed the perpendicular representing mountain height {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation pp. 188–189}. The [[invention/al-biruni-earth-radius|record of the measurement]] says to read the figures carefully: the dip is 0;34 degrees, meaning 34 arcminutes, not 0.34 degrees, and the height is 652;3,18 regional cloth-measuring cubits {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation pp. 188–189}. It retains the numbers in their unit rather than selecting a modern cubit length to obtain an impressive answer {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation pp. 188–189}.

## Why they did it
The museum's records hold no passage in which al-Biruni says why he measured the Earth, and they do not supply a motive for him. What they show is that his book is titled the Determination of the Coordinates of Positions for the Correction of Distances between Cities, that it places Earth measurement within correcting distances between cities, and that the passage's explicit advantage is avoiding long desert traverses to obtain circumference {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation title; pp. 183–189}. The preface of his other book, [[work/al-qanun-al-masudi|al-Qanun al-Mas'udi]], is quoted by its editor: he did not follow earlier zij-makers, who confined themselves to tabular settings and concealed how they had founded their rules, but joined to every procedure its reasons, so that the reader could approve or correct rather than imitate {cite:qanun-masudi-jundi-v1 p. 38} {cite:qanun-masudi-jundi-v1 p. 39}. He opens that book with praise of God and of the sultan Mas'ud, and says that the rank of learning is the noblest in his eyes {cite:qanun-masudi-jundi-v1 p. 80} {cite:qanun-masudi-jundi-v1 p. 81}.

## Two views
Who first used the method? The translator's preface calls deriving the circumference from horizon dip the author's invention: Jamil Ali's reading {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation preface xi; pp. 185–189}. The main text reports Sanad ibn Ali accompanying al-Ma'mun on a campaign against the Byzantines; the caliph ordered him up a mountain near the sea to measure the sun's dip, from which he derived Earth's circumference {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation pp. 185–186}. This is al-Biruni's transmitted account, not Sanad's field record independently inspected here; the record is marked "contested attribution" while retaining al-Biruni's specific contribution {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation preface xi; pp. 185–189}.

Researchers also differ on his school affiliation. Britannica explicitly identifies him as a Muslim scholar, and Günay Tümer discusses his commitment to Islam. Tümer's reading, which challenges a settled Shia classification, comes first, followed by the Shia and Zaydi classifications he reports from other researchers, without a museum verdict {cite:britannica-al-biruni} {cite:tdv-biruni-tumer religious-affiliation discussion}.

## Often misunderstood
It is easy to suppose that al-Biruni was the first to know that the Earth is round. The documented contribution is exposition, proof and numerical application, not the first recognition that Earth is spherical {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation preface xi; pp. 183–189}. The passage begins with a method in Ptolemy's Geography, and the contrast is not between a flat Earth and a newly discovered sphere, but between ways to determine the size of the sphere already assumed in the calculation {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation p. 183}. A date is also misread: 416 AH/1025 CE belongs to the book reporting the experiment, not to the experiment itself {cite:tdv-biruni-tumer biography and works} {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation p. 188}.

## How do we know?
The witness actually read is an illustrated English translation by Jamil Ali, published by the American University of Beirut, with a February 1967 preface {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation title; preface ix–xii; pp. 183–189}. Tümer identifies the book's manuscript in the Fatih collection, 3386, and Bulgakov's 1962 Cairo edition; Ali's preface says his translation used that edition {cite:tdv-biruni-tumer works} {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation preface ix–xii}. The preface warns about computational and figure-letter problems, sometimes difficult to assign to author or copyist {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation preface ix–xii}. The record has not inspected the original manuscript or read the whole book {cite:biruni-tahdid-ali-translation preface ix–xii} {cite:tdv-biruni-tumer works}. On accuracy, MacTutor offers a favourable 6339.6 km assessment, while Maslikov's abstract cautions about accuracy and the conversion of the historical unit; only the abstract was read {cite:biruni-mactutor geodesy paragraph} {cite:maslikov-biruni-earth-2019 abstract}.

## Think about it
Jamil Ali's preface calls deriving the circumference from horizon dip the author's invention, while the text reports that Sanad ibn Ali applied the same principle under al-Ma'mun. Take a position on whether the museum should call al-Biruni the originator of the method, and answer the strongest objection to it: can someone who left a detailed exposition and application be an originator of a method that another is reported to have used first?
