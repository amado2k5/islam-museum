## Brief
This lesson asks you to show how mass, volume and density are related, and to determine whether an object floats, given its density, in different fluids. The museum offers two medieval accounts of how weight and displaced water were used to compare materials; the calculations and the comparison of floating and sinking in several fluids are for the class to carry out. {cite:ontario-science-tech-2022}

## What is being measured, and what could be lost?
The expectations relate three quantities. For each device you read about, write down what it measures directly, what is worked out from it, and where the measurement could go wrong.

## Go deeper
Robert Hall, whose account the museum's record follows, places [[invention/balance-of-wisdom|the balance of wisdom]] at Merv under Sultan Sanjar and says it was built for the treasury {cite:khazini-hall-dsb biography}. He also passes on an older story: an earlier balance, made by al-Asfizari, was destroyed out of fear that it would expose fraud. The record treats this with care. It calls it a transmitted account, not a modern eyewitness finding {cite:khazini-hall-dsb Kitab Mizan al-Hikma}. Afterwards [[person/al-khazini|al-Khazini]] described a similar, further refined instrument and left a substantial technical account of it {cite:khazini-hall-dsb Kitab Mizan al-Hikma}.

## Why they did it
The museum's records preserve no sentence in which al-Khazini says why he took up this work. They show three things. The balance was made for a ruler's treasury, to investigate metals and gems {cite:khazini-hall-dsb The Balance of Wisdom}. His own preface, printed in Arabic and English, identifies Muhammad as his prophet {cite:khazini-khanikoff-1859 p. 3}. And his treatise traces a history that begins with Archimedes: he does not present himself as the first person to discover weighing, and he connects the accuracy of the balance to the contributions of those before him {cite:khazini-hall-dsb Kitab Mizan al-Hikma}.

## Often misunderstood
The balance of wisdom is sometimes presented as al-Khazini's invention from nothing. Hall credits al-Asfizari with the earlier movable pans and regards most of the fundamental theory as inherited; the museum classes al-Khazini's work as an improvement: a refined version and its calibration procedures {cite:khazini-hall-dsb Al-Khazini and the Science of Weights in Islam}. The same care applies to [[invention/biruni-displacement-vessel|al-Biruni's vessel]]: the drawing that names it reached us through a nineteenth-century publication whose publisher acknowledges a deficient, erroneous manuscript {cite:khazini-khanikoff-1859 pp.53–54}.

## Think about it
The story of the destroyed balance is a transmitted account, and no one today can check it. Should a historian of science still tell it, and what would have to be found for you to treat it as a fact?
