## Brief
This lesson asks you to explain what medical devices were used for and to describe technologies and techniques for repairing the body. The museum offers three instruments and materials as one surgeon described them, each with its use and with the record's own limits. Modern diagnostic devices, prosthetics and artificial organs are for your class to bring. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## What does the device do, and where?
For each instrument, write what it was meant to do, on which part of the body, and how it was worked. Then ask what a modern device for the same task would have to do better.

## Go deeper
Read one page of [[invention/zahrawi-intestinal-suture|the record on the intestinal filament]] and notice what it says about its own limits. [[person/al-zahrawi|Abu al-Qasim al-Zahrawi]] describes animal-intestinal filaments in a discussion of intestinal suturing in the surgical book of al-Tasrif, and Leclerc's French translation preserves an important qualification: this approach and the neighbouring ant-closure account remain experimental {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861 ch.87 p.211}. On page 211 the filament description follows an account attributed to physicians of the empirical school involving ants; an experimental qualification then applies to both approaches {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861 ch.87 p.211}. Leclerc's footnote reports Guy de Chauliac's rejection of the ant method; this later surgeon's judgment is known here through the translator, and it concerns ants, not testing intestinal thread {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861 ch.87 p.211 footnote}. The experimental qualification belongs to the evidence; it should not disappear to make the achievement sound more triumphant {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861 ch.87 p.211}.

## Why they did it
The museum's records hold no preface or statement in which al-Zahrawi says why he described these instruments, and they do not supply a motive for him. What they show is the aim the text states for [[invention/zahrawi-mishab|the stone drill]]: the human problem in the passage is obstructed urine flow, and the stated aim is avoiding incision when fragmentation is feasible {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1288}. In the translated passage, the author reports trying the method and avoiding incision in some cases, and the historians take this as evidence of experience {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1288–1289}. For [[invention/zahrawi-dental-scrapers|the scrapers]], the chapter attends to differing tooth surfaces and deposits, not extraction alone; the next chapter's opening favours reluctance to lose a tooth {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861 ch.29–30 pp.97–98}.

## Often misunderstood
It is easy to take al-Zahrawi for the first to stitch wounds or the founder of stone-breaking; the records are narrower. The chapter on intestinal suturing presents inherited wound-suturing knowledge and explicitly draws on Galen {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861 ch.87 pp.207–208}. Spink and Lewis judge the scraper account original against the classical writings they discuss, which is the editors' assessment within their reading, not a survey of every culture or text {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973 bk.2 ch.29 p.272 commentary}. The 2003 historians call the drill an original contribution, but their abstract's "founder of lithotripsy" language is broader than the question the record settles {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1283, 1288–1289}. The museum therefore publishes a described improvement without adjudicating priority for every fragmentation technique across civilizations {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1287–1289}.

## How do we know?
Our evidence is a printed text and manuscript-derived pictures, not original metal instruments {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861 ch.29 p.98} {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973 bk.2 ch.29 pp.272–275}. Leclerc's page 98 visibly refers to figure 54, the 1973 edition labels the scraper collection figure 65, and the 1861 plate itself was not located in this reading, so it is not claimed as inspected {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861 ch.29 p.98} {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973 bk.2 ch.29 pp.272–275}. The drill passage is read in a 2003 translation of a 1993 Riyadh Arabic edition, and the record read the annotated translation, not the chapter's manuscript independently {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1284} {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1284, 1288}. The selected image is a 15th-century copy, and it does not identify any drawing in it as the drill {cite:commons-zahrawi-instruments metadata}.

## Think about it
Spink and Lewis judge the scraper account original against the classical writings they discuss {cite:zahrawi-spink-lewis-1973 bk.2 ch.29 p.272 commentary}, while the chapter on intestinal suturing presents inherited suturing knowledge and explicitly draws on Galen {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861 ch.87 pp.207–208}. Take a position on whether the museum should write "invented" or "described" when it attributes these instruments to al-Zahrawi, and answer the strongest objection to your position: is the absence of these instruments from the writings the editors read not evidence that he came first?
