## Brief
This lesson asks you to analyse a technological device that applies the principles of linear or circular motion. The museum offers two water-lifting machines from one medieval book, in which turning motion becomes back-and-forth motion; the forces, speeds and calculations, and a device of your own choice, are for your class. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## Where do circular and linear motion meet?
Follow the motion from the part that turns to the part that moves along a line, and mark the place where one becomes the other. Which quantities would you need to measure to analyse it?

## Go deeper
Follow the turning to the line in [[invention/jazari-double-piston-pump|al-Jazari's double-piston pump]]. Hill's translation of Category V, Chapter 5 describes two drive alternatives: a vertically mounted vaned wheel turned by flowing water like a mill, or a horizontal paddle wheel driving a gear train. Both end in a disc with an eccentric dowel {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.186}. The slotted lever is housed in a triangular mulberry-wood box, pivoting at a corner and receiving the rotating dowel in its slot {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.186–187}. The chapter explains successive portions of the rotation: changing dowel position produces lever oscillation and movement of its connected rods, not a piston that rotates like the wheel {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.186–187}. The text offers two ways to drive one mechanism, and no requirement to install both {cite:jazari-hill-1974 p.186}.

## Why they did it
No passage in the records gives al-Jazari's reason for these two machines. They show the practical problem of the scoop lifter: raising water from a well or pool, a task associated with supplying water to a channel {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185} {cite:met-jazari-scoop-451298 Catalogue Entry}. In it, gears carry rotation to a pin that moves through a slot in the ladle stem, raising and lowering the scoop {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185}. If operated as described, the scoop would move water upward without a person carrying each scoop by hand, but the sources do not document who operated a historical installation or who received its water {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185}.

## Two views
When was the book finished? The encyclopedia dates completion to 1205, and al-Hassani gives 1206, while the Science Museum catalogue calls the published design c. 1205 {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506} {cite:fstc-jazari-mechanical-genius biography} {cite:smg-jazari-double-pump catalogue}. Hill's introduction gives 1204 or 1206 based on differences among manuscripts {cite:jazari-hill-1974 introduction-life}. The record keeps the differences and repairs none. They are dates of the book, not of a pump built in a particular well {cite:jazari-hill-1974 introduction-life} {cite:tdv-cezeri pp.505–506}.

## How do we know?
The scoop lifter survives as a picture. The Met holds folio 55.121.11, and its record dates the copy to 715 AH/1315 CE and identifies Farrukh ibn Abd al-Latif as copyist {cite:met-jazari-scoop-451298 Object record}. The image shows a manuscript drawing, not the physical machine described in the text {cite:met-jazari-scoop-451298 Object record}. The folio does not provide a complete engineering plan, verified scale, or proof that the design was constructed as drawn {cite:met-jazari-scoop-451298 Object record} {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185}.

## Think about it
Sadettin Ökten emphasizes double action, the conversion of rotation into reciprocation and what he calls the first known use of a suction pipe, and he calls the pump an early example for later pumps and the steam engine {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. Should a physics teacher repeat such a claim, and how would you answer the objection that students remember an origin story better than a caveat?
