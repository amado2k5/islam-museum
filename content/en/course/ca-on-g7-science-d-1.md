## Brief
This lesson asks you to evaluate the environmental, social and economic factors to weigh when designing and building structures that meet specific needs of individuals and communities. The museum offers one structure built for a public need, the sabil; your class must bring other structures and judge the factors itself. {cite:ontario-science-tech-2022}

## Who needs it, who pays, what does it use?
Sort what you find into three kinds: what it takes from the surroundings, whom it serves, and what keeps it running. Note which of your points the record states and which you add yourself.

## Go deeper
Al-Haddad, whose study the record on [[concept/sabil|the sabil]] follows, counts sabils an important branch of civil building in Islamic architecture, falling under what he calls "charitable buildings or foundations" {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.7}. Sabils received great care in their siting, building and decoration, and in the many endowments that kept them working {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.9}. They drew water from wells, pools and springs or from rain and floods through well-built channels or pipes hidden in walls, and some had one or two cisterns {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.49} {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.50}. In the Encyclopaedia of Islam's account of the [[concept/waqf|waqf]], a person, intending a pious deed, declares part of their property unalienable and names persons or public utilities as beneficiaries of its yield {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 67}.

## Often misunderstood
It is easy to take "sabil" to mean only a drinking fountain. The author holds that in the Middle Ages sabil did not mean only a drinking building but was a term for many buildings endowed "in the way of God": water sabils, troughs for animals, sabil schools for orphans, sabil khans for strangers and sabil mills {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.7} {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.8}. The word then clung to water buildings alone until it prevailed over other terms, especially in the Mamluk period {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.8}.

## Think about it
The record says sabils drew water from wells, pools, springs or rain, and that many endowments kept them working {cite:haddad-asbila-makka-madina-2004 p.9}. If you built a fountain for your own neighbourhood today, which would you plan first: the water source, the people it serves, or the money to keep it running, and why would the other two fail without it?
