## Brief
This lesson asks you to describe the basic structure and function of vital organs, here the heart and the lungs, and how body systems relate to each other. The museum offers one physician's account of the route the blood takes between the two sides of the heart through the lung. Other organs and other systems are for your class to study. {cite:ontario-science-tech-2022}

## What path does blood follow through the heart and lungs?
Draw the heart as two sides, with the lungs beside it. Mark where blood enters, where it leaves and which organ it passes through, then compare your drawing with a partner's.

## How does one organ depend on another?
Choose the heart or the lungs. Ask what the other organ would have to do differently if the first stopped working well, and what you could measure to find out.

## Go deeper
Your heart has two big rooms, one on the right and one on the left, with a wall between them {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}. About eight hundred years ago, doctors thought this wall had tiny holes, too small to see, and that blood went through them from one side to the other {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}. [[person/ibn-al-nafis|Ibn al-Nafis]] wanted to explain Ibn Sina's anatomy, and when he reached the heart he departed from Ibn Sina and from Galen before him {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Ibn al-Nafis}. The physician looked and said there are no holes here and the wall is solid: the blood leaves the right room, goes to the lung and comes back to the left room {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}.

## Think about it
Doctors had taught that blood crosses the wall of the heart through tiny holes, but Ibn al-Nafis looked and said the wall is solid. Whom would you believe, an old teaching or a doctor who looked, and why?
