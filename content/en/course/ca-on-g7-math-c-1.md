## Brief
This lesson asks you to add and subtract monomials of degree 1, to evaluate algebraic expressions, and to solve equations with several terms and verify the solutions. The museum offers two glimpses: al-Khwarizmi's six types of equation and al-Karaji's arithmetic of composite expressions; decimal numbers in equations and the contexts of real life are for your class to bring. {cite:ontario-curriculum-elementary-mathematics}

## How do you know an answer is right?
Put the answer back into the original problem and see whether the equality holds. If an expression has like terms, where do you collect them before you evaluate it?

## Go deeper
One of al-Khwarizmi's six types has two answers. The problem is “a square and twenty-one equal ten of its roots”, which has two answers: root three and square nine, or root seven and square forty-nine {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.34}. He says this type alone “works by both addition and subtraction” {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.34}. If the square of half the roots is less than the number, he calls the problem “impossible” {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.35}. All of this is in the record of [[concept/al-durub-al-sitta|the six types]].

## Why they did it
The records preserve no sentence in which al-Karaji says why he extended arithmetic to expressions with an unknown. They show a historian's account of what moved the work. MacTutor explains the new direction through the encounter between Diophantine arithmetic and Arabic algebraic concepts, with an emphasis on generalization and on more widely applicable methods {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. TDV connects al-Fakhri with the patronage of Fakhr al-Mulk in the Buyid setting {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography} {cite:tdv-kereci pp. 277–278}.

## Often misunderstood
“Polynomial arithmetic” is the museum's modern name for part of [[invention/karaji-polynomial-arithmetic|al-Karaji's work]], not a claim that he used today's definitions or notation {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. The record says the work was neither algebra created from nothing nor the invention of the letters and exponents used in its examples {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. His division was of a composite quantity by a single term, not a method for dividing any polynomial by any other {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}.

## Think about it
The record says that substitution can uncover an arithmetic mistake, but that agreement at one value does not prove a rule for every value {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. Why is one value not enough, and what else would you want to see before you trusted a rule?
