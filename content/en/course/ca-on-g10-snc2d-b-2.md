## Brief
This lesson asks you to use a research process to investigate a disease or abnormality of the tissues, organs or systems of humans or plants. The museum offers one medieval physician's comparison of two diseases, as a document in the history of medicine; the research on a disease of your own choice is for your class. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## What makes a good comparison of two diseases?
Look at the expectation: it asks for a research process. Ask what you would list first, what you would set side by side, and what you would call a difference rather than a resemblance.

## Go deeper
Read the preface as the treatise's own account of why it exists. In [[invention/al-razi-smallpox-measles|the record of al-Razi's comparison]], the author's preface describes discussion at the home of a man interested in useful learning, followed by a request for a connected, organized treatment of the disease {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848 pp.23–24}. The record presents this as the text's account of its occasion, not an independent record proving the host's identity or the meeting's date. The opening then discusses what its author considers references in Galen; he reports asking people familiar with Syriac and Greek and criticizes the inadequacy of earlier explanation as it reached him {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848 pp.27–28}. The record reads this as testimony about his reading and project, not comprehensive proof that no earlier observer in any language distinguished the diseases. It is a document in medical history, not a guide to diagnosing or treating illness today.

## Why they did it
The motive the record preserves is the preface's stated purpose, the request for a connected, organized treatment of the disease {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848 pp.23–24}. The record sums up its significance as an educational purpose: gathering dispersed knowledge into an accessible arrangement {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848 pp.23–24}. In his opening, by his own assessment, he asks for fuller and better-organized explanation than he found {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848 pp.23–28}. His frame of explanation is humoral: the text connects the differences it draws to a humoral explanation involving blood, bile and vessels near the spine {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848 pp.34–35}, and the record says humours are an older explanatory framework, not another name for viruses or immunity {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848 pp.34–35}. The record reports no other statement of motive.

## Two views
Was al-Razi the first to tell the two diseases apart? TDV calls him the first to diagnose the two diseases correctly, and the record identifies this as Kaya's assessment, not an independent comparison of all earlier writings performed for the record {cite:tdv-razi-ebu-bekir pp.479–485}. The record's own reading is narrower: the chapter on the comparison establishes that he did compare the diseases, while the opening shows how he judged predecessors, which supports documented improvement rather than absolute priority {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848 pp.27–28,34–35}. It adds that calling the treatise the beginning of every differential diagnosis goes beyond this reading {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848 pp.27–28,34–35}. The museum gives no verdict.

## Often misunderstood
The treatise is sometimes made to stand for much more than it does. The record does not credit it with vaccines or smallpox eradication; it explains that WHO dates eradication to 1980 and describes present-day measles as viral and vaccine-preventable, different historical layers from the humoral explanation read here {cite:who-smallpox-overview eradication} {cite:who-measles-overview overview}. It also says that distinguishing comparison from modern prevention clarifies the contribution rather than diminishing it, and that the medieval comparison must not be used as a home diagnostic test {cite:who-smallpox-overview eradication} {cite:who-measles-overview overview}.

## How do we know?
The museum reads selections from William Alexander Greenhill's English translation, printed in London, not an independently collated Arabic original or all its manuscripts {cite:wellcome-razi-greenhill-1848 catalogue}. Greenhill expressly says the composition date is unknown, while an internal reference places the work after the Mansuri {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848 p.3}. Greenhill describes an Arabic-to-Syriac-to-Greek route while acknowledging unknown translators and dates {cite:britannica-al-razi biography} {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848 pp.3–4}. He also notes that two Arabic terms rendered with words for pustules and vesicles need not carry the precise definitions used by physicians in his own time {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848 p.32}. The passages supply neither a patient count nor diagnostic sensitivity and specificity {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848 pp.32–33}.

## Think about it
TDV's entry calls al-Razi the first to diagnose the two diseases correctly, while the museum's reading of the comparison chapter and the opening supports only documented improvement. Which claim is easier to test, and how? What would you need to compare in earlier texts before the word first could be justified, and why could a single earlier text undo it?
