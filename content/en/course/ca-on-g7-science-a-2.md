## Brief
This lesson asks you to analyse contributions to science and technology from various communities. The museum offers two: the decimal reckoning that entered the Islamic world through a book by al-Khwarizmi, and the moving of paper-making to Baghdad. The contributions of other communities your class must add from elsewhere. {cite:ontario-science-tech-2022}

## Whose contribution is it?
Each record separates what one community brought from what another took up. As you read, name separately the origin, the people who carried it and the place where it was taken up.

## Go deeper
In the preface of his algebra, [[invention/algorism|al-Khwarizmi]] describes the decimal build of numbers himself. He found, he says, that everything people need of reckoning is number, and that all numbers are composed of one. The spoken numbers from one to ten follow the pattern of one; then ten is doubled and tripled as one was, giving twenty and thirty up to a hundred; then a hundred is doubled and tripled up to a thousand; and so the thousand is repeated at every order, to the furthest number that can be grasped {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 30}. The TDV encyclopaedia describes this passage as a short account of how number is expressed in a positional system with base ten {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227}.

## Why they did it
The Arabic original of al-Khwarizmi's book on Indian reckoning is lost {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227}, so the museum holds no preface of that book. [[work/al-jabr-wal-muqabala|His algebra]] has one. In it he says he wrote with the encouragement of the caliph al-Ma'mun, whose love of learning and closeness to scholars he praises {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.29}, and that he made it "comprehensive of the fine and the weighty in reckoning" for what people need in their inheritances, bequests, partitions, judgements and trade, in measuring land, digging canals, geometry and the like {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.30}. The book opens with a sermon praising God and blessing the Prophet {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.29}.

## Two views
The museum's record of [[invention/baghdad-papermaking|papermaking in Baghdad]] gives two accounts. Osman Ersoy, in the TDV encyclopaedia, attributes the proposal for a paper workshop in Baghdad to [[person/al-fadl-ibn-yahya-al-barmaki|al-Fadl ibn Yahya al-Barmaki]] in 178 AH/794–795, under Harun al-Rashid {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}. Jonathan Bloom, in Iranica, supports manufacture in Baghdad by the late eighth century without independently confirming that year or proposer {cite:iranica-paper-i early history}. The record notes that no contemporary foundation document or workshop accounts were read for it {cite:tdv-kagit Baghdad paragraph}. The museum gives no verdict between the two.

## Think about it
Al-Khwarizmi says he wrote for the reckoning people require in their inheritances, bequests, trade and the measuring of land {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 30}. The record also says the numerals and the decimal system are Indian, and that Indian reckoning was in Baghdad before him {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227}. For a person who needs to reckon in trade or in dividing an inheritance, does it matter more who first invented the numerals or that a clear book taught how to use them, and why?
