## Brief
This lesson asks you to explain the anatomy of the circulatory system and its function in carrying substances vital to health. The museum does not teach that anatomy. It offers one record, on how the route of the blood through the lungs was first described, and your class must bring the anatomy itself from elsewhere. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## What should a diagram of the heart let you check?
Look for the chambers, the vessels that enter and leave them, and the direction in which blood is said to move. Ask which claims about the route you can trace on the diagram and which you must take from the text.

## Go deeper
Look at the second passage and the diagram. On folio 65 recto and verso, [[person/ibn-al-nafis|Ibn al-Nafis]] repeats that there is no passage at all between the ventricles, and that the septum is thicker than the rest of the heart to stop the blood passing {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}. Then he says that the blood enters the left ventricle from the lung, after it has been heated in the right ventricle and risen from it {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}. In a third passage, on folio 46, he says that there are perceptible passages, *manafidh*, between the pulmonary artery and the pulmonary vein {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}. The diagram in the record of [[invention/pulmonary-circulation|the pulmonary circulation]] sets two schemes side by side: Galen's, in which the blood crosses the septum, and Ibn al-Nafis's, in which it passes through the lung {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}. Trace each route on a drawing of the heart.

## Why they did it
No sentence in the record gives Ibn al-Nafis's reason for denying the pores in his own words. What it shows is the problem they solved. The pores puzzled anatomists for more than a thousand years, yet Galen's scheme needed them, because nobody yet knew that a large amount of blood flows from the lungs to the heart {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}. His book is a commentary on the Canon: he takes Ibn Sina's framework and parts from it at the septum {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}.

## Two views
Did Servetus know Ibn al-Nafis's work? The source does not dispute that Ibn al-Nafis described the passage of blood through the lungs about three hundred years before anyone in Europe; the question it raises is whether Servetus and those after him knew his work {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}. Servetus's words on the septum and the lungs resemble Ibn al-Nafis's, which might suggest he knew the earlier work {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}. A 1940 study by Owsei Temkin pointed out inconsistencies between the two statements, and the 1547 Latin translation of the Commentary on Compound Drugs apparently said nothing about the pulmonary circulation {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}. West writes that many historians believe Servetus did not know Ibn al-Nafis's work; if so, both deserve credit, each in his own time {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}.

## How do we know?
The source does not say that the account spread in Islamic medicine after Ibn al-Nafis, and it does not trace the copies of the commentary or its readers {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880}. The passage is not known to have reached the West before the twentieth century {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}. Muhyi al-Din al-Tatawi came across the manuscript in the Prussian State Library in Berlin while writing a doctoral thesis, submitted in 1924, and the thesis was never published; Max Meyerhof learned of the discovery, wrote about it and translated the passages, and so saved it from oblivion {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}. The Wellcome Collection's catalogue describes its manuscript, WMS Or. 51, as containing the first description of the pulmonary circulation {cite:wellcome-wms-or-51 catalogue record}.

## Think about it
The record gives three pieces of evidence on whether Servetus read Ibn al-Nafis: a resemblance between the two statements, inconsistencies that Temkin pointed out, and a Latin translation of another book that apparently said nothing about the lungs {cite:west-2008-ibn-al-nafis pp. 1877–1880, §Pulmonary circulation}. Which of the three matters most for deciding, what would a critic say in reply, and why?
