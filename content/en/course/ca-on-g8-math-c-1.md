## Brief
This lesson asks you to solve equations that involve multiple terms, integers and decimal numbers in various contexts, and to verify your solutions. The museum offers two cards, on the science of algebra and on the types of equation in al-Khwarizmi's Book of al-Jabr and al-Muqabala; solving the equations of this grade is for your class. {cite:ontario-curriculum-elementary-mathematics}

## What stays true when you change both sides?
Write an equation with terms on both sides, then add the same quantity to both sides or remove the matching term from both. At each step check that the equation is still true for the solution. Then put the solution back into the original equation.

## Go deeper
Al-Khwarizmi shows the two operations of [[concept/algebra|algebra]] in the phrases of his problems. In his problems *al-jabr* is completing a quantity that has a deficiency or deduction by adding its like to both sides, as in “restore the thirty by the thing and add it to the two things” {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.121}. *Al-muqabala* is casting away like terms from both sides, as in “you cast away a square by a square” {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.69}. In his worked problems, as [[invention/algebra|the record of his algebra]] describes them, the restoring step appears again and again: a quantity “less ten things” is restored and the ten things are added to the other side {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 pp. 51–53}.

## Why they did it
His preface, as [[invention/algebra|the record of his algebra]] reports it, praises God and asks blessings on the Prophet Muhammad, then says what moved him to write {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 29}. The caliph al-Ma'mun loved learning, and this encouraged him {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 29}. He limited the book to what people need constantly: inheritance, bequests, the division of estates, judgements, trade, land measurement, digging canals, engineering, and other matters {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 30}. A long Book of Bequests in the same work applies its rules to shares of estates and bequests {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p. 83}.

## Two views
Where does the word *jabr* come from? The Encyclopaedia of Islam, in its article on [[person/al-khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]], reports Gandz's proposal that it comes from Assyrian *gabru*, and then notes a difficulty: the word is not attested in any intervening language such as Greek {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111}. The article judges it more likely that the word comes from medical vocabulary, where *jabr* means setting a dislocated limb {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111}. The record reports both and decides nothing.

## Think about it
Al-Khwarizmi says what his book is for, and the encyclopaedia proposes where its key word came from. Which of the two would you trust to tell you why the book was written, and why?
