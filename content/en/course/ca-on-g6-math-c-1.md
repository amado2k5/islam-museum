## Brief
This lesson asks you to determine pattern rules and use them to extend patterns, to predict and justify predictions, and to find missing elements. The museum offers one rule for building a table of numbers from the row before it; it does not serve growing, shrinking or linear patterns, graphs or algebraic expressions, which your class brings from elsewhere. {cite:ontario-curriculum-elementary-mathematics}

## How can you state a rule so that someone else can continue the pattern?
Write the rule in words first, then in a table of values. Predict the next two rows, and check your prediction by applying the rule.

## Go deeper
Al-Karaji's rule builds each new row of numbers from the row before it. The sources describe it so: the square gives one, two and one {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. To get the next list, keep a one at each end, add one and two to get three, and then two and one to get another three {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. The page he wrote is not in front of us: the record says that al-Samawal transmits the account of an original computation that no longer survives {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography} {cite:samawal-dsb algebra}.

## Why they did it
The records preserve no sentence in which al-Karaji says why he worked out these numbers, and the museum does not supply one. They show where the rule belongs: early-eleventh-century Baghdad algebra {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography} {cite:samawal-dsb algebra}. MacTutor reports that he calculated the cube of a sum in al-Fakhri, and the cube of a difference and fourth power of a sum in al-Badi {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. Al-Samawal admired him but also identified shortcomings and developed improvements {cite:samawal-mactutor Biography} {cite:samawal-dsb algebra}.

## Often misunderstood
The table is often called Pascal's triangle. The record says the name does not imply that the triangle was unknown before Pascal, and that MacTutor says so expressly {cite:pascal-mactutor Biography}. The history of Jaina mathematics describes a triangle in a tenth-century commentary on an older work {cite:jaina-mathematics-mactutor combinations}. For China, MacTutor describes Jia Xian's table {cite:jia-xian-mactutor Biography}. The record calls this comparable work, not something shown to have been borrowed from al-Karaji {cite:jia-xian-mactutor Biography} {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}.

## Think about it
The record checks the rule with chosen numbers, but says this does not alone prove the rule for every power {cite:karaji-mactutor Biography}. Why is an example that agrees not enough, and what else would you want to see before you trusted the rule for every row?
