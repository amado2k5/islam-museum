## Brief
This lesson asks you to analyse, from your own inquiry, the effects of different treatments on the growth of a disease-causing organism. The museum cannot supply that laboratory work. It offers one text on a question every such inquiry meets: what must be known before one accepts that a treatment caused an improvement. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## What else could explain the result?
Before you credit a treatment, list the other things that changed. Then decide which of them your inquiry controls and which it does not.

## Go deeper
Read two of the seven conditions in [[invention/ibn-sina-seven-drug-assessment-conditions|the record on Ibn Sina's conditions]]. The first concerns the substance's condition: Ibn Sina separates a drug's power from qualities acquired through temporary heating or cooling, changes in its substance or contact with another material; the observation may reflect a temporary condition rather than the property being investigated {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.318}. The second is a simple condition: with several conditions present, a symptom may disappear because one component changes, and the result may wrongly be attributed to direct action on another component; the text therefore distinguishes direct from incidental benefit {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.318}. The record adds that this is not a description of random allocation between two groups, but an attempt to reduce ambiguity within its account of disease causation {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.79}.

## Why they did it
The museum's records hold no statement in which Ibn Sina says why he wrote this chapter, and they do not supply a motive for him. What they show is the setting: the study places the beginning of the Canon in Jorjan, its continuation in Rayy and its completion in Hamadan, without independently dating or locating the writing of these particular conditions {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.78}. The chapter explains power through qualities such as hot and cold and through the distinction between an intrinsic property and an incidental effect {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319}, and its opening names analogy alongside experience {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.318}. The practical difficulty the conditions address, as the record puts it, is how to avoid assigning a substance a power on inadequate grounds {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.318}.

## Two views
The modern study uses the conditions to discuss confounding and animal-to-human inference, comparisons that illuminate the historical questions {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 pp.79–80}. Britannica, for its part, warns against assuming that his contribution meant medieval physicians possessed modern research protocols {cite:britannica-avicenna final-evidence-discussion}. The record says that similar questions do not prove direct descent of every later drug test or establish randomization, masking and placebo comparison in the passage {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319}. The record presents both readings and does not choose between them.

## Often misunderstood
It is easy to take the seven conditions for the invention of experiment or for the first clinical trial. The contribution presented is that organization of interpretive conditions within humoral medicine, not the invention of all experimentation or the birth of a modern clinical trial {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 pp.79–80}. "Power" here is not a modern laboratory measurement: it concerns a substance's capacity to act, explained through qualities such as hot and cold and through the distinction between an intrinsic property and an incidental effect {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319}. Hot and cold here do not mean readings on a modern thermometer {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319}.

## How do we know?
The directly examined witness consists of printed pages 318–319, which establishes where the conditions stand in the inspected edition, not the survival of the author's personal experimental records {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319}. The study identifies a different witness, the Rome 1593 printing in the Sibbald Library, with Tibi translating the text it presents {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 pp.78–79}. The presence of the passage in both witnesses neither equates their pagination nor turns a later printing into the author's manuscript {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319} {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.79}. The English translation inserts a bracketed word when explaining progression, whereas the Arabic discusses power, disease strength and the weaker case; the bracketed word is not passed off as the Arabic author's own {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 p.318} {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.80}. Ask who supplied each interpretation: Ibn Sina, the translator or the commentators.

## Think about it
The record says the conditions are not a description of random allocation between two groups {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 p.79}, and that the modern study uses them to discuss confounding and animal-to-human inference {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 pp.79–80}. Take a position on whether these conditions belong to the history of controlled comparison or to something else, and answer the strongest objection to it: is the question Ibn Sina asks not the same question that a controlled comparison asks today?
