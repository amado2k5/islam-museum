## Brief
This lesson asks you to investigate the laws of reflection with plane and curved mirrors, to predict and test the images they form, and to describe those images with ray diagrams. The museum offers one geometrical problem about a circular mirror from the Islamic world, which is a construction on paper and not a laboratory result; the mirrors, the measuring and the images are for your class. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## What must be equal for a ray to reflect?
Look at the expectation: it asks for the laws of reflection and for ray diagrams. Ask against what line the angles are measured when the mirror is curved, and why a flat mirror does not raise the question.

## Go deeper
Read how the problem is set, and what its first steps look like. In [[invention/ibn-al-haytham-circular-reflection|the record]] the givens are a circle representing the mirror in a plane, its center, and two points representing object and eye. The task is to construct a rim point satisfying equal incidence and reflection angles measured against the local normal {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.64–65}. This differs from drawing a chord or checking a ray already supplied, because the meeting point itself is unknown {cite:mathworld-alhazen-problem problem statement}. In the first two lemmas, for example, Ibn al-Haytham treats a segment cut off between a circle and its diameter, separately handling its location inside or outside {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.62–63}. Some work uses circle–hyperbola intersections, and drawing both curves does not make the construction a straightedge-and-compass operation {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.59,62–63}.

## Why they did it
The museum's records preserve no sentence in which [[person/ibn-al-haytham|Ibn al-Haytham]] says why he took up this problem, and no preface that states a purpose. What they show is where it sits. The lemmas occur within an optical investigation but form linked geometrical problems: one lemma assists another, then the results return to locating reflection points {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.59–65}. The Encyclopaedia of Islam article says that in the fifth book of the Optics his mathematical genius reached its height when he resolved the problem that now bears his name {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 820}. The same article says he held that rays of light travel from the object to the eye, and not the reverse as Euclid, Ptolemy and al-Kindi maintained {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 821}.

## Often misunderstood
A famous name can suggest that one man began everything. The record cautions against that. His contribution here is geometrical lemmas and applications in Book V of the [[work/kitab-al-manazir|Book of Optics]], not invention of mirrors or discovery of reflection's law from nothing {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.59,64–65}. The name does not erase the earlier question attributed to Ptolemy, which the record reports through MathWorld, not from Ptolemy's own text {cite:mathworld-alhazen-problem history} {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 p.62}. Nor should he be credited with a general algebraic quartic formula: the historian's note states a modern equivalence to a fourth-degree equation and expressly says medieval Arabic mathematicians were unaware of that equivalence {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 p.70 note7}.

## How do we know?
The museum's reading is partial and says so. It read pages 59–75 of Hogendijk's 1996 study from the author's hosted copy and inspected images 59–65, which was not reading the entire paper, an Optics manuscript or the complete Sabra 1982 article {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.59–75}. The Arabic Optics source the museum holds covers Books 1–3, not Book V {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 reliability note}. The study translates al-Mu'taman's lemmas, and his adaptations must not become quotations from Ibn al-Haytham {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.59–75}. Hogendijk compares al-Mu'taman's sequence with that of the Optics and regards the correspondence as evidence of use, although al-Mu'taman does not name his sources; the record calls the attribution of the simplifications historical inference, not a signed declaration {cite:hogendijk-alhazen-lemmas-1996 pp.59–61}.

## Think about it
For the claim that this problem is Ibn al-Haytham's, the record relies on two kinds of source: MathWorld, which attributes an earlier formulation to Ptolemy, and Hogendijk's study, which reads and compares the lemmas. The record says the first is reported through the reference actually read, not from Ptolemy's text. Which source should carry more weight on who first posed the problem, and which on who first solved it, and why? What would you need to see to change your answer?

## Try this
Read the record of [[invention/ibn-al-haytham-circular-reflection|Ibn al-Haytham's circular-mirror reflection method]]. On a drawn circle, mark an object and an eye, draw the radius to a chosen rim point, and test whether the angles on its two sides are equal.
