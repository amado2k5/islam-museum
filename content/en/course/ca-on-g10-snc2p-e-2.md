## Brief
This lesson asks you to explain how a property of light is used in how an optical device works. The museum offers one experiment of Ibn al-Haytham, a dark room with a small hole, which a modern encyclopedia calls the basis of today's camera; other devices, and the properties of colour, are for your class. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## What must light do for the hole to work?
The expectation asks you to link a property of light to how a device works. Say what the light does on its way through the hole and what you would see on the wall.

## Go deeper
Read the first version of the experiment, the one with sunlight. When sunlight enters a dark room through a hole, and the air of the room is clouded with dust or smoke, the light can be seen stretching in a straight line from the hole to the spot where it falls on the floor or wall {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p. 73}. Set an opaque body anywhere on that line and the light falls on the body and vanishes from the spot beyond; remove the body and the spot returns. Lay a straight ruler along the line between the hole and the spot and the light keeps to the ruler's edge: its path is perfectly straight {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p. 74}. [[person/ibn-al-haytham|Ibn al-Haytham]] says the same holds for the light of the moon {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p. 74}. The record of [[invention/camera-obscura|the dark room]] gives the other versions.

## Why they did it
The record preserves no sentence in which Ibn al-Haytham says why he made this experiment. It does report the editor's description of the whole book as a study of the properties of light in its three states, travelling straight, reflected and bent, built on experiment, which Ibn al-Haytham called i'tibar, together with mathematical methods {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p. 9}. The first book opens with the basmala {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p. 60}. TDV lists, besides his works in mathematics, astronomy and optics, treatises in which he answered Abu Bakr al-Razi's criticism of prophethood and took issue with the Mu'tazila on the divine attributes {cite:tdv-ibnul-heysem pp. 82–87}. How he joined these two kinds of writing is not answered in the record.

## Often misunderstood
A reader may take Ibn al-Haytham to be the inventor of the dark room. The record says no one source names a first inventor of the dark room, and that he does not claim the title: he presents light through holes as a familiar sight and turns it into a test {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p. 73}. The museum therefore records him as an improver {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p. 73}. TDV credits him with devising the dark-room experiment to prove that light travels in straight lines {cite:tdv-ibnul-heysem pp. 82–87}.

## How do we know?
The record shows three limits. First, the Manazir carries no date of writing; the third list of his works runs to the end of 429 AH (1037–1038 CE), holds 92 titles and names the Manazir among them {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p. 27}, and the editor believes Ibn al-Haytham may have drawn it up himself {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p. 30}. Second, the Latin translation, through which the book reached Europe, lacks the first three chapters of the first book {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp. 49–50}, so the dust and ruler passage of chapter 3 did not reach Latin readers {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 pp. 49–50}. Third, the Arabic text is edited for the first three books only, by A. I. Sabra, Kuwait, 1983 {cite:manazir-sabra-1983}.

## Think about it
The test lays a ruler along the light and sets an opaque body on its line {cite:manazir-sabra-1983 p. 74}. Which step would convince you more that light travels in a straight line, the cutting body or the ruler, why, and what would a doubter say in answer?
