## Brief
This lesson asks you to show how changing dimensions affects perimeter, area and volume, and to solve problems with the relationships among volumes. The museum offers the square whose side doubles and the one-third rule for the volume of a cone or pyramid; prisms, cylinders and surface area are for your class to bring. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-mathematics}

## What changes when a length is doubled?
Draw the square with side one, two and three, and record the areas. Ask how the area changes when the side doubles, then think about what might happen to a volume when all the dimensions double.

## Go deeper
In al-Khwarizmi's chapter on [[concept/ilm-al-misaha|mensuration]], as the record reports it, the volume of a pointed cone or pyramid is a third of its base times its height, and a truncated one is completed to a point and the added part subtracted {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.80} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.81}. The study of [[invention/kashi-muqarnas-mensuration|al-Kashi's muqarnas measurement]] carries a related lesson on scale. Its Figure 8 gives approximately 0.707107 for a 45-degree rhombus and 0.353553 for its half; these are areas in squared-module units, not centimetres or volumes of cavities {cite:dold-samplonius-muqarnas-1992 pp210-213}. The record adds that doubling every length quadruples area, so scale and units are part of the mathematical answer {cite:dold-samplonius-muqarnas-1992 fig8-p213}.

## Why they did it
[[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]] says in his preface that the measurement of land is among the needs he wrote his book for {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.30}. The record preserves no sentence in which he or al-Kashi explains why they treated solids or ceilings. For al-Kashi, Dold-Samplonius separates what the text says from what she infers: her explanation for his relatively careless treatment of simple muqarnas is an interpretation, not direct testimony about the author's intentions {cite:dold-samplonius-muqarnas-1992 pp193-194-and211}.

## Often misunderstood
Many printed digits can suggest absolute accuracy, but the record says they do not promise absolute accuracy for every curved surface {cite:dold-samplonius-muqarnas-1992 pp196-and209-221}. For curved muqarnas the cell bases are summed and multiplied by a coefficient; the historian explains it as an average that unifies the calculation, not a magical constant valid for any chosen curvature {cite:dold-samplonius-muqarnas-1992 pp214-221}.

## How do we know?
Dold-Samplonius identifies a projection inconsistency: equating the roof area with the area on which the muqarnas is built conflicts with inclined roofs in the definition and the examples {cite:dold-samplonius-muqarnas-1992 pp210-211}. She also does not know how some of the values given for the intermediate elements were obtained, and her reconstructions yield different numbers {cite:dold-samplonius-muqarnas-1992 pp214-221}. The record says that reading should be neither a celebration that suppresses the inconsistency nor a wholesale dismissal because it exists {cite:dold-samplonius-muqarnas-1992 pp210-211}.

## Think about it
Dold-Samplonius finds that one step of al-Kashi's method conflicts with the inclined roofs in his own definition and examples. Take a position: should a student still learn the method? What is the strongest objection to your position, and how do you answer it?
