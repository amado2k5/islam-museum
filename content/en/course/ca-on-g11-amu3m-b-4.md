## Brief

This lesson asks you to gather information from reliable sources on the background of musicians and to analyse it so as to sharpen your judgements. The museum offers one case: a ninth-century musician whose life reaches us through a much later author. Audience response and contemporary criticism are the work of your class. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-arts}

## Who tells the story, and when?

For each report in the record, write who told it, how long after the events, and whether a contemporary witness exists. What makes one source more reliable than another?

## What is left out of a famous story?

See how the record describes what was left out of the famous portrait and what was added to it. Does criticising a portrait mean that everything in it is false?

## Go deeper
Here is Ziryab's story as Maqqari tells it, in the records. At Baghdad the oud of his teacher Ishaq al-Mawsili stands beside his own, and the two compete over singing and the refinement of the instrument {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 pp.122–123}. He meets Harun al-Rashid, refuses to use the teacher's oud, prefers an instrument made by his own hands, departs westward and finds generous Andalusi patronage {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 pp.122–125}. The [[person/ziryab|record on Ziryab]] says the account has him leave amid professional jealousy, and calls it an attributed narrative, not a contemporary proceeding the museum inspected {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 pp.122–124}. The [[invention/ziryab-fifth-string|fifth-string record]] adds that it gives a broad ninth-century setting and no year, day or court session for the added string {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari v.3 p.126}.

## Two views
The fifth-string record sets two readings of the same attribution side by side. The TDV encyclopaedia of Arslan and Erkocoglu phrases priority more categorically, and the record keeps its authors' attribution visible without treating a later encyclopaedia as an independent ancient artefact confirming the claim {cite:tdv-ziryab-arslan-erkocoglu pp.464–465}. Dwight Reynolds compares Ibn Hayyan and Maqqari and finds omissions of rivals, of the children's achievements and of multiple voices; the record says this qualifies confidence in the composite portrait and is not an experiment proving the string itself false {cite:reynolds-ziryab-2008 pp.158–166}. The museum records both and decides between neither.

## How do we know?
The chain is long. The record says Maqqari wrote much later, and that Reynolds's comparison reveals multiple, sometimes contradictory sources behind the Ibn Hayyan material he used {cite:reynolds-ziryab-2008 pp.155–158}. The museum has not read Ibn Hayyan's original: its record relies on the scholar's comparison {cite:reynolds-ziryab-2008 p.161}. A modern Al Jazeera account adds a plectrum of seven eagle-wing feathers soaked in oil, which the record keeps apart as modern elaboration, not text verified in Maqqari {cite:aljazeera-ziryab-2022 plectrum discussion}. And the photograph shows a much later oud in Cleveland, not one surviving from his court {cite:cleveland-oud-1918-350 catalogue}.

## Think about it
Reynolds finds that Maqqari left out rivals and less flattering anecdotes in a portrait evoking an ideal Andalusi past, yet the record says criticism of the portrait is not proof that every detail is false {cite:reynolds-ziryab-2008 pp.158–166}. Take a position and answer the strongest objection to it: should a researcher use Maqqari's story of the red string at all, and if so, in what words?
