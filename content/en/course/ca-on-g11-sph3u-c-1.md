## Brief
This lesson asks you to give examples of different forces and to describe the effect of each on an object's velocity. The museum offers one historical description of attraction, a medieval author's explanation of why objects fall towards the Earth. Friction, normal force and the effects on velocity are for your class to bring. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## Which force is acting, and on what?
Look at the expectation: it asks for examples of forces and their effect on velocity. For the description, ask what is pulling, what is being pulled, and whether it says anything about the object's speed or direction.

## Go deeper
Read one comparison in a preface. TDV records a physical explanation in [[person/al-idrisi-muhammad-ibn-abd-allah|al-Idrisi]]'s preface: he compares the earth's attraction of objects with a magnet attracting iron, explaining why airborne objects fall towards the earth {cite:tdv-idrisi-serif pp.493–495}. The book itself begins with the shape of the earth. The earth, al-Idrisi writes, is round like a ball, with the water clinging to it, and earth and water rest in the middle of the heavens like the yolk in an egg {cite:nuzhat-al-mushtaq-2002 p. 7}. The museum attributes the magnet analogy to him rather than presenting a modern gravitational law or experimental discovery {cite:tdv-idrisi-serif pp.493–495}. The comparison is part of the explanation of the world that comes before the book's maps and distances {cite:tdv-idrisi-serif pp.493–495}.

## Why they did it
Al-Idrisi's preface tells the story of the work as the story of the king. [[person/roger-of-sicily|Roger]], he writes, was learned in the mathematical sciences, and when his realm had grown he wanted to know its lands with certainty {cite:nuzhat-al-mushtaq-2002 p. 5}. The books did not satisfy him, so he questioned those who knew the subject and then the travellers, keeping what they agreed on and setting aside what they disputed, for about fifteen years {cite:nuzhat-al-mushtaq-2002 p. 6}. In the preface it is Roger who drives the inquiry, and al-Idrisi presents himself as the one who carried out his order {cite:nuzhat-al-mushtaq-2002 pp. 5–7}. The records give no purpose of al-Idrisi's own beyond that order.

## Often misunderstood
A comparison is not a law. The museum attributes the magnet analogy to al-Idrisi and does not present it as a modern gravitational law or an experimental discovery {cite:tdv-idrisi-serif pp.493–495}. The record adds a second caution: a spherical earth and a correct distribution of climates and habitation are separate claims, and accepting the former does not make every regional description accurate {cite:tdv-idrisi-serif pp.493–495}.

## How do we know?
In the record, the magnet comparison rests on TDV's reading of the preface, while the story of Roger's inquiry and the shape of the earth come from the Arabic preface, in the Cairo edition of 2002 {cite:tdv-idrisi-serif pp.493–495} {cite:nuzhat-al-mushtaq-2002 pp. 3–14}. TDV also notes the limits of the book: its reports on the lands of the Turks are fuller than those of earlier geographers but at times blurred by legend {cite:tdv-idrisi-serif pp. 493–495}. The sources read for the record say nothing of what became of the silver disc. Ask which statements about the falling of objects you could check in al-Idrisi's own words, and which you receive through a summary.

## Think about it
Does comparing the earth's pull to a magnet's pull explain why objects fall, or only name it, and what does the comparison leave unsaid about how a falling object's speed or direction changes?
