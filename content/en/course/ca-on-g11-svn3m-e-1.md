## Brief
This lesson asks you to explain current practices for dealing with substances that are not environmentally friendly. The museum offers two religious answers about reusing treated sewage and household wastewater; how treatment works, and how it is tested, is for your class to explain. These answers are rulings on ritual purity (tahara), not safety tests, and the museum gives no safety guidance. The records name treatment only in passing; the processes themselves come from your class. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## What counts as clean?
Compare the two answers: what each says treatment achieves, what it still leaves, and what use each allows. Then ask what a laboratory would measure.

## Go deeper
Read the question each body was asked, because the two questions differ. In the first, the questioner said the hospital gardens are watered with recycled sewage and that staff are exposed to its spray {cite:binbaz fatwa 3123}. He asked whether this water is impure and breaks ablution, and whether the fruit of trees watered with it may be eaten {cite:binbaz fatwa 3123}. In the [[work/fatwa-aliftaa-jo-reclaimed-wastewater-in-mosques|second]], the questioner asked about using reclaimed water in mosques to wash courtyards, for cleaning and for ablution, noting that it is reclaimed at treatment plants by chemical treatment and physical separation of impurities, and that it originates as household wastewater {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3886}. Only the second question names a treatment method; the [[work/fatwa-binbaz-recycled-sewage-water|first]] does not. Each record reports what was said in the answer, and neither issues a ruling of its own.

## Two views
[[person/ibn-baz-abd-al-aziz|Ibn Baz]] answered that once the water has been treated until the colour, taste and smell of impurity are gone and it has become fresh and sound, it takes the ruling of purity {cite:binbaz fatwa 3123}. If traces of impurity remain, it is not to be used for irrigation or anything else, and should be diverted where it harms no one {cite:binbaz fatwa 3123}. Jordan's General Iftaa Department answered that sewage water is impure, however large its quantity, because its qualities have changed; impure water becomes pure by adding a large quantity of water until the whole returns to its original state, as it quoted from [[person/al-nawawi|al-Nawawi]]'s Minhaj al-Talibin {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3886}. It said purification by chemical treatment and physical separation is not enough to deem the water pure, since it has not truly shed the impurity {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3886}. It concluded that the water may not be used for ablution or for cleaning the body or clothes, and that cleaning mosques with it should be avoided out of respect for them {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3886}.

## How do we know?
Each record reports a published answer, not a measurement. The answer of Ibn Baz is published on binbaz.org.sa, the official site of Ibn Baz's legacy, as fatwa 3123 in the Fatawa al-Durus collection {cite:binbaz fatwa 3123}. Jordan's General Iftaa Department published its fatwa on aliftaa.jo as no. 3886, dated 6 June 2024 {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3886}. The Department notes that Jordanian standard no. 893/2021 for reclaimed household wastewater does not allow its use for every purpose {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3886}. The museum records each text as a historical document attributed to its author or issuing body, and it issues no ruling of its own {cite:binbaz fatwa 3123} {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3886}.

## Think about it
Both answers speak of the colour, taste and smell of impurity. Are a person's senses enough to know that what was in treated water is gone, or would a measurement be needed to show what remains? Take a position, and answer the strongest objection to it, the Department's statement that chemical treatment and physical separation are not enough to deem the water pure since it has not truly shed the impurity {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3886}: why does your position still stand?
