## Brief
This lesson asks you to evaluate food-distribution systems by their impact on local and global food security. The museum offers one legal answer on one practice, withholding food to wait for higher prices; the systems themselves, such as fair trade and local versus imported food, are for your class to bring and evaluate. The record is a ruling on one practice, not a system. {cite:ontario-curriculum-social-sciences-humanities}

## Who gains and who loses when food is held back?
Identify who holds the food, who needs it and when. Ask which part of food security, availability or access, the practice changes, and what evidence your class would need to measure it.

## Go deeper
Read how one answer is built. A food trader asked [[work/fatwa-aliftaa-jo-hoarding-food-for-higher-prices|Jordan's General Iftaa Department]] about merchants who withhold goods because they know prices will rise soon {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3856}. The Department answered that the sharia forbids hoarding because it harms people, defining it as holding what was bought at a time of high prices to sell it dearer when need is acute {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3856}. It said hoarding covers all goods people need if withholding them harms people, the Maliki view and that of Abu Yusuf among the Hanafis, quoting Ibn Rushd al-Jadd's al-Bayan wa-l-Tahsil and al-Kasani's Bada'i al-Sana'i {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3856}. It said hoarding staple foods is more gravely forbidden, quoting Zakariyya al-Ansari's Asna al-Matalib that it is forbidden because it straitens people, Ibn Hajar al-Haytami's al-Zawajir counting it among the major sins, and his report that al-Ghazali extended staples to what supports them, such as meat and fruit {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3856}. The Department published the fatwa as no. 3856, dated 7 March 2024 {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3856}. The answer gives harm to people as its reason and rests on jurists it names.

## How do we know?
A fatwa is an answer a body published to a question, so it witnesses what the Department answered {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3856}; the museum records it as a document attributed to the body that issued it, and issues no ruling {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3856}. The Department quotes jurists and books by name, and the record does not print those texts. In [[concept/ihtikar|the record of hoarding]] al-Maraghi defines it as withholding goods to shrink supply, raise prices and let the hoarder dictate sales {cite:min-qadaya-al-mal-wal-amal-azhar p. 34}, and cites the hadith "whoever hoards is a sinner" while noting in the margin that some of the chapter's wording is weak {cite:min-qadaya-al-mal-wal-amal-azhar p. 34}. Neither record names a merchant who hoarded or a penalty that fell on one; both witness what was ruled, not what happened in markets.

## Think about it
The Department defines hoarding as holding what was bought at a time of high prices to sell it dearer when need is acute {cite:aliftaa-jo fatwa 3856}; al-Maraghi defines it as withholding goods to shrink supply, raise prices and let the hoarder dictate sales {cite:min-qadaya-al-mal-wal-amal-azhar p. 34}. Which definition would be easier for an official to apply to a particular merchant, and why?
