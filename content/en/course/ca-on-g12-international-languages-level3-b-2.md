## Brief
This lesson asks you to identify conventions of greeting and address, and to use them appropriately when you speak. The museum offers two: how a greeting is given on meeting and on parting, and how a person is honoured by a kunya. The source for the greeting is a television programme and a lesson. The phrases you practise, regional variants, and everyday formality, your class brings. {cite:ontario-curriculum-classical-studies-intl-languages}

## When do you greet, and how do you address someone?
Notice the occasions for the greeting in the record: on meeting and on parting. Then compare how you would address an older person, a friend and a stranger, and what changes between them.

## Go deeper
The Kuwaiti encyclopaedia of jurisprudence sets the [[concept/kunya-fiqh|kunya]] beside two other kinds of name. A laqab is a byname that praises or blames, and in grammar the ism is a word that signifies a meaning in itself without a time attached {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13538} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13539}. On the kunya itself, most jurists hold it recommended for people of merit, men and women, and for a man to take the kunya of his eldest son, and a woman that of her eldest {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13541}. Ibn Muflih cites the report of Hani ibn Yazid, who was called Abu al-Hakam and was given the kunya Abu Shurayh after his eldest son; another son may be used, as the Prophet was given the kunya Abu Ibrahim {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13541}.

## Two views
Under the heading on using the kunya of the Prophet, the encyclopaedia says scholars hold five views {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13539}. The first: no one may use the Prophet's kunya Abu al-Qasim during his life; after his death it is permitted, whether or not the bearer is named Muhammad, on a hadith, name yourselves by my name but do not take my kunya, and on the report of Abu Hurayra that the Prophet forbade combining his name and kunya {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13539} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13540}. Al-Rafi'i said this seems the soundest, since people have always done it without objection {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13540}. The fourth view forbids naming someone Muhammad and taking the kunya Abu al-Qasim altogether; Umar is reported to have said not to name anyone after a prophet, and Iyad thought he meant it out of reverence {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13540}. The fifth forbids the Prophet's kunya during his life, and after his death forbids it to men named Muhammad or Ahmad but permits it to others; Ibn Hajar called it the most equitable, though rare {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13540} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13541}.

## How do we know?
The five views are reported in one jurists' encyclopaedia, which names their grounds: the hadith quoted above, and the report of Abu Hurayra that the Prophet forbade combining his name and kunya {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13539} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13540}. It also names the scholars who weighed the views: al-Rafi'i, Iyad and Ibn Hajar {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13540} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13541}. The record reports the views and their grounds and does not say which is right.

## Think about it
Al-Rafi'i said the first view seems the soundest since people have always followed it without objection, and Ibn Hajar called the fifth the most equitable, though rare {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13540} {cite:fiqh-mawsua-kuwaitiyya-noor-v3 p.13541}. Defend your answer against the other side: when sources disagree about a custom, should long practice among people or the balance among the reports count for more?
