## Brief
This lesson asks you to describe the contributions of scientists, among them Canadians, to the fields you are studying. The museum offers one person of the Islamic world, an engineer, described in a single reference work; the Canadian scientists and the contributions closest to your own course are for your class to bring. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## What counts as a contribution?
A contribution can be a book, a device, a method or a question. For each person you meet, ask what they added, who says so, and what the record does not claim for them.

## Go deeper
Look at one machine from [[person/al-jazari-engineer|al-Jazari]]'s book, the elephant clock. The Met's catalogue describes the rider and the bird moving, a ball passing to a dragon and then to a vessel and gong every half hour, and the counting of balls to find how much time has passed {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}. Ask what each moving part is for, and what is being counted.

## Why they did it
The records hold no sentence in which al-Jazari says why he built his machines. They show where he stood. TDV places him in a tradition running from Hellenistic engineering to the Banu Musa, and describes an engineer improving earlier work {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. About the [[invention/jazari-double-piston-pump|double-piston pump]], TDV judges water-raising machines economically more important than elaborate clocks, although the book gives them less space {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}.

## Often misunderstood
It is easy to read a book of machines as a list of inventions. TDV describes an engineer improving earlier work, not the inventor of every underlying principle in his book {cite:tdv-cezeri pp. 505–506}. For the [[invention/jazari-animal-scoop-water-lifter|animal-powered scoop lifter]], the sources support his authorship and engineering context, not a claim that he built the device or originated every component {cite:jazari-hill-1974 pp.184–185} {cite:tdv-cezeri pp.505–506}.

## How do we know?
The picture of the elephant clock is a copy. Met folio 57.51.23 preserves the image in a copy by Farrukh ibn Abd al-Latif, dated 715/1315, probably produced in Syria or Iraq {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Overview}. The record says the copy is not an original working machine {cite:met-elephant-clock-575123 Catalogue Entry}. So the page shows how the book travelled and was illustrated.

## Think about it
TDV describes al-Jazari as an engineer improving earlier work. Would you count improving a machine as a contribution to science, and what is your reason?
