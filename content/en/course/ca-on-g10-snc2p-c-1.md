## Brief
This lesson asks you to analyse how chemical reactions are used in a product or process, and to identify their use in a particular profession, with its hazards. The museum offers one craft, the making of lustre pottery, as one author describes it; other products and professions, and the hazards of handling and disposing of chemicals, are for your class. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## What is changed, and by what, in a potter's workshop?
The expectations ask what the reaction does in making the product. List the materials the record names, the order of the steps and what the vessel looks like at the end.

## Go deeper
Follow the steps Arthur Lane describes for lustre pottery. The pigment consists essentially of sulphur in various forms with silver oxide, which stains glass or glaze yellow, and copper oxide, which gives the lustrous element {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 29}. It is mixed with an earthy vehicle such as red or yellow ochre and painted, using vinegar or wine-lees, on a glazed vessel already fired once {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 29}. The vessel is fired again lightly in a reducing kiln with little air and much smoke, and the ochre is rubbed away to leave a glittering metallic film imperceptible to the touch {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 29}. Read these as three steps of a flow chart: what goes on, what happens in the kiln, and what is left when the ochre is removed. The record of [[concept/islamic-lustreware|Islamic lustreware]] gives Lane's account.

## How do we know?
Much of this account is one scholar's reading, so ask what each claim rests on. Lane thinks Baghdad potters took their secrets to Cairo; the evidence includes the elephant bowl signed by its maker, a fragment dedicated to al-Hakim, and the signatures "Muslim" and "Sa'd" {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 36} {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 37}. The earliest dated Persian lustre piece is a Rayy bottle in the British Museum dated 575/1179 {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 56}. For Kairouan, [[person/rexford-newcomb|Newcomb]] notes lustre tiles said to have been brought from Baghdad under the Aghlabids {cite:newcomb-decorative-tiles-north-africa-1929 p.18}, while Lane says bichrome lustre tiles were sent to the [[building/jami-qayrawan|Great Mosque of Kairouan]] about 862 with a Baghdad potter {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 30}. Which of these rests on a signature or a date, and which on a report?

## Think about it
Lane thinks Baghdad potters took their secrets to Cairo {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 36}, and he sees the end of lustre in Egypt coinciding with its rise in Persia and at Raqqa, the secret being held by a very few families {cite:lane-early-islamic-pottery-1947 p. 56}. Take a position and answer the strongest objection to it: is a match in timing, together with signed pieces, enough to say that craftsmen carried a chemical process from one place to another, and why?
