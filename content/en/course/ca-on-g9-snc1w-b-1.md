## Brief
This lesson asks you to investigate factors and processes, including soil health and water quality, and to explain how they contribute to ecosystem sustainability. The museum offers one historical way of judging garden land and its water: a classification of land types and water sources. Biodiversity, air quality, succession and the link to ecosystem sustainability are for your class to investigate. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## How would you test a soil or a water source?
The expectation asks you to investigate. Think of what you could observe or measure about soil and water at a local site, and which of your observations would tell you whether plants could grow there across the seasons.

## Go deeper
Read how [[invention/ibn-bassal-soil-water-matching|Ibn Bassal]] looks at ground before he judges it. The author does not merely declare land good or bad. His soil chapter warns that the surface can differ from what lies beneath, and that judgment requires examination and repeated practical experience {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha ch.2}. Soft land, for example, is described through open pores admitting air and water; heavy land through changing behaviour in heat and rain {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha ch.2}. The water chapter distinguishes rain, rivers, springs and wells, then discusses their relations to plants, surface residues and seasonal effects {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha ch.1}. The record adds that these descriptions belong to an older account of nature and suitability, not to conductivity readings or chemical analyses performed on the text {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha ch.1}.

## Why they did it
The record preserves no sentence in which Ibn Bassal says why he wrote. What it shows is a practical question and a setting. The record says his writing addresses a recurring practical question: why does a plant respond differently in different ground? {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha chs.1–2} In Seville, the accounts connect him with a garden for al-Mutamid and a community of agronomists {cite:tdv-ibn-bassal biography}. As for the frame of understanding, the explanation employs natures and temperaments to understand plants and land, and the record says these deserve to be seen beside observation, not erased to make the book resemble a modern textbook {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha chs.1–2}. The records read here report no preface in which he states a religious purpose.

## Two views
Why does Ibn Bassal's book name no earlier agronomist? The Filaha Texts Project notes that his book does not cite earlier agronomic writers and reads this as evidence of personal experience's prominence {cite:ibn-bassal-filaha-profile biography}. The museum's record gives the counter-argument: failure to name an earlier book does not prove absence of inherited knowledge or the contribution of surrounding workers {cite:ibn-bassal-filaha-profile biography}. It also notes that Ozel describes his association with Ibn Wafid and work in the garden {cite:tdv-ibn-bassal biography}. The record's own reading is that a mode of authorship and the source of every practice are different questions, and it decides nothing between the two.

## Often misunderstood
It is easy to hear "ten land types and four waters" as a modern soil science. The record says otherwise. The contribution is this network of relationships, not invention of farming or the first recognition that soils differ {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha chs.1–2}. Its ten categories are not a modern laboratory standard, nor are the water names a table of chemical analyses {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha chs.1–2}. The categories mix criteria: colour, texture, location and a condition arising from previous cultivation {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha ch.2}. The record also declines to say that four water names mean four drinking-safety grades, or that ten land categories are quality scores from one to ten.

## How do we know?
The direct witness is the Filaha project's Arabic text. It is not a manuscript image or our own collation of every witness {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha contents-chs.1–2}. The record says its reading covers the contents, two complete chapters and the opening of chapter ten, not every chapter or fruit description {cite:ibn-bassal-qasd-filaha contents-chs.1–2}. Its sources also disagree on his life: Ozel says life dates are unknown, while the project offers broad approximations, and their relocation chronology differs, with Ozel placing the move after Toledo's fall and the project before it {cite:tdv-ibn-bassal opening} {cite:ibn-bassal-filaha-profile biography} {cite:tdv-ibn-bassal biography}. The record keeps the discrepancy rather than assemble a dated itinerary.

## Think about it
The Filaha Texts Project reads Ibn Bassal's lack of citations as a sign of personal experience, while the museum's record answers that not naming an earlier book does not prove he inherited nothing. Take a position on which reading is stronger, answer the other side, and say what evidence you would need to settle it. Why does your answer need that evidence?
