## Brief
This lesson asks you to read and interpret signs, symbols and style in art works. The museum links records on the Arabic script and its early styles, and on Islamic calligraphy; your class will add the signs and styles of other art works. {cite:ontario-curriculum-elementary-arts}

## What is a letter telling you?
Look at the expectation: it asks you to read signs and style. Choose a line of writing and ask what its letters stand for, then ask what its style adds to the words.

## Go deeper
Who wrote, and for what, shaped how they wrote. The Encyclopaedia of Islam says that government scribes, calligraphers working for luxury patrons, pious copyists of the Qur'an, scholars taking dictation, merchants writing letters and makers of amulets did not all write alike, and that how legible a text had to be shaped its letters. It cites decorative scripts on objects, such as figural letters on inlaid metalwork and rigid angular figures on astrolabes. It also says that leading calligraphers often drew models on paper for inscriptions made in other materials, so it rejects a sharp divide between inscriptional and manuscript scripts {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1159}. See [[concept/islamic-calligraphy|Islamic calligraphy]].

## Why they did it
The records print no sentence in which [[person/yaqut-al-mustasimi|Yaqut al-Musta'simi]] says why he fixed the six principal hands. They show where he worked and who patronised him. Sheila Blair says he became a secretary in the Abbasid chancery, and that after Hulegu took Baghdad in 1258 his career flourished under Mongol patronage. He taught calligraphy to the sons of Ata-Malik al-Juvayni, who governed the city for the Mongols {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 282}. The TDV encyclopaedia says that in the seventh century AH he fixed the six principal hands, and that his great change was to cut the reed pen at a slant {cite:tdv-hat p.427-437}.

## Often misunderstood
The older view holds that Kufic and naskhi were two originally separate scripts, one angular and one flexible. The Encyclopaedia of Islam rejects this and stresses the basic unity of early Arabic writing. It still places in the Umayyad period the start of the two branches that later diverged {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1165}.

## Think about it
Kufic and naskh look very different on the page, yet the encyclopaedia stresses the unity of early Arabic writing. What can you tell about a script's family just by looking at its letters, what can you not, and why?
