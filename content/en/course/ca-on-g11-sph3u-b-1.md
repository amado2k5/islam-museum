## Brief
This lesson asks you to describe the characteristics of a projectile's motion in the vertical and horizontal planes and to give examples. The museum offers one historical case of a thrown object: a stone that scholars argued about. The descriptions and measurements of projectile motion are for your class to bring. {cite:ontario-curriculum-secondary-science}

## What does a thrown object do in each direction?
Look at the expectation: it separates the vertical plane from the horizontal one. In this case, ask what moves up and down, what moves across, and what a source tells you or fails to tell you about either.

## Go deeper
Read how one commentator tests a thrown stone. In his commentary on al-Tusi's Tadhkira, [[person/al-sharif-al-jurjani-ali-ibn-muhammad|al-Sharif al-Jurjani]] (740–816 AH) repeatedly used the ship analogy {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 27}. He reported the view of some ancients that the daily motion of the stars is due to the earth's rotation from west to east. He then examined the two usual refutations: that a stone thrown upward would fall west of its starting point, and that birds and arrows would be affected {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 28} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 29} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 30}. He judged them unsatisfactory, since the air might move congruously with the earth and carry with it what it contains {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 28} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 29} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 30}. The record is [[concept/earth-rotation-ship-analogy|the ship analogy in Islamic astronomy]]; it reports what is moving and what carries what, and gives no measurement.

## Why they did it
The record does not say why al-Jurjani took up the question. It places his repeated use of the ship in a commentary on al-Tusi's Tadhkira, with arguments largely borrowed from Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 27}. It does give the reason he held for the earth's rest: not the refutations he had just set aside, but that the earth is by nature the principle of straight-line attraction {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 30} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 31}. That is his reason as Anton Heinen reports it.

## Two views
Does the earth turn? The record gives two positions, each with its holder. Al-Jurjani reported the view of some ancients that the daily motion of the stars is due to the earth's rotation from west to east {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 28}. He himself reaffirmed the earth's rest {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 30} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 31}. The museum states no verdict, and the record reports no measurement on either side.

## Often misunderstood
It would be easy to read al-Jurjani as an early defender of a turning earth, because he found the refutations of it unsatisfactory. The record says he then reaffirmed the earth's rest {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 30} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 31}. The comparison with Galileo is Anton Heinen's: he compares al-Jurjani's falling-stone argument with the examples of "The Second Day" in Galileo's Dialogue {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 30}.

## How do we know?
The stone passage reaches us in layers: al-Jurjani's commentary, and Anton Heinen's study of the debate, written to explain a variant in one copy of al-Suyuti's *al-Hay'a al-saniyya* {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 24} {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 25}. Heinen says al-Jurjani's arguments were largely borrowed from al-Shirazi {cite:heinen-islamic-cosmology p. 27}. Ask whose argument you are reading: al-Jurjani's, al-Shirazi's, or Heinen's account of them.

## Think about it
Al-Jurjani says the usual refutation fails because the air might move with the earth and carry along what it contains. Using your class's description of horizontal and vertical motion, would a stone thrown straight up land west of its starting point if the air and the thrower both moved with the earth, and what does your answer say about the refutation?
