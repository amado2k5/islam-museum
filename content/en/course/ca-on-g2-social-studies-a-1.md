## Brief
This lesson asks you to ask good questions about the traditions and celebrations of your own family and community, and to describe some important ones. The curriculum also names First Nations, Métis and Inuit celebrations; the museum links only a celebration kept by Muslim families, so your class adds the others. {cite:ontario-curriculum-sshg-2023}

## What do you want to find out about a celebration?
The expectations ask for questions about how a celebration was kept in the past and how it is kept now. Think of who, what, where, when and why, and decide who could answer each question.

## How would you describe a celebration to a friend?
Say what people do, when it happens and who takes part. Then say what your family does differently from a classmate's family.

## Go deeper
The Encyclopaedia of Islam describes how people spend the day. On both festivals, it says, people put on new clothes or at least their best, visit one another, congratulate one another and exchange presents {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039}. Eid al-Fitr, the festival of breaking the fast, is celebrated on the first of Shawwal and the days that follow {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1040}. The article notes that these customs are more usual at Eid al-Fitr than at Eid al-Adha, and that because the hardships of the fast of [[practice/sawm-ramadan|Ramadan]] are over, it is celebrated with much more joy {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1039} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1040}.

## Think about it
The article says people visit one another and exchange presents at the festival. If you asked two families how they keep a day like this, why might their answers be different?
