## Brief
This lesson asks you to build and take apart structures, and to name the flat shapes and solid objects you find in them. The museum offers one record on a ceiling made of cells; sorting and naming cubes, prisms, pyramids, cylinders and cones is the class's own work. {cite:ontario-curriculum-elementary-mathematics}

## What shapes can you find in a structure?
Build a small structure from blocks, then take it apart. List the flat shapes and the solid objects you used. Did any piece count twice?

## Go deeper
Look up at a muqarnas ceiling: it seems built of small hollows in steps. In the definition discussed by the study, a cell consists of two facets and a roof, and cells with bases in one horizontal plane form a tier {cite:dold-samplonius-muqarnas-1992 p202}. In his [[invention/kashi-muqarnas-mensuration|measurement of muqarnas]], al-Kashi selects a module, counts elements by shape, and adds the surfaces of facets, roofs and connecting pieces {cite:dold-samplonius-muqarnas-1992 pp193-194-and202-205}. The record says some visible pieces belong to cells and others fill gaps between them, and that counting hollows in a photograph is not enough to determine their exposed surface area {cite:dold-samplonius-muqarnas-1992 p202}.

## Think about it
Some visible pieces of the ceiling are cells and others fill the gaps between them. Why does that make counting hollows in a photograph a poor way to find the surface of the ceiling?

## Into the museum
- [[invention/kashi-muqarnas-mensuration|Al-Kashi's measurement of muqarnas]]: how the surfaces of the small parts were added.
- [[concept/muqarnas|Muqarnas]]: the tiered cellular construction that articulates and decorates ceilings.
- [[person/al-kashi|Al-Kashi]]: the author of the Key to Arithmetic, where this measurement is set out.

## Try this
Read the record on al-Kashi and the muqarnas. Look at a picture of a muqarnas ceiling and list the different shapes you can name.
