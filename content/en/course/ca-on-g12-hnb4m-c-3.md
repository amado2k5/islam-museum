## Brief
This lesson asks you to analyse the impact of globalization on the fashion industry; the Ministry's examples include local craftspeople displaced by mass-produced fabric. The museum holds one historical case: the Ottoman weavers facing Indian and then European cotton. It is not modern globalization, so your class adds the agreements, offshore factories and present-day markets. {cite:ontario-curriculum-social-sciences-humanities}

## Who was affected when the market changed?
Follow what came of cloth arriving from far away: who kept working, who stopped, and what disappeared with the guild. Compare it with a present-day case your class chooses.

## Go deeper
Follow a weavers' town before the cheap cloth arrived and after it. The weavers of a town agreed the quantity and size of cloth among themselves, and the qadi certified it through the ahi baba. {cite:tdv-cullah p.83-84} A selected inspector checked output: cloth that exceeded the size or was faulty was torn and the weaver fined, and the torn cloth was shown in public and often given to the poor. {cite:tdv-cullah p.83-84} The [[job/weaver|weavers]] often worked to orders from the cloth merchants and became dependent on them, and could not sell their own cloth in the market, which the entry says caused frequent disputes. {cite:tdv-cullah p.83-84} From the seventeenth century cheap Indian cotton yarn and cloth flooded the Ottoman markets, and the weavers used the yarn and imitated Indian cloth. {cite:tdv-cullah p.83-84}

## Often misunderstood
It is easy to picture the craft ending on the day the cheaper cloth arrived. The record gives a longer course. In the second half of the eighteenth century European cottons imitating muslin entered. {cite:tdv-cullah p.83-84} The entry calls the European mechanisation of the 1830s a turning point, and says cheap imports in local markets hastened the collapse of the guilds by the 1850s. {cite:tdv-cullah p.83-84} The First World War disrupted weaving, and it revived briefly in the early Republic before machines ended it. {cite:tdv-cullah p.83-84}

## How do we know?
Everything here comes from one entry, the TDV entry on the cullah by Feridun Emecen. {cite:tdv-cullah p.83-84} The judgements are the entry's own: it calls the 1830s a turning point and says that cheap imports hastened the collapse of the guilds. {cite:tdv-cullah p.83-84} The record on the weaver notes that the entry gives no wages and names no individual weaver, {cite:tdv-cullah p.83-84} so it cannot say what a weaver earned before or after the change, or how any one family fared. It also says that the entry's bibliography was not read. {cite:tdv-cullah p.83-84}

## Think about it
The entry says that a selected inspector checked output and that faulty cloth was torn and the weaver fined, {cite:tdv-cullah p.83-84} and also that the weavers depended on the merchants and could not sell their own cloth in the market. {cite:tdv-cullah p.83-84} Give a reason from the entry: which counts for more in judging what the guild gave a weaver, the checking of cloth or the dependence on merchants, and what would the other side answer?
