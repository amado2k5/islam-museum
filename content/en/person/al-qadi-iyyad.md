## Summary
Abu al-Fadl Iyad ibn Musa ibn Iyad ibn Amrun al-Yahsubi, the Maliki judge, born and resident in Ceuta and of Andalusian descent, was the leading hadith scholar of his time and the author of [[work/al-shifa-bi-tarif-huquq-al-mustafa|al-Shifa bi-Ta'rif Huquq al-Mustafa]] and *Mashariq al-Anwar*. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.3} {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.4} He was long judge of [[place/ceuta|Ceuta]], then of [[place/granada|Granada]], and died in [[place/marrakesh|Marrakesh]] in 544 AH. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.4} {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.5} {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.619}

## Lineage and family
Ibn Farhun relates from Iyad's son Muhammad that their forebears lived long ago in al-Andalus, then moved to Fez, and had also settled at Qayrawan — whether before or after al-Andalus is not known — and that their ancestor Amrun moved from Fez to Ceuta. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.3} The nisba al-Yahsubi refers to Yahsub ibn Malik, a clan of Himyar. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.5}

## Learning and character
His biographer describes him as the leading authority of his time in hadith and its sciences, learned in exegesis, a jurist and legal theorist, versed in grammar, the Arabic language and the battle-days and genealogies of the Arabs, skilled in judgements and in drafting contracts, a master of Malik's school, a fine poet and eloquent preacher, patient, forbearing, good company, generous and given to charity, and firm in the truth. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.3} Ibn al-Imad reports that he was exceptionally sharp and strongly attached to the Sunna, and that he ordered al-Ghazali's books burned over something he suspected in them, which prompted verses defending Iyad and alluding to his opponents. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.619}

## Travels and teachers
In 509 AH he travelled to al-Andalus in search of knowledge and studied in [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]] with the judge Abu Abd Allah Muhammad ibn Ali ibn Hamdin, Abu al-Husayn ibn Siraj and Abu Muhammad ibn Attab; Abu Ali al-Ghassani gave him a licence (*ijaza*). {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.3} He studied with the judge [[person/al-sadafi|Abu Ali Husayn ibn Muhammad al-Sadafi]], wrote to [[person/al-maziri|Abu Abd Allah al-Maziri]] asking for a licence, received one from [[person/al-tartushi|Abu Bakr al-Turtushi]], and counted among his teachers the judge [[person/ibn-rushd-al-jadd|Abu al-Walid ibn Rushd]] and the judge [[person/abu-bakr-ibn-al-arabi-qadi|Abu Bakr ibn al-Arabi]]. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.3} His son Muhammad says that between those he heard and those who licensed him he had a hundred teachers. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.3}

## Judgeship and public life
On his return from al-Andalus, the people of Ceuta had him sit for disputation on the *Mudawwana* when he was about thirty, then appointed him to the council (*shura*), and then he served a long and well-regarded term as judge of his city; in 531 AH he was moved to the judgeship of Granada, which did not last long, and then he was judge of Ceuta a second time. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.4} The author of *al-Sila* says Iyad came to Cordoba and they studied with him, and al-Khatib reports that he built the western extension of Ceuta's great mosque and the well-known *rabita* by the harbour. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.4}

## The Almohads and his death
When the Almohads rose he hastened to submit to them and travelled to meet their emir at Salé, who rewarded him generously; but when their affairs were disturbed in 543 AH his position collapsed, and he went to Marrakesh "driven from his homeland", where he died. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.4} He died in Marrakesh in Jumada II — or, some say, Ramadan — of 544 AH and was buried at Bab Aylan inside the city; the biography also records, without support, a claim that he died of poison. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.5} *Shadharat al-Dhahab* gives his birth as 476 AH. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.619}

## Works
His biographer lists among his works *Ikmal al-Mu'lim*, a commentary on Sahih Muslim; *al-Shifa*, in which he "showed full originality" and whose copies spread east and west; *Mashariq al-Anwar*, on the rare words of the hadith in the Muwatta', al-Bukhari and Muslim and the exact reading of words and narrators' names; *al-Tanbihat al-Mustanbata* on the *Mudawwana*; *Tartib al-Madarik*, on the scholars of Malik's school; *al-I'lam bi-Hudud Qawa'id al-Islam*; *al-Ilma'*, on the rules of transmission; *Bughyat al-Ra'id*, on the lessons of the hadith of Umm Zar'; *al-Ghunya*, on his teachers; and books of answers, legal cases and judges' conduct, as well as his sermons, for he preached only what he had composed himself. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.4} {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.5} The data also attribute to him [[work/al-ilam-fi-hudud-al-ahkam|al-I'lam fi Hudud al-Ahkam]], following *Kashf al-Zunun*. {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan} He wrote graceful verse, including two lines likening green shoots of grain bending before the wind, with anemones among them, to a routed green squadron bearing wounds. {cite:al-shifa-ilmiyya-shumunni p.5}

## For young visitors
Qadi Iyad was a judge and great scholar from Ceuta, on the Strait of Gibraltar. He travelled to al-Andalus to learn hadith, came home to become his city's judge, and wrote many books — the most famous, *al-Shifa*, is about loving the Prophet and knowing his worth.
