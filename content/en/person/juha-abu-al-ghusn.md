## Overview
Juha Abu al-Ghusn is the man of the anecdotes, proverbial for foolishness and heedlessness, and the best-known name in Arab and Islamic humour. What the museum knows of him comes from the introduction to the edition of Nawadir Juha al-Kubra prepared by Darwish al-Juwaydi, which takes his biography from al-Zirikli's al-A'lam and its older sources {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 4-6}. The anecdotes told in his name are a cumulative literature that cannot be fixed on one person, and the museum presents them that way: a report about a man, and stories that grew around his name.

## Name and origin
The editor opens by arguing that the name is Arabic, citing the entry in Lisan al-Arab: the verb means to settle in a place, and Juha is a man's name that takes no nunation {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 4}. The Arab Juha, he writes, is Abu al-Ghusn, Arab by descent, of the tribe of Fazara, raised in Kufa, whose mother served the mother of Anas ibn Malik {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 4}. Al-Jawhari names him Juha in the Sihah; the author of al-Qamus al-Muhit corrects him, saying Juha is a nickname and the name is Dujayn ibn Thabit. Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani, in Lisan al-Mizan, has an entry for a Basran hadith transmitter called Dujayn ibn Thabit al-Yarbu'i, known as Abu al-Ghusn, and rejects the claim that he is Juha {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 5}. The name itself is an old dispute between lexicographers and hadith scholars.

## His time
The editor records that Juha witnessed Abu Muslim al-Khurasani's revolt against the Umayyads, and that his patron Yaqtin brought him before Abu Muslim, where he asked: Yaqtin, which of you two is Abu Muslim? {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 4-5}. In the margin of two manuscripts of al-Zamakhshari's al-Mustaqsa there is a verse attributed to Umar ibn Abi Rabi'a that mentions the madness of Juha; if the attribution holds, Juha was famous more than forty years before Abu Muslim. A verse in the diwan of Abu al-Atahiya uses him as a byword for fame. A late manuscript in the Bibliothèque Orientale in Beirut says that Abu al-Ghusn Juha of Baghdad was a man of banter, jest and anecdotes who died in the caliphate of al-Mahdi {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 5}.

## Juha in the books
The editor reports from Charles Pellat that al-Jahiz was the first Arab author to mention Juha, in his epistle on Ali and the two arbiters and in the Book of Mules. Ibn al-Nadim's Fihrist lists a book called Nawadir Juha among the books on the heedless. Al-Zamakhshari said the stories about him are too many to count {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 5}. A marginal note quoted by the editor says he was a learned man given to jest, and that people composed many anecdotes in his name as they did for others {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 5-6}.

## The Arab Juha and the Juha of Rum
The editor separates the Arab Juha from Nasreddin Hoca, to whom the Turks gave Juha's stories and added many times as many. The book printed in Egypt and Beirut as Nawadir Juha is translated from Turkish and attributed to Nasreddin, and stories of the Arab Juha entered it among the Arabic books translated into Turkish {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 5}. From the mixing of the two came the claim of some writers that Juha is a fictional figure, and other peoples know him under similar names {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 6}.

## Why the anecdotes lived
In the editor's view the Juha literature is cumulative, the work of no single person or age: the figure grows with time, and when nations pass through hardship and unjust rule, someone appears who speaks for them through cunning and the joke, so that the smile and the tear go together {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 4}. This is the editor's opinion, and the museum reports it as his.
