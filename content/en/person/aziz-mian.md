# Aziz Mian

## Summary

Aziz Mian Qawwal (1942 – 6 December 2000) was a Pakistani qawwal famous for singing ghazals in his own style of qawwali, counted among the greatest qawwals of South Asia and known as 'Shahenshah-e-Qawwali', the 'military qawwal' and 'the Nietzschean qawwal'. {cite:wikipedia-aziz-mian-en} {cite:wikidata-aziz-mian}

## Birth and study

He was born at Meerut in Uttar Pradesh; Wikidata and Dawn give 17 April 1942, the article's text 27 July 1942, and the museum gives both. After 1947 he moved to Lahore and took two master's degrees from the University of the Punjab, in Urdu literature and in Islamic studies and philosophy. {cite:wikipedia-aziz-mian-en} {cite:wikidata-aziz-mian}

## Career

He began singing naats as a boy at the shrine of Data Ganj Bakhsh in Lahore and rose to the front rank of qawwals with a raspy, powerful voice; he was the only prominent qawwal to write his own lyrics, though he also sang others' poetry, among them [[person/muhammad-iqbal|Muhammad Iqbal]], Qamar Jalalvi, Jigar Moradabadi and Qateel Shifai. He was a contemporary and often a rival of the [[group/sabri-brothers|Sabri Brothers]]. {cite:wikipedia-aziz-mian-en}

He was called the military qawwal because most of his early concerts were in army barracks; he recorded for EMI Pakistan and became widely known on Pakistan Television in the 1970s. He holds the record for the longest commercially released qawwali, 'Hashr Ke Roz Yeh Poochhunga', at just over 115 minutes. Nadeem F. Paracha wrote in Dawn (2015) that he deliberately worked his audiences into a state in which many lost all control, which he explained as a threshold to a direct spiritual connection. {cite:wikipedia-aziz-mian-en}

## Honours

The Government of Pakistan gave him the [[award/pride-of-performance|Pride of Performance]] in 1989, and a bridge near his shrine in Multan bears his name. {cite:wikipedia-aziz-mian-en}

## Death

He died at Qom in Iran on 6 December 2000 of liver failure after the last concert of a tour there; his body was brought home, prayed over in Rawalpindi and again in Multan, and buried in Multan beside the shrine of his spiritual master. His sons, among them Tabraiz and Imran Aziz Mian, carry on his style. {cite:wikipedia-aziz-mian-en} {cite:wikidata-aziz-mian}
