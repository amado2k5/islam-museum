## Overview
Ahmad ibn Shu'ayb ibn Ali ibn Sinan ibn Bahr al-Nasa'i, the muhaddith of Nasa in Khurasan and author of the Sunan counted among the six books {cite:sunan-al-nasai} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1023}. See [[work/sunan-al-nasai|Sunan al-Nasa'i]].

## His life
Born at Nasa in 215/830, with 214 also reported {cite:tazkirat-al-huffaz j.2 p. 617} {cite:wafayat-ayan i p. 26-27}. He journeyed to Qutayba ibn Sa'id at fifteen and stayed a year and two months, then heard from a host of shaykhs after 240/854 in Khurasan, Iraq, al-Sham, Egypt, the Hijaz and the Jazira, travelling to Marw and Nishapur {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 106}.

He settled in Egypt at Zuqaq al-Qanadil, where people transmitted from him and his works spread; he campaigned with the amir of Egypt, and al-Daraqutni calls him the most learned of Egypt's shaykhs of his age in law, hadith and the narrators {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 106, p. 108-109} {cite:wafayat-ayan i p. 26}.

## His last year
He left Egypt in Dhu al-Qa'da 302 for Damascus, having composed the Khasa'is on the virtues of Ali ibn Abi Talib; asked there for the virtues of Mu'awiya he refused, was kicked in the groin until carried from the mosque, and borne to al-Ramla {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 109} {cite:tazkirat-al-huffaz j.2 p. 618}. He died on Monday 13 Safar 303/915, or — on another report — at Mecca, buried between al-Safa and al-Marwa in Sha'ban of that year {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 109} {cite:tazkirat-al-huffaz j.2 p. 620}.

## Name and dates in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam gives his name as Abu Abd al-Rahman Ahmad ibn Ali ibn Shu'ayb ibn Bahr ibn Sinan, which places Ali before Shu'ayb in the chain of forebears, unlike the form given in the overview above; it dates him 215/830 to 303/915 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1023}. The article remarks that very little is known about him. It says he is reported to have travelled widely to hear traditions, to have settled in Egypt and afterwards in Damascus {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1023}.

## The article's account of his death
The article states that he died as a result of ill-treatment he suffered at Damascus or, according to others, at Ramla, and it gives as the reason his feelings in favour of [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] and against the [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyads]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1023}. It adds that, because of this unnatural death, he is called a martyr, and that his tomb is at [[place/mecca|Mecca]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1023}. The sources cited in the earlier sections of this page frame the occasion differently, as a refusal to speak of the virtues of Mu'awiya, and place his burial differently as well; the museum records the accounts side by side and gives no verdict between them.

## His Sunan
According to the article, his collection of traditions is divided into 51 chapters, each subdivided into sections called babs {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1023}. Considerable space goes to traditions on the ceremonial duties of worship {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1023}. The chapters on ihbas, nuhl, rukba and umra, which deal with forms of bequest and donation, do not occur in any of the other canonical collections, although part of their material appears there under other headings {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1023}. By contrast, the article notes that his book has no chapters on eschatology, such as fitan and qiyama, on the recounting of virtues, such as manaqib, or on the Qur'an {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1023} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1024}. See also [[concept/kutub-al-sitta|the Six Books]].

## Other works
The article, following Sezgin and Brockelmann, mentions nine further works by him, some extant and some known only from citations {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1024}. They include a work on the virtues of Ali, the Khasa'is, printed at Cairo in 1308/1890-1, now recorded as [[work/khasais-amir-al-muminin|Khasais of Amir al-Mu'minin]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1024}. They also include a work on weak and unreliable narrators, the Du'afa' wa-l-matrukin, printed in 1323/1905-6, recorded as [[work/kitab-al-duafa-wa-al-matrukin|Kitab al-Du'afa' wa-l-Matrukin]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1024}. Two further titles named are Tasmiyat fukaha' al-amsar and a Tafsir {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1024}.
