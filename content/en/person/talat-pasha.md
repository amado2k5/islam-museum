# Mehmed Talat Pasha

## Summary
Mehmed Talat Pasha (1 September 1874 – 15 March 1921) was an Ottoman politician, minister of the interior and, from February 1917 to October 1918, grand vizier of the Ottoman Empire. With Enver and Cemal he was one of the "Three Pashas" of the Committee of Union and Progress who ruled the empire during the First World War. The English article says he is widely considered one of the main architects of the Armenian Genocide by historians. {cite:wikipedia-talat-pasha-en}

## From the post office to the committee
He was born in Kırcaali (Kardzhali), in the Adrianople Vilayet, into a middle-class family of Romani, Turkish and Pomak descent; his father Ahmed Vasıf was a kadı from a nearby village and his mother Hürmüz came from a Turkish family that had migrated from Dedeler, near Kayseri. The family fled to Constantinople when Russian troops occupied their home in the war of 1877–1878, and his father died when Talat was eleven. Expelled from military secondary school without a certificate, he worked as a postal clerk at Adrianople and taught Turkish after hours at the Alliance Israélite school. Arrested in 1893 over a tampered telegram, he joined the Committee of Union and Progress, was imprisoned again in 1896, and was exiled to Salonika, where as a postman he smuggled dissident newspapers and built the Salonika organisation: the Ottoman Freedom Committee he co-founded there in 1906 merged with Ahmed Rıza's Paris CUP in 1907, making Salonika the party's internal centre and Talat briefly its secretary-general. {cite:wikipedia-talat-pasha-en}

## The Unionist regime and 1915
After the 1908 revolution he sat in parliament as deputy for Adrianople and rose through the party to the ministry of the interior. In the First World War the levers of demographic policy were in his hands. The article recounts that on 24 April 1915 he ordered the closure of all Armenian political organisations in the empire and the arrest of Armenians connected to them — the night arrests in Constantinople of 235 to 270 Armenian community leaders, including parliamentarians, clergymen, physicians, authors and teachers, the majority of whom were eventually murdered — and that these arrests are considered by many commentators to be the start of the Armenian genocide. He then issued the order for the Tehcir Law, in force from 1 June 1915 to 8 February 1916, which allowed the mass deportation of Armenians, a principal means, the article says, of carrying out what is now recognised as a genocide against Armenians: the deportees received no humanitarian assistance, and were subject to periodic rape and massacre on the marches to the Syrian desert. Talat conducted the policy partly by a telegraph machine installed in his own home. Confronted by the American ambassador Henry Morgenthau, he told him on 2 August 1915 that "our Armenian policy is absolutely fixed and that nothing can change it", and refused him the list of Armenian holders of American insurance policies. On 4 February 1917 he became grand vizier while keeping the interior ministry — the first member of parliament to become prime minister in Ottoman history — and presided over social reforms, the Brest-Litovsk treaty, and the empire's final defeats, resigning on 8 October 1918. {cite:wikipedia-talat-pasha-en}

## Exile, assassination and reburial
On the night of 1–2 November 1918 he fled Istanbul on a German torpedo boat with the other Unionist leaders, and settled in Berlin under the name "Ali Sâî Bey", founding the Oriental Club and lobbying against the partition of the empire; the Istanbul court-martial sentenced him to death in absentia. On 15 March 1921, as he left his Hardenbergstraße flat, he was shot dead by Soghomon Tehlirian, a Dashnak agent whose family had been killed in the genocide, acting under Operation Nemesis; a German court acquitted Tehlirian after a two-day trial, on grounds of temporary insanity. First buried in Berlin, Talat's remains were brought to Turkey in 1943 and reburied at the Monument of Liberty cemetery in Istanbul with a state funeral on 25 February 1943. {cite:wikipedia-talat-pasha-en} {cite:wikipedia-talat-pasha-tr}

## The question of his faith
Talat's case differs from those of Enver and Cemal. The English article's infobox has no religion field, but its text states that he was a member of the Bektashi order — the Sufi order long intertwined with the Janissaries and with Anatolian religious life — and cites two scholarly works for the statement: Hans-Lukas Kieser's 2018 biography of Talat and M. Şükrü Hanioğlu's entry in the TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi. The Turkish article reports the same membership in its text, hedged as "according to some sources" and citing the same encyclopaedia entry, and its infobox gives his religion as Islam with the Bektashi affiliation cited to Feroz Ahmad's history of the CUP. Membership attested in article text on independent scholarship is more than an infobox value: the museum records him as sufi, documented, on the Bektashi membership. No source names a Sunni legal school for him, and Wikidata gives only Islam, imported from the Turkish infobox — a generic value that by itself would have settled nothing. {cite:wikipedia-talat-pasha-en} {cite:wikipedia-talat-pasha-tr} {cite:wikidata-talat-pasha}
