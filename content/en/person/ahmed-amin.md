## Summary
Egyptian thinker, historian and man of letters (1886–1954), a pioneer of modern authorship on Islamic civilisation. Author of the series Fajr al-Islam, Duha al-Islam, Zuhr al-Islam and Yawm al-Islam, and the memoir Hayati (My Life); he edited the journal Al-Thaqafa and worked in the sharia judiciary and the university. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-amin}

## Life and career

Ahmed Amin was born in Cairo in 1886, studied at al-Azhar, then joined the Sharia Judiciary School and graduated as a qadi, serving in the courts for years before turning to teaching and cultural administration. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-amin}

He taught in the Faculty of Arts at Cairo University, edited the journal Al-Thaqafa from its launch in 1939, served as dean of the faculty, and led cultural and translation committees in Egypt. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-amin}

## Historical project

He launched his great project in 1928 with Fajr al-Islam on the Jahiliyya and early Islam; readers received it enthusiastically, so he continued with Duha al-Islam in three volumes on the Umayyad and Abbasid eras, Zuhr al-Islam in four on the medieval centuries, and finally Yawm al-Islam on the modern age. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-amin}

He aimed to present the history of Islamic civilisation in readable literary language joining research to fluency, drawing on Arabic sources and orientalist studies, and acknowledged in his memoirs that his work opened a door rather than said a last word. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-amin}

## Legacy

Among his other books are To My Son, Fayd al-Khatir, a dictionary of Egyptian customs and expressions, and Hayati — memoirs describing Egypt's cultural passage between al-Azhar and the modern university. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-amin}

He died in Cairo in 1954; his series remains the best-known general Arabic gateway to the history of Islamic civilisation, though specialists have debated some of its judgements and sources. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-amin}
