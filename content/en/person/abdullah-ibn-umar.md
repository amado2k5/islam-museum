## Brief
[[person/abdullah-ibn-umar|Abdullah ibn Umar]] ibn al-Khattab al-Qurashi al-Adawi; his mother was Zaynab bint Maz'un al-Jumahi {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.917}. He was born in the third year of the mission, as al-Zubayr ibn Bakkar affirmed. Al-Zubayr said he emigrated at the age of ten; Ibn Manda said he was eleven and a half; and Ibn Hajar notes that it is established from him that he was thirteen on the day of Badr {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.917}.

## Conversion and campaigns
He embraced Islam with his father and emigrated. He was presented to the Prophet at Badr and was judged too young; the same happened at Uhud. At the [[event/battle-of-the-trench|Trench]] he was permitted, at fifteen years old, as the Sahih establishes {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.917}. Ibn Hajar also cites al-Baghawi's report, from Anas and Sa'id ibn al-Musayyib, that Ibn Umar witnessed Badr. The opposing report, from al-Bara', runs: "I and Ibn Umar were presented at Badr, and he sent us back" {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.917}.

## Standing in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, describes Ibn Umar as one of the most prominent figures of the first generation of Muslims and one of the authorities most often quoted for Traditions. It says his reputation rested not only on being the caliph's son but also on moral qualities that won the admiration of his contemporaries, and that while Muslims were being drawn by their passions into civil war he kept himself apart from the conflict {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 72}. The article adds that he followed the precepts of Islam so scrupulously that later generations took him as a pattern, to the point that reports were gathered on how he dressed and how he cut and dyed his beard. It says the biographies are full of anecdotes about his wit, piety, gentleness, modesty and self-restraint, grants that some of them may have been invented, and judges his nobility of soul beyond dispute. As a transmitter, the article says, he has been regarded as the most scrupulous in adding nothing to the hadiths he narrated and omitting nothing from them {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 72} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 73}.

## Campaigns and public role according to the Encyclopaedia
The article says he was born before the hijra, on an unspecified date. It reports that the Prophet sent him back for his age when he offered himself to fight at [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]] and at [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]], and accepted him at the siege of Medina, when he was about fifteen; it notes that this later served as a precedent in similar cases {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 73}. It then lists his later campaigns: the expedition to [[event/battle-of-mutah|Mu'ta]] in 7 AH, the [[event/conquest-of-mecca|conquest of Mecca]] in 8, the wars against the claimants to prophethood [[person/musaylima|Musaylima]] and [[person/tulayha-ibn-khuwaylid|Tulayha]] in 12, the [[event/conquest-of-egypt|Egyptian campaign]] of 18 to 21, the battle of [[event/battle-of-nihawand|Nihawand]] in 21, the expedition of 30 AH to [[place/jurjan|Jurjan]] and [[place/tabaristan|Tabaristan]], and the expedition against [[place/constantinople|Constantinople]] in 49 AH, which the article describes as Yazid's {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 73}.

In politics, the article says he first appears as an adviser to the council that was to choose the next caliph from among its own members, with no vote and no eligibility himself. At the elections of the caliphs who came to power in his lifetime, it says, he conformed to the will of the majority. It reports that he withheld allegiance to [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] because he was waiting for the community to agree on the choice, and that when no agreement came and civil war broke out he stayed neutral. It adds that he refused to recognise Yazid as heir presumptive, which the article reads as disapproval of the innovation [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Muawiya]] introduced into the succession, yet paid him homage after his father's death. The article says Ibn Umar held no important administrative office apart from a few missions, and relates that he declined the post of judge, fearing he might not interpret the divine law correctly {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 73}.

## The offers of the caliphate
According to the article, the caliphate was offered to him three times: right after the death of [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]] in 35 AH, during the negotiations of the two arbiters at [[event/arbitration-of-siffin|Siffin]] in 37 to 38 AH, and after the death of Yazid I in 64 AH. The article says he refused each time because he wanted his election to be unanimous and wished to avoid bloodshed. It notes that Lammens has suggested this refusal may reflect narrow-mindedness, leaves that question open, and itself judges that Ibn Umar lacked energy {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 73}. The al-Hasan al-Basri report about the refusal after Uthman's death, given below from Ahmad's book, is a separate account.

## His wound and death according to the Encyclopaedia
The article says Ibn Umar died of septicaemia in 73 AH, corresponding to 693 CE, well over eighty years old. It attributes this to a wound in the foot made by a soldier of [[person/al-hajjaj-ibn-yusuf|al-Hajjaj]] with the lower end of a lance, in the crowd of pilgrims returning from [[place/arafat|Arafat]]. It relates that al-Hajjaj visited him during the illness and asked whether he knew the man who had wounded him, so that he could be punished; Ibn Umar reproached al-Hajjaj for letting his men carry arms in the holy places and so causing the injury. The article reasons that this reproach probably gave rise to the story, found in certain later sources, that al-Hajjaj commissioned an assassin to wound Ibn Umar with a poisoned lance tip {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 73}. The sources cited earlier on this page give differing years of death.

## Transmission and its extent
He is among the most prolific narrators from the Prophet {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.917}. He also narrated from Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, Abu Dharr, Mu'adh and Aisha. Among the Companions who narrated from him were Jabir and Ibn Abbas. His sons included Salim, Abdullah, Hamza, Bilal and Zayd. Among the senior Successors were Sa'id ibn al-Musayyib, Alqama ibn Waqqas, Abu Abd al-Rahman al-Nahdi, Masruq and Jubayr ibn Nufayr; and among their freedmen, Nafi' and Abdullah ibn Dinar {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.918}.

## What is said of his piety and merit
In the Sahih, Salim reports that Ibn Umar had a dream which Hafsa related to the Prophet. The Prophet said: "What a fine man Abdullah is — if only he prayed at night." After that he slept only a little at night {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.918}. In Ahmad's Kitab al-Zuhd, Ibn Mas'ud is reported as saying: "The young man of Quraysh most self-controlled in worldly matters is Abdullah ibn Umar" {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.918}. Jabir said, as reported by Ibn al-A'rabi with a sound chain: "None of us who lived to see this world was not tilted by it and did not tilt it — except Abdullah ibn Umar" {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.918}. On the authority of Sa'id ibn al-Musayyib, with a good chain in al-Baghawi: "Had I to bear witness for any one of the people of Paradise, I would bear witness for Ibn Umar" {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.918}.

## Following the Prophet's traces
Al-Zubayr ibn Bakkar said: Ibn Umar would memorise what he heard from the Prophet, and when he was absent he would ask those present about the Prophet's words and deeds. He would follow the Prophet's traces in every mosque where the Prophet had prayed; he would take the road on which he had seen the Prophet turn his she-camel; and when he stood at Arafat, he stood at the spot where the Prophet had stood {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.919}. From Malik: Ibn Umar remained sixty years after the Prophet, with delegations of people coming to him; he was "one of the imams of religion"; and Zayd ibn Thabit was the imam of the people after Umar, and then Ibn Umar was their imam after Zayd {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.919}.

## His refusal to answer what he did not know
Ibn al-Mubarak, in al-Zuhd, reports that Ibn Umar was asked about something and said, "I do not know." Then he said: "Do you want to make our backs bridges over Hell?" {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.919}.

## His age and death
Al-Bukhari, in al-Tarikh, reports from Malik that he reached eighty-seven years; others besides Malik said he lived eighty-four, and Ibn Hajar judges the first figure the more reliable {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.920}. Damra ibn Rabi'a said he died in the year seventy-two or seventy-three, and once firmly affirmed seventy-three; so did Abu Nu'aym, Yahya ibn Bukayr and the majority, and some added that it was in Dhu al-Hijja. Al-Fallas once said it was "the year four" (the source gives only the final digit), and Khalifa, Sa'id ibn Jubayr and Ibn Zabr affirmed that date {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.920}.

## Letting the turban's end hang
Ibn Ishaq reports from Ata' ibn Abi Rabah that a man from Basra asked Ibn Umar about letting the end of a turban hang down behind the wearer. Ibn Umar answered with an account of a gathering of the Prophet in the mosque, where he was the tenth of ten. In it, a young Ansari asked about the best and most discerning believers; the Prophet related [[work/hadith-of-the-five-afflictions|the hadith of the five afflictions]]; and the Prophet turbanned Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf with his own hand and let four fingers' length hang behind him. See [[event/sariyya-of-abd-al-rahman-ibn-awf-to-dumat-al-jandal|the expedition]]. {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/280}

## For Sprouts
A noble companion from Medina who deeply loved the Prophet and carefully followed his peaceful example and devotion.

## For Explorers
Abdullah ibn Umar ibn al-Khattab was a distinguished companion, scholar, and one of the most prolific narrators of Hadith. Migrating to Medina as a youth, he was renowned for meticulously emulating the Prophet's practices in devotion, travel, and kindness, while remaining completely detached from political ambitions and devoted to charity.

## For Scholars
Abu Abd al-Rahman Abdullah ibn Umar ibn al-Khattab (c. 10 BH – 73 AH / 613–693 CE), an eminent companion, jurist, and one of the prominent narrators of Hadith. Accepting Islam in childhood and migrating with his father Umar, he witnessed the Trench, the Pledge of Ridwan, and early campaigns. Renowned in Islamic memory for his meticulous emulation of the Prophet's personal habits and devotional practices, he epitomized strict moral asceticism, steadfastly declining caliphal office and remaining neutral during civil discords while serving as a senior legal authority in Medina.

## From Omar Abdelkafy's series on the Hikam of Ibn Ata Allah
Omar Abdelkafy relates that a man from Iraq asked Abdullah ibn Umar about the blood of a mosquito, and Ibn Umar marvelled that they asked about mosquito blood when they had killed the son of the Prophet's daughter. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep15 01:14:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep15 01:15:00}

## In Ahmad ibn Hanbal's Fada'il al-Sahaba
The chapter on Ibn Umar in [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]]'s [[work/fadail-al-sahaba-ahmad|Fada'il al-Sahaba]] has Jabir ibn Abd Allah's saying: "I met no one whom the world had not swayed, except Abd Allah ibn Umar" — a sound chain according to the editor {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1130-1131}. It narrates [[person/abdullah-ibn-masud|Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud]]'s saying that among the young men of Quraysh the most in control of himself against the world was Abd Allah ibn Umar — sound according to the editor {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1132}. From Mujahid it narrates that Ibn Umar was present at the Conquest aged twenty, with a stubborn horse and a heavy spear; the editor grades this weak because of Ibn Abi Najih's concealment of sources (*tadlis*) {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1131}. From al-Hasan al-Basri it narrates that after what befell Uthman, when people were in confusion, they came to Ibn Umar and said, "You are our chief and the son of our chief; come out and people will pledge allegiance to you", and he refused {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1132}. Ibn Umar is also the narrator of the reports at the start of the book ranking Abu Bakr, then Umar, then Uthman; see [[question/who-is-best-after-the-prophet|Who is the best of this community after its Prophet?]] {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.104-108}.
