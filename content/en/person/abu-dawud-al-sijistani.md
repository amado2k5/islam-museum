## Overview
Abu Dawud Sulayman ibn al-Ash'ath al-Azdi al-Sijistani, the hadith scholar of [[place/sijistan|Sijistan]], is the author of the [[work/sunan-abi-dawud|Sunan of Abu Dawud]], one of the six books {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}. The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls him a traditionist, born in 202/817, who travelled widely in pursuit of his studies and gained a reputation for his knowledge {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}. He died on the sixteenth of Shawwal in the year two hundred and seventy-five {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 362}, February 889 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}.

The Encyclopaedia article on him is short, and this page rests mainly on it together with the biography in al-Dhahabi's history. The article is chiefly about his book.

## His journey in quest of hadith
He prayed over 'Affan at Baghdad in 220, entered [[place/basra|Basra]] just after the death of 'Uthman ibn al-Haytham the mu'adhdhin in Rajab of that year, and heard al-Qa'nabi and Sulayman ibn Harb at Mecca in the pilgrimage days {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 358}. He entered Kufa in 221 and Damascus in 222 {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi pp. 358, 362}, hearing a great many teachers in the Hijaz, Egypt, al-Sham, the frontier and Khurasan, in the Jazira, at Aleppo, Harran and Homs {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 359}, and Qutayba and Ibn Rahwayh in Khurasan {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 359}. He read law with [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]], kept his company for a time and was among the elite of his pupils {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 360}.

## His book, the Sunan
The Sunan is counted among the six canonical books of tradition accepted by Sunnis {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}. Abu Dawud said: I wrote down five hundred thousand traditions from the Messenger of God and selected from them what I placed in the Sunan, four thousand eight hundred traditions, naming the sound ones and those resembling or approaching them {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 360}. The Encyclopaedia gives the same figures on the authority of [[person/ibn-dasa|Ibn Dasa]], and records that the book holds sound traditions, those that seem so, and those that are nearly so {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}. It is said he presented the work to the Imam Ahmad, who approved it {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 359} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}.

The work bears the generic title Sunan and deals mainly with matters ordained, allowed or forbidden by law {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}.

## Method and notes on the traditions
According to the Encyclopaedia, Abu Dawud also declared that he had made clear the traditions in his book that carry great weakness, and that those on which he said nothing are good, some being sounder than others {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}. This refers to the notes he often adds to his traditions to give his opinion of their value {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}.

The article sets this beside the introduction that [[person/muslim-ibn-al-hajjaj|Muslim]] wrote to his Sahih, where general questions of criticism are discussed. It says Abu Dawud was the first to give such detailed notes, paving the way for the more systematic criticism of individual traditions in the collection of his pupil [[person/al-tirmidhi-muhammad-ibn-isa|al-Tirmidhi]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}. It adds that he quotes men who are not found in the two Sahihs, his principle being that transmitters are counted trustworthy unless there is formal proof to discount them {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}.

## Reception and transmission
The article reports high praise. Abu Said [[person/ibn-al-arabi-ahmad|ibn al-A'rabi]] is quoted as saying that anyone who knew only the Qur'an and this book would have sufficient knowledge {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}. The article finds it surprising that, although many in the fourth century praised the Sunan, the Fihrist does not mention it and names Abu Dawud only as the father of his son {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}.

Later scholars voiced some criticisms. [[person/abd-al-azim-al-mundhiri|Al-Mundhiri]], who made a summary called al-Mujtaba, criticised some traditions that lack notes, and [[person/ibn-qayyim-al-jawziyya|Ibn al-Djawziyya]] added further criticisms {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}. The article concludes that despite the faults found, the work keeps an honoured place {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}.

The Sunan was handed down through several lines of transmission, and some versions are said to contain material absent from others. The version of [[person/abu-ali-al-lului|al-Lu'lu'i]] is the one that gained most favour. Several editions have been printed in the East {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}. The article also notes a small collection of mursal traditions by Abu Dawud, titled Kitab al-Marasil, published in Cairo in 1310/1892 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}.

## Settling at Basra
The amir Abu Ahmad al-Muwaffaq asked him to move to Basra to revive it with learning after the ordeal of the Zanj, to transmit the Sunan to his sons and to hold them a separate session; Abu Dawud accepted the first two and refused the third, saying: people are equal in knowledge {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi pp. 361-362}. He made Basra his home until his death {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}. The Encyclopaedia says this settlement is probably why some wrongly derived his nisba al-Sijistani from a village near Basra of that name, not from the province {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}.
