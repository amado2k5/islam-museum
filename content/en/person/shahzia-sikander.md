## Summary
Shahzia Sikander is a Pakistani-American visual artist, born in Lahore, known for reviving the practice of traditional Indian miniature painting. {cite:wikipedia-shahzia-sikander-en} {cite:britannica-shahzia-sikander} Her work across drawing, painting, printmaking, animation, installation, performance, video and sculpture brings historical narratives into conversation with contemporary issues of race, gender, postcolonial identity and the immigrant experience. {cite:britannica-shahzia-sikander} Two sources describe her as a Muslim woman; neither names a school for her, and her data records her faith as unknown, with the evidence stated there. {cite:wikipedia-shahzia-sikander-en} {cite:britannica-shahzia-sikander}

## Training and The Scroll
She was born on 6 March 1969 in Lahore. {cite:britannica-shahzia-sikander} At the National College of Arts in Lahore, where she enrolled in 1987, miniature painting was unpopular among young Pakistani artists and much of the region's manuscript heritage had been removed to Western collections under imperial rule; she nonetheless studied the traditional craft with the master miniaturist Bashir Ahmad. {cite:britannica-shahzia-sikander} {cite:wikipedia-shahzia-sikander-en} She earned her BFA in 1991 and became the first woman to teach in the college's Miniature Painting Department. {cite:wikipedia-shahzia-sikander-en} Her thesis work The Scroll (1989-90), a five-foot watercolour and gouache painting on tea-stained wasli paper, depicts a ghostlike, faceless figure of herself moving through the rooms of her family home; widely considered her breakout work, it launched what came to be called the neo-miniature. {cite:britannica-shahzia-sikander} {cite:wikipedia-shahzia-sikander-en}

## Move to the United States
In 1993 she moved to the United States for the MFA programme at the Rhode Island School of Design, earning the degree in painting and printmaking in 1995, and continued to develop her visual language while grappling with the challenges of being an immigrant and a Muslim woman in an art world that, as Britannica puts it, both fetishised and excluded multiculturalism. {cite:britannica-shahzia-sikander} {cite:wikipedia-shahzia-sikander-en} A two-year fellowship at the Glassell School of Art in Houston followed, and in 1997 she moved to New York, where she had been invited to exhibit in the Whitney Biennial. {cite:britannica-shahzia-sikander}

## Motifs, the veil, and animation
Her early paintings integrate Mughal (Islamic) and Rajput (Hindu) forms, including the gopi, the female devotees of Krishna, used to locate forms within miniature painting that can generate multiple meanings. {cite:wikipedia-shahzia-sikander-en} The veil recurs throughout her work as a symbol of both religion and womanhood; in a performance piece she wore an elaborate lace veil for several weeks and documented her peers' reactions, finding in it an ultimate sense of security and control. {cite:wikipedia-shahzia-sikander-en} From 2001 she turned increasingly to digital animation — drawing, she says, is the fundamental element of her process — beginning with works built by scanning and threading ink drawings into movement, such as SpiNN (2003), in which a throng of gopis infiltrates a seventeenth-century Mughal throne room. {cite:wikipedia-shahzia-sikander-en}

## Sculpture and Witness
Serving on New York City's Mayoral Advisory Commission on City Art, Monuments, and Markers in 2017 drew her to sculpture and to the contested histories of public monuments. {cite:britannica-shahzia-sikander} Her project Havah... to breathe, air, life (2023), commissioned for Madison Square Park and a nearby court building, comprises the sculptures NOW and Witness and a video animation; both figures have faces resembling hers and hair braided in coils resembling horns. {cite:britannica-shahzia-sikander} When Witness was installed at the University of Houston in 2024 it drew protests from the anti-abortion Christian group Texas Right to Life, which called it "satanic", and several months later a vandal beheaded it; Sikander chose not to repair it, writing that the work is now "a witness to the fissures in our country". {cite:britannica-shahzia-sikander} {cite:wikipedia-shahzia-sikander-en}

## Recognition
She received the MacArthur Fellowship in 2006, the U.S. Department of State's Medal of Art in 2012, and the Asia Society Award for Significant Contribution to Contemporary Art in 2015. {cite:britannica-shahzia-sikander} The retrospective Shahzia Sikander: Extraordinary Realities, covering the first fifteen years of her career, opened at the Morgan Library & Museum in New York in 2021 and travelled to RISD and the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston. {cite:britannica-shahzia-sikander}
