## Overview
Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq ibn al-Sabbah al-Kindi, called the "Philosopher of the Arabs". Ibn al-Nadim describes him as the most excellent man of his age and the unique one of his time in knowledge of the ancient sciences, and says his books were on logic, philosophy, geometry, arithmetic, music and the stars. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 357}

Al-Mas'udi counts him among those who engaged in the sciences of astronomy after the appearance of Islam, together with [[person/al-khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]], Abu Ma'shar, al-Farghani, al-Battani and others. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 519}

Al-Mas'udi says that he wrote a treatise in two parts refuting the claims of those who claim to make gold and silver without their ores. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 619-620}

Ibn al-Nadim adds that he "was a miser". {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 357}

## Life and times
The Encyclopaedia of Islam article on al-Kindi, whose account this page follows alongside the sources above, gives his full name as Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq and notes that the bio-bibliographers supply a distinguished genealogy for him. It says he was born before the end of the second Islamic century and died around the middle of the third, perhaps roughly 185 to 252 AH, which is 801 to 866 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 140}

The article describes him as an eminent universal scholar and philosopher who lived through a period of intellectual ferment in the sciences and in kalam, the age of the translations and of the controversies over Mu'tazilism. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 140}

He was a companion of the caliphs [[person/abd-allah-al-mamun|al-Ma'mun]] and [[person/al-mutasim|al-Mu'tasim]]. He dedicated his work On first philosophy to the latter, and he dedicated a number of other treatises to al-Mu'tasim's son Ahmad, who was his pupil. The article adds that he fell into disfavour under [[person/al-mutawakkil|al-Mutawakkil]] and was even deprived for a time of his large library. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 140}

## The range of his writings
According to the Fihrist, he wrote almost 250 titles, covering all the sciences cultivated in his day. The article says these include astrology but not alchemy, which he regarded as a form of trickery, and also technical subjects of interest to the ruling circles around him, such as the manufacture of glass, jewellery, armour and perfume. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 140}

Fewer than forty of his titles survive, about as many philosophical as scientific, and the editing and evaluation of some unpublished ones was still incomplete when the article was written. For that reason the article says a full account of his thought cannot be given and offers only an outline. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 140}

## Reasons for suspecting Mu'tazili leanings
The article says that his association with al-Ma'mun and al-Mu'tasim, together with his fall from favour under al-Mutawakkil and the temporary loss of his library, leads one to suspect at least a tendency towards [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazilism]]. It reports that this hypothesis is supported by several passages in his known works, which refer to the negation of divine attributes or to the excellence of God's works, and by the titles of works known only from the bibliographies. This is the article's reading of the evidence, and it sits alongside the differing views set out under affiliation below. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 140}

## Philosophy and revelation
The article finds his general position best expressed in the introductory chapter of On first philosophy. Besides definitions and technical statements about the principles of philosophy, the four causes and the four scientific questions, the chapter is in effect both an advertisement for philosophy and a defence of it, which fits a treatise written for a caliph. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 140}

Taking his inspiration from the opening of a book of Aristotle's Metaphysics, and at times borrowing from it closely, al-Kindi describes how true knowledge has accumulated over time through the efforts of philosophers. From this he infers that truth must be acquired from whatever source it comes, and that the statements of earlier philosophers have to be re-examined and completed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 140} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 141}

He sharply criticises those who attack philosophy in the name of religion, saying that they themselves lack religion. The article reports that, for him, the content of the science of things and their true natures, that is philosophy, is the same as the content of the prophets' message: the science of divine sovereignty and unity, and of morality and ethics. The chapter closes with a short borrowing from Aristotle's Protreptic, which the article says is again unacknowledged, and then with a prayer asking God's help in the pursuit of knowledge. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 141}

The article concludes that he set out an intellectual orientation opposed in two ways to that of the traditionalists: knowledge can come from several sources and can still develop, yet he claims to respect the prophetic message. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 141}

His epistle on the number of Aristotle's works follows the same spirit. It compares human knowledge, which depends on long effort and a regulated method, with divine knowledge, by which God inspires the prophets when He wishes, in a condensed revelation of a few phrases that the philosopher can explain only through a lengthy process of elucidation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 141}

## Contemporary assessments and sources
The article notes that the bio-bibliographers stress several things. Ibn al-Nadim, in the [[work/al-fihrist|Fihrist]], says he had an unequalled knowledge of the ancient sciences. Ibn Juljul says that of all Islamic philosophers he was closest to Aristotle, and also that he wrote an essay on tawhid in the manner of the logicians. Al-Qifti says he studied the branches of Greek, Persian and Indian wisdom in depth. Al-Bayhaqi says he combined the principles of the Law with those of the rational sciences. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 140}

The Fihrist lists his works systematically, including a section of dialectical or controversial books, many of which the article judges must have treated specific questions of kalam, such as prophecy, divine unity and the creation of the body and of the atom. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 140}

Among the Greek philosophers he names only Plato and Aristotle. The article says he used Aristotle's treatise on the heavens, commissioned a translation of the Metaphysics and revised the translation of the Theology attributed to Aristotle. It also finds that he knew, directly or indirectly, authors such as Epictetus, Proclus and probably John Philoponus, and echoes the late Alexandrian school. These borrowings, it says, raise historical and critical problems that are far from solved, and a philosophical problem that could be settled only by studying his whole corpus, its chronology, and the consistency and development of his thought and vocabulary. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 140} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 141}

## Concepts, style and harmonising aims
From the Aristotelian tradition, the article says, he took concepts such as the four causes and the categories of change, and propositions such as the finiteness of the world, the impossibility of an actual corporeal infinite, and the mechanics of intellectual perception. From the Platonist tradition he took speculation on the soul, its relation to the body and to divine light, and its ascent to and beyond the heavens. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 141}

His style, the article observes, is exhortatory and semi-mystical in the latter case, but elsewhere very precise, abstract and strained, proceeding by axioms and deductions with geometrical regularity. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 141}

Where the Greeks disagree with Qur'anic revelation, as on creation and the life-span of the universe, the article says he stops following them. It adds that he was interested in establishing agreements between certain philosophical ideas and certain articles of the Islamic faith, even Mu'tazili ones. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 141}

On first philosophy, in the part that survives, moves from reflections on the one and the many, where echoes of Proclus are detected, to a kind of philosophical [[concept/tawhid|tawhid]] and a criticism of those who ascribe attributes to God. The same theme appears in his essay on the unity of God and the finite nature of the world. His epistle on the number of Aristotle's works contains a philosophical commentary on verses 78 to 82 of surah 36, and his epistle on the lowest prostration of the body one on verse 6 of surah 55. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 141}

The article also points to his choice of language. He calls God al-wahid al-haqq, a name it describes as both Qur'anic and Neoplatonist, and he uses the religious term rububiyya in the title of the Theology attributed to Aristotle. It calls it important to analyse these agreements and differences at this early stage of the history of [[concept/falsafa|falsafa]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 141}

## Science: optics and medicine
The article says his scientific work follows the same scheme as his philosophy: to revise and develop what the ancients found, in light of new interests. He wrote essays on Euclid, Archimedes, Ptolemy, the astrolabe and Hippocratic medicine, and drew heavily on Asian sources, especially for remedies. In optics and pharmacology he also followed ideas of his own. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 141}

His work on perspective, known only in Latin as De causis diversitatum aspectus, follows Euclid without following him blindly. It sets out the rectilinear propagation of light, which Euclid postulated and al-Kindi demonstrates, then a theory of vision in which the eye illuminates the object, a Euclidean theory that al-Kindi modifies by giving the rays emitted by the eye three dimensions where Euclid had geometric lines, and finally a theory of mirrors. His study of burning mirrors is described as a revision, criticism and completion of what Anthemios of Tralles had written. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 141}

In his treatise On compound medicines the same method is applied. The ancients had studied the proportions of the four qualities, hot, cold, dry and wet, in simple medicines, and al-Kindi extends this to compound ones, explaining mathematically how increasing the parts of each quality relates to the increase in the medicine's effect on the body. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 141}

## Legacy
The article says he left a few pupils, among them [[person/ahmad-ibn-al-tayyib-al-sarakhsi|Ahmad ibn al-Tayyib al-Sarakhsi]] and Abu Ma'shar, but not a school in the strict sense. He survived above all as a universal scholar and astrologer, and those of his works available in Latin were still read in medieval times. [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]] quotes him at several points in the [[work/al-muqaddima|Muqaddima]] but does not include him in his list of Islamic philosophers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 141}

## Letter analysis and cryptanalysis
In [[invention/frequency-analysis-al-kindi|his cryptanalytic method]], al-Kindi counts and ranks a known-language sample, compares message symbols, warns about short messages and tests guesses through adjacency and conventional openings {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 216–218}. He distinguishes prose from verse, using metre and rhyme in the latter {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 219}. TDV calls his the earliest surviving treatise in the field while naming earlier concealment traditions {cite:tdv-muamma pp. 319–320}.

The opening addresses Abu al-Abbas and says the work answers his request; neither that address nor the editors' identification becomes a fixed composition year here {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 213–214}. A seven-leaf count follows, but editorial notes emend numbers and identify three omitted letters: these are not presented as timeless Arabic frequencies {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 235–237}.

## Calculating compound-drug degrees
In [[invention/kindi-drug-degrees|his calculation of compound-drug degrees]], al-Kindi reinterpreted inherited Galenic qualities through numerical ratios: regular steps in degree corresponded to geometric increases in the ratio, leading to the problem of combining ingredients with different quantities and qualities {cite:kindi-drug-degrees-prioreschi-2002 p. 18}. Prioreschi distinguishes this treatise from the formulary, attributes its twelfth-century Latin translation to Gerard of Cremona and reports Bacon's assessment of its mathematical difficulty {cite:kindi-drug-degrees-prioreschi-2002 pp. 18–20}. This is a historical medical model, not a prescription or clinical validation; the study supplies no specific composition year.
