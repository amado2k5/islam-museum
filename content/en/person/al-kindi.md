## Overview
Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq ibn al-Sabbah al-Kindi, called the "Philosopher of the Arabs". Ibn al-Nadim describes him as the most excellent man of his age and the unique one of his time in knowledge of the ancient sciences, and says his books were on logic, philosophy, geometry, arithmetic, music and the stars. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 357}

Al-Mas'udi counts him among those who engaged in the sciences of astronomy after the appearance of Islam, together with [[person/al-khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]], Abu Ma'shar, al-Farghani, al-Battani and others. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 519}

Al-Mas'udi says that he wrote a treatise in two parts refuting the claims of those who claim to make gold and silver without their ores. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 619-620}

Ibn al-Nadim adds that he "was a miser". {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 357}

## Letter analysis and cryptanalysis
In [[invention/frequency-analysis-al-kindi|his cryptanalytic method]], al-Kindi counts and ranks a known-language sample, compares message symbols, warns about short messages and tests guesses through adjacency and conventional openings {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 216–218}. He distinguishes prose from verse, using metre and rhyme in the latter {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 p. 219}. TDV calls his the earliest surviving treatise in the field while naming earlier concealment traditions {cite:tdv-muamma pp. 319–320}.

The opening addresses Abu al-Abbas and says the work answers his request; neither that address nor the editors' identification becomes a fixed composition year here {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 213–214}. A seven-leaf count follows, but editorial notes emend numbers and identify three omitted letters: these are not presented as timeless Arabic frequencies {cite:kindi-cryptanalysis-1987 pp. 235–237}.

## Calculating compound-drug degrees
In [[invention/kindi-drug-degrees|his calculation of compound-drug degrees]], al-Kindi reinterpreted inherited Galenic qualities through numerical ratios: regular steps in degree corresponded to geometric increases in the ratio, leading to the problem of combining ingredients with different quantities and qualities {cite:kindi-drug-degrees-prioreschi-2002 p. 18}. Prioreschi distinguishes this treatise from the formulary, attributes its twelfth-century Latin translation to Gerard of Cremona and reports Bacon's assessment of its mathematical difficulty {cite:kindi-drug-degrees-prioreschi-2002 pp. 18–20}. This is a historical medical model, not a prescription or clinical validation; the study supplies no specific composition year.
