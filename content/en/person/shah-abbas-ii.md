## Summary

Shah Abbas II (30 August 1632 – 26 October 1666), born Soltan Mohammad Mirza, was the seventh shah of Safavid Iran (r. 1642–1666) and the eldest son of Shah Safi. He came to the throne aged nine under the regency of the grand vizier Saru Taqi, then asserted personal rule in 1645. He retook Kandahar from the Mughals in 1649 and held it against three sieges, kept the peace with the Ottoman Empire, and is remembered as the last strong king of the Safavid dynasty. He died at Khosrowabad near Damghan and was buried in Qom.{cite:wikipedia-shah-abbas-ii-en}{cite:wikipedia-shah-abbas-ii-ar}

## A child shah

Abbas II was born at Qazvin on 30 August 1632, the date a Dutch East India Company report preserves; a rival statement in the structured data gives 20 December 1633, and both readings are kept in the record.{cite:wikipedia-shah-abbas-ii-en}{cite:wikidata-shah-abbas-ii} Crowned in May 1642 at the age of nine, he spent his first years under the control of the grand vizier Saru Taqi and the queen mother Anna Khanum. Saru Taqi's government was corrupt and he made powerful enemies; in 1645 a conspiracy of courtiers had him assassinated, with the young shah's connivance, and Abbas II then took personal charge of his empire at thirteen.{cite:wikipedia-shah-abbas-ii-en}

## Kandahar and the quiet west

His reign's great military fact was Kandahar. The city had been surrendered to the Mughals in 1638; in February 1649 Abbas II retook it, and then held it against three Mughal sieges, the last and largest led by Aurangzeb in person. Westward he kept the long peace with the Ottomans, though he quarrelled sharply with them over Basra's treatment of Iranian merchants. Internally the reign grew harder with the years: the treasury emptied, the army weakened, Isfahan itself was threatened by Uzbek raids, and the shah's drinking deepened. Yet later judgement remembers him kindly — efficient and just by the standards of his house, and the last strong king of the Safavid dynasty.{cite:wikipedia-shah-abbas-ii-en}

## Religion — a flagged record

Abbas II reigned as the Twelver Shi'i shah of Safavid Iran, and his coins bore the Shi'a slogan and the names of the Twelve Imams.{cite:wikipedia-shah-abbas-ii-fa} The English article attests his personal practice in detail: he commissioned Persian translations of Shi'ia jurisprudence from the Arabic and consulted the ulama about them, kept friendly relations with Mohsen Fayz Kashani, Mohammad Bagher Sabzevari and Mohammad Taqi Majlesi, honoured his ancestral Safaviyya order, and in 1653 let a Shi'ia scholar persuade him to give up wine for a time — while noting that he was not a zealous Shiite, and that his reign saw forced conversions of Iran's Jews.{cite:wikipedia-shah-abbas-ii-en} The Persian article, however, states the opposite inclination for the man himself: that Abbas II, unlike his father, showed greater interest in the Sunni school, which earned him the enmity of many of the ulama of his father's court.{cite:wikipedia-shah-abbas-ii-fa} Because two independent texts point in contrary directions, this museum records his faith as unknown with both characterisations stated in full, and flags the settlement for a founder ruling — dynastic confession is context, not attestation.

He died on 26 October 1666 at Khosrowabad near Damghan — the date and place the English lead, the Persian article and the structured data share, though the English narrative once gives 25 September 1666, the structured data also carries 5 October 1666, and the English death section names Behshahr instead; he was buried at Qom.{cite:wikipedia-shah-abbas-ii-en}{cite:wikipedia-shah-abbas-ii-ar}{cite:wikipedia-shah-abbas-ii-fa}{cite:wikidata-shah-abbas-ii}
