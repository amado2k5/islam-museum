## Summary
Mohamed Aly El-Erian is an Egyptian-American economist, author and financial adviser. The Muslim 500 lists him in its Business section as President of Queens' College, Cambridge and chief economic adviser at Allianz; Wikipedia records his earlier leadership of PIMCO, one of the world's largest bond investors, where he served as chief executive officer and co-chief investment officer from 2007 to 2014. {cite:muslim500-mohamed-el-erian} {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-el-erian}

## Upbringing and formation
El-Erian was born in 1958 in Cairo into an Egyptian family connected to diplomacy and scholarship; his father served as Egypt's ambassador, and the family lived abroad during his youth. He studied economics at Queens' College, Cambridge, and took his doctorate at Oxford before joining the International Monetary Fund in Washington, where he spent fifteen years, rising to deputy director — a formation that mixed the discipline of a multilateral institution with long exposure to emerging markets and currency crises. {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-el-erian}

## Career in finance
After the IMF he moved to bond investing, first at Salomon Smith Barney and then at PIMCO, the Pacific Investment Management Company in Newport Beach, California. He became chief executive and co-chief investment officer alongside founder Bill Gross, and was the public intellectual of the firm through the global financial crisis: PIMCO's coinage of the term "new normal" for a low-growth post-crisis world is widely credited to El-Erian. In 2014 he left PIMCO and joined its German parent, Allianz, as chief economic adviser. {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-el-erian}

## Public service and teaching
Beyond markets, El-Erian chaired President Barack Obama's Global Development Council from 2012 to 2017, served on the boards of the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace and other institutions, and in 2020 became President of Queens' College, Cambridge — a return to the college where he had studied. He also held a senior role at Gramercy Funds Management and has advised governments and central banks. {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-el-erian}

## Books and ideas
El-Erian writes a widely syndicated column for Bloomberg Opinion and contributes to the Financial Times. His books include When Markets Collide ⁦(2008)⁩, written on the eve of the financial crisis and praised for anticipating the breakdown of old market assumptions; The Only Game in Town ⁦(2016)⁩, on the exhausted role of central banks; and Permacrisis ⁦(2022)⁩, co-authored with Gordon Brown, Michael Spence and Reid Lidow, on navigating a world of compounding crises. {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-el-erian}

## Significance
For readers of the museum, El-Erian represents a figure of the global Muslim diaspora whose influence runs through institutions rather than public religiosity: an Egyptian-born economist whom The Muslim 500 counts among the most influential Muslims in business, whose commentary moves between Cambridge, Washington and the bond markets of California, and whose career links development economics to the management of vast private capital. {cite:muslim500-mohamed-el-erian} {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-el-erian}

## Egypt, markets and identity
El-Erian's public identity is professional rather than confessional: an Egyptian who carries his origin into American and British institutions. In his writing he returns often to the economies of the region he left — warning that the Middle East's growth must translate into jobs for its youth, and that globalisation's losers would eventually vote against it, a line of argument the decade after When Markets Collide made look prescient. The Muslim 500 counts him in Business; the museum records the listing, and the secular professional standing it describes, without overstating what his Muslim identity means in the sources — an influence exercised as economist and adviser, across a career that made a man from Cairo one of the most quoted interpreters of the world's money. {cite:muslim500-mohamed-el-erian} {cite:wikipedia-mohamed-el-erian}

## A voice for the patient economy
In his more recent writing El-Erian has returned to a single worry: that the institutions that govern money were built for a world of predictable shocks and are now facing a world of permanent ones. His columns and books argue for what he calls resilience — the capacity of households, firms and states to absorb disorder without breaking — and against the temptation to treat every problem as one that a central bank can fix. For the museum's record the point is simpler: a Muslim economist at the centre of the Western financial conversation, warning that the conversation itself may be missing the deeper shifts. {cite:muslim500-mohamed-el-erian}
