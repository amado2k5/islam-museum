Allal al-Fassi (1910–1974) was a Moroccan scholar, jurist and politician, born at Fez on 10 January 1910 into a wealthy family of Andalusian origin.{cite:wikipedia-allal-al-fassi-fr}{cite:wikidata-allal-al-fassi} His father, Abd al-Wahid al-Fassi, was one of the leading ulama of Fez — qadi, mufti and professor at the Qarawiyyin — and Allal himself studied at the Qarawiyyin, taking his licence in 1932.{cite:wikipedia-allal-al-fassi-fr} Strongly influenced by the Salafiyya, he animated the first nationalist circles at Fez from 1927, and the protest against the Berber Dahir of 1930, which threatened to remove the Berber tribes from the sultan's jurisdiction, made him a national figure; after two years of teaching at the Qarawiyyin he renounced his post rather than submit to the protectorate's injunctions.{cite:wikipedia-allal-al-fassi-fr}{cite:wikipedia-allal-al-fassi-en} In 1934 he founded, with Mohamed Hassan Ouazzani and Ahmed Balafrej, the Moroccan Action Committee, the first national movement grouping the young activists of the cities, which drafted the reform plan presented in Paris.{cite:wikipedia-allal-al-fassi-fr}

Arrested as the national movement grew, al-Fassi was exiled by the French to a remote village in Gabon in 1937 and held there for nine years in near-total isolation, refusing every colonial attempt at negotiation, until his pardon in 1946.{cite:wikipedia-allal-al-fassi-fr} The Istiqlal Party had been founded in December 1943 by Balafrej and companions freed before him; after independence he became the party's leader in 1960 and served as minister of Islamic affairs from 1961 to 1963.{cite:wikipedia-allal-al-fassi-fr}{cite:wikipedia-allal-al-fassi-en} As a jurist, the Arabic biography describes a long reach and firm footing in Islamic jurisprudence, especially Maliki fiqh and comparative fiqh, with opinions cited by the scholars of the Maghreb; the reform plan he led had demanded the unification of the legal system under Maliki law, and he helped codify the Mudawana personal-status code on a Maliki basis while arguing for ijtihad within that framework and criticising the Maliki traditionalism of his day.{cite:wikipedia-allal-al-fassi-ar}{cite:wikipedia-allal-al-fassi-en} His books include Self-Criticism (1952), described as his most thoughtful and original work, and The Purposes of the Islamic Sharia and its Noble Qualities.{cite:wikipedia-allal-al-fassi-en} He died at Bucharest on 13 May 1974, during an official mission.{cite:wikipedia-allal-al-fassi-fr}{cite:wikidata-allal-al-fassi}
