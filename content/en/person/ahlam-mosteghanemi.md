## Summary
Ahlam Mosteghanemi is an Algerian poet and novelist and one of the most widely read living writers in Arabic. The Muslim 500 lists her in Arts & Culture as a bestselling Arab novelist who won the Naguib Mahfouz Medal for Literature; Wikipedia records that she was the first Algerian woman to publish poetry and fiction in the Arabic language. {cite:muslim500-ahlam-mosteghanemi} {cite:wikipedia-ahlam-mosteghanemi}

## Formation between languages
She was born in 1953 in Tunis, where her father, the militant nationalist Mohammed Cherif, had taken refuge during Algeria's war of independence; the family returned to an Algeria still at war. She grew up between French colonial schooling and an Arabic literary ambition — she wrote a prize-winning essay in Arabic as a schoolgirl — and took her doctorate in sociology at the Sorbonne in Paris. Her whole oeuvre inhabits the tension between the two languages of Algerian life. {cite:wikipedia-ahlam-mosteghanemi}

## Memory of the Flesh
Her breakthrough novel Dhakirat al-Jasad — Memory of the Flesh — appeared in 1993 and became a phenomenon: a love story braided with the story of Algeria itself, told partly through a painter and the woman he loves. It is counted among the best-selling Arabic novels ever published, won the Naguib Mahfouz Medal for Literature, and opened a trilogy completed by Fawda al-Hawas ⁦(Chaos of the Senses)⁩ and Aber Sarir ⁦(Bed-Hopper)⁩ — the three volumes moving between love, art and the national wound. {cite:wikipedia-ahlam-mosteghanemi} {cite:muslim500-ahlam-mosteghanemi}

## Poet first
Before the novels she was a poet: the first Algerian woman to publish an Arabic poetry collection, and for years a daily columnist — her writing on love, exile and the nation making her, by the count of polls and circulation figures the Muslim 500 follows, among the most influential women in the Arab world. She has lived between Beirut, Paris and Amman, her life crossing the Arab world like her readership. {cite:wikipedia-ahlam-mosteghanemi} {cite:muslim500-ahlam-mosteghanemi}

## Significance
In the museum's record, Mosteghanemi stands for the Arabic-language novelist as national voice: a Maghribi woman who chose Arabic over French and was rewarded by a readership that made her the bestselling woman novelist in the language — literature as the shared memory of a wounded country. {cite:muslim500-ahlam-mosteghanemi} {cite:wikipedia-ahlam-mosteghanemi}

## The daughter of the revolution's writer
Mosteghanemi's lineage matters to her public identity: her father, Mohammed Cherif, was a militant of the Algerian revolution and a figure of its intellectual establishment, and she has written about growing up in a household where politics, literature and the nation's wounds were the daily subject. That formation — a novelist raised inside the story of independence — is the biographical fact behind the political charge her readers find in the books: the memory of the body is also, in her telling, the memory of a country that never quite received the revolution it was promised. The Muslim 500 lists her for the reach of that memory in Arabic literature. {cite:muslim500-ahlam-mosteghanemi} {cite:wikipedia-ahlam-mosteghanemi}

## The novelist as memory
Mosteghanemi's record is the museum's example of the novelist who treats memory as a political act: the writer who understood that the stories Algeria told about itself were the stories the revolution had failed to keep, and who made the case in Arabic for an audience that had never been asked to remember. The argument is hers; the platform is the novel; and the result — an Algerian woman whose books are read across the Arab world as a record of what the revolution cost — is the profile her Muslim 500 entry records. {cite:muslim500-ahlam-mosteghanemi}
