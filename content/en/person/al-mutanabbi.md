## Overview
Al-Mutanabbi, Abu al-Tayyib Ahmad ibn al-Husayn al-Mutanabbi, whom Ibn al-Imad describes as the greatest of the poets of Arabic. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 14-15}

## In Egypt
He came to Egypt as a guest of Kafur al-Ikhshidi. Fatik al-Rumi al-Ikhshidi was known for generosity, but he could not go to seek al-Mutanabbi's service for fear of Kafur. They met by chance in the desert without an appointment, and Fatik gave him a gift worth a thousand dinars, then followed it with other gifts. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 14-15}

He asked Kafur's leave to praise Fatik, and was given it. He praised him with an ode beginning "You have no horses to give nor money". {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 14-15}

## The lament for Fatik
Fatik died in Egypt in Shawwal 355 AH, and al-Mutanabbi lamented him with an ode beginning "Grief disturbs and consolation restrains". Ibn al-Imad describes it as one of the outstanding elegies, and says he left Egypt with it. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 14-15}

## His journeys
Born at Kufa in 303/915, he was taken by his family to the Samawa at the end of 312 and returned at the beginning of 315. He left Kufa at the end of 316 and moved between Baghdad and Syria, then led a rising from Latakia into the Samawa, was defeated and imprisoned at Hims at the end of 322 for two years. He became court poet to Badr al-Kharshani at Damascus at the beginning of 328, then official poet of Sayf al-Dawla at Aleppo from 337 for nine years until he fled secretly; at Fustat he praised then satirised Kafur, leaving on the day of sacrifice in 350 for Iraq. At the beginning of 354 he went via Arrajan to Shiraz, where he praised Adud al-Dawla, and was killed returning to Baghdad at the end of Ramadan 354. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 769-771}

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "A Story and an Idea"
[[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] makes al-Mutanabbi the subject of the fourth episode of his programme [[work/kissa-wa-fikra-program|"A Story and an Idea"]], on "the power of the word", which he opens with the poet's best-known boast verses. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:00:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:01:30} The figures and dates below are his.

He names him Abu al-Tayyib Ahmad ibn al-Husayn, "the emperor of poets", born in 303 AH; he composed poetry at nine and lived among the Bedouin to master pure Arabic. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:02:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:03:30} It is reported, he says, that he claimed prophethood and that the amir of Hims jailed him in 321 AH, releasing him after a poem asking pardon, and that the nickname stayed with him; al-Suwaidan adds that the claim is not established. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:04:30} He says the poet's life was free of wine and vice, and that his diwan holds more than five thousand verses in more than three hundred poems, ordered by the poet himself, with more than forty commentaries. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:05:30}

[[person/sayf-al-dawla|Sayf al-Dawla]], at war with the Byzantines, needed a poet who would be his voice, and the "Sayfiyyat" are al-Mutanabbi's finest poems. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:08:00} The Hamdanid state was small and his ambition large, so he left for Egypt, where [[person/kafur-al-ikhshidi|Kafur al-Ikhshidi]], shrewd and literate, kept him about four years between hope and despair without giving him the post he sought. The poems of those years are the "Kafuriyyat"; at the end he satirised Kafur and fled. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:10:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:13:00} Al-Suwaidan sets the earlier praise beside the later satire and calls the turn a betrayal of conscience: when his interests ended, his principles ended. He adds that the great historians describe Kafur as an experienced, forbearing and generous statesman. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:16:00}

In 354 AH, on the road to Baghdad, he was waylaid by Fatik ibn Abi Jahl al-Asadi, the maternal uncle of Dabba, whom the poet had satirised. He tried to flee, but a servant reminded him of his own verse about the horses and the night; he turned back and was killed with his son Muhassad. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:20:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:20:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:21:00} Many of his verses became proverbs. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:21:30} Al-Suwaidan's lesson is that an idea loses its force when put in weak language; he tells of verses written in the voice of al-Aqsa that reached [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Saladin]], who vowed not to laugh until Jerusalem was freed. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:23:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep04 00:25:00}
