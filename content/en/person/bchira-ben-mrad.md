Bchira Ben Mrad (1913–1993) was a Tunisian feminist and nationalist activist, born into an old Tunis family of religious scholars of Ottoman origin.{cite:wikipedia-bchira-ben-mrad-en}{cite:wikipedia-bchira-ben-mrad-fr} She was the daughter of Mohamed Salah Ben Mrad, shaykh al-Islam of Tunisia, and the granddaughter of the mufti Hmida Ben Mrad; her mother Sallouha was a daughter of another shaykh al-Islam, Mahmoud Belkhodja.{cite:wikipedia-bchira-ben-mrad-en}{cite:wikipedia-bchira-ben-mrad-fr} Her father had her and her sisters given a traditional education with home tutoring, and the Arabic biography adds that she was taught by Zaytuna shaykhs, among them her grandfather and Mohamed Manachou, memorised part of the Quran and learned Arabic and much poetry, which equipped her for journalism and public speaking.{cite:wikipedia-bchira-ben-mrad-ar} She married — the English and French biographies name her husband Ahmed Zahar, while the Arabic biography names Sheikh Saleh al-Zahhar, married in 1929 and died in 1952, who encouraged her national and women's work.{cite:wikipedia-bchira-ben-mrad-en}{cite:wikipedia-bchira-ben-mrad-ar}

After hearing nationalist leaders, among them Mahmoud El Materi, discuss the country's plight, Ben Mrad conceived a framework in which women could act within the national movement.{cite:wikipedia-bchira-ben-mrad-en} In 1936, when a fair organised by Ali Belhouane and others to raise money for North African students in France failed, she organised one with women, winning the initially sceptical agreement of Belhouane and Mongi Slim; the organising committee, which included Tawhida Ben Cheikh, the first woman physician of Tunisia, gathered some 9,000 people at Dar El Fourati and collected a significant sum for the nationalist leaders.{cite:wikipedia-bchira-ben-mrad-fr} A week later, in May 1936, she founded the Muslim Union of Tunisian Women (UMFT), the first Tunisian women's organisation, and chaired it until its dissolution in 1956.{cite:wikipedia-bchira-ben-mrad-en} The union worked with the Neo Destour; its statutes aimed to build knowledge among women and direct them to education within the limits of morality and religion, and it obtained official recognition only in 1951.{cite:wikipedia-bchira-ben-mrad-fr} With her father's support she published numerous articles in his journal Shams al-Islam and in other Tunisian periodicals, arguing for the education of Tunisian girls.{cite:wikipedia-bchira-ben-mrad-ar}

After independence Bourguiba dissolved her movement, launched his own national women's union and never cited her name, leaving her deeply embittered despite the esteem in which he had held her; she was nevertheless made a commander of the Order of Independence in 1989.{cite:wikipedia-bchira-ben-mrad-fr} She died on 4 May 1993, and streets in Tunisia bear her name.{cite:wikipedia-bchira-ben-mrad-fr}{cite:wikidata-bchira-ben-mrad}
