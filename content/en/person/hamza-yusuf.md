## Summary
Hamza Yusuf (born Mark Hanson, 1958) is an American Islamic scholar, described by the English article as a pioneer of Islamic neo-traditionalism who has promoted the Islamic sciences and classical teaching methods throughout the world. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

He co-founded the Zaytuna Institute in Berkeley, California in 1996, which opened in 2010 as Zaytuna College, the first accredited Muslim liberal arts college in the United States, and he is listed in the top fifty of The 500 Most Influential Muslims. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

The Guardian has called him "arguably the West's most influential Islamic scholar", and The New Yorker "perhaps the most influential Islamic scholar in the Western world", as the article's references record. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

## Early life and conversion
He was born as Mark Hanson in 1958 in Walla Walla, Washington, to two academics working at Whitman College, and was raised in northern California; his father was a Catholic of Irish descent, his mother — half Greek, half Irish — was Greek Orthodox, and he was raised in his mother's faith and educated at preparatory schools on both coasts. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

The sources conflict on his birth year: the English article and Wikidata give 1958, while the Arabic article gives 1 January 1960; this record follows 1958 and records the variant. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en} {cite:wikidata-hamza-yusuf} {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-ar}

In 1977, after a near-death experience in a car accident and reading the Qur'an, he converted to Islam — the Arabic article places the conversion at Santa Barbara, California, at the age of eighteen, after encounters with Black American Muslims had set him searching — and he has described concepts present in both Christianity and Islam and called the conversion a "fulfilment". {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en} {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-ar}

## A decade of study in the Muslim world
Impressed by a young Saudi couple who were followers of Abdalqadir as-Sufi, the Scottish convert who led the Darqawa Sufi order and the Murabitun movement, he moved to Norwich, England, to study directly under as-Sufi. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

In 1979 he moved to Al Ain in the United Arab Emirates, where he spent four years studying the sharia sciences at the Islamic Studies Institute of the United Arab Emirates University, mostly one-to-one with scholars; he became fluent in Arabic and learned Qur'anic recitation, rhetoric, poetry, Maliki jurisprudence and theology, among other classical disciplines. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

In 1984 he formally disassociated himself from as-Sufi's teachings and moved in a different intellectual direction under the influence of Mauritanian scholars resident in the Emirates; he then studied in Algeria and Morocco, as well as Spain and Mauritania, where he formed his most lasting scholarly relationship, with Sidi Muhammad Ould Fahfu al-Massumi, known as Murabit al-Hajj. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

The Arabic article names his Mauritanian teachers in the Emirates — among them Shaykh Baya bin Salik, head of the sharia courts, whom it calls his true teacher before Mauritania — and records his later companionship of Abdallah bin Bayyah, and that he received ijazahs in several branches of the Islamic sciences from scholars of several countries. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-ar}

## Zaytuna
With Zaid Shakir and Hatem Bazian he founded the Zaytuna Institute in Berkeley in 1996, dedicated to the revival of traditional study methods and the sciences of Islam. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

In the fall of 2010 it opened its doors as Zaytuna College, a four-year Muslim liberal arts college — the first of its kind in the United States — combining the classical liberal arts of the trivium and quadrivium with rigorous training in the traditional Islamic disciplines, aiming to "educate and prepare morally committed professional, intellectual, and spiritual leaders"; it became the first accredited Muslim campus in the United States on approval by the Western Association of Schools and Colleges. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

He is an adviser to the Center for Islamic Studies at the Graduate Theological Union in Berkeley and to the Islamic Studies programme at Stanford University, and the Arabic article adds that he has lectured in history at the University of al-Qarawiyyin in Fez. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en} {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-ar}

## Further education
Alongside his traditional training he earned an associate degree in nursing from Imperial Valley College and a bachelor's degree in religious studies from San José State University. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

In 2020 he completed his PhD at the Graduate Theological Union; his dissertation, "The Normative Islamic Tradition in North and West Africa", is a case study of the transmission of authority in Ibn Ashir's Al-Murshid al-Mu'in, the classic Maliki manual of worship. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

## Positions and public roles
This section reports positions as the sources attribute them; it is not a verdict by the museum.

He serves as vice-president of the Global Center for Guidance and Renewal and of the United Arab Emirates-based Forum for Promoting Peace in Muslim Societies, both presided over by Abdallah bin Bayyah; the Forum has attracted wide controversy, the article records, for its close ties to the UAE government, its support for authoritarian leaders since the Arab Spring and its support for the Abraham Accords, and Yusuf denied in his 2023 address at Toronto's RIS conference that the Forum's statements reflect his personal opinions and that he has "never been in favour of normalization". {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

He condemned the 9/11 attacks as "an act of mass murder, pure and simple", and said that "Islam was hijacked ... on that plane as an innocent victim"; he is a signatory of A Common Word Between Us and You and of the open letter to the ISIS leader Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi refuting that organisation's principles. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

In a 2011 interview he argued that constitutional monarchy is a stronger form of governance than democracy, on the ground that a king, already satiated, is incorruptible in a way the wealthy are not. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

His 2019 remarks on the Syrian revolution — "if you humiliate a ruler, God will humiliate you" — were read by some as support for the Syrian regime, an interpretation he rejected; and his December 2016 comments on Black Lives Matter, in which he called the United States "one of the least racist societies in the world" in its laws and cited homicide statistics, drew wide criticism, which Zaid Shakir answered by writing that there is "not a racist bone in Shaykh Hamza's body". {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

Jordan's Royal Islamic Strategic Studies Centre places him 36th in its list of the 500 most influential Muslims in the world, and its 2016 edition, edited by John Esposito and Ibrahim Kalin, described him as "one of the foremost authorities on Islam outside of the Muslim world"; the journalist Graeme Wood has called him one of the two most prominent Muslim scholars in the United States. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

## Belief and affiliation
This section reports what the sources record of Yusuf's belief, without a verdict from the museum.

Two independent sources name his school. The English article's infobox gives his denomination as Sunni, his jurisprudence as Maliki and his creed as Ash'ari. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en}

The Arabic article states in terms that he is "Sunni in creed, moderate in approach, Maliki in jurisprudence". {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-ar}

The record accordingly enters sunni-maliki as documented, with the Ash'ari creed — named by one of the two sources — stated here rather than compounded into the value; his decade of Maliki study, from Al Ain to Mauritania and a doctorate on a Maliki manual, is the formation behind the attestations, while Wikidata records only Islam in general. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-en} {cite:wikidata-hamza-yusuf}

## Personal life
The Arabic article records that he is married with five children and lives in northern California; the museum publishes no further family detail. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-ar}

His lectures and translations circulate widely in Arabic and English media alike; the Arabic article notes his three seasons presenting the programme Rihla on Saudi MBC and his originating the programme idea of Yalla Shabab. {cite:wikipedia-hamza-yusuf-ar}
