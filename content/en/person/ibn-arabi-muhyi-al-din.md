## Overview
Muhyi al-Din Ibn al-Arabi, the Greatest Shaykh, author of *al-Futuhat* (The Meccan Openings). {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari p. 172-173}

Al-Maqqari relates that his son Muhammad, called Sa'd al-Din, was born in Malatya in Ramadan 618 AH, and was buried beside his father on the slope of Qasiyun in Damascus. {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari p. 172-173}

## Life
According to the Encyclopaedia of Islam, Muhyi al-Din Abu Abd Allah Muhammad ibn Ali ibn Muhammad ibn al-Arabi al-Hatimi al-Ta'i, known as al-Shaykh al-Akbar, lived from 560/1165 to 638/1240 and was one of the greatest Sufis of Islam. The article notes that he is usually called Ibn Arabi without the article, to tell him apart from the traditionist Ibn al-Arabi Abu Bakr; in Turkey he is often called Muhyi al-Din Arabi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 739}

He was born at [[place/murcia|Murcia]] on 27 Ramadan 560, which the article equates with 7 August 1165, into a family claiming descent from [[person/hatim-al-tai|Hatim al-Ta'i]]. Some Sufi adepts were among his near relatives. His father later moved to [[place/seville|Seville]], where his formal education began, and as a young man he is said to have served as secretary to governors. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 739} In the course of an illness he had a vision that changed his life, and he came to regard his earlier years as a time of ignorance. The philosopher [[person/ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd]], judge of Seville and a friend of his father, was impressed by the genuineness of this conversion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 739}

Although Ibn al-Arabi said his knowledge came to him without an intermediary, his works name many shaykhs whose company he sought, among them a disciple of [[person/abu-madyan|Abu Madyan]] and two women, Fatima bint al-Muthanna and Shams Umm al-Fukara. He called Abu Madyan his shaykh, but the article says the two never met. He spent about ten years with such teachers in Spain and North Africa, and Seville remained his home until 590/1194. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 739}

In that year he went to [[place/tunis|Tunis]], then to [[place/fez|Fez]], where he wrote *K. al-Isra* in 594/1198. In 595/1199 he was in [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]] for Ibn Rushd's funeral, and then in [[place/almeria|Almeria]]. After a return to Tunis he travelled by way of [[place/cairo|Cairo]] and [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]] to perform the pilgrimage. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 740} He stayed two years at [[place/mecca|Mecca]], moved by the Kaaba as the meeting point of the unseen and the seen worlds. There he began his great *al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya*, wrote *Ruh al-quds* and *Taj al-rasa'il*, and composed the love poems of the *Tarjuman al-ashwaq*, addressed to the daughter of an Isfahani resident of Mecca. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 740}

In 600/1204 he met Anatolian pilgrims at Mecca and travelled home with them by way of [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] and [[place/mosul|Mosul]], reaching [[place/malatya|Malatya]] in 601/1205. The Seljuk sultan [[person/ghiyath-al-din-kaykhusraw-i|Kay-Khusraw I]] loaded him and his companion with gifts. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 740} Further journeys took him to Jerusalem, Cairo and Mecca; he was at [[place/konya|Konya]] in 606/1209-10 and again at Baghdad in 608/1211-2. He visited [[place/aleppo|Aleppo]] and [[place/sivas|Sivas]], finished a commentary at [[place/aksaray|Aksaray]] in 612/1215, and from 612/1216 lived mainly at Malatya, where his son Sa'd al-Din Muhammad was born in 618/1221. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 740}

The article does not know why or when he left Anatolia for [[place/damascus|Damascus]], where he is first found in 627/1230. It suggests he met some discomfort from orthodox critics but found protectors in the Ibn Zaki family of judges and in members of the [[polity/ayyubid-dynasty|Ayyubid]] house. He led a quiet life of reading, teaching and writing; after a dream in 627/1229 he composed the *Fusus al-hikam*, and from 630/1233 he completed and revised the *Futuhat*. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 740} The article rejects the report that he forbade the reading of his works near the end of his life, since he heard and approved a text twenty days before his death and his disciple Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi, who was with him at the end, spent his life teaching and commenting on his works. He died in the house of the judge Muhyi al-Din Ibn al-Zaki on 28 Rabi II 638, given as 16 November 1240, and was buried in that family's tomb on the slope of [[place/mount-qasioun|Mount Qasiyun]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 740}

He married several times and presumably had many children, but only two sons are known: the poet Sa'd al-Din Muhammad, born at Malatya in 618/1221 and dead at Damascus in 656/1258, and Imad al-Din Abu Abd Allah, who died at Damascus in 667/1269. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 740} After his Egyptian campaign, the Ottoman sultan [[person/selim-i-ottoman|Selim I]] ordered the tomb rebuilt, with a mosque and a takkiyya nearby, and on that occasion a fatwa praising Ibn al-Arabi was given by [[person/ibn-kamal-pasha|Kemal-Pasha-zade]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 740}

## Works
The article calls him the most prolific of Sufi writers. Brockelmann lists 239 works, and Ibn al-Arabi's own lists survive in three differing versions that name 248 and 289 works, so the article concludes that some 400 works are probably his. It adds that some he had given away, some were in circulation and some he kept until God's command to release them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 740} Many of his books passed to Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi, who left them as a waqf to his library at Konya; many survive there and in other Turkish libraries. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 740} He also wrote outside Sufism, including an abridgement of Sahih Muslim, though those works are not known to survive. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 740}

The article singles out several Sufi works. *Al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya*, begun at Mecca in 598/1201, is a full exposition of his Sufi doctrine in six sections and 560 chapters; the author's own copy of the second recension, in 37 volumes dated 633-7/1235-9, is in Istanbul. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 740} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 741} *Fusus al-hikam*, which he said was dictated to him in a dream, summarises the teaching of 28 prophets from Adam to Muhammad; it has been printed often and has attracted at least 35 commentaries, including those of [[person/afif-al-din-al-tilimsani|Afif al-Din al-Tilimsani]], [[person/dawud-al-qaysari|Dawud al-Qaysari]] and [[person/jami-abd-al-rahman|Jami]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 741} *K. al-Isra* is a short rhymed-prose account of an ascent from the world of being to a station in God's presence. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 741}

Others include *Muhadarat al-abrar*, a collection of anecdotes; *Kalam al-Abadila*, sayings attributed to imaginary figures called Abd; *Mawaqi al-nujum*, written at Almeria; and *Ruh al-quds*, a letter from Mecca with much information on the shaykhs he knew in Spain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 741} *Al-Tanazzulat al-mawsiliyya* is a work of 55 chapters composed at Mosul on the inner meaning of religious duties, and *K. al-Asfar* treats three journeys, to, from and in God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 741}

The [[work/tarjuman-al-ashwaq|*Tarjuman al-ashwaq*]] contains 61 love poems with two contradictory prefaces: one says they were written for Nizam bint Makin al-Din, the other that they are to be read allegorically. His commentary says it was written because the poems stirred gossip in Syria. The article suggests the poems may fall into two groups, one from 598/1201-2 and one from about 610/1213, joined when the commentary was prepared. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 741} It lists among spurious attributions a *Tafsir*, *al-Shajara al-nu'maniyya* and a popular dream-interpretation book. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 742}

## Thought
The article stresses that only a few of his writings have been studied, so its summary rests chiefly on the *Futuhat*. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 742} It reports that, like most Sufis, he held reason to be limited and divided knowledge into three kinds: what reason attains, what is gained through spiritual states, and knowledge of mysteries breathed into the heart, which is marifa and comes from God, with or without an angel. He claimed a similar authority for his teachings as heir of the prophets within sainthood, but did not claim prophethood. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 742}

The article says his sources included the Qur'an, which he interpreted freely, and earlier mystics such as al-Junayd, [[person/bayazid-al-bistami|Bayazid al-Bistami]], [[person/al-hallaj|al-Hallaj]] and [[person/al-qushayri-abu-l-qasim|al-Qushayri]]. It also finds Neoplatonic influence and notes that he used terms from different traditions as interchangeable, which makes his writing hard to read. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 742}

In the article's account, he regarded God as an Existence free of all attributes, and described the emanation of other beings from it in a way the article calls very confused, though in essentials close to the Neoplatonist and Batini position. Man makes three journeys: from God into this world, to God with a guide, and in God, which has no end. The traveller on the third journey keeps the obligatory duties of the sharia outwardly while inwardly dwelling with God. Only a specially endowed few reach the vision of God, and the conditions for the seeker are silence, withdrawal, hunger and wakefulness. Through sincere intention a love grows in the heart; the veils are lifted, God manifests Himself, and union is achieved. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 742}

## Reception and influence
According to the article, he was in danger for his opinions only once in his lifetime, in Egypt, and neither he nor his followers founded an order. His teaching spread chiefly through Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi and his circle at Konya, among them the poet [[person/fakhr-al-din-iraqi|Iraqi]], whose Persian *Lama'at* carried it to eastern Iran. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 742}

Criticism came early. In Yemen, especially Zabid, fatwas declared his ideas innovation and every word of the *Fusus* unbelief, among them fatwas by [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] and [[person/taqi-al-din-al-subki|Taqi al-Din al-Subki]]; [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]] found his thought meaningless and heretical. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 742} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 743} Polemics against him were written by Ibn al-Ahdal and al-Biqai, and al-Taftazani attacked the *Fusus* in Persia and India. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 743} Defenders appeared later: [[person/al-suyuti|al-Suyuti]], al-Idrisi, [[person/al-sharani|al-Sharani]], Abd al-Ghani, and above all the fatwa of Kemal-Pasha-zade. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 743} Even so, the Hanafi al-Halabi and [[person/ali-al-qari|Ali al-Qari]] wrote refutations of the *Fusus*. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 743}

In Anatolia his works became textbooks in Ottoman madrasas, with commentaries by al-Qaysari and Yazijizade, and the two great commentators on Rumi's [[work/mathnawi-rumi|Mathnawi]], [[person/ismail-ankaravi|Ismail Ankaravi]] and Sari Abd Allah, read it in his light. The article says his doctrine of the unity of being became the main current of Anatolian Sufism and of diwan poetry from then on. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 743} The article adds that he may have influenced Raymond Lull, and that a suggestion that his account of the ascent influenced Dante has been made and is discussed elsewhere. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 743}

## In al-Dhahabi's Mizan al-I'tidal
In [[work/mizan-al-itidal|Mizan al-I'tidal]], [[person/al-dhahabi|al-Dhahabi]] gives an entry to Muhammad ibn Ali ibn Muhammad al-Hatimi al-Ta'i al-Andalusi, "author of the book *Fusus al-Hikam*", and records that he died in the year [6]38 — the margin of one manuscript adds "and six hundred". {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 659} He says he saw that Ibn Arabi had narrated from Abu al-Hasan ibn Hudhayl by licence, about which he has some reservation, that al-Dhahabi's own teacher Muhammad ibn Abi al-Dhikr al-Siqilli heard from him Abu Amr al-Dani's *al-Taysir*, and that he narrated hadith from a number of teachers. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 659}

**Ibn Abd al-Salam's story.** In an addition from one manuscript, al-Dhahabi reports from his companion Abu al-Fath al-Ya'mari, from [[person/ibn-daqiq-al-id|Ibn Daqiq al-Id]], that when Ibn Arabi was mentioned before [[person/izz-al-din-ibn-abd-al-salam|Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam]], he called him an evil, lying shaykh. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 659} Ibn Abd al-Salam explained this with a story: in Damascus they were discussing marriage with jinn, and Ibn Arabi said it was impossible, since humans are dense bodies and jinn subtle spirits; shortly afterwards Ibn Abd al-Salam saw him with a head wound, and Ibn Arabi said he had married a jinn woman and had three children by her, and that one day she grew angry, struck him with a bone and left. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 659} Al-Dhahabi comments: "I do not think Muhyi al-Din deliberately lied", and holds that seclusion and hunger produced in him fancy and a touch of madness. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 659}

**Scholars' views of his books.** Al-Dhahabi says that he wrote on the Sufism of the philosophers and of the people of oneness and said objectionable things on which scholars differed: some counted them apostasy and heresy; some counted them allusions of the gnostics and symbols of the wayfarers; some counted them ambiguous speech whose outer sense is unbelief and error but whose inner sense is truth and gnosis, holding him sound in himself and of great standing. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 659} {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 660} Others say: he did say this falsehood, but who says he died on it? To them it is apparent that he turned back and repented, since he was learned in reports and Sunna and broadly versed in the sciences. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 660}

**Al-Dhahabi's own view.** Al-Dhahabi says it is possible that he was one of God's friends whom the Real drew to Himself at death and granted a good end. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 660} As for his words, whoever understands them according to the principles of the Ittihadiyya (the doctrine of union), knows the aims of that school and gathers the strands of their expressions will see the truth to lie against their teaching; whoever looks deeply into *Fusus al-Hikam* will be amazed. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 660} An intelligent reader who reflects on those sayings, he holds, is one of two men: either inwardly one of the Ittihadiyya, or one of the believers who count this creed among the worst of unbelief. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 660} He concludes that a Muslim who lives ignorant behind the cattle, knowing nothing but a few suras of the Qur'an to pray with and believing in God and the Last Day, is far better off than with this gnosis, even had he read a hundred books or made a hundred retreats. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 660}
