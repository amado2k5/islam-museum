## Summary
Leo Africanus was born al-Hasan ibn Muhammad al-Wazzan in Granada around 1494 and raised in Fez, where he studied at the University of al-Qarawiyyin. {cite:wikipedia-leo-africanus-en} {cite:wikipedia-leo-africanus-ar} As a young man he travelled on Wattasid diplomatic missions — to Timbuktu around 1510, and in 1517 toward Constantinople, turning home through Cairo, Aswan and the Red Sea, probably performing the pilgrimage to Mecca. {cite:wikipedia-leo-africanus-en} On the return voyage in 1518 Spanish corsairs captured him; recognised as a scholar, he was carried to Rome, freed, pensioned, and in 1520 baptized in St Peter's Basilica, taking the name Johannes Leo de Medicis. {cite:wikipedia-leo-africanus-en}

## The Description of Africa
In Rome in 1526 Leo completed his manuscript on African geography. Published by Giovanni Battista Ramusio in Venice in 1550 as the Descrittione dell'Africa, the Description of Africa was reprinted five times and translated into French, Latin and English; among European geographers it remained the most authoritative treatise on the Maghreb and the Nile Valley until the modern exploration of Africa. {cite:wikipedia-leo-africanus-en} He also wrote an Arabic-Hebrew-Latin medical vocabulary for the Jewish physician Jacob Mantino, an Arabic grammar of which a fragment survives, an Arabic translation of the Epistles of Paul dated January 1521, and a biographical encyclopedia of Arab scholars. {cite:wikipedia-leo-africanus-en} In his own surviving signature he rendered his Christian name back into Arabic as Yuhanna al-Asad al-Gharnati — John the Lion of Granada. {cite:wikipedia-leo-africanus-en}

## Between two faiths
The baptism of 1520 and Leo's public life as a Christian author under papal patronage are documented facts. {cite:wikipedia-leo-africanus-en} {cite:wikidata-leo-africanus} What he inwardly held is a different question, and scholarship has never settled it. The German orientalist Johann Albrecht Widmannstetter reported that Leo had returned to Tunis and reconverted to Islam. {cite:wikipedia-leo-africanus-en} The Tunisian historian Muhammad ibn al-Khuja held that his conversion had been only outward, pointing out that his book on the lands of Islam contains nothing that disparages the faith. {cite:wikipedia-leo-africanus-ar} Natalie Zemon Davis's Trickster Travels (2007) reads the man's allegiances as deliberately layered between the two worlds he moved in. No source names a legal school for him at any stage of his life, and this museum records the allegiance as debated rather than settled. {cite:wikipedia-leo-africanus-en} {cite:wikipedia-leo-africanus-ar}

## The unknown end
Where Leo died is not documented either. One theory keeps him in Rome until about 1550, the year the Description appeared. A second has him leave before the Sack of Rome in 1527, return to North Africa and live in Tunis until some time after 1550. A third sends him on to Morocco after Charles V captured Tunis in 1535 — back toward the country of his birth, which his book says he wished to regain "by God's assistance". {cite:wikipedia-leo-africanus-en} The Arabic tradition likewise judges it most likely that he returned to Tunis and died there, little known in the Islamic world compared with his fame in the West. {cite:wikipedia-leo-africanus-ar}
