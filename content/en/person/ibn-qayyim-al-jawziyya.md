## Overview
The shaykh Shams al-Din Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr, Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya, a Hanbali of Damascus, author of *I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in* (Informing the Signatories) on behalf of the Lord of the Worlds. {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p.518}

## Death and works
Haji Khalifa gives his death as 751 AH. The conversion to the Gregorian calendar is derived. {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p.518} Among his works is [[work/ilam-al-muwaqqiin|I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in]]. {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p.518}

He is counted among the teachers of [[person/ibn-rajab|Ibn Rajab]]. {cite:ibn-rajab-jami-ulum-hikam p.11}

## Life and education in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam article on him, by F. de la Granja, calls him a Hanbali theologian and jurisconsult, born in [[place/damascus|Damascus]] on 7 Safar 691/29 January 1292 and dying there in 751/1350. He came from a humble family: his father was the superintendent, the *kayyim*, of the Jawziyya madrasa, which also served as a court for the Hanbali chief judge of Damascus. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 853}

The article describes his education as unusually wide and sound. Among his main teachers it names the judge Sulayman ibn Hamza, who died in 711/1311, and the shaykh Abu Bakr, a son of the traditionist Ibn Abd al-Da'im, who died in 718/1318. Above all, from 713/1313 he was the most famous pupil of [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]]. The article says he absorbed all his master's ideas and helped to spread his work while keeping a personality of his own. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 853}

Like his master, he was well versed in the main disciplines of the age: Qur'anic exegesis, hadith, the principles of jurisprudence and its detailed rulings. Like him he also opposed the monist school, the *ittihadiyya*, which the article says arose from the teaching of [[person/ibn-arabi-muhyi-al-din|Ibn al-Arabi]]. It adds that, unlike his master, he was strongly drawn to mysticism, and that he took a particular interest in the *Manazil* of [[person/al-ansari-al-harawi-abd-allah|al-Ansari]], a work that enjoyed great prestige under the Mamluks. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 853}

The article contrasts the two men in manner. It presents Ibn Qayyim as much less a polemicist than Ibn Taymiyya and much more a preacher, and judges that he left a justified reputation as a writer of great talent, whose eloquence contrasts with the incisive dryness of his master's succinct prose. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 853}

## Imprisonment, pilgrimage and public career
In 726/1326 he was imprisoned in the citadel of Damascus at the same time as Ibn Taymiyya, and he was released only in 728/1328, after his master's death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 853} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 854} In 731/1331-2 he made the pilgrimage to [[place/mecca|Mecca]]. The article notes, citing [[person/ibn-kathir|Ibn Kathir]], that the Syrian caravan, which left Damascus under the amir Izz al-Din Aybak, included a considerable number of jurisconsults and traditionists. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 854}

The article describes his career as modest and hampered by the opposition that Ibn Taymiyya's neo-Hanbalism met in the governing circles of the Mamluk state. On 2 Rajab 736/15 February 1336 he delivered the sermon, the *khutba*, for the first time, in a mosque recently founded by Nadim al-Din ibn Khalikhan in the gardens of the Ghuta, outside the Bab Sharqi and Bab Tuma. On 6 Safar 743/11 July 1342 he gave his inaugural lecture at the Sadriyya madrasa, where he was to teach until his death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 854}

## Disagreements over points of law
The article records two occasions on which he disagreed over points of law with [[person/taqi-al-din-al-subki|Taqi al-Din al-Subki]], the Shafi'i chief judge of Damascus, without either dispute turning into a serious quarrel. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 854}

The first, in Muharram 746/May-June 1345, concerned whether a race or shooting contest in which each of the two competitors puts down a stake is lawful without a third competitor, called a *muhallil*, who takes part without contributing a stake. Such a third party makes lawful an arrangement that might otherwise be regarded as gambling. Expressing the ideas of Ibn Taymiyya, Ibn Qayyim held that the *muhallil* was not necessary. Summoned by the Shafi'i chief judge, he had to submit to that judge's opinion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 854}

The second, in 750/1349, arose because he had issued fatwas on [[concept/talaq|repudiation, talaq]] in line with the doctrine of Ibn Taymiyya. The Bedouin amir Sayf al-Din ibn Fadl reconciled him with his adversary. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 854}

## Death and burial in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The article gives his death at Damascus on 23 Rajab 751/26 September 1350 and says he was buried beside his mother in the [[place/bab-al-saghir-cemetery|cemetery of Bab al-Saghir]]. His son Jamal al-Din Abd Allah, who died in 756/1355, succeeded him in teaching at the Sadriyya. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 854} The day of the month differs from the one given by Ibn Hajar in the section on al-Durar al-Kamina below.

## Works as the Encyclopaedia of Islam presents them
The article calls his doctrinal and literary output considerable and points to a list of his works in the Dhayl of Ibn Rajab. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 854} It singles out several titles.

- [[work/madarij-al-salikin|Madarij al-Salikin]] is a commentary on the *Manazil al-Sa'irin* of al-Ansari, and the article judges it the masterpiece of Hanbali mystical literature. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 854}
- [[work/ilam-al-muwaqqiin|I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in]], described as a guide for the perfect mufti, is a treatise on legal methodology that follows Ibn Taymiyya's ideas in that field. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 854}
- In politics, the *Kitab al-Turuq al-Hukmiyya* rests on ideas set out by Ibn Taymiyya in his *Hisba* and his *al-Siyasa al-Shar'iyya*. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 854}
- In the principles of religion, the article names the [[work/al-qasida-al-nuniyya-ibn-qayyim|Qasida Nuniyya]], an important profession of faith in verse directed mainly against the *ittihadiyya*, and a polemical treatise against the [[group/jahmiyya|Jahmiyya]], [[work/al-sawaiq-al-mursala|al-Sawa'iq al-Mursala]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 854}
- His [[work/badai-al-fawaid|Fawa'id]] is discussed, for its place in the history of rhetoric, in the article on bayan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 854}

## Pupils and later reputation
According to the article, several Mamluk-period scholars were his pupils or were influenced by him to varying degrees. It names the Shafi'i traditionist and historian Ibn Kathir, who died in 774, Zayn al-Din [[person/ibn-rajab|Ibn Rajab]], who died in 795/1397 and whom it calls the last great representative of medieval Hanbalism, and [[person/ibn-hajar-al-asqalani|Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani]], who died in 852/1449. It adds that he remains today an author highly esteemed among the Wahhabiyya, among the Salafiyya and in many circles of North African Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 854}

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lectures on Sharh Kitab al-Fawa'id

The Sheikh explains that the nisba al-Jawziyya comes from a city of that name, and that Ibn al-Qayyim's father was its custodian (qayyim), which is why he was known as Ibn al-Qayyim (the Sheikh's explanation; needs_source: no source given in the lecture). {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep01 01:03:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep01 01:04:00}

The Sheikh holds that the choice fell on Ibn al-Qayyim's book al-Fawa'id because of its influence on the souls of many readers, the Sheikh among them. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep02 00:25:00}

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lecture series Al Dinu Al Qayem

Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy says that neglecting the obligations loses the voluntary acts, which are the cause of the servant's love for his Lord, and he attributes to Ibn al-Qayyim a saying on this (needs_source: the attribution is as the sheikh gives it) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep01 00:10:30}. He recounts from Ibn al-Qayyim that the road back to the Paradise from which humanity was expelled passes through the valley of those who pray at night (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep01 00:11:00}.

He recounts that Ibn al-Qayyim described his teacher Ibn Taymiyya as one who subdued his desire until desire no longer ruled him (needs_source: the description is paraphrased) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep03 01:13:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep06 00:39:00}.

He recounts that when Ibn al-Qayyim was asked how one can know that a speech is true, he answered that truth has a radiance of its own (needs_source: the answer as the sheikh gives it) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep05 00:47:00}.

## In the 'Al-Mubdi'un' programme
Tareq al-Suwaidan presents Ibn al-Qayyim — Ibn Taymiyya's greatest student — as a creator whose rare gift was the craft of masterful authorship, distinguishing his surname: 'qayyim of the Jawziyya' means overseer of the Jawziyya school his father supervised {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:04:00}.
Of his life: born 7 Safar 691 AH into a learned family, he kept to Ibn Taymiyya until counted his heir and the authority on the school after him; Ibn Hajar described him as 'bold-hearted, broad of wisdom, learned and versed in the scholars' disagreements' {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:02:30}.
He used his sojourns at Mecca for authorship, saying of Miftah Dar al-Sa'ada it was 'among the gifts God opened upon me in my retreat to Him at His house'; and he composed on his journeys Zad al-Ma'ad, Rawdat al-Muhibbin, Bada'i' al-Fawa'id and Tahdhib Sunan Abi Dawud — so how much did he write while settled {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:07:30}.
He was an avid collector of books: over a hundred references stand behind Ijtima' al-Juyush al-Islamiyya, and some thirty titles behind Ahkam Ahl al-Dhimma {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:09:30}.
Of his stands: he gave fatwas on Ibn Taymiyya's positions — among them that a triple divorce uttered in one sitting counts as one, now the settled view (the speaker notes) — and was imprisoned for denouncing the saddling of mounts to the Friend's tomb, as his student Ibn Rajab reports {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:11:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:12:30}.
Of his character: Ibn Kathir described him 'fine of character, envying none, harming none, hating none'; and of his wisdom 'whoever wrongs you then comes apologising, humility obliges you to accept his excuse even if hollow, leaving his conscience to God' {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:16:00}.
Of his worship: Ibn Rajab said 'he was given to worship, night prayer and the longest salah; devoted to remembrance, passionate for God's love — I never saw broader in knowledge nor more knowing of the meanings of Qur'an and Sunna; he is not infallible, but I have seen none like him'; imprisoned with Ibn Taymiyya in the citadel he occupied himself with recitation and contemplation and was released only after his shaykh's death {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:18:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:18:30}.
After the dawn prayer he sat remembering God until sunrise, saying 'this is my morning provision; were I to miss it my powers would fail'; and his maxim 'through patience and certainty is leadership in religion attained' {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:19:30}.
He derives from him a method of authorship: mastery of the subject first (the speaker advises reading fifty to seventy books before writing), then teaching it before composing, then fine division and indexing — contrasting him with Ibn al-Jawzi: Ibn al-Qayyim the scholar of precise writing, Ibn al-Jawzi nearer the preacher {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:20:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:22:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:23:30}.
He closes: Ibn al-Qayyim follows Ibn Taymiyya in method and principles yet is distinctive in his subjects — and among God's greatest gifts to a scholar is a student who immortalises his views {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:24:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep15 00:24:30}.

## In Ibn Hajar's al-Durar al-Kamina
[[person/ibn-hajar-al-asqalani|Ibn Hajar]] gives an entry for Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr al-Zur'i al-Dimashqi, Shams al-Din Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya the Hanbali: born in 691, he studied law and legal theory with Ibn Taymiyya among others, taught at the Sadriyya and led prayer at the Jawziyya. {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 3/400} {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 3/401} Ibn Hajar says it was he who edited his teacher's books and spread his learning, and that he was imprisoned with him in the Citadel and released after his death. {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 3/401} His works include al-Hady, I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in, Bada'i' al-Fawa'id, Miftah Dar al-Sa'ada and al-Ruh. {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 3/402} He died on 13 Rajab 751. {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 3/403}

## In «Asdaf al-Lu'lu'» by Abu Ishaq al-Heweny
[[person/abu-ishaq-al-heweny|Abu Ishaq al-Heweny]] says that among the first books of fiqh he bought was Ibn al-Qayyim's «Zad al-Ma'ad», which then came out in small fascicles from the Halabi press as he recalls, and he collected four or five of them. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep17 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep17 00:02:30} He relates that when he heard Shaykh Kishk cite a hadith on the merit of Abu Bakr that troubled him, he searched a bookshop next to the mosque for a book on weak and fabricated hadith and found Ibn al-Qayyim's «al-Manar al-Munif», where the hadith stood near the beginning of the virtues of Abu Bakr, judged fabricated. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep14 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep14 00:11:30} He reports from Ibn al-Qayyim that a scholar said Surat Yusuf holds a thousand benefits, and that about a year before travelling to Germany he thought of beginning a tafsir of the sura to reach a thousand. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep17 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep17 00:10:00} He also quotes Ibn al-Qayyim's description of his teacher Ibn Taymiyya's digressive method. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep21 00:09:30}

## In Abd al-Rahman Mansour's programme 'Ahalina'
In the first episode of his programme [[work/ahaleena|'Ahalina']], [[person/abd-al-rahman-mansour|Abd al-Rahman Mansour]] quotes Ibn al-Qayyim, after the verse on the creation of spouses for tranquillity, affection and mercy: God made the wife a place of rest for the husband in which his heart finds calm, and set between the spouses affection, which is pure love, joined with mercy. {cite:assabile-abd-al-rahman-mansour ahaleena/ep01 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-abd-al-rahman-mansour ahaleena/ep01 00:05:00} From this he argues that the marital bond holds a distinctive place in Islam and needs means to preserve it. {cite:assabile-abd-al-rahman-mansour ahaleena/ep01 00:05:00}
