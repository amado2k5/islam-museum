## Summary

Sheikh Mansur (c. 1760 – 13 April 1794), born Ushurma, was a Chechen imam and military commander who led a resistance movement against Russian expansion into the Caucasus from 1785 until his capture in 1791 {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. He is considered the first leader of the North Caucasian resistance to Russian imperialism, and remains a hero of the Chechen and North Caucasian peoples {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}.

## Origins

He was born at Aldi (Aldy), a small village on the Sunzha river in Chechnya near modern Grozny, into a family of poor Chechen farmers of the Elistanzhi clan; his father's name was Shebesse or Shahbaz, and the family had earlier lived at Khattuni {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. His birth name was Ushurma or Uchermak — the TDV encyclopedia gives the form Uçermak, which the Russians rendered Ushurma {cite:tdv-seyh-mansur}. Contemporary Russian and Ottoman sources report that he was illiterate, though he may have had some basic teaching from a local mullah or from scholars in neighbouring Dagestan, then a significant centre of Islamic learning {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. In his youth he tended his neighbours' flocks {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. He married a woman named Checha; the sources divide between a son and two daughters, and two children {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}.

## The call

At about twenty-six he renounced ordinary life, divorced his wife and secluded himself; he related a vision of two mounted messengers from the Prophet Muhammad who tasked him with preaching Islam to the Chechens and nearby peoples {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. In 1784 he adopted the name Mansur — "victorious" — and began to style himself sheikh and imam of the mountain peoples, calling on them to adhere closely to the Sharia and to abandon what he held to be un-Islamic practices, such as customary law (adat), wine and tobacco {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. The egalitarian edge of the message won him the indigent peasants and the marginalised, at a time when Russian expansion was sharpening the gap between commoners and a nobility that often collaborated with the Russians {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. Like earlier leaders who claimed supernatural powers, he often covered his face before his disciples; some Russian sources wrongly claimed he called himself a prophet {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}.

## The war of 1785

In March 1785 two earthquakes shook the region and may have been read as omens {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. Russia sent a force under Colonel Pieri to seize him; it assaulted and destroyed Aldi, and on its march back Mansur's followers ambushed it, killing some 600 Russian soldiers and officers including Pieri himself {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. The victory was read as proof of divine support, and hesitant nobles from Kabarda and Dagestan now joined him {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. In the summer of 1785 his army, reportedly 10,000 strong, besieged Kizlyar but was beaten off by its cannon, failed at Grigoriopolis, and failed again in a second attack on Kizlyar in August; a defeat by Colonel Nagel's contingent in November 1785 cost him his noble allies {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}.

## Circassia, Anapa and captivity

Mansur sought and failed to obtain a truce on any terms short of unconditional surrender, and carried the war west to the Circassians, whose Ottoman garrisons at Anapa and Soğucak now saw in him a means of uniting the tribes {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. He raided successfully across the Kuban before the Russo-Turkish war, but a Russian expedition at the war's opening defeated him repeatedly, and after his last battle on the Urup river on 20 September 1787 he fled to Anapa {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. When Anapa fell in 1791 he was captured and taken to Saint Petersburg, where Catherine the Great inspected him; held first in the Peter and Paul Fortress, he was moved after stabbing a guard during interrogation to the fortress of Shlisselburg, where he died on 13 April 1794 {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en} {cite:tdv-seyh-mansur}.

## Appearance and legacy

Imam Shamil, born three years after Mansur's death, reported from people who had known him that Mansur had a manly, fascinating appearance and, though unable to read or write, an extraordinary gift of speech, and that he was so tall he seemed in a crowd to be sitting astride a horse {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. The historian John F. Baddeley judged that Mansur was the first to preach and lead holy war against the Russians in the Caucasus and the first to teach that in religious reform lay the one chance of preserving their liberty {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. Shamil revered him and credited him with effecting the Muslim revival in the North Caucasus {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. In the 1990s he became a central symbol of Chechen nationalism: Grozny's airport bore his name for a time, as did its main square, and a Chechen unit in the Russo-Ukrainian war, the Sheikh Mansur Battalion, is named after him {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}.

## Faith

Mansur's documented preaching was a strict Sharia programme — adherence to the revealed law, the abandonment of customary law, wine and tobacco {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. Whether he belonged to a Sufi order is the contested question of his biography, and the attestation is recorded here in full. Mustafa Aydın's entry in the TDV Islam Encyclopedia asserts his personal initiation in terms, writing that he "was initiated into the Naqshbandi order in Dagestan" {cite:tdv-seyh-mansur}. The Arabic article likewise states that he was affiliated with the Naqshbandi tariqa {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-ar}, and the Urdu article has him enter a Naqshbandi madrasa in Dagestan, citing Schaefer's history {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-ur}; Wikidata files him with structured religion values of Sunni Islam and Naqshbandi {cite:wikidata-sheikh-mansur}. Against this, the English article, citing Michael Kemper's Encyclopaedia of Islam Three entry, reports that the affiliation is frequently assumed but that there is no evidence of it, and Alexander Knysh's Encyclopaedia of Islam entry remarks that Mansur lacked the Sufi credentials of later Caucasian leaders {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. No text read names an initiating shaykh or a datable act of initiation. Under the museum's rulings, a single contested tradition of this kind does not settle an order, so his faith is recorded as unknown, with the attestation preserved entire.

## Notes for researchers

His birth year is the most disputed fact of his life. The English article infers c. 1760 from his own statement in 1791 that he was a little over thirty {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}; Wikidata carries both 1760 and June 1762, the latter followed by the Urdu article's lead {cite:wikidata-sheikh-mansur} {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-ur}; the TDV encyclopedia records researchers giving 1722 and 1748 {cite:tdv-seyh-mansur}; the Arabic article's infobox gives 1732 while its body gives 1748 {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-ar}; and the earliest dates descend from the eighteenth-century Florentine hoax that identified him with a former Dominican monk, Giovanni Battista Boetti {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en}. His death, by contrast, is unanimous: Shlisselburg, 13 April 1794 {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-en} {cite:wikipedia-sheikh-mansur-ar} {cite:tdv-seyh-mansur} {cite:wikidata-sheikh-mansur}.
