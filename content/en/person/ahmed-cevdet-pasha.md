## Summary
Ahmed Cevdet Pasha (born at Lovech in 1822 – died in Istanbul in May 1895) was an Ottoman scholar, historian, jurist and statesman, chairman of the commission that produced the Mecelle, the first codification of Islamic law as a civil code, author of the twelve-volume Tarih-i Cevdet, and holder in turn of the ministries of justice, education, pious foundations, interior and commerce. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-ar} The Arabic article dates him by the Hijri years 1237–1312, and he was the father of Fatma Aliye, the first woman novelist of Turkish literature. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-ar} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en}

## Career
Ahmed was born in 1822 at Lofça (Lovech) to a Turkish family of notables that had served the state and the faith in military, administrative and religious office; the name "Cevdet" was given him by his tutor Süleyman Fehim Efendi in 1843. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} The articles agree on the year and split on the day: 22 March in the English article, with Wikidata, and 27 March in the Turkish article's lead and infobox alike. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-tr} He began with Arabic grammar under Hafız Ömer Efendi, mufti of Lovech, moved to Istanbul in 1839, studied theology, mathematics, geology and astronomy in its medreses, took the icazet diploma qualifying him for the ilmiye, and studied Persian until in 1844 he was licensed to teach Rumi's Masnavi. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en}

After graduating he met Mustafa Reşid Pasha, who wanted a member of the ulema to teach him enough Islamic law to introduce reforms without immediate open conflict; Cevdet was assigned to Reşid's house, tutored him and his children, and stayed until his patron's death in 1858. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} He directed a school for training teachers for the new secular system in 1850–51 and was chief scribe to the Council on Education; his historical career began in 1852 with the Society of Knowledge, commissioned to write the empire's history from Küçük Kaynarca (1774) to the destruction of the Janissaries (1826), and he served as state chronicler from 1855 to 1861 and as kadı of Galata in 1856. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} On the Council of the Tanzimat he virtually wrote the new landownership and cadastral regulations, and he was the principal author of the regulation that replaced the council in 1861 with the Supreme Council of Judicial Ordinances, of which he became a member. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} He was sent as special agent to Albania in 1861 and as inspector general to Bosnia in 1863–64, settled nomadic tribes at Kozan in 1865, and became governor of Aleppo in 1866 on his official transfer to the scribal service. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en}

In 1868 the Supreme Council was divided into legislative and judicial bodies and Cevdet became chairman of the judicial branch and the first Minister of Justice, writing the legislation that began the secular Nizamiye court system. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} In his last two decades he rotated through ministries — pious foundations in 1873, education in 1874, justice in 1876, interior in 1877, pious foundations in 1878, commerce in 1879 and justice again from 1880 to 1882 in the English article; the Turkish article counts five justice, three education, three pious-foundations, one interior and one commerce-and-agriculture ministries in all. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-tr} He retired for some years to educate his daughters Fatma Aliye and Emine Semiye and to finish his histories, returned as Minister of Justice in 1886, resigned in 1890 after quarrelling with Grand Vizier Yusuf Kamil Pasha, and remained an elder statesman until his death in Constantinople — on 25 May 1895 by the English article and one Wikidata value, 26 May by the Turkish article and the other — and was buried, by the English article, in the graveyard of the Fatih Mosque. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-tr} {cite:wikidata-ahmed-cevdet-pasha}

## Works
His most famous work is the Mecelle, the Ottoman civil code whose commission he chaired after defeating Âli Pasha's push for an imported French civil law: a code based on traditional Islamic law with modifications for the requirements of the time, in 1,851 articles, which occupied him until the last volume appeared in 1876 — some sixteen years — and which remained in force in several Arab states into the early and mid twentieth century. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} His Tarih-i Cevdet, in twelve volumes, abandoned the annalistic approach for problems and topics examined critically from the sources; his Tezakir (memoirs) and Maruzat complete his historical trilogy. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-tr} He also wrote the Kısas-ı Enbiya, a plain-language history of the prophets and of Islam, and the Kavâ'id-i Osmâniyye, counted by the Turkish article the first grammar of Turkish written in Turkish, besides works on law, grammar, linguistics, logic and astronomy, in Turkish, Arabic, Persian, French and Bulgarian. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-tr}

## Positions and criticism
This section sets out positions as reported in the sources; it is not a verdict by the museum.

The English article presents him as a conservative who held military and administrative reform necessary but refused European law as the model, preferring Islamic law: his two-fold answer was to appeal to traditional values through better education and communication, and to create conditions eliminating corruption and raising efficiency while preserving the empire's fundamental political and social concepts. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} It also records that in 1861 the vizierate was denied him because of strong ulema opposition to his enlightened and liberal interpretation of religious matters, and that in 1876, disliked by constitutionalists and government alike, he was sent out of Istanbul as inspector general to Rumelia and then governor of Syria, before becoming close to Abdülhamid II. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} The Arabic article claims he served as Grand Vizier for ten days in 1879 after the dismissal of Khayreddine Pasha; neither the English nor the Turkish article carries the claim, and his fully listed ministerial career in both leaves it unsupported, so this account does not follow it and records it here as a stated discrepancy. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-ar} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en}

On his school — the most delicate question in this record: the English article's religious-biography infobox gives religion Islam, denomination Sunni and jurisprudence Hanafi, the jurisprudence field citing Kızılkaya's 2011 article on the historical evolution of qawāʿid literature. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} But the English article's text nowhere names his personal school; "Hanafi" occurs in that article only in the infobox and in the Hanafi-scholars and Maturidi navboxes at its foot — one article, one family. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en} The Turkish article's text says Cevdet and those who thought like him defended systematising and codifying Hanafi fiqh, a branch of Islamic law, and the Arabic article's text says his draft legislation rested on Hanafi fiqh, against the ideas Âli Pasha favoured; both are attestations about the code he championed — his professional scholarship. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-tr} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-ar} Wikidata carries no religion value for him at all. {cite:wikidata-ahmed-cevdet-pasha} Since the lane rule records a school only when two independent sources name it as the man's own, and leaves doubt unclassified, his faith is recorded as unknown, with every reading preserved in the record's faith note for any future sourced enrichment. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-cevdet-pasha-en}
