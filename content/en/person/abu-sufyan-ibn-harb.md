## Summary
Abu Sufyan ibn Harb led Mecca in its open war against the Muslims, as commander at Uhud and at the Trench. He then embraced Islam on the eve of the conquest and died an elder Muslim. His son founded the Umayyad state.

## Life
A merchant prince of [[group/banu-umayya|Banu Umayya]], he led the caravan whose escape brought about [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]]. He then commanded Mecca's army at [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]] (625 CE) and the confederate coalition at [[event/battle-of-the-trench|the Trench]] (627 CE) {cite:ibn-ishaq-guillaume} {cite:watt-1956-medina}.

When Mecca could no longer hold out, he went out to the Muslims' camp and was granted safe conduct. The tradition preserves the Prophet's saying that whoever enters Abu Sufyan's house is safe. A few weeks later he fought alongside the Muslims at [[event/battle-of-hunayn|Hunayn]], and the tradition says he lost his second eye at al-Ta'if {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

In the Rashidun period he was given a post, and he saw his sons placed in the new order: [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya]] in Syria, and [[person/umm-habiba-bint-abi-sufyan|Umm Habiba]], wife of the Prophet himself. He died about 653 CE, a very old man {cite:tabari-donner-1993}.

## In the Encyclopaedia of Islam

The second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam describes Abu Sufyan ibn Harb ibn Umayya as a prominent merchant and financier of [[place/mecca|Mecca]], of the clan of [[group/banu-abd-shams|Abd Shams]] of [[group/quraysh|Quraysh]]. His name was Sakhr, and his kunya is sometimes given as Abu Hanzala. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 170}

The article says that Abd Shams had once belonged to a political group called the Mutayyabun, which included the clan of Hashim, but that around the time of [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] it drew away and cooperated in some matters with the rival group made up of [[group/banu-makhzum|Makhzum]], [[group/banu-jumah|Jumah]], [[group/banu-sahm|Sahm]] and others. As head of Abd Shams he joined in opposing Muhammad in the years before the hijra, though the article judges that his opposition was less violent than that of [[person/abu-jahl|Abu Jahl]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 170}

He led caravans in person on several occasions. The article singles out 2 AH, 624 CE, when a caravan of a thousand camels coming back from Syria under his command was threatened by Muhammad. The Meccans sent out about a thousand men under Abu Jahl to help. By skilful leadership Abu Sufyan eluded the Muslims, but Abu Jahl was eager to fight. In the battle that followed, his son Hanzala was killed, and his wife [[person/hind-bint-utba|Hind]] lost her father Utba. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 170}

According to the article, Abu Sufyan seems to have been in charge of the preparations to avenge Badr, and he commanded the large army sent against [[place/medina|Medina]] in 3 AH, 625 CE, probably by virtue of a hereditary privilege, the kiyada. It says he wished to attack the main settlement of Medina after Uhud but was prevented by [[person/safwan-ibn-umayya|Safwan ibn Umayya]] of Jumah, possibly out of jealousy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 170}

He also organised the great confederacy that besieged Medina in 5 AH, 627 CE. When this proved a failure, the article suggests, he perhaps lost heart, and resistance to Muhammad in Mecca came to be directed by the leaders of the rival group, Safwan ibn Umayya, [[person/suhayl-ibn-amr|Suhayl ibn Amr]] and [[person/ikrima-ibn-abi-jahl|Ikrima ibn Abi Jahl]]. Abu Sufyan is not mentioned in connection with the peace of [[event/treaty-of-hudaybiyya|al-Hudaybiyya]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 170}

In 8 AH, 630 CE, allies of Quraysh openly broke the peace, and Abu Sufyan went to Medina to negotiate. The article says what happened is not clear but that he possibly reached some understanding with Muhammad. It adds that Muhammad's marriage to his daughter [[person/umm-habiba-bint-abi-sufyan|Umm Habiba]] may have softened his attitude, even though she had spent some fifteen years in [[place/abyssinia|Abyssinia]] as a Muslim. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 170}

When Muhammad marched on Mecca soon afterwards, Abu Sufyan and [[person/hakim-ibn-hizam|Hakim ibn Hizam]] came out and submitted to him, apparently now becoming Muslims, and those who took refuge with Abu Sufyan were guaranteed security. The article concludes that he did much to bring about a peaceful surrender at the [[event/conquest-of-mecca|conquest of Mecca]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 170}

He took part in the battle of Hunayn and the siege of [[place/taif|al-Ta'if]], where he is said to have lost an eye. The article notes that, like the other Meccans, he would have been well aware that [[group/hawazin|Hawazin]] and [[group/thaqif|Thaqif]] were as hostile to Mecca as to Muhammad. In the distribution of the spoils he and Hakim seem to have received an especially large gift in recognition of their services. After al-Ta'if submitted, Abu Sufyan, who had business and family connections there, helped to destroy the idol of [[object/al-lat|al-Lat]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 170}

The article says he was appointed governor of [[place/najran|Najran]] and perhaps also of the [[place/hijaz|Hijaz]], but that it is disputed whether the appointment came from Muhammad or from [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]]. It observes that if he was in Mecca at Muhammad's death and spoke against Abu Bakr, he cannot have been governor of Najran then, and it suggests that the alleged speech, like many statements about him, may be anti-Umayyad propaganda. He was present at the battle of [[event/battle-of-yarmuk|the Yarmuk]], but the article thinks he may have done little more than exhort the younger men, since he was about seventy. He is said to have died about 32 AH, 653 CE, aged about eighty-eight. Of his sons, [[person/yazid-ibn-abi-sufyan|Yazid]] died as a Muslim general in Palestine about 18 AH, 639 CE, and Mu'awiya was the first Umayyad caliph. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 170}

## For young visitors
Abu Sufyan fought the Muslims for six years. Then he made peace, joined them, and lived to see his son become a caliph.

## The Rabigh detachment (The Sealed Nectar, p.198)
[[person/ubayda-ibn-al-harith|Ubayda ibn al-Harith]] met him at Batn Rabigh with two hundred men, and no fighting took place ([[event/expedition-of-rabigh|the Rabigh detachment]]) {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.198}.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lecture series Al Dinu Al Qayem

Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy mentions Abu Sufyan ibn Harb in several places in the series. He recounts Abu Sufyan's remark that he never saw anyone loved by others as the Companions loved Muhammad {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep02 00:43:00}, and that a word from Abu Sufyan steadied the people at Hunayn {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep03 00:46:30}.

He tells that Hind bint Utba complained to the Prophet that Abu Sufyan was somewhat stingy, and the Prophet told her to take from his wealth in a fair manner {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep09 01:07:00}.

In his account of Badr, he says Abu Sufyan secured the caravan by taking another route and succeeded, while Abu Jahl pressed on toward Badr {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep10 00:03:30}.

In another account of the enemy camp, Abu Sufyan called out to the men, asking each for his name and tribe, and suspicion spread among them {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep10 00:16:30}. He recounts that Hudhayfa was within a spear's reach of Abu Sufyan and held back, because the Prophet had charged him to create no disturbance among them {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep10 00:17:00}.
## For Sprouts
Abu Sufyan Sakhr ibn Harb was a chief of Quraysh who led its caravans and armies against the Muslims at Badr and the Trench. He accepted Islam at the conquest of Mecca, witnessed Hunayn and Ta'if with the Muslims, lost his eye at the Yarmuk, and died an old man in Medina.

## For Explorers
Abu Sufyan Sakhr ibn Harb al-Umawi was a chief and trader of Quraysh who led Quraysh's great caravan — the cause of Badr — and carried the polytheists' banner at Uhud and the Trench. He stood at the head of the first feud against the call, then accepted Islam in 8 AH just before the conquest of Mecca — some say willingly, some by circumstance — and the Prophet received him and told the Meccans that whoever entered Abu Sufyan's house was safe. He witnessed Hunayn with the Muslims and was given from the share of 'those whose hearts are to be reconciled'; at the Yarmuk he lost his second eye, having lost the first earlier. He was Mu'awiya's father and grandfather of Yazid and Mu'awiya II, and died an old man in Medina around 32 AH.

## For Scholars
Abu Sufyan Sakhr ibn Harb ibn Umayya al-Umawi was a chief and merchant of Quraysh and the most visible leader of the feud against the call in its two decades. The sira books record him as commander of Quraysh's great caravan — whose escape caused the march to Badr — and as banner-bearer of the polytheists at Uhud and the Trench and leader of the Confederates; his stand against the call is the frame history records before his Islam. He accepted Islam in 8 AH at the stage before the conquest of Mecca — the reports differ on how willing he was — and the Prophet received him and told the Meccans that whoever entered Abu Sufyan's house was safe, appointing him over some affairs in some accounts. He witnessed Hunayn and received shares of the reconciled hearts' money, and at the Yarmuk lost his second eye after losing the first earlier. Father of Mu'awiya, founder of the Umayyad state, and grandfather of Yazid and Mu'awiya II, he died an old man in Medina around 32 AH. Historians read him as a model of Qurashi statesmen who moved from leading the feud into building Islamic rule.

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "Secrets of Prophetic Leadership"
In his programme [[work/asrar-al-qiyada-al-nabawiyya-program|"Secrets of Prophetic Leadership"]], [[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] takes Abu Sufyan as his example of "the supporting follower": high leadership ability, no challenge after his conversion, and limited effectiveness. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan asrar-al-qyada-al-nbawya/ep07 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan asrar-al-qyada-al-nbawya/ep07 00:30:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan asrar-al-qyada-al-nbawya/ep07 00:33:00} Leadership ability, he says, is measured apart from faith and morals; after Badr, when the chiefs of Quraysh were killed and Abu Lahab died days later, Abu Sufyan remained Quraysh's sole leader. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan asrar-al-qyada-al-nbawya/ep07 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan asrar-al-qyada-al-nbawya/ep07 00:11:00} He cites Abu Sufyan's exchange with Heraclius during the Hudaybiyya truce as a model of analytical questioning. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan asrar-al-qyada-al-nbawya/ep07 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan asrar-al-qyada-al-nbawya/ep07 00:21:00} The Prophet's remark that Abu Sufyan "loves pride" is no blame, he holds; what is blameworthy is despising people and rejecting the truth. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan asrar-al-qyada-al-nbawya/ep07 00:23:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan asrar-al-qyada-al-nbawya/ep07 00:24:00} He relates that Abu Sufyan was at Yarmuk urging the men to stand firm, and gives his age then as 76. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan asrar-al-qyada-al-nbawya/ep07 00:32:30} In episode 25 he makes him the example of "the frustrated" in [[concept/al-qiyada-al-mawqifiyya|situational leadership]]: high competence that found no fitting role after his conversion. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan asrar-al-qyada-al-nbawya/ep25 00:33:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan asrar-al-qyada-al-nbawya/ep25 00:34:00}
