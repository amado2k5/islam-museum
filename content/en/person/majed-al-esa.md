## Summary
Majed al-Esa is a Saudi music-video producer and filmmaker, co-founder of the Riyadh production house 8ies Studio, best known for the viral videos Hwages (2016) and Salma (2017), which drew hundreds of millions of views and made him one of the most discussed young directors in the kingdom's emerging entertainment scene. The Muslim 500 lists him under Arts and Culture for a body of short online films that brought questions about Saudi women's lives to a mass audience. {cite:themuslim500-majed-al-esa}

## 8ies Studio and the new Saudi screen
Al-Esa emerged in the mid-2010s through 8ies Studio, a small Riyadh outfit producing short, polished videos for YouTube at a moment when Saudi Arabia had no commercial cinemas — cinemas were banned until 2018 — and online video was the principal space where young Saudi filmmakers could build audiences. His work, marked by stylised choreography, deadpan humour and everyday settings, arrived as the kingdom's entertainment sector was beginning its rapid state-backed expansion. {cite:themuslim500-majed-al-esa}

## Hwages
Hwages, released at the end of 2016, is the video that made his name. Built around a folkloric chant — the title evokes a women's wedding dance — it shows three niqab-wearing women stepping out of a car driven by a boy, then skating, dancing and playing street sports while lyrics address the constraints placed on women. It passed a hundred million views, was picked up by international media from the BBC to American television, and was read simultaneously as satire of Saudi restrictions and, by some viewers, as gentle state-aligned messaging in the reforming climate of Vision 2030. Al-Esa himself described it as a work about women's issues made for discussion rather than as a political manifesto. {cite:themuslim500-majed-al-esa}

## Salma and later work
In 2017 he followed with Salma, a stylised video referencing reincarnation that again circulated widely. His later output continued in the same register of short, high-production online films, making him part of the first generation of Saudi directors whose reputations were built entirely on internet release. His videos' cumulative views were counted by the Muslim 500 in the hundreds of millions. {cite:themuslim500-majed-al-esa}

## Reception and notes on this record
Reception of his work has been divided between readings of it as sharp social satire smuggled past censors and readings of it as officially tolerated soft messaging; Saudi commentators have themselves been split, and the museum reports these views attributed, without verdict. This record's sourcing is thin — it rests on the Muslim 500 profile and press coverage of the videos rather than on a full independent biography — and that thinness is stated on the record. No source read states his birth year or names a religious school, and this record records neither. {cite:themuslim500-majed-al-esa}

## Context
Al-Esa's career illustrates the particular conditions in which a Saudi visual culture formed online: a young population with among the world's highest YouTube consumption, a censorship regime that channelled creative work onto the internet, and then, after 2016, a state programme to build an entertainment industry almost from scratch. Within that setting, his short films operated as both entertainment and as a venue where questions that newspapers addressed cautiously — women's mobility, dress, leisure — could be played out in choreographed ambiguity, leaving viewers to argue about what they had just watched. {cite:themuslim500-majed-al-esa}
