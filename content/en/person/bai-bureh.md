## Summary
Bai Bureh (15 February 1840 – 24 August 1908) was a Sierra Leonean ruler, military strategist and Muslim cleric who led the Temne and Loko uprising against British rule in 1898 in northern Sierra Leone, as the English article describes him, with the French article and Wikidata agreeing on his birth and death dates and on Kasseh as the place of both. {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en} {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-fr} {cite:wikidata-bai-bureh} He led the Hut Tax War of 1898, holding off the British for months with guerrilla tactics before his surrender on 11 November 1898 and exile to the Gold Coast; he was brought back in 1905 and reinstated as chief of Kasseh until his death, and is remembered today as Sierra Leone's greatest hero. {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en}

## Early life
Bai Bureh was born in 1840 in Kasseh, a village near Port Loko in northern Sierra Leone; his father was a Muslim cleric and an important Loko chief and his mother a Temne trader from Makeni, in the English article's account, while the French article calls the father an ulama of the Loko engaged in the practice of Islam. {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en} {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-fr} As a young man his father sent him to the village of Gbendembu in northern Sierra Leone to be trained as a warrior; there he showed himself a formidable fighter and was given the nickname Kebalai, "one who doesn't tire of war", and on his return to his home village he was crowned ruler of Kasseh. {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en} The Sierra Leone Web biography adds that in his youth he served under a Soso ruler in a long jihad to establish correct Islamic practices, and dates his crowning as ruler of Kasseh, and his taking of the title Bai Bureh, to 1886. {cite:sierra-leone-web-heroes}

## Career
During the 1860s and 1870s Bureh became the top warrior of Port Loko and of the entire northern Sierra Leone, fighting and winning wars against villagers and tribal leaders opposed to his plan to establish correct Islamic and indigenous practices throughout the north; in 1882 he fought the Susu from French Guinea who had invaded Kambia, defeated them, pushed them back into French Guinea and returned the land to the local Kambia people, and after several major victories he was crowned chief of Northern Sierra Leone in 1886. {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en} As a ruler he never wanted to co-operate with the colonial government in Freetown: he refused to recognise a peace treaty the British had negotiated with the Limba without his participation, and on one occasion his fighters raided across the border into French Guinea. {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en} When the colonial government instituted the hut tax from 1 January 1893, Bureh opposed it, arguing that his people should not be compelled to pay tribute to foreign rulers and insisting that local chiefs should settle their own affairs without British control; after he refused to pay on several occasions a warrant was issued for his arrest, Governor Frederic Cardew offered one hundred pounds for his capture, and Bureh reciprocated by offering five hundred pounds for the capture of the governor, declaring war on the British in Sierra Leone in 1898 — the war that became known as the Hut Tax War of 1898. {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en} Most of his fighters came from Temne and Loko villages under his command, with others sent from Limba, Kissi and Kuranko villages; on 19 February 1898 his forces completely severed the lines of communication between Freetown and Port Loko, blocking road and river, and he held the advantage over the colonial government's forces for several months of a war that ultimately cost hundreds of casualties on both sides. {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en} He finally surrendered on 11 November 1898, tracked down in swampy, thickly vegetated countryside near Port Loko by a small patrol of the newly organised West African Regiment; taken under guard to Freetown, where crowds gathered around his quarters day and night to glimpse him, he was treated as a political prisoner. {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en} The colonial government exiled him to the Gold Coast (now Ghana) along with the Sherbro chief Kpana Lewis and the Mende chief Nyagua; both of them died in exile, but Bai Bureh was brought back to Sierra Leone in 1905 and reinstated as chief of Kasseh, and he died in 1908 — on 24 August, by the leads of both articles and Wikidata. {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en} {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-fr} {cite:wikidata-bai-bureh}

## Legacy
The significance of his war lies not in its outcome but in the fact that a man with no formal military training resisted the British for several months; the tactics he employed were forerunners of guerrilla warfare worldwide, grounded in his expert knowledge of the terrain and joined to a sense of humour that survived into the counter-reward he offered for the governor. {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en} There is a very large statue of him in central Freetown, his portrait appears on Sierra Leone's one-leone banknote issued in 2022, a professional football club in Port Loko bears the name Bai Bureh Warriors, and the only known photograph of him — discovered for sale on eBay in August 2012 by a former Peace Corps volunteer and his colleague — went on display in the Sierra Leone National Museum in 2013. {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en}

## Positions and criticism
This section is read as a presentation of positions reported from the sources, not as a verdict of the museum.

The English article records that his men also killed dozens of Creoles living in northern Sierra Leone because the indigenous people thought they supported the colonial government, most notably the trader John "Johnny" Taylor, killed in his house; the article reports it within its war narrative without justification, and the museum reports it as transmitted. {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en} The sources differ in two details, which the museum states rather than merges: the Sierra Leone Web biography dates his crowning at Kasseh, and his taking of the Bai Bureh title, to 1886, where the English and French articles crown him at Kasseh on his return from training and date his crowning as chief of all northern Sierra Leone to 1886; and his father is a "Loko war-chief" in the popular biography but a Muslim cleric and ulama in the two articles. {cite:sierra-leone-web-heroes} {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en} {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-fr} His faith is recorded as unknown as to school and order in his data record: the sources independently attest his Islam and describe him as a Muslim cleric and religious guide, but none of them names a madhhab or a tariqa for him, and the broadly Maliki character of Sierra Leonean Islam is the milieu he lived in, not an attestation of the man's own school. {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-en} {cite:wikipedia-bai-bureh-fr} {cite:sierra-leone-web-heroes}
