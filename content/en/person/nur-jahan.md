## Overview
Nur Jahan was the name given to Mihr al-Nisa', the wife of the Mughal emperor [[person/jahangir-mughal|Jahangir]]. She was born at Kandahar in 985 AH, 1577 CE, while her father [[person/mirza-ghiyas-beg|Ghiyath Beg]] was migrating from Persia to Hindustan {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 135}.

## First marriage
The Encyclopaedia of Islam article says that under Akbar she married Ali Quli Beg, a Persian who had given distinguished military service and was known as Sher Afkan for his bravery. It adds that opinion differed about his killing: some saw a repeat of the story of David and Uriah, others thought he had been suspected of disloyalty {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 135} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 136}.

## Marriage to Jahangir
Four years later, in 1020 AH, 1611 CE, she became Jahangir's wife at the age of thirty-four, and in the eleventh year of his reign her name was changed from Nur Mahall to Nur Jahan {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 136}.

## Her influence at court
The article describes her as extraordinarily beautiful, well read in Persian literature in an age when few women were, ambitious and masterful, so that she dominated her husband until he was king in name only. Chroniclers record that she sometimes sat in the window of public appearance, that coins were struck in her name and that she even issued farmans {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 136}. It says she set the fashion in dress and is said to have invented a special kind of rose-water {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 136}. Helped in politics by her father I'timad al-Dawla and her brother Asaf Khan, she dispensed patronage, which brought her into conflict with the older nobles led by [[person/mahabat-khan|Mahabat Khan]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 136}.

## Her last years
The article says the last years of Jahangir's reign are the history of her efforts to pave the way for her son-in-law's succession, that the death of her father and Asaf Khan's support for his own son-in-law, Prince Khurram, weakened her, and that at Jahangir's death in 1037 AH, 1627 CE, Asaf Khan outwitted her, her candidate was defeated and Khurram became Shah Jahan {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 136}. It adds that the Mughal historians record little of the last eighteen years of her life, under Shah Jahan {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 136}.
