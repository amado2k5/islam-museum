## Overview
Nasreddin Hoca, whom the editor of Nawadir Juha al-Kubra calls Shaykh Nasr al-Din Juha al-Rumi, is the man of the anecdotes in Anatolia, and the Turks attribute the Juha stories to him. Everything on this page about his life is taken from the introduction to that edition, and the editor himself points out that his dates are confused {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 6-8}.

## His life as the editor tells it
He was a Turk of Anatolia, born in Sivrihisar and died in Akşehir. He studied the religious sciences in Akşehir and Konya, served as a judge in districts near Akşehir, then as preacher in Sivrihisar, was appointed teacher and imam in several towns, and travelled in the provinces of Konya, Ankara and Bursa {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 7}. The editor describes him as a preacher who put his counsel in the form of anecdotes, bold before emirs, judges and rulers; an ascetic who ploughed his own land and cut his own firewood; and a man whose house was a stopping place for strangers and peasants {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 7}.

## His time and the conflicting dates
The editor thinks it most likely that he lived from the reign of Sultan Orhan into that of Sultan Yıldırım Bayezid, and then says he died in 673 AH aged about sixty; the two statements do not agree, and he leaves them as two reports {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 7}. He quotes a Turkish writer who visited the tomb in the great cemetery of Akşehir and read the number 386 on the stone, was puzzled, and concluded that the number was carved backwards and should be read 683 {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 8}. The editor also records that his mediation saved his town from Timur, a story that is told but does not fit the death date given {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 7}. The museum's card dates him to about 1208–1284 CE from the Turkish Encyclopaedia of Islam.

## His tomb and the customs of Akşehir
The source describes his tomb in Akşehir as a dome on four columns, renewed in recent times; it says the townspeople laugh a great deal at his grave, and that a bride and groom begin by visiting the tomb and inviting the shaykh to the wedding {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 8}. These are local customs reported by the source; the museum describes them and does not endorse them.

## Between the Arab Juha and the Juha of Rum
The editor reports that common people claim Nasreddin is the Juha proverbial for heedlessness, and that he is not; that the Turks gave him the Arab Juha's stories and added to them, and that his anecdotes were then translated into Arabic, so the two bodies of stories mixed {cite:juha-nawadir-kubra-djuwaydi p. 5-6}. Nawadir Juha al-Kubra itself contains anecdotes set in Akşehir and Sivrihisar and with Timur, which belong to Nasreddin's legacy; the Juha and Islamic Folklore Browser lists them with the rest.
