## Summary
Plato of Tivoli (Plato Tiburtinus) was a translator from Tivoli who flourished at [[place/barcelona|Barcelona]] in the first half of the twelfth century; his is the surviving Latin translation of [[work/al-zij-al-sabi|al-Battani's Zij]]. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 65}

## His translation of al-Battani
The year he finished it is unknown; Nallino holds it was certainly in the first half of the twelfth century, when Plato was at Barcelona. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 65} In his preface Plato says he found [[person/al-battani|al-Battani]] among the Arabs to be a perfect follower of [[person/batlamus|Ptolemy]], compressing his prolixity and correcting his errors — which are very rare and which al-Battani imputes not to Ptolemy but to Hipparchus' root. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 34} [[work/de-motu-stellarum-plato-tiburtinus|His translation]] was first printed at Nuremberg in 1537. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 59}

## Later judgement on it
Halley judged that Plato was neither skilled enough in the languages nor trained in astronomy, so that his version is obscure, in barbarous Latin, and senseless in many places; Nallino adds that this holds for nearly all medieval translations. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 65} Like other translators of his age, Plato kept the Arabic article in proper and geographical names — hence the very form "Albategnius". {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 39} Even so, Nallino finds that Plato's exemplar and the Escorial manuscript derive from one archetype, and that Plato has a few words missing from the Escorial text. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 71}

## His other translations
Boncompagni credits him with the Spherics of Theodosius from Arabic; the Propositions of Almansor, which he says he completed at Barcelona on 18 Dhu al-Hijja 530, mid-September 1136; Abu al-Qasim ibn al-Saffar's book on the use of the astrolabe; and Savasorda's Liber embadorum from Hebrew. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 65} Steinschneider also gave him the version of Ahmad ibn Yusuf ibn al-Daya's commentary on the pseudo-Ptolemaic Centiloquium, which Nallino restores to Plato's friend John of Seville. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 66}
