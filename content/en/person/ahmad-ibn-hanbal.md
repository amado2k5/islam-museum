## Summary
Imam Ahmad authored no book of fiqh; the most he wrote on it was a letter on prayer, written to an imam who prayed behind him and erred in his prayer {cite:madkhal-ibn-badran-turki p. 124}. He disliked writing down books containing branching questions and opinion, holding that engaging with transmitted reports came first {cite:madkhal-ibn-badran-turki p. 123}. His companions wrote down more than thirty volumes of his words and fatwas {cite:madkhal-ibn-badran-turki p. 124}.

## The Musnad
The book mentions that the Musnad contains thirty thousand hadiths {cite:madkhal-ibn-badran-turki p. 104}. It transmits from his son Abdullah a report saying his father gathered it from more than seven hundred thousand hadiths, and that whatever the Muslims differed on of the Messenger of God's hadith is referred back to it {cite:madkhal-ibn-badran-turki p. 104}. The book's margin discusses the attribution of this report to him {cite:madkhal-ibn-badran-turki p. 104}.

## The creeds
In its first division the book presents the creeds transmitted from Imam Ahmad {cite:madkhal-ibn-badran-turki p. 47}.

## In the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam describes him as the imam of Baghdad, a celebrated theologian, jurist and traditionist who lived from 164 to 241 AH, 780 to 855 CE, and as one of the most vigorous personalities of Islam, whose influence reached both its historical development and its modern revival {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 294}. The article presents him as the founder of the [[group/hanbali-madhhab|Hanbali school]], one of the four major Sunni schools, and says that through his later disciple [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] he stands as the distant progenitor of Wahhabism and, to a degree, an inspiration of the conservative Salafiyya reform movement {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 294}. These are the article's own characterisations of his legacy.

### Family and early life
According to the article, he was an Arab of the Banu Shayban of Rabi'a, a tribe that had played an active part in the conquest of Iraq and Khurasan; the family first lived in [[place/basra|Basra]] and later moved to Marw, and the article mentions a grandfather who governed Sarakhs under the Umayyads and was among the early Abbasid propagandists {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 294}. He was born in 164 AH, 780 CE, a few months after his father Muhammad ibn Hanbal, who served in the army of Khurasan, had settled in [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]]; the father died three years later {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 294}. Ahmad inherited a small family estate that gave him a modest but independent livelihood {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 294}.

### Studies and journeys
After studying lexicography, jurisprudence and tradition in Baghdad, he devoted himself from 179 AH, 795 CE, to the study of tradition and travelled for it in Iraq, the Hijaz, Yemen and Syria; the article dismisses reports of visits to Iran, Khurasan and the Maghrib as legendary {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 294}. He was already in [[place/kufa|Kufa]] in 183, went to Basra in 186 and again in 190, 194 and 200, and made the pilgrimage to [[place/mecca|Mecca]] five times, in 187, 191, 196, 197 and 198, with periods of pious retreat at [[place/medina|Medina]] after the pilgrimage of 197 and into 199, after which he visited the traditionist [[person/abd-al-razzaq-al-sanani|Abd al-Razzaq]] at [[place/sanaa|Sanaa]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 294}.

He studied under a great many teachers. In Baghdad he studied regularly with [[person/hushaym-ibn-bashir|Hushaym ibn Bashir]], a disciple of [[person/ibrahim-al-nakhai|Ibrahim al-Nakha'i]], from 179 to 183 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 294}. His principal teacher afterwards was [[person/sufyan-ibn-uyayna|Sufyan ibn Uyayna]], whom the article calls the greatest authority of the Hijaz school; other important teachers were [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-mahdi|Abd al-Rahman ibn Mahdi]] of Basra and [[person/waki-ibn-al-jarrah|Waki' ibn al-Jarrah]] of Kufa {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 294}. Citing Ibn Taymiyya, the article says his juristic formation came above all from the schools of hadith and of the Hijaz, so that he should not simply be regarded as a disciple of [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]], whose legal work he knew at least in part but whom he seems to have met only once, in Baghdad in 195 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 294}.

### The inquisition
Towards the end of his reign the caliph [[person/abd-allah-al-mamun|al-Ma'mun]], under the influence of [[person/bishr-ibn-ghiyath-al-marisi|Bishr al-Marisi]], gave official support to the doctrine of the [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazila]], which opened for Ibn Hanbal a period of persecution; this is the episode known as the [[event/mihna-of-the-quran|mihna]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 294}. He refused to accept the doctrine that the Qur'an was created, which the article calls contrary to orthodoxy. Al-Ma'mun, then at [[place/tarsus|Tarsus]], ordered him sent to him together with another objector, Muhammad ibn Nuh; the two were put in chains, but soon after leaving [[place/raqqa|Raqqa]] they heard that the caliph had died {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 294} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}. They were sent back to Baghdad; Ibn Nuh died on the journey, and Ibn Hanbal was imprisoned in turn at the Yasiriyya, in a house of the Dar Umara and in the common prison of the Darb al-Mawsili {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}.

The new caliph, [[person/al-mutasim|al-Mu'tasim]], was inclined to abandon the inquisition but was, the article says, persuaded by the Mu'tazili judge [[person/ahmad-ibn-abi-duad|Ahmad ibn Abi Du'ad]] that giving up an officially adopted position would endanger the authority of the state {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}. Ibn Hanbal was summoned before the caliph in Ramadan 219, still refused to acknowledge that the Qur'an was created, and was severely beaten; he was then allowed to go home after about two years of imprisonment in all {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}.

### Retirement, return and death
Throughout al-Mu'tasim's reign he lived in retirement and stopped giving lectures on tradition. When [[person/al-wathiq|al-Wathiq]] acceded in 227 AH he tried to resume teaching but soon preferred to stop, though not formally forbidden, to avoid exposure to the Mu'tazili judges, and he is said at times to have stayed in hiding {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}. After [[person/al-mutawakkil|al-Mutawakkil]] restored Sunnism on his accession in 232, he could teach again; the article notes that he is not listed among the traditionists the caliph appointed in 234 to oppose the Jahmiyya and the Mu'tazila {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}. Ibn Abi Du'ad was removed from office in 237, and in some traditions his successor Ibn Aktham was recommended to the caliph by Ibn Hanbal {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}.

In 237 al-Mutawakkil invited him to [[place/samarra|Samarra]]. The article suggests the caliph wanted him to teach hadith to a young prince and perhaps to use his fame for a policy of restoring the sunna; the surviving narratives show him welcomed by the chamberlain Wasif, lodged in the palace of Itakh, loaded with gifts and presented to al-Mu'tazz, but excused at his own request from any special charge because of his age and health, and he returned to Baghdad without seeing the caliph {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}.

The article gives his death as 241 AH, 855 CE, at the age of 75, after a short illness, and says he was buried in the Martyrs' cemetery near the Harb gate; other sources in this record give his age as seventy-seven. It judges the accounts of his funeral partly legendary but conveying genuine popular emotion, and adds that his tomb drew such devotion that the authorities guarded the cemetery and that it became one of the most visited places of pilgrimage in Baghdad {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}. In 574 AH, 1178 CE, the caliph [[person/al-mustadi-billah|al-Mustadi]] gave it an inscription praising him as the most faithful defender of the Sunna; a Tigris flood washed the tomb away in the 8th century AH {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}.

### Sons
By each of two legitimate wives he had one son, Salih and Abd Allah, besides six children by a concubine who are not otherwise known {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}. [[person/salih-ibn-ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Salih]], born in Baghdad in 203, died as judge of Isfahan in 266 and is said to have transmitted a large part of his father's fiqh {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}. [[person/abdullah-ibn-ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Abd Allah]], born in 213 and died in Baghdad in 290, was chiefly interested in hadith, and through him most of his father's literary work was transmitted; veneration passed to his tomb when the father's was swept away {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}. The article counts both sons among the chief architects of the collective structure that is the Hanbali madhhab {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}.

## The Musnad and other works according to the Encyclopaedia of Islam
His most celebrated work is the collection of traditions known as the Musnad. The article says that Ahmad himself gave it exceptional importance, but that his son Abd Allah collected and classified the enormous mass of material and added to it, and that the Baghdad disciple Abu Bakr al-Qati'i transmitted this recension with further additions {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}. The traditions are arranged not by subject, as in the Sahihs of al-Bukhari and Muslim, but under the name of the first transmitter, so that the work is a series of individual musnads, from Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali and the leading Companions to the Ansar, the Meccans, the Medinans, the people of Kufa and Basra, and the Syrians {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}. The article calls this order evidence of intellectual probity but hard to use for anyone who did not know the work by heart, which led later scholars such as [[person/ibn-kathir|Ibn Kathir]] to rearrange its contents alphabetically by Companion {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 295}.

Other works named in the article are two short doctrinal treatises, a refutation of the Jahmiyya and the heretics and the Kitab al-Sunna, in which he restates and defines the main points of his creed, and a treatise on prayer stressing communal observance, transmitted by Muhanna ibn Yahya al-Shami {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 296}. The Kitab al-Wara' collects his opinions on cases where scrupulousness seems called for; its reporter Abu Bakr al-Marwazi added the views of other scholars, apparently to show that Ibn Hanbal's teaching on piety and asceticism compares well with that of [[person/ibn-adham-ibrahim|Ibrahim ibn Adham]], [[person/al-fudayl-ibn-iyad|al-Fudayl ibn Iyad]] and [[person/dhu-al-nun-al-misri|Dhu al-Nun al-Misri]]; the article adds that the work is quoted extensively by [[person/abu-talib-al-makki|Abu Talib al-Makki]] and taken up again by [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 296}.

### The Masa'il and the collection of his opinions
He was constantly consulted on questions of doctrine, ethics and law. The article says that, whether or not he forbade writing down his answers as formally as some reports claim, he warned questioners against a codification of his thinking that might replace the guidance of the Qur'an and the Sunna, and unlike al-Shafi'i he never presented it systematically; it reads the aim of his teaching as a reaction against the codification of fiqh {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 296}. His responsa were written down and arranged under the headings of fiqh by Salih, Abd Allah and other disciples, among them [[person/ishaq-ibn-mansur-al-kawsaj|Ishaq ibn Mansur al-Kawsaj]], [[person/al-athram|al-Athram]], [[person/hanbal-ibn-ishaq|Hanbal ibn Ishaq]] and [[person/abu-dawud-al-sijistani|Abu Dawud al-Sijistani]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 296}. These materials were gathered in the Kitab al-Jami' by [[person/abu-bakr-al-khallal|Abu Bakr al-Khallal]], which the article reports as twenty volumes and now lost apart from a fragment; Ibn Taymiyya called al-Khallal's Kitab al-Sunna the fullest source for Ibn Hanbal's doctrinal views {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 296}. Because of the divergences in this corpus, the Hanbalis distinguish between the text of the founder, the teachings ascribed to him, the indications suggested by him and the mere views of his disciples {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 296}.

## Doctrine in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The article notes that Hanbalism has often been judged harshly by opponents and by Western orientalism, which have seen in it an anthropomorphist theology, a sectarian traditionalism and a spirit of intolerance, and it says that a direct study of Ibn Hanbal's works does not support these summary judgements {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 296} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 297}. What follows is the article's own account of his positions.

### God's attributes and the Qur'an
For him, the article says, belief in God is belief in the description God gave of Himself in the Qur'an, so that attributes such as hearing, sight, speech and will, and the ambiguous terms about God's hand, throne and the vision of Him by believers, are affirmed as realities {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 297}. He also holds that God is not comparable to anything created, rejects both negation of attributes and allegorical interpretation, and rejects the anthropomorphism of the Mushabbiha, including the Jahmiyya among them as unconscious anthropomorphists {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 297}. One believes without asking how, bila kayf, and renounces the subtleties of [[concept/ilm-al-kalam|dogmatic theology]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 297}. The article adds that [[person/al-ashari|al-Ash'ari]], on leaving Mu'tazilism, sought to place himself under Ibn Hanbal's patronage, either for tactical reasons or in sincere acceptance {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 297}.

For him the [[concept/kalam-allah|Qur'an]] is the uncreated word of God, and merely saying it is the word of God without further specification is, in his view, the heresy of the Waqifa, which he judged graver than that of the Jahmiyya {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 297}. On the pronunciation of the Qur'an the article finds his position hard to define: some traditions say he held it uncreated, while in the Kitab al-Sunna he goes no further than calling a Jahmi anyone who says our recitation is created, and he condemns the Lafziyya without a positive formula of his own, which embarrassed later Hanbalis {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 297}.

### Method and sources of law
Unlike al-Shafi'i he wrote no treatise on legal methodology, and the article warns that the later works of his school do not rigorously express his thought {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 297}. His doctrine rests on the Qur'an read literally and on the [[concept/sunna|Sunna]]; by his own statement in the Musnad he aimed to gather the hadiths generally accepted in his time, including sound ones and those that benefit from a presumption of authenticity {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 297}. The article reports that only much later, with Ibn al-Jawzi's formalist rigour, was he reproached with admitting fabricated hadiths, an accusation contested by Ibn Taymiyya and [[person/ibn-hajar-al-asqalani|Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani]], and says the prevailing opinion finds sound, good and rare traditions in the Musnad but none strictly unacceptable {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 297}.

A third, derived source is the fatwas of the Companions, who understood and practised the Qur'an and the Sunna better than later generations; where they differ, the juster view is found by reference to the Qur'an and the Sunna {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 297}. He ranked Abu Bakr first, then Umar, then the six men of the consultative council, and then the fighters of Badr, the Emigrants and the Ansar; the article says this acknowledges the standing and legitimacy of Ali while also rehabilitating his opponents, first of all Mu'awiya {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 297}. The sayings of the leading Successors are also weighed, while [[concept/ijma|consensus]], on this view, expresses a concentration around a truth founded on the Qur'an and Sunna and is not in itself an independent source of law {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 298}.

For the mufti, the article says, the first duty is to follow the legacy of the early generations and avoid innovation, so Ibn Hanbal condemns gratuitous personal opinion, ra'y, without demanding passivity before the texts; he does not reject [[concept/qiyas|analogy]] but does not value it as a tool of legal system-building as Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn al-Qayyim later did {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 298}. He made wide use of istishab, the maintenance of a legal status until a new circumstance changes it, and of dhara'i, by which whatever is indispensable to a command or leads to a prohibited act is likewise commanded or prohibited; the article adds that the notion of public interest, [[concept/al-maslaha-al-mursala|maslaha]], also accords with his doctrine, though he did not develop it as Ibn Taymiyya and [[person/najm-al-din-al-tufi|al-Tufi]] did {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 298}. Citing [[person/ibn-qayyim-al-jawziyya|Ibn al-Qayyim]], it explains that the great Hanbalis never called for reopening [[concept/ijtihad|ijtihad]] because they held that its continual use was needed to apply legal doctrine {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 298}.

### Politics and community
In political matters, directed in the article's reading against the Kharijites and the Shi'a, he affirmed the legitimacy of the Qurayshi caliphate and said no one may contest it or rebel against it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 298}. In the Shu'ubiyya dispute of his day he defended the Arabs without claiming superiority for them {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 298}. He founded the legality of a caliph's naming of a successor on the precedents of Abu Bakr and Umar, provided the designation is followed by a pledge of allegiance, [[concept/bayah|bay'a]], and he left the imam wide freedom to act for the common good, in which the article sees the germ of the concept of [[concept/siyasa-shariyya|siyasa shar'iyya]] later developed by Ibn Aqil, Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn al-Qayyim {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 298}.

Believers owe the imam obedience whatever his moral quality, and [[concept/jihad|jihad]], the Friday prayer and the pilgrimage are to be performed with rulers good or bad; but if a ruler commands disobedience to God he is to be refused on that point, without armed revolt while he keeps up the prayer, and each member of the community must command the good and forbid the evil according to knowledge and means {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 298}. The article describes his policy as one of communal concentration, opposing disunity, fitna, with unity of the group, jama'a, and says that on [[concept/takfir|excommunication]] he adopted a tolerance akin to the [[group/murjia|Murji'a]]: only a narrow set of acts brought exclusion, and in other cases he replaced it by refusing to associate with the heretical, for instance by not praying behind innovators {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 298}.

### Ethics and religious practice
The article calls his doctrine entirely dominated by ethical concerns, with action aimed at the service of God {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 298}. Against the Jahmiyya and the Murji'a he held that [[concept/iman|faith]] is word, act, intention and attachment to the Sunna, that it can increase and diminish, and that one should say one is a believer with the conditional "if God wills" {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 298} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 299}. Faith thus implies a system of moral convictions: sincerity, renunciation, courage, fear of God and a scrupulous mind that avoids doubtful matters, [[concept/wara|wara']] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 299}.

The article singles out, citing Ibn Taymiyya, a rule it finds characteristic of early Hanbalism: only the religious practices God has explicitly prescribed impose obligations, and only what the Qur'an and Sunna have forbidden is forbidden, so that strictness governs acts of worship and wide tolerance governs customs and contracts, in which only speculation and usury are excluded {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 299}. It explains the school's rigorism less by attention to detail in worship than by its refusal to give legal value to forms of worship introduced by the ijtihad of ascetics or mystics or by administrative decision, an attitude of hostility to [[concept/bidah|innovation]] that it says appeared with particular violence in [[person/al-barbahari-al-hasan-ibn-ali|al-Barbahari]] and the early Wahhabiyya {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 299}. Finally it observes that later compendia such as al-Khiraqi's Mukhtasar and Ibn Qudama's Umda give a restrictive codification of his thought rather than his own opinions {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 299}.

## His biography in the introduction to Fada'il al-Sahaba
Wasi Allah Abbas, editor of [[work/fadail-al-sahaba-ahmad|Fada'il al-Sahaba]], gives his lineage: Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn Hanbal ibn Hilal ibn Asad ibn Idris ibn Abd Allah ibn Hayyan ibn Abd Allah ibn Anas ibn Awf ibn Qasit ibn Mazin ibn Shayban al-Shaybani {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.25}. He was born in Rabi' I 164 AH, as his sons [[person/salih-ibn-ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Salih]] and [[person/abdullah-ibn-ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Abd Allah]] reported from him; his father died when he was small and his mother raised him, taking away his clothes when he wanted to set out early for hadith and saying "Wait until people call the dawn prayer, or until morning" {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.25}.

### Study and travel
Ahmad said the first person he wrote hadith from was Abu Yusuf, that he began seeking hadith at sixteen, that he first heard from Hushaym in 179, and that Hushaym died when he was twenty {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.25}. He travelled to [[place/kufa|Kufa]], [[place/basra|Basra]], [[place/mecca|Mecca]], [[place/medina|Medina]], Yemen, Syria and the Jazira; his first journey was to Basra in 186, and he entered it five times {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.26-27}. His son Salih relates that he went on pilgrimage with [[person/yahya-ibn-main|Yahya ibn Ma'in]], intending to go on afterwards to [[person/abd-al-razzaq-al-sanani|Abd al-Razzaq]] in Sanaa; they met Abd al-Razzaq in the circumambulation, and Yahya proposed hearing from him in Mecca to save a month's journey each way, but Ahmad refused to spoil his intention and went on until he heard from him in [[place/sanaa|Sanaa]] {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.26}. His money ran out there; he refused Abd al-Razzaq's dirhams as gift or loan, and made drawstrings and broke his fast on what they earned {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.26}. Salih also reports him saying: had I had fifty dirhams I would have gone to Rayy, to Jarir ibn Abd al-Hamid {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.27}.

### Breadth of transmission and praise
Salih reports: my father said, "I wrote a million hadiths in my own hand, besides what was written for me"; Abd Allah said: my father wrote a million hadiths and left out two hundred thousand from people he would not narrate from {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.27}. The editor quotes [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]] as saying that when he left Baghdad he left no one there more scrupulous, more God-fearing or more learned in law than Ahmad, and that Ahmad was an imam in eight things: hadith, law, language, the Qur'an, poverty, asceticism, scrupulousness and the Sunna {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.27-28}. Abu Ubayd al-Qasim ibn Sallam said that knowledge ended with four — Ahmad, Ali ibn al-Madini, Yahya ibn Ma'in and Abu Bakr ibn Abi Shayba — and that Ahmad was the best jurist of them {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.28}. Ibrahim al-Harbi said that in Ahmad it was as though God had gathered the knowledge of the first and the last {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.28}.

### The Mihna
The editor regards his stand during the controversy over the createdness of the Qur'an in the [[event/mihna-of-the-quran|Mihna]] as the most prominent fact of his life; he says it appeared under Harun al-Rashid and grew under al-Ma'mun, that many scholars gave in out of *taqiyya*, and that Ahmad held firm and was flogged {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.28-29}. He quotes one of the floggers as saying he gave him eighty lashes that would have brought down an elephant {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.29}.

### His death
He died on Friday 12 Rabi' I 241 AH, aged seventy-seven {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.29}.

### His works
From Ibn al-Nadim the editor lists: *al-Ilal wa-Ma'rifat al-Rijal*, the *Tafsir*, *al-Nasikh wa-l-Mansukh*, *al-Zuhd*, *al-Masa'il*, *al-Fada'il*, *al-Fara'id*, *al-Manasik*, *al-Iman*, *al-Ashriba*, *Ta'at al-Rasul*, *al-Radd ala al-Jahmiyya* and the *Musnad*, which Ibn al-Nadim says holds over forty thousand hadiths {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.29-30}. From al-Khatib he adds the *Tarikh*, *Hadith Shu'ba*, *al-Muqaddam wa-l-Mu'akhkhar fi Kitab Allah*, *Jawabat al-Qur'an* and *Hadith al-Shuyukh*; from Brockelmann *Kitab al-Sunna*, *al-Salat* and *Usul al-Sunna*; from Sezgin *al-Wuquf wa-l-Wasaya*, *Ahkam al-Nisa'*, *al-Tarajjul*, his answer on the createdness of the Qur'an and others {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.30-31}. He also names works not listed by his biographers, among them a *Kitab al-Fitan* in the Zahiriyya, *Fada'il Ahl al-Bayt* mentioned by al-Hakim, a *Musnad Ahl al-Bayt* in the Taymuriyya library, and *al-Asma' wa-l-Kuna* {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.32}. A footnote explains that the *Masa'il* are parts transmitted by Abu Dawud, Ishaq al-Kawsaj, Abd Allah, al-Baghawi, Ibn Hani', Hanbal, al-Maymuni, al-Marrudhi, Harb and al-Harbi, which al-Khallal gathered in *al-Jami' li-Ulum al-Imam Ahmad* {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.29-30}.

### His definition of a Companion
The editor quotes Ahmad, when the people of Badr were mentioned before him, as saying that the best of people after them are the Companions of the Messenger of God: everyone who accompanied him for a year, a month, a day or an hour, or saw him, is one of his Companions, with companionship in proportion to how long he accompanied him {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.11}. See [[concept/companionship-suhba|Suhba]].

## In Ibn Asakir's History of Damascus
In the [[work/tarikh-dimashq|History of the City of Damascus]], Ibn Asakir gives Ahmad ibn Hanbal a long entry, the longest in volume five, running from his lineage to the reports of his death and the dreams related after it. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 252} {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 335} He calls him "one of the luminaries among the imams of Islam", of [[place/marw|Marw]] origin, born and raised in Baghdad. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 252} He reports from al-Bukhari, through some of his companions, that Ahmad said: I was carried from Marw while my mother was pregnant with me. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 314} One report gives his birth as the year 164. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 326}

**His lineage and the dispute over it.** Ibn Asakir traces his descent to Shayban and Bakr ibn Wa'il, reports Abbas al-Duri and Abu Bakr ibn Abi Dawud placing him among Banu Dhuhl ibn Shayban, then al-Khatib saying this is a mistake: he belonged to Banu Shayban ibn Dhuhl ibn Tha'laba, Dhuhl ibn Tha'laba being the paternal uncle of Dhuhl ibn Shayban, so that he should simply be called "al-Dhuhli". {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 254} {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 255}

**Why he is in the History of Damascus.** Ibn Asakir records that he heard from Damascenes such as al-Walid ibn Muslim and Abu Mushir, and that he set out for Syria to reach Muhammad ibn Yusuf al-Firyabi at [[place/qaysariyya|Caesarea]], learned of his death on the way, turned to [[place/homs|Homs]] and heard there from Abu al-Yaman al-Hakam ibn Nafi' and others, passing through Damascus or its districts. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 252} {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 253} Among his teachers he names [[person/sufyan-ibn-uyayna|Ibn Uyayna]], Hushaym, [[person/ibn-ulayyah|Ibn Ulayya]], [[person/yahya-ibn-said-al-qattan|Yahya al-Qattan]], [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-mahdi|Ibn Mahdi]], Waki' and Abd al-Razzaq; among those who narrated from him, his sons Abd Allah and [[person/salih-ibn-ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Salih]], his cousin [[person/hanbal-ibn-ishaq|Hanbal ibn Ishaq]], [[person/al-bukhari|al-Bukhari]], [[person/muslim-ibn-al-hajjaj|Muslim]], [[person/abu-dawud-al-sijistani|Abu Dawud]], [[person/ibrahim-al-harbi|Ibrahim al-Harbi]], Abu Zur'a, Abu Hatim and Baqi ibn Makhlad. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 253}

**Praise of him.** He reports from al-Fath ibn Shakhraf that al-Harith al-Muhasibi said: there came upon Ahmad ibn Hanbal what never came upon Sufyan al-Thawri and al-Awza'i. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 310} He reports from Ishaq ibn Rahawayh that [[person/abd-allah-ibn-tahir|Abd Allah ibn Tahir]] said: I love Yahya ibn Yahya and Ahmad ibn Hanbal, though they do not come near the ruler — not out of opposition, but because of rulers' injustice — and that he took Ahmad's letter to Ishaq and placed it under his prayer mat. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 310} {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 311} He reports that [[person/ali-ibn-al-madini|Ali ibn al-Madini]], hiding during the Mihna, kissed Ahmad's letter when it reached him, and said Ahmad in his time was more excellent than Sa'id ibn Jubayr in his, since Sa'id had peers. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 314} {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 315}

**The Mihna.** Ibn Asakir relates with chains from al-Rabi' ibn Sulayman that [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]] wrote from Egypt to Ahmad saying he had seen the Prophet in a dream bidding him greet Ahmad and tell him he would be tested and called to say the Qur'an was created, and should not give in; Ahmad wept and gave al-Rabi' the shirt he wore next to his skin. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 311} {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 312} He relates from Abu Ja'far al-Ansari that he crossed the Euphrates to Ahmad as he was being carried to al-Ma'mun and told him: you are a leader and people follow you; if you give in, a great many will give in; and Ahmad wept, saying: what God wills. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 312} He relates from Abu Dharr, one of those who flogged Ahmad before [[person/al-mutasim|al-Mu'tasim]], that they were a hundred and fifty floggers taking turns. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 313} He relates that the governor of Baghdad, [[person/ishaq-ibn-ibrahim-ibn-musab|Ishaq ibn Ibrahim]], gathered opponents to dispute with him over a hadith and a verse, then had his fetters removed and let him go. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 313} {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 314} He quotes Abu al-Walid al-Tayalisi: had what befell Ahmad happened among the Children of Israel, it would have become a legend. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 314} One report says he was flogged in the last ten days of Ramadan in the year 220. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 326} The Mihna as a whole is described under [[event/mihna-of-the-quran|the Mihna over the Qur'an]].

**His death.** He died in Baghdad on a Friday in 241; the reports differ on whether the month was Rabi' al-Awwal or Rabi' al-Akhir; Muhammad ibn Abd Allah ibn Tahir led the funeral prayer, and very large, varying numbers are reported for those who attended. {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 327} {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 328} {cite:tarikh-dimashq-amrawi-v5 p. 331} The details are under [[event/death-of-ahmad-ibn-hanbal|the death and funeral of Ahmad ibn Hanbal]].

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "A Story and an Idea"
Ahmad ibn Hanbal appears in three episodes of [[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]]'s programme [[work/kissa-wa-fikra-program|"A Story and an Idea"]]: the sixteenth, on high aspiration and "the resting mind"; the eighteenth, on the journey of [[person/baqi-ibn-makhlad|Baqi ibn Makhlad]]; and the twenty-sixth, on tolerance. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep18 00:11:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep26 00:00:30} What follows is his account.

He opens the sixteenth episode with Abu Sa'id al-Wasiti, who visited Ahmad in prison before the flogging and said that, having a family, he would be excused if he gave his questioners what they wanted; Ahmad answered that if this was his reasoning, he had "rested". {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:01:30} He says Ahmad was born in 164 AH and grew up an orphan, raised by a mother who did not remarry. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:02:00} He memorised the Qur'an young and leaned from fiqh towards hadith, travelling far, sometimes for very few hadiths. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:02:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:03:00} He had more than a hundred teachers, among them [[person/hushaym-ibn-bashir|Hushaym ibn Bashir]], with whom he stayed four years, [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]], from whom he learned the principles of legal deduction, and [[person/abd-al-razzaq-al-sanani|Abd al-Razzaq al-San'ani]] and [[person/yazid-ibn-harun|Yazid ibn Harun]]. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:04:30} He lived in a narrow house on dates or bread dipped in water, accepted no gift or stipend, shunned the caliphs' money, lived on the rent of property from his father and wove cloth when it did not suffice; he refused office, judgeships included, seeing service of unjust rulers as helping them. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:07:00}

Al-Suwaidan explains the doctrine of the created Qur'an: in his view the Mu'tazila meant to exalt God above change, but the doctrine denied the divine attribute of speech. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:09:00} [[person/abd-allah-al-mamun|Al-Ma'mun]] first announced it without compulsion, then enforced it and had the judges and jurists of Baghdad tested; the first seven senior scholars all gave way out of fear, which he calls state "intellectual terrorism" by the Mu'tazila. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:10:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:10:30} Of a second group only Ahmad and the young Muhammad ibn Nuh held out, and they were sent in irons to al-Ma'mun at Tarsus. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:12:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:12:30} On the way a Bedouin of Rabi'a urged Ahmad to stand firm because people would follow him, and Ahmad later said the words strengthened him. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:12:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:13:00} A servant came weeping with word that al-Ma'mun had sworn to kill him; Ahmad prayed, and that night news came that al-Ma'mun had died. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:14:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:16:00}

[[person/al-mutasim|Al-Mu'tasim]] kept the doctrine and relied on [[person/ahmad-ibn-abi-duad|Ahmad ibn Abi Du'ad]]; Muhammad ibn Nuh fell ill and died on the road, telling Ahmad as he died that he was a man people followed. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:18:30} Al-Suwaidan's lesson: in a time of trial ordinary people may take the dispensation of acting under duress, but a scholar whom people follow may not. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:18:00} Before al-Mu'tasim, Ahmad asked what the Prophet had called people to, cited the hadith of the delegation of Abd al-Qays on faith and said he affirmed all of it; for days the scholars debated him, and to arguments of theology and logic he answered that he wanted evidence from revelation. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:20:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:20:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:21:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:22:30} On the third day he was bound and flogged in relays until he fainted; when thousands gathered at the palace and unrest loomed, he was released, having spent, al-Suwaidan says, twenty-eight months in prison. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:23:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:24:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:24:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:25:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:25:30}

[[person/al-wathiq|Al-Wathiq]] kept the doctrine but did not touch him, forbidding him to teach and confining him to his house; [[person/al-mutawakkil|al-Mutawakkil]] ended it and drew close to the Sunnis and to Ahmad. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:26:00} Ahmad forgave all who had harmed him except the people of innovation. He died on a Friday, 12 Rabi' al-Awwal 241 AH, at seventy-seven, and hundreds of thousands attended his funeral. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:27:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:27:30} In the twenty-sixth episode al-Suwaidan tells how a doctor later cut dead flesh from the flogging scars on his back without anaesthetic while Ahmad kept praying for forgiveness for al-Mu'tasim, a kinsman of the Prophet, because he did not want to meet the Prophet's kin as an adversary on the Day of Judgement. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep26 00:00:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep26 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep26 00:01:30} In the eighteenth episode he tells how Ahmad, under house arrest, received the Andalusian Baqi ibn Makhlad at his door each day disguised as a beggar, and later seated him beside him in his circle and visited him in sickness. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep18 00:20:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep18 00:20:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep18 00:22:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep18 00:23:30} His lesson from the sixteenth episode: some rise to support the truth, some are beaten and come to side with falsehood, and some look on and "rest". {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:28:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep16 00:28:30}
