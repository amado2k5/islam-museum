## Summary
Ahmad Kaftaru (December 1915 – 1 September 2004) was the Grand Mufti of Syria from 1964 until his death, and the sheikh of the Naqshbandi Sufi order in Syria. Born in Damascus to a family of Kurdish origin, he received a classical Islamic education, rose through the religious administration from mosque teacher to Mufti of Damascus and then Grand Mufti of the republic, and built a network of schools, orphanages, charities and mosques. His devotional community, the Kaftariyya, survived his death under his son. He was a prominent public advocate of interreligious dialogue. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-kuftaro-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-kuftaro-ar}

## Early life and education
He was born in Damascus — the Arabic article places the birth in the Rukn al-Din quarter, which remained his base — to a Kurdish family that had moved to Damascus in 1878 from the village of Karma near Ömerli in the Mardin region. The English article and Wikidata date the birth to 1915 (December, in the English text); the Arabic article gives 1912, a discrepancy his record preserves rather than resolves. His father, Muhammad Amin Kaftaro, was himself a sheikh of the Naqshbandi-Khalidi branch of the order, and the son was trained in the classical curriculum: Qur'an memorisation, the Shafi'i school of law, hadith, Arabic, exegesis, logic, and the Naqshbandi-Khalidi path under his father's guidance. He took the ijaza in Sufism and the general teaching licence from his father's own sheikh, and later received two honorary doctorates, from Omar Al-Mukhtar University in Libya and from a university in Pakistan. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-kuftaro-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-kuftaro-ar} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-kuftaro-ur}

## Career in the ifta administration
His official rise was steady and entirely within Syria's religious bureaucracy. He began as a religious teacher in the mosques of Quneitra in 1948 while teaching in Damascus mosques, was appointed a fatwa registrar in 1949, served as Mufti of the Shafi'i madhhab in Damascus in 1952, and as an administrator of fatwa in 1955. The Arabic article adds that he joined the official religious institute alongside the senior scholars of his day and became its president. Ba'ath-era Syria kept him: under the Ba'athist regime he signed fatwas condemning the Muslim Brotherhood, and in 1964 he was elected Grand Mufti of the Syrian Arab Republic — the office he would hold for the remaining forty years of his life. In 1974 he was chosen president of the Supreme Fatwa Council. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-kuftaro-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-kuftaro-ar}

## Institutions and works
Kaftaru founded and presided over a small educational empire: Islamic schools in Damascus founded from 1972, including the Institute for Memorisation of the Qur'an, institutes for males and females, and a large orphanage; he chaired the Islamic Sciences dissemination association founded by his father, and served as general director of the Abu al-Nur Islamic Foundation — the complex that later bore his own name — and of the Sheikh Ahmad Kaftaro Academy branch of Omdurman University. He founded the Kaftaro Charitable Society in Minya, Egypt, and headed the Union of Ulama of Bilad al-Sham. His written works include a book on dialogue, civilisation and contemporary Muslim reality, one on the Naqshbandi Sufi path, and lectures on Islamic society in the age of globalisation; two works appeared in English — a selected biography of Muhammad, and a work of prayers and supplications translated by his son. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-kuftaro-ar} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-kuftaro-en}

## Interreligious engagement
The English article emphasises his public role as an advocate of interfaith dialogue: he met Pope John Paul II, corresponded with the Archbishop of Canterbury Rowan Williams (who cited him in a 2004 lecture as an example of a Muslim religious figure committed to interfaith relations), and hosted at his institute the Dalai Lama and the American pastor and peace activist John Dear. The same article also contains a claim this museum does not use — that he signed the Amman Message of 2004 — because the Message was issued in November 2004, two months after his death; the impossibility is recorded in the source entity itself. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-kuftaro-en}

## Death and succession
He died in Damascus on 1 September 2004, still Grand Mufti; the Arabic article dates it 17 Rajab 1425 AH. His son, Salah al-Din Kaftaro, succeeded him as sheikh of the Naqshbandi-Khalidi path and as head of the community — which in 2012 renamed itself in Arabic from the Kaftariyya to "the people of the Sunna and the community", a step the English article reads as partly reflecting the decline of Sufism's fortunes in the Syrian war years. On doctrine, the museum records what the texts attest for the man himself: the English article's lead calls him "a Sunni Muslim of the Naqshbandi Sufi order" and the Arabic article independently names him sheikh of the Naqshbandi Sufi order in Syria, with the Urdu article to the same effect; his Shafi'i education and his tenure as the capital's Shafi'i mufti are education and office, and his record therefore carries his Naqshbandi affiliation as the value, with the Sunni attestation preserved in the note. {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-kuftaro-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-kuftaro-ar} {cite:wikipedia-ahmed-kuftaro-ur} {cite:wikidata-ahmed-kuftaro}
