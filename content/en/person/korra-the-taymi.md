## Overview
Korra The Taymi is a young debater and religious speaker of Sudanese heritage who publishes under the name Korra The Taymi; her channel introduces her in her own words: "I go by Korra, and I'm a Muslim religious speaker". {cite:youtube-korra-the-taymi}

Her channel's tagline is "Bridging The Gap Between Reason and Revelation". {cite:youtube-korra-the-taymi}

The Blood Brothers podcast of the 5Pillars news site described her as a "viral Niqabi debater and Muslim polemicist" and discussed with her her experience of Speakers' Corner in London, managing virality at a young age, her focus on Christianity, Muslim women in dawah, and — given her Sudanese heritage — her thoughts on the situation in Sudan. {cite:blood-brothers-korra-the-taymi}

Her channel had about 121,000 subscribers as of October 2026, a platform figure that changes constantly. {cite:youtube-korra-the-taymi}

## Career and work
Her channel's videos centre on filmed debates against Christian interlocutors, among them a debate against ten Christian apologists, a one-versus-ten debate she later rewatched and critiqued her own answers in, and a joint debate alongside The Orthodox Muslim against Cliffe and Stuart Knechtle. {cite:youtube-korra-the-taymi}

She has also spoken at the ICNA Canada convention, in a talk titled "Stop Being Afraid to Be Muslim", carrying her activity from the platform to conferences. {cite:youtube-korra-the-taymi}

The podcast asked her how she entered debates and polemics and which scholars she learns from or consults, and about her response to the TikTok "Pink Cherry Islam" phenomenon, topics the episode treated as the axes of her public career. {cite:blood-brothers-korra-the-taymi}

## Positions and criticism
This section should be read as a presentation of different positions reported by the sources, not as a verdict of the museum on them.

Her performances were praised by the Black Dawah Network site, which described her debate against twenty Christian women as showing composure and methodological precision in questioning, and read her spread as a sign of a generational shift in digital dawah; the site attributes to her — as do her critics — the age of nineteen, which is the sources' claim rather than a documented birth date, so the museum records no birth date for her. {cite:black-dawah-network-korra-the-taymi}

Against this, the SalafiQA site published an article criticising her platforming as a public debater in the name of dawah; its author argues that celebrating her debate victories departs from the way of the Salaf as he presents it, and that a woman's place, in his framing, is teaching from behind a veil rather than debating before cameras and crowds. {cite:salafiqa-korra-the-taymi}

The contrast marks a debate within English-language dawah over women's presence in public debates: supporters count it effective outreach, Salafi critics count it a breach of limits drawn by their method; the available sources attribute no named school to Korra herself, so the museum records no school for her. {cite:blood-brothers-korra-the-taymi} {cite:salafiqa-korra-the-taymi}
