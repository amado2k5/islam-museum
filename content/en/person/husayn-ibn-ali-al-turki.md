# Al-Husayn I ibn Ali

Al-Husayn I ibn Ali (1675–1740), Husayn ibn Ali al-Turki, known as Hussein I Bey, was the founder of the Husainid dynasty, which ruled Tunisia until the abolition of the monarchy in 1957 {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-en}. He was born at Le Kef a Kouloughli — his father, Ali Turki, was a janissary officer of Cretan Greek origin who commanded the garrison of Le Kef under the Muradids, and his mother, Hafsia Cherni, was a Tunisian of the Charni tribe settled around Le Kef {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-fr} {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-ar}. The Arabic article's prose gives his birth in 1675, as do the English, French, Italian and Spanish articles, while its structured fields and categories give 1669 — the year the queue row carried — and the Wikidata item carries both a 1669 and a 1670 statement {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-ar} {cite:wikidata-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki}.

He rose through the service of the last Muradid beys — treasurer (khaznadar) under Muhammad Bey ibn Murad II, agha of the Turkish sipahis under Ramadan Bey, and kahia at Dar El Pacha under Murad III — and then as lieutenant of Ibrahim Sharif, who expelled the Muradids in 1702 {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-ar}. When the Dey of Algiers captured Ibrahim at the battle of Le Kef on 8 July 1705, Husayn brought the army back to the capital, took control of the Turkish militia, and was proclaimed bey on 12 July 1705, taking office on 15 July and founding the Husainid line {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-fr} {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-ur}. He had Sharif assassinated at Ghar el-Melh on his release from captivity, completing his grip on the country {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-en}.

The chronicler Muhammad Saghir Ben Youssef, as the French article reports him, describes a prince who restored the honour of the Sunna and showed the keenest piety, visiting holy men in their homes and zawiyas with his rosary always in his fingers {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-fr}. His buildings matched the programme: the Madrasa of the Dyers (1727) and the al-Husseyniah madrasa in Tunis, the mosque of Le Bardo, madrasas at Kairouan, Sfax, Sousse and Nafta, public fountains and cisterns, and in 1726 the El Jedid Mosque in Tunis {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-en} {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-fr}. He settled the succession by primogeniture on his sons Muhammad and Ali (born 1710 and 1712); his nephew Ali Pasha, the displaced heir presumptive, escaped surveillance in 1728, won over the Hanencha and Ousseltia tribes, and took refuge in Algiers {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-fr}.

Released by the Dey, Ali invaded; Husayn was defeated at the battle of Smindja on 4 September 1735, fled to Sousse and then Kairouan, and Tunis capitulated, Ali entering on 7 September 1735 and proclaiming himself bey {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-en} {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-fr}. Husayn held out at Kairouan for five years until Yunus took the city; he was captured and beheaded there in 1740 — the French, Italian and Spanish articles date it 13 May, the English article 13 September, and the Wikidata item carries both dates {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-it} {cite:wikipedia-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki-en} {cite:wikidata-husayn-ibn-ali-al-turki}. The queue row's 1735 is thus the end of his reign, not his death.
