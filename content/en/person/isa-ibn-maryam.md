## Overview
Isa is the Qur'anic name for Jesus, son of Mary. According to the Encyclopaedia of Islam article, the Qur'an refers to him in fifteen suras and gives him ninety-three verses, and these verses are the foundation of Muslim Christology. The article adds that various traditions, which took in material from apocryphal gospels of the childhood of Jesus and from mystical Christian literature, enriched this picture and in some respects brought it closer to the Christian one, while Muslim-Christian polemic over the centuries hardened the positions on both sides. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 90}

This page sets out the Muslim understanding first, as the article reports it from the Qur'an, the commentators and the tradition, and labels the Christian, academic and sectarian views separately where the article gives them. It gives no verdict between them. Further down are the reports about him in the *Sahih* of al-Bukhari and two talks by a modern preacher that were already recorded on this page.

## The name and his titles
The article lists several explanations of the form Isa. Some western writers, it says, held that Jews led the Prophet to use a form derived from Esau, because Jews in hatred are said to have called Jesus by that name. Others derive Yasu' from the Syriac Yeshu, itself from the Hebrew Yeshua, with the ending adjusted to match Musa. A further modern suggestion linked it to a name in pre-Islamic inscriptions, a theory that G. Ryckmans strongly rejected. These are the views of the scholars cited, not of the Muslim sources. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 90}

The Qur'an gives him several names and titles. The article lists al-Masih, which occurs eleven times; prophet and messenger; son of Mary, which occurs thirty-three times; one of those brought near to God; worthy of esteem in this world and the next, which al-Baydawi explains as a prophet on earth and an intercessor in heaven; blessed; and servant of God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 90}

## Annunciation, conception and birth
The article observes that the Qur'an has features that reflect the Gospels, and it cites the view of P. Hayek that, apart from the Muslim rejection of the doctrine that Mary is the mother of God, other Christian dogmas about her find some support, although weak, in the Qur'an. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 90} Mary, who had devoted her virginity to God, is visited by the Spirit of God in the form of a man without fault, whom tradition identifies with the archangel [[person/jibril|Gibril]]. He announces the birth of a son and tells her that it is easy for the Lord, who will make her a sign and a mercy for people. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 90}

The article notes that the Qur'an does not sharply separate annunciation from conception, and that some thought the angel was the father of Jesus, an idea the article ties to certain exegetes and to a European writer, Gerock. It then states the main point: the conception resulted from a creative decree of God, and the creation of Jesus is likened to that of [[person/adam|Adam]], which was at least as marvellous. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 90}

Overtaken by labour beside a palm tree, Mary brings the child to her family. To silence their reproaches the infant speaks from the cradle, declares himself a servant of God to whom the Book was given, and speaks of prayer, almsgiving and care for his mother. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 91} The article notes that Islam does not admit original sin, and reports a tradition accepted by al-Bukhari and Muslim that every newborn is touched by Satan except the son of Mary and his mother. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 91}

## His mission and miracles
Jesus is a prophet and a messenger, and like every prophet he has a mission to a particular people. The article lists the signs, called proofs, that the Qur'an attributes to him, each by God's leave: speaking in the cradle with the authority of a grown man; fashioning birds from clay and breathing life into them; healing the man born blind and the leper; raising the dead; and bringing down a table of food at the request of the apostles, which was a feast and a sign of his mission. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 91}

The article adds scholarly comments, labelled as such. The making of clay birds appears in apocryphal childhood gospels. The Ahmadiyya read that miracle symbolically. Writers have linked the table to the Last Supper or to the feeding of the multitude, and the article thinks an apocryphal tradition that has not survived may have influenced the passage. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 91}

Jesus confirms the Torah, and the scripture given to him is the Gospel, [[work/injil|the Injil]], which the Qur'an judges favourably because it fills the hearts of its followers with meekness and pity. His prophetic authority extends over the Gospel, the Torah and the earlier writings, all taught to him by God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 91}

## Messiah and servant of God
The article says that the Qur'an gives Jesus the title Messiah from his birth, in a narrow sense that does not correspond to the Christian concept. In the Qur'an he is one prophet in a series that ends with [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]], and like them an ordinary human being. The word Masih is used eleven times, always in Medinan passages, and the article says it is of Jewish origin, passed on through Syriac, and that al-Zamakhshari and al-Baydawi admit it is a foreign word. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 91}

Arab writers derived it from two roots: masaha, to rub or anoint, giving the senses that he was anointed with blessings, shielded by the wing of Gabriel, or anointed the eyes of the blind and the sick; and saha, to travel, from which Muslim mystical writers made him the model and imam of the wanderers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 91} The title servant of God, the article says, stresses that he is a created being, and the infant's first words in the cradle are reported to have been to recognise that status; the article warns against reading the word in a Judaeo-Christian sense. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 92}

## The Word of God and the Spirit
The article gives four Muslim interpretations of the Qur'anic statements that Jesus is a word from God: he is the fulfilment of God's creative word uttered at his conception; he is the prophet announced in the word that earlier messengers received and preached; he speaks for God and so guides people; or he is himself good tidings. It notes that scholars have discussed a possible link with the Christian Logos. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 92} He was strengthened by the Holy Spirit, first at birth and then in his ministry among the Jews, a formula the Qur'an uses of him alone, although it uses a similar one for believers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 92}

## Jesus and Muhammad
Muslim commentators, relying on a Qur'anic verse in which Jesus announces a messenger to come after him, say that he foretold Muhammad, whom they recognise in the name Ahmad. Islam also sees the promise of the Paraclete in the Gospel of John as referring to him. The article reports a further explanation from western writers: the Greek word in the Gospel text may have been read as periklytos, meaning famed, and rendered Ahmad, and Muslim commentators accuse Christians of changing the word. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 92}

## Divinity, Trinity and Christian doctrine
According to the article, the Qur'an places Jesus among those near to God, as it does the angels, yet both remain created beings. In its reading, Muhammad, for all his veneration of the son of Mary, was clear that he is not divine, and the article connects one passage to the reply made to the Christian delegates of [[place/najran|Najran]] in 631. The Qur'an rejects the doctrine that God is the Messiah, and the sonship of Jesus. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 92}

The article points out that the Trinity the Qur'an rejects is not the one defined by the Christian councils. It seems to be a triad of God, Mary and their child, a concept it relates to the Marian cults of certain Christian groups in Arabia, the Mariamites and Collyridians, which Christian orthodoxy itself regarded as heretical. It adds that some modern Muslim writers, informed by Christian explanations, are inclined to recognise the basic monotheism of Christianity without accepting its revealed nature. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 92}

## The crucifixion
The Qur'an, the article says, states its position categorically: against those who claimed to have killed the Messiah, it says they neither killed nor crucified him, but that a likeness of him was shown to them, and that God raised him up to Himself. Muslim tradition adds that a double replaced him, or Simon of Cyrene, or one of the apostles. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 92} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 93}

Some philosophers and Ismaili commentators held that only his human nature was crucified, while his divine nature remained alive, but the article says that interpretation was not generally accepted and that there is unanimous agreement in denying the crucifixion. It explains the denial as consistent with the Qur'an's pattern, called God's practice, of faith finally triumphing over adversity. It also mentions the scholarly view of H. Gregoire that the idea has gnostic and docetic origins, a view the article reports without adopting. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 93}

On the question of his death, the article reads the key Qur'anic passage as saying that he was raised to heaven alive, and gives the sequence as apparent death, ascension, a later natural death and the general resurrection. A later tradition it cites says that this natural death will come when he returns at the end of time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 93}

## The return of Jesus
The article says the only Qur'anic basis cited for the return is a verse with variant readings, which commentators read as making him a sign or knowledge of the Hour. Tradition, it says, accumulated much detail. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 93} In it Jesus descends at the eastern gate of [[place/damascus|Damascus]], or on a hill in the Holy Land, and kills the Antichrist, [[concept/dajjal|al-Dajjal]]. He then goes to [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]] at the time of the dawn prayer and prays behind the imam, following the practice of Muhammad. He breaks the cross, and all the People of the Book come to believe in him, leaving a single community, that of Islam. Justice reigns and peace extends even to the animals. He remains forty years, then dies, and the Muslims bury him at [[place/medina|Medina]] beside Muhammad, in the place left between him and [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 93}

The article says that the doctrine of the Mahdi came to be joined to this belief and sometimes merged with it. It also records a view of one group: the Ahmadiyya hold that Jesus, after an apparent death on the cross, emigrated to India and Kashmir, lived to the age of 120, and is buried at [[place/srinagar|Srinagar]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 93}

## Jesus and the Last Judgement
On the night of his ascent, Muhammad met Abraham, Moses and Jesus, and when Jesus was asked about the Hour, he replied that God alone knows when it will occur. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 93} The article draws two conclusions: Jesus knows the Hour will come but not its time, and he will not take part in the Judgement, which belongs to God alone. On the day of resurrection he will be a witness against Christians who regarded him and his mother as equal to God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 94}

## In tradition and in mystical writing
Meditation by ascetics and mystics produced an abundant literature about Jesus' poverty, detachment from the world, teaching and prayer. Such material, with anecdotes, sermons and advice attributed to him, appears in works such as the *Hilyat al-awliya'* of Abu Nu'aym, the *Qut al-qulub* of al-Makki and, above all, the works of [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 94} The article notes that Asin Palacios collected the texts in al-Ghazali's works and Michel Hayek classified them in a Christology, and that when Sufis later met the Gospels they took from them what matched their ascetic ideas. It also reports that [[person/ibn-arabi-muhyi-al-din|Ibn Arabi]] said Jesus deserves the seal of universal sainthood and will descend as a judge according to the law of Muhammad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 94}

## Modern writing and polemic
The article says that Muslim-Christian polemic on the Incarnation and the Redemption produced a classic system of apologetics that has been repeated almost unchanged. It then describes two Cairo works as a new tendency: *'Abqariyyat al-Masih* by 'Abbas Mahmud al-'Aqqad, 1952, which approaches Christ with respect and defends the historicity of Jesus and the authenticity of the Gospels, though Christian critics noted that the author declares that at the Passion history ends and belief begins; and *Qarya zalima* by Kamil Husayn, 1954, a personal meditation on the trial of Christ. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 94}

## In the Sahih of al-Bukhari

- Al-Bukhari relates that he was one of the three who spoke in the cradle. {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 3436}
- He also relates that Satan did not touch him, nor his mother Maryam at her birth, unlike all the other children of Adam. {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 3431}
- In it the Prophet says that he is the nearest of people to Isa son of Maryam in this world and the next, and that the prophets are brothers from different mothers with one religion. {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 3443}
- On the night of the Night Journey, the Prophet described him as of medium height, well-proportioned, with a reddish complexion. {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 3207}


## For Sprouts
Jesus son of Mary was a blessed prophet born by a divine miracle, who taught people love, mercy, healing, and helping those in need.

## For Explorers
Jesus son of Mary was a noble messenger among the five of resolute determination, created through God's word without a father and honoring his mother Mary. God revealed to him the Gospel (Injil) and filled his message with mercy, blessing him with miracles including healing the blind and leper by God's leave, and speaking in infancy to defend his mother.

## For Scholars
The Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, is a preeminent prophet and messenger among the resolute five, recognized in Islam as God's Word bestowed upon the Virgin Mary and a Spirit from Him. Islamic theology emphasizes his miraculous virginal conception, speaking from the cradle in vindication of his mother, and the revelation of the Gospel (Injil) confirming the Torah and heralding the Prophet Muhammad. Sustained by miracles performed solely by divine leave, the Qur'an records his being raised to heaven, standing as an exemplar of spiritual asceticism, humility, and compassion.
