## Summary
Abu Abd Allah Muhammad ibn Jabir ibn Sinan al-Harrani al-Raqqi, known as al-Battani, was one of the leading observers, geometers and astronomers of the third Islamic century and the author of the Zij in which he set down his observations of the Sun and Moon and corrected their motions as [[person/batlamus|Ptolemy]] had fixed them in the [[work/al-majisti|Almagest]]. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17} [[person/al-masudi|al-Mas'udi]] counted him among those who cultivated astronomy after the coming of Islam {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 519}, and relied on his Zij for the reckoning of the caliphs' reigns from the Hijra to the year 336. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 736}

## The sources for his life
Almost everything known about al-Battani goes back to [[person/ibn-al-nadim|Ibn al-Nadim]]'s Fihrist and [[person/al-qifti|al-Qifti]]'s History of the Sages; Nallino believes that what al-Qifti alone adds comes from the Tabaqat al-umam of [[person/said-al-andalusi|Sa'id al-Andalusi]]. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 18} [[person/ibn-khallikan|Ibn Khallikan]] took his entry in the Wafayat al-a'yan from Ibn al-Nadim, [[person/abu-al-fida|Abu al-Fida]] followed Ibn Khallikan in his Annals, and Bar Hebraeus and al-Ayni drew on the same stock. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 18} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 19} Ali ibn Zayd al-Bayhaqi (History of the Sages of Islam) and al-Shahrazuri (Nuzhat al-arwah) give notices that may come from another source, but al-Bayhaqi confused his grandfather Sinan with Sinan son of [[person/thabit-ibn-qurra|Thabit ibn Qurra]], making his lineage "Muhammad ibn Jabir ibn Sinan ibn Thabit ibn Qurra" — a plain error for Nallino, since Sinan ibn Thabit died fourteen years after al-Battani. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 20}

## Name and lineage
His given name was Muhammad, his kunya Abu Abd Allah, his lineage Muhammad son of Jabir son of Sinan, and he carried several nisbas: al-Harrani for his origin in Harran, al-Raqqi for his residence in al-Raqqa, al-Battani, and al-Sabi. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 21} Ibn Khallikan derived al-Battani from "Battan", a district of Harran, and reported that [[person/hibat-allah-ibn-al-akfani|Ibn al-Akfani]] pronounced it al-Bittani; [[person/yaqut-al-hamawi|Yaqut]] lists Battan among the villages of Harran. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 19} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 21} Nallino doubts the village existed, suspects the geographers drew its name out of the nisba itself, and judges it wiser to admit ignorance while holding it certain that al-Battani and his family came from the Harran district; he rejects Chwolsohn's view that he was born at Batnan (Saruj). {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 22} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 23}

## Upbringing and family
Nothing certain is known of his childhood or household; Nallino holds that he was born before 244 AH, since he began observing in 264 and completed some of his main observations three years later. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 23} His father seems to have been the Jabir ibn Sinan al-Harrani whom Ibn al-Nadim lists among the famous makers of astronomical instruments; perhaps he learned the rudiments from him, which would explain why he excelled in observation and devised a new and very convenient armillary sphere. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 23}

[[place/harran|Harran]] was an ancient city long devoted to the cult of the Moon; its people kept their pagan worship through the first four centuries after the Hijra and, from 215 AH, called themselves Sabians so as to be treated as a people of the book paying the jizya, as Nallino relates. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 23} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 24} Many Harranians moved to [[place/raqqa|al-Raqqa]] in the third century, and al-Battani's father may have been among them. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 25} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 26}

## Observations at al-Raqqa and Antioch
Al-Battani spent most of his life in al-Raqqa and observed the sky there for many years to set the elements of his tables for that city's meridian and latitude. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 26} He measured the Sun's course over successive years, fixed the solstices and equinoxes above all in 882 and 883 CE, determined the longitudes of certain fixed stars in 879–880, and observed solar and lunar eclipses in 883 and 891. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 26} He later stayed for some time in [[place/antioch|Antioch]], where he observed the solar eclipse of 23 January and the lunar eclipse of 2 August 901. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 26} Ja'far ibn al-Muktafi reported from him that he began observing in 264 AH and continued to 306, and that he fixed the stars in his Zij for the year 299. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17}

Nallino rejects the story current among some Europeans that he was a prince or king of Syria, saying he found nothing of the kind in the Arabic authors. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 26} He infers al-Battani's devotion to study and his indifference to praise from the advice he gives to observers and from his great modesty. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 26}

## Standing among his contemporaries
Signs of the esteem in which the learned of his day held him are the epistle the vizier [[person/ali-ibn-al-furat|Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Muhammad ibn al-Furat]] asked of him, his conversations with [[person/jafar-ibn-al-muktafi|Ja'far ibn al-Muktafi]], and the mention of his observations by Thabit ibn Qurra. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 26} Ibn al-Nadim and al-Qifti say that no one in Islam was known to equal him in correcting the observation of the stars and testing their motions. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17}

## Last journey and death
Al-Battani came to [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] with the Banu al-Zayyat of al-Raqqa, who sought redress for taxes unjustly exacted from them; on his way back he died at Qasr al-Jiss in 317 AH. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17} Nallino thinks these Banu al-Zayyat were descendants of the vizier [[person/muhammad-ibn-abd-al-malik-al-zayyat|Muhammad ibn Abd al-Malik al-Zayyat]]. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 26} Fifty-five lunar years had passed since he began to observe. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 27}

[[place/qasr-al-jiss|Qasr al-Jiss]], "the Plaster Palace", was one of the palaces al-Mu'tasim built near [[place/samarra|Samarra]], west of the Tigris on the Ishaqi canal. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 27} Ibn Khallikan's text reads "Qasr al-Hadr", which Nallino takes for a copyist's corruption in Ibn Khallikan's exemplar, since al-Hadr is never called a qasr and the road from Baghdad to al-Raqqa does not pass its ruins. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 27} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 28}

## Writings
Ibn al-Nadim and al-Qifti list: [[work/al-zij-al-sabi|the Zij]] in two recensions, the second better than the first; [[work/kitab-marifat-matali-al-buruj-battani|the Book on Knowing the Ascensions of the Signs between the Quadrants of the Sphere]]; [[work/risalat-tahqiq-aqdar-al-ittisalat|the Epistle on Verifying the Quantities of Applications]], written for Abu al-Hasan ibn al-Furat; and [[work/sharh-al-maqalat-al-arba-battani|a Commentary on Ptolemy's Four Books (Tetrabiblos)]]. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 17} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 18} Several Latin works were attributed to him that Nallino considers spurious, among them an epitome of Archimedes that is really by Ibn al-Haytham, an epitome of the Almagest, and astrological tracts under the name "Bethem". {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 32} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 33} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 40}

## Sayings attributed to him
Al-Bayhaqi reports two maxims from him: that the misery of life lies in a disobedient son, a bad neighbour and an ill-natured wife; and that some things are never small even in small quantity — debt, enmity and illness. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 20} Nallino does not dare decide whether they are truly al-Battani's or whether al-Bayhaqi confused him with someone else. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 20}

## The question of his faith
:::perspectives
- **Sunni: Ibn Khallikan and Abu al-Fida**: Ibn Khallikan said he had not found that al-Battani embraced Islam, but that his name Muhammad shows he became a Muslim; Abu al-Fida added that the preface of his Zij shows it too. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 18} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 19}
- **Academic: Nallino**: the surest witness to his Islam is the Zij's preface, which cites many Qur'anic verses, and the Muslim formulas closing its chapters; the nisba al-Sabi could pass from father to son, or stick to a Harranian; Reiske, Montucla and Reinaud had called him a Sabian. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 24} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 25}
:::

## Legacy
His Zij remained a reference for later astronomers East and West, and passed into Europe through the Latin translations of [[person/robert-of-ketton|Robert]] and [[person/plato-of-tivoli|Plato of Tivoli]] and a Castilian version made under Alfonso X. {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 43} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 58} {cite:nallino-albatenii-opus-astronomicum p. 66} Nallino's survey of his influence among Muslims, Jews and Europeans is summarised on the page of the Zij.
