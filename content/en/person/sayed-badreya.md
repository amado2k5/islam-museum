## Summary
Sayed Badreya — El Sayed Badreya — is an Egyptian-American actor and filmmaker who has spent four decades working in Hollywood, both as a performer and as an advocate for how Arabs and Muslims appear on American screens. The Muslim 500 lists him in Arts & Culture, citing roles in Iron Man and The Dictator and his leadership of the Zoom in Focus workshops; Wikipedia records his long Hollywood filmography. {cite:muslim500-sayed-badreya} {cite:wikipedia-sayed-badreya}

## From Port Said to Hollywood
Badreya was born in 1957 in Port Said, Egypt, studied filmmaking at New York University and settled in Los Angeles. His early career ran the familiar course of the Arab actor in Hollywood — small parts, often typed — but he built it into one of the most durable Arab-American presences in American film and television, with dozens of screen credits. {cite:wikipedia-sayed-badreya}

## Screen work
His roles range across blockbusters and independent film: appearances in Independence Day, Three Kings, Stargate, Iron Man, The Dictator, and television work, alongside his own independent productions. His casting history is itself a document of Hollywood's evolving — and limited — imagination of Arab characters, a subject he has made central to his work off-camera. {cite:wikipedia-sayed-badreya} {cite:muslim500-sayed-badreya}

## Changing the portrayal
Badreya founded Zoom in Focus, a training and advocacy project teaching media literacy and filmmaking to young Arab and Muslim Americans, and has written and lectured on the industry's stereotyping — arguing that the Arab face on screen should be allowed the ordinary human range of any other. His independent films and documentaries address Arab-American life directly. {cite:muslim500-sayed-badreya} {cite:wikipedia-sayed-badreya}

## Significance
In the museum's record, Badreya stands for the working actor as advocate: the Egyptian who made a career inside the machine that types Arabs on screen and spent it pushing the machine to widen the frame — influence measured in roles played and in the next generation he trained. {cite:muslim500-sayed-badreya} {cite:wikipedia-sayed-badreya}

## The actor as activist
Badreya's career is also a history of the Muslim-American actor's dilemma: the roles offered to men with his face were for decades the roles Hollywood gave to men with his face, and he took them — and made a documentary about taking them. His film AmericanEast, which he wrote, produced and starred in, is the record of that tension: a story about Arab-American life told from inside, by the actor who had spent years playing its outside. The Muslim 500 lists him for that double presence: a working Hollywood actor who is also a chronicler of the typecasting he worked within. {cite:wikipedia-sayed-badreya} {cite:muslim500-sayed-badreya}

## The actor as archivist
Badreya's record is the museum's example of the actor who treats his own career as an archive: the Egyptian-American who understood that the roles he was offered were the roles the industry thought he deserved, and who made the case in film for an audience that had never been asked to see the pattern. The argument is his; the platform is the documentary; and the result — a Muslim actor whose work is a record of what Hollywood thought of him — is the profile his Muslim 500 entry records. {cite:muslim500-sayed-badreya}
