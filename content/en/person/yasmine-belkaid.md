## Summary
Yasmine Belkaid is an Algerian immunologist whose work changed how scientists understand the relationship between the immune system and the microbes that live in and on the human body. The Muslim 500 places her in Science & Technology; Wikipedia records her long career at the US National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases and her appointment in 2023 as President of the Institut Pasteur in Paris, where she took office in January 2024. {cite:muslim500-yasmine-belkaid} {cite:wikipedia-yasmine-belkaid}

## Formation
Belkaid was born in 1968 in Algiers, the daughter of academics; she studied biochemistry at the University of Algiers and then moved to France for a doctorate at the Pasteur Institute in Lille. A postdoctoral fellowship took her to the United States, where she joined the National Institutes of Health in Bethesda and eventually ran the NIAID Microbiome Program, building one of the leading laboratories in her field. {cite:wikipedia-yasmine-belkaid}

## Scientific contribution
Her research asked how the immune system keeps the peace with trillions of commensal microbes — the bacteria, viruses and fungi that inhabit skin and gut — while still fighting pathogens. Her laboratory showed that microbes resident in tissues help calibrate immune responses, that immune regulation is tissue-specific, and that the microbiome shapes wound healing and defence against infection. This body of work, built over three decades, helped turn the microbiome from a curiosity into a central theme of immunology. {cite:wikipedia-yasmine-belkaid}

## President of the Institut Pasteur
In March 2023 the Institut Pasteur announced Belkaid as its next president; she took office on 20 January 2024 for a six-year term, becoming the first woman to lead the foundation founded by Louis Pasteur in 1887. She moved to Paris while keeping research ties across the Atlantic, and her appointment was noted widely in the francophone and Algerian press. {cite:wikipedia-yasmine-belkaid}

## Honours and influence
Belkaid's honours include election to the US National Academy of Sciences and the French Academy of Sciences, and major prizes in immunology. The Muslim 500 cites her as a scientist whose findings transformed understanding of immunity and the microbiome — an Algerian researcher at the summit of two scientific worlds, American and French. {cite:muslim500-yasmine-belkaid} {cite:wikipedia-yasmine-belkaid}

## The microbiome before the word was famous
Colleagues describe Belkaid's contribution as one of framing: before the microbiome became a headline subject, her laboratory was already asking the immune system's oldest question — when to fight and when to tolerate — in the tissues where microbes actually live. The answers reshaped textbooks: immune regulation is not a central office but a set of local settlements negotiated at each barrier surface of the body. For a generation of young scientists, especially women from the Maghreb watching from Algiers, her career is also a map: from an Algerian laboratory, through Lille and Bethesda, to the presidency of the institute Pasteur himself founded — the arc that made her appointment a moment of national pride reported across Algeria's press. {cite:wikipedia-yasmine-belkaid} {cite:muslim500-yasmine-belkaid}

## The quiet authority
Belkaid's scientific persona is marked by a particular economy: she rarely speaks on issues beyond her expertise, and when she does — on the representation of women in science, on the value of long-term basic research — it is with the credibility of the scientist who has spent a career asking one deep question well. For the museum's record the point is simple: an Algerian scientist who became the leader of one of France's oldest research institutions, and whose work has changed how the world understands the relationship between the body and the life that lives on it. {cite:muslim500-yasmine-belkaid}
