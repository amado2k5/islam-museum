## Overview
Abu Uthman Amr ibn Bahr ibn Mahbub al-Kinani al-Laythi, known as al-Jahiz, a man of letters and scholar from Basra. He was born around 150 AH (767 CE) and died in 255 AH (869 CE). {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba} Al-Mas'udi says that he died in Basra in Muharram 255 AH, though it is also said 256 AH. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.568}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, describes him as a famous Arab prose writer, the author of works of adab, of Mu'tazili theology and of politico-religious polemics. It gives his full name as Abu Uthman Amr ibn Bahr al-Fukaymi al-Basri. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 410} The sources the museum holds differ on the year of his birth: the article puts it at about 160 AH/776 CE, while the other works cited below give about 150 AH/767 CE, or 148 AH in one edition of the Book of Animals. They agree that he died in Basra in the middle of the third Islamic century.

## Origins and the Basra years
According to the Encyclopaedia, he was born in [[place/basra|Basra]] into an obscure family of clients from the Banu Kinana, probably of Abyssinian origin. His nickname, the article explains, comes from a malformation of the eyes, a projecting cornea. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 410} Little is known of his childhood. The article says that from an early age a great desire for learning and a restless curiosity drew him towards an independent life and, to his family's despair, towards idleness. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 410}

He mixed with the groups that gathered in the mosque to discuss many questions, watched the philological discussions held at [[place/al-mirbad|the Mirbad]], and followed the lectures of the leading scholars of philology, lexicography and poetry, among them [[person/al-asmai|al-Asma'i]], Abu Ubayda and Abu Zayd. The article says that he soon mastered the Arabic language together with the usual traditional learning. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 410}

His quick mind won him a place in [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazili]] circles and in the salons of the educated middle class. There, the article says, conversation was often light but was also stirred by the questions then facing the Muslim conscience: in theology, how to reconcile faith and reason; in politics, the question of the caliphate, which the opponents of the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasids]] kept raising; the conflicts between Islamic sects; and the claims of non-Arabs. Observing the varied population of the city and reading the many kinds of books then circulating there broadened his outlook. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 410}

The Encyclopaedia judges that Basra in its prime shaped his mind decisively, leaving on it a rationalist and realist stamp. In its view he could be seen as the most complete representative of the city, since what he later learned in Baghdad changed his cast of mind very little, and Basra is, in its words, the continuous thread through all his works. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 410} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 412}

## Entry into the capital
The first sign of his literary activity that can be dated falls around 200 AH/815-6 CE. The article connects it with an event that shaped his later career: some works on the imamate, a subject it calls characteristic, earned him the praise of the caliph [[person/abd-allah-al-mamun|al-Ma'mun]], and with it the recognition of the capital that many provincials sought. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 410} From then on, without fully leaving Basra, he often stayed for long periods in [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] and later in [[place/samarra|Samarra]], devoting himself to writing, a good part of which has survived. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 410}

## Livelihood and patrons
The article says it is not really known what he lived on in Basra. In Baghdad he served as a scribe for three days and for a very short time assisted [[person/ibrahim-ibn-al-abbas-al-suli|Ibrahim ibn al-Abbas al-Suli]] at the chancellery. It considers it probable that he also taught, and notes that he himself reports an interview with the caliph [[person/al-mutawakkil|al-Mutawakkil]], who wished to entrust him with the education of his children but dismissed him because of his ugliness. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 410}

According to the Encyclopaedia, he held no regular post, though he admits to receiving large sums for some of his books, and for a time at least he drew an allowance from the diwan. The article suggests that, unlike some of his fellow countrymen, he did not live as a courtier but acted as a kind of unofficial adviser. His early writings on the caliphate, it says, were certainly meant to justify the Abbasid rise to power and were the first of a series of short treatises addressed to the authorities, if not inspired by them, on current events. Many begin with a formula such as "you asked me about such a question", and the article presumes that in many cases the question really was put to him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 410}

Although he never entered the caliphs' intimate circle, it says, he was in constant touch with leading political figures. He attached himself in turn to [[person/muhammad-ibn-abd-al-malik-al-zayyat|Muhammad ibn Abd al-Malik al-Zayyat]], whose fall from favour in 233 AH/847 CE almost proved fatal to both men; to the chief judge [[person/ahmad-ibn-abi-duad|Ahmad ibn Abi Duad]], who died in 240 AH/854 CE, and to his son Muhammad, who died in 239 AH/853 CE; and finally to [[person/al-fath-ibn-khaqan|al-Fath ibn Khaqan]], who died in 247 AH/861 CE. The article adds that he nevertheless kept ample independence. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 410} On doctrine, the article itself says that too little is known of his position to say more than to refer readers to its entry on the Mu'tazila. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 412}

## A writer and a man of letters
The Encyclopaedia holds that, although his place in the development of Muslim thought is far from negligible, he is chiefly of interest as a writer and an adib, since with him form is never overshadowed by content, even in technical works. It does not call him the first of the great Arab prose writers: in rhetoric [[person/ibn-al-muqaffa|Ibn al-Muqaffa]] and [[person/sahl-ibn-harun|Sahl ibn Harun]] were, it says, his masters. Yet it judges that he gave literary prose its most perfect form, as was recognised first by the politicians who used his talent for the Abbasid cause and then by Arab critics, who unanimously asserted his superiority and made his name a symbol of literary ability. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 412}

The article describes his writing as marked by deliberately contrived disorder and many digressions. His lively individual style, it says, lies in a concern for the exact term, a foreign word if need be, and in vivid phrases and sentences that are almost always unrhymed but balanced by restating one idea in two forms. What a modern reader might take for pointless repetition arose, in its reading, from the wish to be clearly understood and to give ordinary prose the symmetry of verse. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 412}

## Humour and range
The article says that for most literate Arabs he remained, if not a complete buffoon, then something of a jester. It attributes this reputation partly to his fame and his ugliness, which made him the hero of many anecdotes, and partly to his habit of slipping anecdotes, witty remarks and amusing comments into even his weightiest passages. Troubled by the dullness of many contemporary discussions, it says, he aimed deliberately at a lighter touch, and he often felt the need to defend humour. As the best example it names the Kitab al-Tarbi wa-l-tadwir, which it calls a masterpiece of ironic writing and a survey of questions to which his contemporaries gave habitual answers or none at all. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 412}

It adds that, without stepping outside the bounds of the faith, he claimed the right to examine accepted attitudes towards natural phenomena. At a time when medieval Arabic culture was taking shape, it says, he gathered what seemed to him most valuable, drawing on the Arab heritage, of which he was a passionate defender, and on Greek thought, while taking care to hold back the Persian tradition, which he thought too dangerous for the future of Islam. The article describes this undertaking as resting on a spirit of criticism and systematic doubt about everything not directly concerned with the dogma of Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 412}

## Afterlife
According to the article, the undertaking was largely narrowed and side-tracked in later centuries. He had admirers as notable as [[person/abu-hayyan-al-tawhidi|Abu Hayyan al-Tawhidi]], and also imitators and even counterfeiters who used his name to help their own works sell. The author judges that posterity kept only a deformed and shrunken image of him: at most a master of rhetoric, the founder of a Mu'tazili school whose disciples no one bothers to list, and the author of compilations drawn on for works of [[concept/adab|adab]] and for much recorded information on the Jahiliyya and the early centuries of Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 412}

## His works
Al-Mas'udi says that none of the transmitters is known to have had more books than he did, despite his Uthmani leanings. Among his books is "al-Bayan wa al-Tabyin", which is the most distinguished of them in his eyes: it combines prose and verse, selected poems and eloquent sermons. Others are "al-Hayawan" (Animals), "al-Tufayliyyin" and "al-Bukhala'" (The Misers). {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.568}

He was the pupil and the client of Ibrahim ibn Sayyar al-Nazzam; he took from him and learned from him. When he feared that the reader would tire, he moved from seriousness to levity. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.568}

The data attribute to him the book [[work/akhlaq-al-muluk-jahiz|Akhlaq al-Muluk]]. {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan}

## On the Imamate of the sons of al-Abbas
Al-Mas'udi says that he wrote a book on the Imamate of the sons of al-Abbas, arguing for the doctrine of the Rawandiyya, and in it mentions the action of Abu Bakr at Fadak and his story with Fatima and her claim to her inheritance. Al-Mas'udi says that this was not his doctrine or his belief, and that he did it as a jest and to show his skill. He also wrote "al-Uthmaniyya". {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.224}

## Scattered reports
Al-Mas'udi relates that he was mentioned to al-Mutawakkil for the instruction of one of his sons, and that when he saw him he disliked his appearance, and ordered him ten thousand dirhams and dismissed him. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.486}

His nephew Yamut ibn al-Mazra' relates the account of his illness: he would smear his right half with sandalwood and camphor because of its great heat, while he could feel nothing of the other half, which had gone numb and cold. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.568}

From his letter to Muhammad ibn Abd al-Malik al-Zayyat there are phrases on the effect of morals on hearts, which Ibn al-Nadim gives. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.527}

## In the introduction to Kitab al-Hayawan
In his edition of [[work/kitab-al-hayawan|Kitab al-Hayawan]], Muhammad Basil 'Uyun al-Sud takes al-Jahiz's biography from Shadharat al-Dhahab: Abu 'Uthman of Basra, the Mu'tazili, to whom the Jahiziyya among the Mu'tazila are traced; he studied with the judge [[person/abu-yusuf-al-qadi|Abu Yusuf]], [[person/thumama-ibn-ashras|Thumama ibn Ashras]] and [[person/al-nazzam|al-Nazzam]]; the author of al-Mughni quotes Tha'lab as calling him neither trustworthy nor reliable, while another said his finest works were the Book of Animals and al-Bayan wa-l-Tabyin. {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v1 p. 4} The editor gives his birth as 148 AH and his death as 250 AH; the extract adds that some say he lived until 255. {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v1 p. 1} {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v1 p. 4} At the opening of Kitab al-Hayawan al-Jahiz himself lists many of his books that his addressee had criticised, among them those on the tricks of thieves, the misers, the Qahtaniyya and the 'Adnaniyya, Arabs and clients, the idols, the createdness of the Qur'an, the refutation of the anthropomorphists, the composition of the Qur'an, and his Hashimiyyat epistles. {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v1 p. 7} {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v1 p. 8} {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v1 p. 10} {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v1 p. 11}
