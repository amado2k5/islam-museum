## Summary
Egyptian writer and man of letters (1876–1924), a pioneer of modern Arabic prose. Educated at al-Azhar then turning to journalism, he is famed for his sentimental essays in Al-Nazarat and Al-'Abarat, and for his literary adaptations from French such as Majdulin (Magdalene), Under the Shadow of the Crown and The Poet. {cite:wikipedia-mustafa-lutfi-al-manfaluti}

## Life and career

Mustafa Lutfi al-Manfaluti was born in 1876 in Manfalut, Upper Egypt, into a learned family; he memorised the Qur'an and studied at al-Azhar for years, then joined Ali Yusuf, owner of the newspaper Al-Mu'ayyad, and entered journalism. {cite:wikipedia-mustafa-lutfi-al-manfaluti}

In Al-Mu'ayyad and elsewhere he wrote the sentimental and social essays that made him the most famous essayist of his generation, writing until his early death at 47. {cite:wikipedia-mustafa-lutfi-al-manfaluti}

## Works

He collected his essays in Al-Nazarat (Glimpses) and Al-'Abarat (Tears), short pieces on sorrow, love and fate in a musical, emotion-laden language that moved generations of Arab readers; it was said they taught Arabic to the public more than any school. {cite:wikipedia-mustafa-lutfi-al-manfaluti}

He re-wrote French novels into Arabic without knowing French — working through intermediary translators — producing Majdulin, Fi Sabil al-Taj, Al-Sha'ir and Al-Fadila, among the most widely read books of the Nahda. {cite:wikipedia-mustafa-lutfi-al-manfaluti}

## Legacy

Al-Manfaluti stands as a bridge between scholarly prose and journalistic renewal prose: his language is elevated and direct together, and critics present him as the pioneer of sentimental literature in modern Arabic. {cite:wikipedia-mustafa-lutfi-al-manfaluti}

He died in 1924; his books stayed current in Arab book markets, and his style influenced writers from Taha Husayn to Mustafa Sadiq al-Rafi'i. {cite:wikipedia-mustafa-lutfi-al-manfaluti}
