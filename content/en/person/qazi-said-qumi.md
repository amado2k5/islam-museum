# Qadi Sa'id Qumi

Muhammad-Sa'id ibn Muhammad-Mofid Qumi (1640–after 1696), known as Qadi Sa'id and by the affectionate title Hakim Kuchak, "the Little Sage", was a philosopher, jurist, mystic, poet and physician of Safavid Qom.

## Life

He was born in Qom on 10 Dhu'l-Qa'da 1049 / 3 March 1640. His father, himself a scholar-physician, taught him medicine and philosophy. In philosophy he studied under Rajab Ali Tabrizi, a pupil of Mir Fendereski's circle; in the religious sciences under Fayz Kashani; and — the sources add, though WikiNoor notes the point is disputed — under Abd al-Razzaq Lahiji. He served as qadi of Qom, the office his name preserves, and as its shaykh al-Islam. Summoned to Isfahan by Shah Abbas II, he became a court physician and tutor to the courtiers and led the royal Friday prayer, before returning to Qom to teach and write for the rest of a long life.

His death date is unsettled, and the record preserves the readings rather than choosing silently: the older biographical tradition gives 1103 AH (the stub's 1692), the English article 18 Ramadan 1691, and the Persian article only "between 1100 and 1107 AH" at Qom. Encyclopaedia Iranica shows that he completed the third volume of his great commentary on 18 Ramadan 1107 / 20 April 1696, so he died after that date; his grave in Qom stands at the start of Chaharmardan street, near the shrine of Ali ibn Babawayh. His sons Sadr al-Din Muhammad Sharif and Abu Talib Sharif were scholars of Qom after him.

## Works and thought

His fame rests on the *Sharh Kitab al-Tawhid*: a vast commentary on the *Book of Divine Unity* of Ibn Babawayh (al-Saduq), the Imami traditionist, of which three volumes survive. In it he defended an uncompromising transcendence: God's essence is unknowable, and His attributes are neither simply identical with the essence nor other than it — the traditional Twelver position, held against the identification of essence and attributes taught in the school of Mulla Sadra. He likewise rejected Sadra's theory of substantial motion, accused him of sometimes failing the truth of monotheism, and defended the mystical monotheism of Ibn Arabi, whom he quoted constantly. A Persian treatise, *Kalid-i bihisht* ("The Key of Paradise"), argues the equivocalness of being in Tabrizi's line; his commentary on forty hadith reached the twenty-eighth; and he translated his teacher Tabrizi's Persian work on philosophy into Arabic as *al-Burhan al-qati' wa'l-nur al-sati'*. He also wrote the *Asrar al-salah* on the inner meanings of prayer, glosses on Aristotle's *Logic* and on the commentary to the *Isharat*, and a divan of poetry.

An earlier stub of this record repeated a claim that he studied under Mir Fendereski; no text read supports it, and the chronology forbids it — Fendereski died in 1640, the year Qumi was born — so the claim is recorded here only as refused.

## Faith

His affiliation is documented as Twelver (Imami) Shi'a on the texts' own wording: Iranica introduces him as a "Shiʿite philosopher, jurist, and mystic" and calls his theological position "traditional Twelver Shiʿite"; the Persian article names him a Shi'a marjaʿ and traditionist; WikiNoor counts him among the Imami scholars of the Safavid age.
