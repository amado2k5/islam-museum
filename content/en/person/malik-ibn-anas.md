## Overview
[[person/malik-ibn-anas|Malik ibn Anas]] ibn Malik ibn Abi Amir al-Asbahi al-Madani, whom al-Dhahabi calls the imam of the abode of emigration, is the author of the [[work/muwatta-malik|*Muwatta'*]] {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3145}. He was born in [[place/medina|Medina]] in 93 AH, learned from its scholars, and sat there to give legal opinions and to teach {cite:muwatta-maruf-1997 vol. 1 p.6}. He qualified to give legal opinions at twenty-one; students of knowledge came to him from all regions, and crowded around him in the caliphate of al-Rashid until he died {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3147}. Al-Haytham ibn Jamil reported that he was asked forty-eight questions and answered thirty-two of them with "I do not know" {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3152}. Al-Dhahabi relays the words of [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]]: "Malik is my teacher, and from him I took knowledge" {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3152}.

Al-Dhahabi relays from Ibn Sa'd Malik's account that al-Mansur resolved to have his books copied and sent to the provinces for people to act on, and that Malik advised him to leave people with what each land had chosen for itself {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3153}. [[person/ibn-al-nadim|Ibn al-Nadim]] says that Ja'far ibn Sulayman, the governor of Medina, had him flogged, and that he remained afterwards in high standing {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 280-281}. He died in 179 AH and was buried in [[place/al-baqi|al-Baqi']] {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3145} {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 280-281}. Ibn al-Nadim opens with his entry the section he gives to the Malikis and their books {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 280-281}. The editor of the *Muwatta'* writes that his students, above all those from North Africa, carried his book and his opinions until those lands knew no school of law but the Maliki {cite:muwatta-maruf-1997 vol. 1 p.6}.

## His lineage
His lineage is given as al-Asbahi of Himyar, that is Dhu Asbah. Al-Dhahabi says that there is much difference about his lineage, but that all agree he was an Arab of the Asbah, and that their origin is from Qahtan. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3151}

It is related from Ibn Ishaq that he claimed Malik and his family were clients of the Banu Taym; he was mistaken, and this was among the strongest reasons for Malik's denunciation of him and his criticism of him. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3151}

## Birth and the pursuit of knowledge
He was born, according to the more correct view, in the year 93 AH; it is also said 94 AH. He grew up in care and comfort. He sought knowledge as a youth after the death of al-Qasim and Salim, and took from Nafi', Sa'id al-Maqburi, Amir ibn Abdallah ibn al-Zubayr, Ibn al-Munkadir, al-Zuhri, Abdallah ibn Dinar and others. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3145} {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3151}

Al-Dhahabi says that the *Muwatta'* contains, from the teachers it names, about six hundred and thirty-six hadiths, six hadiths from unnamed sources, and that opinions differ on seventy-one hadiths. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3146} He also says that al-Daraqutni had a part on the parts of the *Muwatta'*, and that the names of those who transmitted from it come to about fourteen hundred. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3146}

## Giving legal opinions and his assembly
He qualified to give legal opinions and sat to instruct at the age of twenty-one. Some transmitted from him while he was young. Students of knowledge came to him from all regions at the end of al-Mansur's reign and after, and they crowded around him in the caliphate of al-Rashid until he died. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3147}

His assembly was one of dignity and majesty, with no dispute, no clamour and no raised voice. He would answer strangers one hadith after another, and sometimes let some of them read to him. He had a scribe who copied his books and read them to the group. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3149}

It is reported that he did not attend the congregational prayer for twenty-five years. When he was asked what prevented him, he said: "The fear that I see something wrong and have to change it." He used to say that not everyone can speak his excuse. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3150} {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3149}

## His method in transmission
Malik said that he had heard many hadiths from Ibn Shihab which he never related. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3149} Ibn Uyayna relates that Malik would only take a hadith that was sound, and would only transmit from a trustworthy source, and he said: "I do not see Medina but that it will be ruined of knowledge after his death." {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3151}

It is related from him that he said: "Knowledge is not to be taken from four: a fool who openly displays folly, however much he transmits; a man of innovation who calls to his own desires; one who lies in the hadith of people; and..." {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3150} It is also related that he would not give a legal opinion until he had asked someone more learned than himself. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3149}

He is attributed with the saying "I do not know". It is said that someone asked him forty-eight questions, and he answered thirty-two of them with "I do not know". {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3152}

## His peers and pupils
Among his peers whom al-Dhahabi names are Ma'mar, Ibn Jurayj, Abu Hanifa, Amr ibn al-Harith, al-Awza'i, Shu'ba, al-Thawri, al-Layth and Hammad ibn Zayd. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3146}

Among those who transmitted from him, as al-Dhahabi counts them, are Abdallah ibn al-Mubarak, Abdallah ibn Wahb, Abd al-Rahman ibn Mahdi, Sufyan ibn Uyayna, Waki', Yahya al-Qattan, al-Shafi'i, Abd al-Razzaq, Abu Nu'aym al-Fadl ibn Dukayn, Abd al-Rahman ibn al-Qasim, and Muhammad ibn al-Hasan the jurist. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3146} {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3147}

## His standing among the scholars
Ibn Uyayna said that Malik was the scholar of the people of the Hijaz and the proof of his age, and al-Shafi'i said that when the scholars are mentioned, Malik is the star. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3147} Al-Shafi'i also said: "Malik is my teacher, and from him I took knowledge." {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3152}

Abd al-Rahman ibn Mahdi said: "The leaders of the people in their time are four: al-Thawri, Malik, al-Awza'i and Hammad ibn Zayd." {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3152}

Al-Dhahabi says that al-Shafi'i debated Muhammad ibn al-Hasan about Malik and Abu Hanifa, and said that Malik was more learned in the Book of God and its abrogating and abrogated verses, in the Sunna of the Prophet and in the disagreements of the Companions, as is related from him. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3152}

## His dealings with the caliphs
Al-Dhahabi says that al-Mahdi sent Malik two or three thousand dinars, and then al-Rabi' asked him to return to Baghdad. Malik said that the Prophet said "Medina is better for them, if they only knew", and the money remained with him as it was. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3149}

It is related that al-Mahdi sent his two sons Harun and Musa to him to listen to him, and he did not respond to them. When al-Mahdi spoke to him, Malik said: "Knowledge is brought to its people", so they went to him and read to him. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3149}

When Harun al-Rashid came to Medina intending to perform the pilgrimage, Malik drew near to him and honoured him. Then Ya'qub Abu Yusuf told him that he had asked a question and had not been answered. Malik said that if he saw him sitting with the people of falsehood, he should come to answer him with them. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3149} Al-Rashid asked him to read to him and to his sons, and he refused until the common people were sent out, so al-Rashid ordered Ma'n ibn Isa to read to him. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3149}

It is mentioned that al-Mansur wanted him to sit, and that the Hashemites used to kiss his hand and foot, and Malik said: "God protected me from that." {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3150}

## His appearance
Al-Dhahabi relays from Isa ibn Umar that he had never seen a whiteness or redness more beautiful than the face of Malik; and from others that he was tall and large, with a great head, fair, with white hair and beard, and he did not shave his moustache, considering shaving it a mutilation. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3150} {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3151} It is related from al-Waqidi that he was of medium height, did not dye his hair, and did not enter the bath. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.3151}

## The Encyclopaedia of Islam on his life and sources
The second edition of the *Encyclopaedia of Islam* describes Malik as a Muslim jurist, the imam of the Maliki school of law that is named after him {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 282}. It begins with the problem of evidence. The oldest long account is that of [[person/muhammad-ibn-sad|Ibn Sa'd]], which rested on [[person/al-waqidi|al-Waqidi]] and placed Malik in the sixth class of the Medinan successors. That account is lost, because the manuscript has a gap, but the article says most of it can be rebuilt from quotations in [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]], in the *Kitab al-Uyun*, in [[person/ibn-khallikan|Ibn Khallikan]] and in [[person/al-suyuti|al-Suyuti]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 282} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 283}.

According to the article, the short notes in [[person/ibn-qutayba|Ibn Qutayba]] and the somewhat fuller ones in the [[work/al-fihrist|*Fihrist*]] both depend on Ibn Sa'd, and so does al-Tabari's entry on Malik in his *Dhayl al-Mudhayyal*. Al-Sam'ani keeps only the legendary version of an incident that is otherwise well established, and Ibn Khallikan and [[person/al-nawawi|al-Nawawi]] carry more legendary features, though each preserves some isolated facts of importance. The article describes al-Suyuti's compilation as drawn from Ibn Sa'd and from later works that it judges unreliable, and says that the later *manakib* literature, such as al-Zawawi's, has no independent value {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 283}.

## Name, descent and birth
The article gives his name as Abu Abd Allah Malik ibn Anas ibn Malik ibn Abi Amir, followed by a longer chain that the scanned text does not give in full. It says he belonged to Himyar, who are counted within the Banu Taym ibn Murra, that is Taym Quraysh {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 283}.

The date of his birth is not known. The dates given range between 90 and 97 AH, which is 708 to 716 CE, and the article treats them as hypotheses that are probably about right. Ibn Sa'd already states that he spent three years in his mother's womb, and Ibn Kutayba says a little over two. The article calls this a legend and traces it to a wrong reading of something Malik is said to have said about the possible length of pregnancy. It also notes a tradition preserved by [[person/al-tirmidhi-muhammad-ibn-isa|al-Tirmidhi]] in which the Prophet is said to have foretold the coming of Malik, [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]] and al-Shafi'i {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 283}.

Al-Sam'ani names Malik's grandfather and his paternal uncle as transmitters of hadith, so, the article remarks, there is nothing odd in Malik becoming a student. The *Kitab al-Aghani* says he first wished to be a singer and turned to the study of law on his mother's advice, because of his ugliness. The article reads such anecdotes as little more than evidence that someone did not admire him particularly {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 283}.

## Teachers and studies
Very little reliable information survives about his studies, according to the article. It regards as hardly an invention, though found only in rather late sources, the report that he studied law with [[person/rabia-al-ray|Rabi'a ibn Farrukh]], who practised personal reasoning in Medina and was called Rabi'at al-Ra'y. Later legends raise the number of his teachers to implausible figures, 900 including 300 successors. He is said to have learned the Qur'anic readings from Nafi' ibn Nu'aym {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 283}.

He transmitted traditions from [[person/muhammad-ibn-shihab-al-zuhri|al-Zuhri]], [[person/nafi-mawla-ibn-umar|Nafi', the freedman of Ibn Umar]], [[person/abu-al-zinad|Abu al-Zinad]], [[person/hisham-ibn-urwa|Hisham ibn Urwa]], [[person/yahya-ibn-said-al-ansari|Yahya ibn Sa'id]], [[person/abd-allah-ibn-dinar|Abd Allah ibn Dinar]], Muhammad ibn al-Munkadir, Abu al-Zubayr and others. The article cautions that a chain of transmitters is not by itself proof that he studied with the person named, and mentions a list of 95 teachers in al-Suyuti {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 283}.

## The rising of 145 AH and his dealings with the caliphs
The article calls his involvement in the rising of the Alid claimant [[person/muhammad-al-nafs-al-zakiyya|Muhammad ibn Abd Allah]] in 145 AH, 762 CE, a fixed chronological point in a life spent mostly in Medina. In 144 AH, 761 CE, the caliph [[person/al-mansur|al-Mansur]] used him to send the Hasanids of [[place/mecca|Mecca]] a demand that the brothers Muhammad and Ibrahim, suspected of aiming at supreme power, be handed over. The article reads this as showing that Malik already enjoyed general esteem and was not openly hostile to the government. He was even rewarded from the proceeds of the property confiscated from the brothers' captured father, Abd Allah. The mission failed {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 283}.

When Muhammad seized Medina in 145 AH, Malik gave a legal opinion that the oath of allegiance to al-Mansur was not binding, since it had been given under compulsion, and the article says many who would otherwise have held back then joined Muhammad. Malik took no active part and stayed at home. After the failure of the [[event/nafs-al-zakiyya-revolt|revolt]], which the article dates to 147 AH, 763 CE, he was flogged by the governor of Medina, Ja'far ibn Sulayman, and suffered a dislocated shoulder. The article says this is thought to have raised his standing further, and that it sees no reason to doubt that the stories of Abu Hanifa's ill-treatment in prison are based on this episode in Malik's life {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 283}.

The article adds that he later made his peace with the government. In 160 AH, 777 CE, the caliph [[person/al-mahdi|al-Mahdi]] consulted him about structural changes to the sanctuary at Mecca, and in the year of Malik's death [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]] visited him during the pilgrimage. It treats the visit as certain, but calls the details in the *Kitab al-Uyun* somewhat legendary and those in al-Suyuti fantastic. It judges quite fictitious the story, found as early as Ibn Sa'd and told again of al-Mahdi and of al-Rashid, that a caliph wished to make the *Muwatta'* canonical and gave up the idea at Malik's urging {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 283}.

## Death and legend
According to the article, Malik died at about the age of 85 after a short illness, in 179 AH, 796 CE, in Medina, and was buried in al-Baqi'. The governor of Medina, Abd Allah ibn Zaynab, led the funeral prayer, and Ibn Khallikan preserves an elegy for him by Ja'far ibn Ahmad al-Sarraj. Two modern works reproduce pictures of the dome over his grave {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 283}.

Ibn Sa'd, certainly following al-Waqidi, gave a fairly full description of Malik's appearance, habits and way of life. The article says this cannot claim to be authentic, nor can the sayings attributed to him, which grew in number as time went on, and that the few certain facts about him have been buried under a mass of legends. It also calls the story of his meeting with the young al-Shafi'i apocryphal, an expression of the view later taken of the relation between the two imams {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 283} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284}. Among those who handed down traditions from him it names Abd Allah ibn al-Mubarak, al-Awza'i, Ibn Jurayj, Hammad ibn Zayd, al-Layth ibn Sa'd, Shu'ba, al-Thawri and Ibn Uyayna, with al-Zuhri and Yahya ibn Sa'id among his own teachers {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 283}.

## The Muwatta' and his other works
The article calls the *Muwatta'* Malik's great work and, setting aside the legal corpus of [[person/zayd-ibn-ali|Zayd ibn Ali]], the earliest surviving Muslim law book. Its aim, as the article states it, is to survey law and justice, ritual and religious practice according to the consensus of Medina and the sunna usual there, and to set a theoretical standard for matters those had not settled. Under the early Abbasids there was a practical interest in a smoothed path, which is roughly what *muwatta'* means, through wide disagreement even on elementary questions. Malik wished to serve it on the basis of Hijazi practice. Tradition, which he interprets from the viewpoint of practice, is for him a means and not an end, so older jurists are hardly ever quoted except as authorities for Malik himself. Because he was concerned with documenting the sunna and not with criticising its form, the article says he is careless about the order of his material, and it sees in the book the transition from the simple law of the earliest period to the pure science of hadith {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284}.

Malik was not alone in this. Al-Majishun is said to have dealt with the consensus of the Medinan scholars without citing traditions, and several Medinan scholars of the same time are recorded as writing in the style of the *Muwatta'*, though nothing of theirs has survived. The article attributes the success of Malik's book to its taking an average view on disputed points {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284}.

Malik did not fix a definitive text, whether oral or by handing over a copy, and the recensions differ in places very much. The article gives two reasons: transmitters of that age took liberties with the wording, and Malik probably did not always teach in exactly the same form. It still holds that the *Muwatta'*, which goes back to Malik himself and is found in all recensions, shows that he meant to create a work in the later sense. Many later regarded it as canonical, and numerous legends deal with its origin. Fifteen recensions are known, of which only two survive in full. About five were studied in Spain in the third and fourth centuries AH, and twelve were still available to al-Rudani, who died in 1094 AH, 1693 CE {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284}.

The article singles out three. One is the widely printed version of Yahya ibn Yahya al-Masmudi, who died in 234 AH, 848 CE, which has many commentaries, among them al-Zurqani's. Another is that of [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-hasan-al-shaybani|Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Shaybani]], who died in 189 AH, 805 CE; the article calls it both an edition and a critical development, since al-Shaybani gives at the end of most chapters his own views and Abu Hanifa's. The third is the recension of [[person/abdallah-ibn-wahb-al-misri|Abd Allah ibn Wahb]], who died in 197 AH, 813 CE, whose fairly comprehensive quotations survive in two fragments of al-Tabari's *Kitab Ikhtilaf al-fuqaha'* and follow Yahya's version closely {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284}.

Whether Malik wrote anything else is, in the article's words, doubtful, and it calls the *Fihrist*'s statements about further works vague. It names a book of the *sunan*, a book of rites, a book of sessions, a treatise on judgements and a treatise on legal opinions, and says that the authenticity of all of them is uncertain even if they go back to his immediate pupils. A work in the Gotha collection it calls certainly apocryphal. A commentary on the Qur'an, a treatise on predestination, a book of the stars and a book of secrets are described as typical of apocryphal literature. The treatise of advice to the caliph al-Rashid, already mentioned in the *Fihrist*, is also viewed with strong suspicion. The article says it looks like a Maliki counterpart of the [[work/kitab-al-kharaj-abu-yusuf|*Kitab al-Kharaj*]] of [[person/abu-yusuf-al-qadi|Abu Yusuf]], and that even al-Suyuti doubted it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284}.

The article names two other main sources for his teaching. The more important is the [[work/al-mudawwana-al-kubra|*Mudawwana*]] of [[person/sahnun-ibn-said|Sahnun]], who died in 240 AH, 854 CE, which holds replies by [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-al-qasim|Ibn al-Qasim]], who died in 191 AH, 807 CE, according to Malik's school or to his own opinion, together with traditions and opinions of Ibn Wahb. The second is al-Tabari, who quotes Malik's traditions and opinions often in his commentary on the legal verses of the Qur'an {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 284} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 285}.

## His place in the history of law
The article places Malik at a stage in the growth of law when reasoning is still occasional and for a particular purpose, and the legal thought of Islam has not yet become jurisprudence. In place he represents the custom of Medina, where it says the decisive foundations of Muslim law were laid. A main feature of the legal thought in the *Muwatta'*, it says, is that religious and moral ideas permeate the whole of legal life. The material they permeate is the customary law of Medina, which the article describes as by no means primitive but suited to a highly developed trading community. In Malik it appears sometimes as sunna, meaning use and wont, and sometimes behind the practice of Medina that he ascertains with great care {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 285}.

The article judges that the Islamisation of law was largely complete before Malik, and that his own achievement can only have lain in developing a system, though how large his share was cannot be established for lack of comparison material. It explains the book's surprising success by its recording the usual consensus of Medina with little work of the author's own, and so coming to be authoritative as an expression of compromise. On this view the *Muwatta'* is evidence of the stage the law had reached more than of Malik's individual activity, and the article adds that this average character may be just what he aimed at. It says the high esteem he enjoys in the older sources is justified by his strict criticism of hadith and not by work in the interests of law, and that even this only means he kept within the later consensus. That al-Shafi'i gave him special attention among the Medinan scholars, as in his book on the differences between Malik and al-Shafi'i, the article explains by his having been Malik's pupil {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 285}.

On his method, the article says hadith is by no means Malik's highest or only court of appeal. He gives the actual, undoubted practice of Medina preference over traditions when these differ, and where neither a Medinan tradition nor a Medinan consensus exists he lays down the law independently. In other words he exercises personal reasoning, to such an extent that he is occasionally reproached with agreement with the Iraqis. A later legend hostile to such reasoning says he repented of it on his deathbed. The article thinks it scarcely to be supposed that he diverged seriously in his results from his Medinan contemporaries {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 285}.

## His pupils and the school
In the strict sense, the article says, Malik no more formed a school than Abu Hanifa did. As evidence it cites the oldest names, People of the Hijaz and People of Iraq, compared with the Companions of al-Shafi'i. Its account of the origin of the [[group/maliki-madhhab|Maliki school]] is that once a regular Shafi'i school had formed, the two older groups also had to combine into regular schools under a typical representative of average views as head, Malik or Abu Hanifa. His personal esteem in his lifetime contributed, but the article attributes his elevation to the head of a school mainly to his pupils. It sees traces of this process in the varying classification of old jurists as of the Hijaz school or as independent *mujtahids* {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 285}.

## For young visitors
Malik ibn Anas, the imam of the abode of emigration, was born in Medina, learned there from the great scholars, and its people came to him for legal opinions. He compiled the book *al-Muwatta'*, and used to say "I do not know" when he did not know the answer.

## Notes for researchers
- The entry is based on al-Dhahabi's *Siyar A'lam al-Nubala'* (Bayt al-Afkar al-Dawliyya edition, 2004), pages 3145–3152, and on a local photographed copy. We have not yet read the following pages (from 3153), which contain the details of his ordeal and his death, and nothing from them is stated here; the death date of 179 AH is taken from the head of the entry.
- Al-Dhahabi gives several views on his birth (93 or 94 AH). We have not chosen between them. The Gregorian date of birth is derived.
- The details of the *Muwatta'* in respect of the number of hadiths and the names of its transmitters are taken from al-Dhahabi. We have not yet checked them against the standard *Muwatta'* edition (Ma'ruf, 1997).
- The attribution of "I do not know" to Malik, al-Shafi'i and Ibn Uyayna are sayings al-Dhahabi relays from others, and have not been checked against originals.
- The accounts of assemblies and caliphs are relayed by al-Dhahabi with chains, and the chains need review before they are relied on for anything beyond a reference.

## For Sprouts
A revered scholar of Medina who compiled the Prophet's traditions and jurisprudence in his famous book, al-Muwatta.

## For Explorers
Malik ibn Anas, known as the Imam of Medina, was one of the four great jurists of Sunni Islam. Born and residing in Medina, he taught Hadith in the Prophet's Mosque and authored al-Muwatta, combining authentic prophetic traditions with the living practice of the community of Medina, renowned for his reverence and caution in legal rulings.

## For Scholars
Abu Abd Allah Malik ibn Anas (93–179 AH / 711–795 CE), the Imam of Dar al-Hijra (Medina) and founder of the Maliki school of law, standing as one of the formative figures of 8th-century Islamic jurisprudence and Hadith scholarship. Studying under premier Successors such as Ibn Shihab al-Zuhri and Nafi, he compiled 'al-Muwatta', the earliest extant standardized legal and Hadith compendium. His jurisprudential methodology is notable for treating the continuous communal praxis of Medina ('amal ahl al-Madina) as a vital normative source alongside the Qur'an, Sunna, and consensus.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lecture series Al Dinu Al Qayem

Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy recounts that al-Shafi'i, then about nine years old by his account, attended Malik's assembly in the Prophet's mosque. He recounts that a man swore divorce on his wife and then asked Malik; the young al-Shafi'i answered first, and Malik ruled that the wife was not divorced (needs_source: the answer and the ruling are unclear in the automatic transcript) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep13 00:46:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep13 00:46:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep13 00:47:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep13 00:47:30}.

## From Omar Abdelkafy's programme Wa Nafsin wa ma Sawwaha
Omar Abdelkafy relates that the Commander of the Faithful asked Malik to write a book avoiding the severities of Ibn Umar, the concessions of Ibn Abbas and the oddities of Ibn Mas'ud, and he wrote al-Muwatta'; when the caliph wanted to copy and distribute it, Malik asked that it be placed on the roof of the Ka'ba for a year to see how sincere he had been, and when they returned after the next hajj not a letter was gone, and he said he knew then that he had been sincere in it (needs_source: the story is the Sheikh's narration). {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy wa-nafsun-ma-sawaha/ep20 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy wa-nafsun-ma-sawaha/ep20 00:09:00}
