Abu Ishaq Ibrahim ibn Yahya al-Zarqali was born in 1029 in a village near Toledo. He trained as a metalsmith and engraver of scientific instruments - his nickname al-Naqqash, "the engraver", is the source of his Latin name Arzachel (also written Azarquiel). From 1048 he worked in Toledo as part of the team of the astronomer Sa'id al-Andalusi, first for al-Ma'mun, the Taifa ruler of Toledo, and later for al-Mu'tamid of Seville {cite:wikipedia-al-zarqali-en}.

## Observations and the motion of the solar apogee

Al-Zarqali observed the Sun for twenty-five years from 1050, and published his results in works written in Arabic and translated into Latin, Hebrew and Castilian. His almanac, composed between 1088 and 1092, gave the true daily positions of the Sun for those years. He was the first astronomer to demonstrate the motion of the solar apogee relative to the fixed stars, measuring it at 12.04 arc-seconds per year - strikingly close to the modern figure of 11.77 {cite:wikipedia-al-zarqali-en}.

## The safiha

The [[invention/universal-astrolabe-al-zarqali|universal zarqaliyya plate]] replaces local latitude plates with coordinate grids and a horizon ruler: an astrolabe improvement, not its invention from nothing {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 47–53}. Puig distinguishes the 100-chapter zarqaliyya treatise from the 60-chapter shakkaziyya treatise, and the former's Castilian translation from the latter's Latin and Hebrew translations; these variants should not be collapsed into one translation story {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Universal means applicable across latitudes, not automatic location without reading and calculation {cite:zarqaliyya-bir-2020 pp. 47–53}.

Bibliotheca Alexandrina's Antiquities Museum names al-Zarqali in its Muslim-astronomers section, the documented biographical basis for the identity update here; it does not attest a personal legal school {cite:bibalex-astrolabe-949 The Role of Muslim Astronomers}. Sunni is the museum's explicit default, not a quotation from al-Zarqali; the Puig biography read names no contrary affiliation {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}.

## The independent equatorium

[[invention/equatorium-al-zarqali|Al-Zarqali's equatorium]] differs from the universal plate: deferents and associated circles occupy both sides of one plate, epicycles a second {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Puig names Ibn al-Samh's circa1025/1026 predecessor and dates construction/use treatises approximately1080/1081 and1081/1082, dedicated to al-Mu'tamid; these clues alone do not settle the construction city {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp. 1258–1260}. Mercury's oval representation within this geometry is not evidence that he formulated Kepler's law of planetary orbits, an exaggeration explicitly cautioned against by MacTutor {cite:zarqali-mactutor Mercury discussion}.

## Tables and geography

Al-Zarqali contributed to the Toledan Tables, and revised al-Khwarizmi's astronomical tables, recomputing many of their longitudes from the meridian of Toledo. Against Ptolemy, he measured the length of the Mediterranean as 42 degrees rather than Ptolemy's 62 - much nearer the modern value. Studying Mercury, he noted that the centre of its eccentric deferent traces an oval, rather than a circular, path {cite:wikipedia-al-zarqali-en}{cite:wikipedia-al-zarqali-ar}.

## Death

When Alfonso VI of Castile took Toledo in 1085, al-Zarqali fled; whether he settled in Cordoba or died at Mocejón or in a refugee camp is not known. Modern references give his death as 1100 at Cordoba, while older references - and one of the two dates carried by Wikidata - give 1087; this record follows 1100 and flags the disagreement {cite:wikipedia-al-zarqali-en}{cite:wikidata-al-zarqali}.

## The solar model and numerical limits

The [[invention/zarqali-moving-solar-apogee|moving solar-apogee model]] is distinct from the universal plate and equatorium: Puig attributes an independently moving apogee and variable-eccentricity solar model to a lost work summarising twenty-five years of observations, probably written circa1075–1080 {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}. Its contents are indirectly known through Arabic and Latin sources, not an original manuscript inspected here; it is not the separate fixed-star-motion treatise surviving in Hebrew {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}.

Puig and MacTutor give about one degree in279 years; modern conversion3600÷279 yields approximately12.9032 arcseconds annually {cite:zarqali-puig-bea solar-work paragraph} {cite:zarqali-mactutor solar-work paragraph}. This is not numerically identical to12.04 in the earlier attributed account above: the accounts are retained without merging their numbers or certifying modern accuracy. The detailed article separates reporting a lost work, checking numerical units and comparing modern explanation without attributing that explanation to its medieval author {cite:zarqali-puig-bea pp.1258–1260}.
