## Summary
Cheikh Anta Diop (29 December 1923 – 7 February 1986) was a Senegalese historian, physicist, anthropologist and politician, born into an aristocratic Muslim Wolof family of the Mouride brotherhood. His works made him one of the most controversial historians of the twentieth century: he argued that ancient Egypt was a Black African civilisation and that African culture formed a connected unity. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en} {cite:wikidata-cheikh-anta-diop}

The main university of Dakar now bears his name: Cheikh Anta Diop University. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

## Early life and education
Diop was born at Thieytou in the Diourbel Region of Senegal and was educated in a traditional Islamic school; he obtained the colonial equivalent of the French baccalauréat in Senegal before moving to Paris. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en} {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-ar}

## Studies in Paris
Diop went to Paris in 1946, aged twenty-three, enrolling first in higher mathematics and then in philosophy, in which he took his licence in 1948, followed by two diplomas in chemistry in 1950. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

In 1948 he co-edited, with the art historian Madeleine Rousseau, a special issue of the journal Musée vivant for the centenary of the abolition of slavery in the French colonies, contributing the article "When will we be able to speak of an African Renaissance?", which proposed rebuilding African culture on the basis of ancient Egypt as European culture had been built on Greece and Rome. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

He registered successive doctoral thesis topics under professors including Gaston Bachelard and Marcel Griaule, and from 1957 specialised in nuclear physics at the laboratories of the Collège de France and the Institut Pierre et Marie Curie; he translated parts of Einstein's theory of relativity into his native Wolof. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

In 1954 he published his thesis as Nations nègres et culture (Negro Nations and Culture), arguing that ancient Egypt had been populated by Black people and that Egyptian language and culture had later spread to West Africa, which made him one of the most controversial historians of his time. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

He obtained his doctorate in 1960 with a comparative study of the political and social systems of Europe and Africa from antiquity to the formation of modern states. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

## Scientific work and UNESCO
Back in Senegal, Diop founded a radiocarbon dating laboratory at the Institut fondamental d'Afrique noire; the Arabic article likewise records his interest in African archaeology and the specialised laboratory he established. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-ar} {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

He served on UNESCO's International Scientific Committee for the Drafting of a General History of Africa and wrote its opening chapter on the origins of the ancient Egyptians, presenting at the 1974 Cairo symposium his anthropological and historical evidence for a close affinity between the ancient Egyptians and Sub-Saharan African peoples. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

His conclusions at the symposium met responses from strong objection to enthusiastic support; a UNESCO multidisciplinary review in 2025 concluded that the data accumulated over the following three decades had aligned in important respects with Diop's views. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

## Political activity
Diop was active in Senegalese politics as a rival of President Léopold Sédar Senghor, and helped found opposition political blocs including the National Front and the National Democratic Rally, as the Arabic article records. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-ar}

His books on the cultural unity of Black Africa and on the foundations of a future African federal state were part of his political programme as much as scholarly works. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

## Death
Diop died at Dakar on 7 February 1986, as Wikidata and the English article record. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en} {cite:wikidata-cheikh-anta-diop}

## Positions and criticism
This section sets out positions as reported in the sources. It is not a verdict by the museum.

**Supporters.** The African-American historian John Henrik Clarke called Diop "one of the greatest historians to emerge in the African world in the twentieth century", and historians such as Bethwell Ogot held that Diop wrested Egyptian civilisation from the Egyptologists and restored it to the mainstream of African history. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

Diop was awarded, jointly with W. E. B. Du Bois, the prize for the most influential African intellectual at the first World Festival of Black Arts in 1966. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

**Critics.** Other historians have held that his work has generally been discredited, some characterising it as Afrocentric pseudohistory; linguists and classicists criticised his method in comparing languages and his use of classical sources, and his framework has been described as trapped within dated models of racial classification. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

## Writings
His works include Nations nègres et culture (1954), L'unité culturelle de l'Afrique noire (1959), L'Afrique noire pré-coloniale (1960), Antériorité des civilisations nègres: mythe ou vérité historique? (1967), The African Origin of Civilization: Myth or Reality in English (1974), and Civilisation ou barbarie (c. 1981). {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

## Legacy and reach
Diop's work is considered foundational to the theory of Afrocentricity, though he never described himself as an Afrocentrist, and his questions about cultural bias in scientific research contributed greatly to the postcolonial turn in the study of African civilisations. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}

The University of Dakar was renamed Cheikh Anta Diop University in his honour, carrying his name into the daily life of his country's capital. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-anta-diop-en}
