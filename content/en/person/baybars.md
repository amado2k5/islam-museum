## Overview
Sultan al-Zahir Rukn al-Din Baybars al-Bunduqdari was the fourth sultan of the Bahri line of the [[polity/mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk sultanate]], in the count of the Encyclopaedia of Islam. A Kipchak Turkish slave soldier who rose through the ranks of the last Ayyubid rulers, he took the throne in 658 AH, answered the Mongol threat, brought most of the Frankish coast of Syria under Mamluk control, and gave the Cairo sultanate a caliph. The same article calls him a legend in his own lifetime. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1184} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1186}

On the day of Ayn Jalut he was at the head of the army of [[person/qutuz|al-Muzaffar Qutuz]], and he pursued the Tatars as far as Aleppo. Ibn al-Imad says that Qutuz promised him Aleppo and then went back on his promise, and estrangement began between them. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.299}

Al-Suyuti says that he agreed with a group of the amirs to kill Qutuz, and they killed him on the road in 658 AH, and he took the sultanate and was titled al-Malik al-Zahir. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.720}

He established the caliphate in Egypt in Rajab 659 AH, and al-Mustansir was given the allegiance. In 674 AH he sent an army to Nubia, which took its king captive, and the jizya was imposed on the people of Dongola. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.723}

Ibn al-Imad says he was given the sultanate on the seventeenth of Dhu al-Qa'da 658 AH, witnessed the battle of al-Mansura, and died in Damascus on the twenty-eighth of Muharram 676 AH, having taken forty fortresses from the Franks. He says that if it were not for his oppression and tyranny at times he would be counted among the just kings; this is Ibn al-Imad's judgement. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.358}

## Origins and early career
The Encyclopaedia of Islam reports that he is said to have been born in 620 AH, that is 1233, and to have belonged to a group of Kipchak Turkish slaves bought by the Ayyubid sultan [[person/al-malik-al-salih-ayyub|al-Malik al-Salih]]. His first master was Aydakin Bundukdar, which is the origin of his name al-Bunduqdari; the article adds that Marco Polo's work gives the same name in the form "Bondocdaire", sultan of Babylonia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1184}

He first appears in the record in 636 AH, in prison at Karak with his master al-Salih. Some months later he was fighting in Syria for the sultan of Egypt, which the article describes as a hard apprenticeship in soldiering, with the intrigues of the last Ayyubid princes as a bleak lesson alongside it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1184}

His first notable military act, according to the article, was taking command of the Egyptian army on the field at al-Mansura, which ended in the decisive victory at Fariskur and the capture of [[person/louis-ix|Louis IX]] of France; see also the museum record of the [[event/battle-mansura-647|battle of Mansura]]. The article then says that it was at his instigation that [[person/turanshah|Turan-Shah]] was assassinated in 648 AH, 1250, the plot being staged as resistance to the enemy. It judges the murder odious, calls the sultan's responsibility undeniable, and sees in it the beginning of the Mamluk sultans' rule. These are the article's own judgements, not findings of the museum. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1184}

## Ayn Jalut and the accession
The article explains that when Qutuz took power the Mongol hordes began their invasion of Syria. The bloody encounter at [[event/ayn-jalut-658|Ayn Jalut]] in Palestine saw Qutuz distinguish himself with great valour, and the Mongol general was killed. The article attributes the decisive Egyptian success to the tenacity of a sultan who had managed to field an army against the odds, and says Baybars fought in the vanguard. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1185}

Little is known, it says, about the sequence of events that led to Qutuz's death. He was killed in his tent by a group of officers that included Baybars, and Baybars gained the throne in 658 AH, 1260. The author mentions clashing ambitions and reflects that the glory of the sovereign hides the treachery of the officer; this is the author's remark and one reading among the accounts above. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1185}

## Consolidating the state
From 659 AH, 1261, the new sultan strengthened the strongpoints from which he would later attack. The article says every citadel the Mongols had destroyed, from Hims to the Hawran, was repaired and supplied with food and ammunition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1185}

He also wanted fast information and fast command. He set up a regular postal service that brought him news from every part of the realm twice a week; in ordinary conditions a despatch took four days from Cairo to Damascus, and more urgent news travelled by pigeon. He had the arsenals rebuilt in full and had warships and cargo vessels built. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1185}

The article compares him with [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Saladin]] on two points, unified command and victorious war against the Franks, and judges the comparison to favour Baybars. It says he ended feudal arrangements rather than creating them, having no family to provide for, and that, unlike Saladin's offensive, his conquests were made to last, each newly taken stretch of land being put at once into a state of defence. The article regards his reorganisation of the state as showing exceptional harmony and balance. Again, these are the article's assessments. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1185}

## The first moves against the Ayyubids
The sultan began by nibbling at the lands of the Ayyubid princes. He sent an officer to administer Shawbak, which passed without a fight, then went to [[place/aleppo|Aleppo]], sounded out the Franks near [[place/antioch|Antioch]], and ended the campaign at [[place/damascus|Damascus]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1185}

In 661 AH, 1263, after a year in [[place/cairo|Cairo]], he threatened [[place/akka|Acre]] and then turned on Karak, removing an Ayyubid principality, before returning to Damascus and going back to Egypt to inspect [[place/alexandria|Alexandria]]. In 662 AH, 1264, he annexed Hims after its Ayyubid prince died without an heir. He then began intensive preparation and soon fielded a large army. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1185}

## The campaigns against the Franks
On 1 Rabi' II 663 AH, 21 January 1265, the army left Cairo under the sultan's command for the first stage of a great offensive against the Franks that would not end until 670 AH, 1271. Their strongholds fell one after another. In 663 AH the capture of the port of [[place/caesarea-maritima|Caesarea]] split the Frankish holdings and isolated Jaffa; 'Athlith and [[place/haifa|Haifa]] were then occupied, and the army turned south to take [[place/arsuf|Arsuf]]. The towns were destroyed so that they could not serve the enemy as bases after a reverse. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1185}

In 664 AH, 1266, simultaneous attacks were made along the whole front, with the main effort against Safad, northwest of Lake Tiberias, which fell after a heavy siege. In 666 AH, 1268, Baybars turned on the enclave of Jaffa, which did not hold out a day; the article notes that an inscription on the gate of the great mosque at [[place/ramla|Ramla]] still records the exploit, and the museum has a record of the [[event/fall-of-jaffa-1268|fall of Jaffa]]. A few weeks later another line of defence was forced, the Litani river and the fortress of [[place/beaufort-castle|Beaufort]], and the Egyptian troops appeared at the northern end of the Latin kingdom. Antioch capitulated. The article says this conquest had a very large effect, perhaps greater than Saladin's capture of Jerusalem, since Antioch had not left Frankish hands once since the start of the Crusades. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1185}

The neighbouring fortresses could resist no longer, and Baybars took the chance to make peace with the king of Little Armenia, who had to give up part of his lands. A last offensive from Hims cut the outer defences of Tripoli, and the strongholds of [[place/safita|Safita]], the Crac and 'Akkar were taken in two months. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1185}

## Pilgrimage, the Ismailis and the last years
The article describes the sultan as dividing his time between Cairo and Damascus. He made the pilgrimage in 667 AH, 1269. In 668 AH, 1270, negotiations led the lord of the Ismaili fortresses to pay him tribute. Preoccupied with the expedition of Saint Louis to [[place/tunis|Tunis]], Baybars thought for a moment of helping the Maghribis; reassured, he went back to the conquest of the Ismaili fortresses and then returned to Cairo. The article then says that the year 670 AH was given to a general inspection of Syria, and notes that historians agree that he would arrive unannounced and change direction on the road so that no one could know his route. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1185}

In 671 AH, 1272-73, he left Damascus for Biredjik and overwhelmed a Mongol detachment near it, while other divisions of the army operated in Nubia, in the region of Barka and in Armenia. After a year of calm he was again in Armenia in 674 AH, 1275, where he took [[place/sis|Sis]] and [[place/ayas|Ayas]]; the same year saw an expedition to Nubia led by his lieutenants. In 675 AH, 1276, he was in Asia Minor and took Caesarea in Cappadocia, [[place/kayseri|Kayseri]], after defeating the Seljuk troops and their Mongol allies. He then returned to Damascus, where he died early in 676 AH, 1277. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1185}

## The balance of the reign
The article estimates the Frankish losses at his death: the principality of Antioch hardly existed, the southern frontier had moved from Jaffa back to Acre, and the Crusaders held only a narrow coastal strip while the Mamluks held all the heights. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1185} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1186}

It gives figures for the seventeen years of his reign: thirty-eight campaigns in Syria; nine battles with the Mongols, of which only the last was begun on the sultan's own initiative, the rest being counter-attacks; twenty-one defeats inflicted on the Franks; and personal command in fifteen battles. It adds that he seems to have spent no more than half his reign in Cairo and travelled some twenty-six thousand kilometres. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1186}

## The caliphate and the article's judgement
The article calls it a stroke of genius that, after the Mongol invasion of 656 AH, 1258, he welcomed a refugee of the 'Abbasid family and recognised him in Cairo as supreme pontiff. In its reading this was more than a spiritual gesture, because it brought immediate practical results, among them suzerainty over the holy cities of the Hijaz. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1186}

The author describes the reign as a splendid example of energy: under such a leader Egypt, newly through an internal revolution and facing the Crusaders, the Mongols and the Ismailis, suddenly became a power in the East. The article adds that the end of the 'Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad, hints of Crusader-Mongol alliance, possible plots by dispossessed Ayyubid princes and the ambitions of senior Mamluk officers made up a tragic mix. It says his deeds made him a legend in his lifetime, and that his death, by drinking poison prepared by another, is part of the romance of his life. These judgements are the author's. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1186}

## Sources and the Sirat Baybars
The article names two main primary sources for his life, the biographies by Ibn 'Abd al-Zahir and Ibn Shaddad, neither fully preserved. A British Museum manuscript of one version of Ibn 'Abd al-Zahir, covering the period to early 663 AH, was published with an English translation, and an incomplete manuscript of Ibn Shaddad's work, covering 670 to 676 AH, was found in Edirne. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1186}

A separate entry in the same work treats the Sirat Baybars, a long Arabic folk narrative presented as the life of the sultan. It says many of the people and events are historical but the overall character and most descriptive detail are fictitious, so that its value lies in showing what a large part of the Muslim population of Cairo accepted as reading matter in the later Middle Ages and after. The novel begins with the end of Ayyubid times and the start of Mamluk rule, up to the accession of Baybars. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1186} The same pages carry a short entry on a different person, Baybars II, so the two should not be confused.

## According to al-Maqrizi
Al-Maqrizi says that the al-Zahiriyya madrasa, founded by al-Zahir Rukn al-Din Baybars al-Bunduqdari, stands at Bab al-Dhahab in the eastern great palace of the Fatimids, on the site of its mihrab. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1032}

He also says that Baybars was among the amirs who left Cairo for Syria after Aqtay's killing, and that they went to al-Malik al-Nasir Yusuf ibn al-Aziz, lord of Syria, who received them. {cite:khitat-maqrizi-1998 p.1081}

## Baybars in Yeomans
Yeomans writes that Baybars led the vanguard at Ayn Jalut and then quarrelled with Qutuz; citing Ibn Taghribirdi, he says Qutuz had promised him Aleppo and reneged, so Baybars and other disaffected amirs plotted to kill him on a hunt; Baybars confessed to the killing and was proclaimed sultan under a Turkish custom that the killer of a ruler becomes ruler. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.125} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.127} Yeomans counts him, after Salah al-Din, the strongest leader to emerge from Egypt and the real founder of the Bahri dynasty: he held the Mongols in check, revived the caliphate by bringing al-Mustansir to Cairo, which drew the Hijaz into the empire and gave Egypt charge of the pilgrimage, and formed diplomatic and trade links with Constantinople, Sicily, Aragon and Italian states. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.127}

His Cairo buildings include [[building/mosque-of-al-zahir-baybars|his mosque]], built between 1266 and 1269, and [[building/madrasa-of-al-zahir-baibars|his madrasa]], dated 1262; of his Citadel palace only the base of the Lions' Tower with its lions remains. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.127} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.129} Yeomans quotes E. W. Lane that Baybars endowed a garden near his mosque, the "garden of the cat", to feed destitute cats. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.129}
