## Summary
Egyptian poet and statesman (1839–1904), regarded as the awakener of modern Arabic poetry and founder of the revival school. He joined the Urabi revolution and served as Egypt's prime minister in 1882, was exiled to Ceylon for seventeen years, and collected and refined his verse in the Diwan al-Barudi and Mukhtarat al-Barudi. {cite:wikipedia-mahmoud-sami-al-barudi}

## Life and political career

Mahmud Sami al-Barudi was born in Cairo in 1839 into an old Circassian family, received both military and literary education, and rose through military and civil posts in Egypt and the Ottoman state. {cite:wikipedia-mahmoud-sami-al-barudi}

He joined the Egyptian national movement and backed Ahmad Urabi, chairing the council of representatives, holding ministerial portfolios and reaching the premiership in 1882 during the Urabi crisis. {cite:wikipedia-mahmoud-sami-al-barudi}

## Exile and poetry

After the British occupation of Egypt in 1882 he was sentenced to exile in Ceylon (Sri Lanka) with his Urabi comrades, spending seventeen years there, learning Persian and English and collecting and refining his diwan. {cite:wikipedia-mahmoud-sami-al-barudi}

His poetry built on reviving old Arabic authenticity in strong language with new meanings in heroism, politics and patriotism — he is said to have founded the revival school of modern Arabic poetry — and his works include the Mukhtarat and his arranged diwan. {cite:wikipedia-mahmoud-sami-al-barudi}

## Legacy

Pardoned by the Khedive, he returned to Egypt in 1899 and spent his last years in his library until his death in 1904; a museum bearing his name was later opened in his Cairo house. {cite:wikipedia-mahmoud-sami-al-barudi}

He is the bridge between the old poetry and the Nahda: he carried Arabic verse from the stagnation of panegyric to a literature of causes, and the next generation — Ahmad Shawqi among them — schooled themselves on his model. {cite:wikipedia-mahmoud-sami-al-barudi}
