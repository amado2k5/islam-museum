Pir Ali Muhammad Rashidi (5 August 1905 – 14 March 1987) was a Sindhi journalist, politician, diplomat and writer, for half a century one of the most connected men in Sindh's public life — a Muslim League organiser before Partition, a provincial and federal minister after it, an ambassador, and the author of the classic Sindhi memoirs *Uhee Denhin Uhee Shenih*. He was born Syed Ali Muhammad Shah Rashidi at the village of Bahman in Ratodero Taluka, Larkana District, into the Rashidi family of Sindhi pirs who trace their line to Pir Muhammad Rashid Rozay Dhani; his father was Syed Muhammad Hamid Shah Rashidi, and the historian Pir Hassam-ud-Din Rashidi was his younger brother. He never attended a school or college: he was educated at home, in Arabic, Persian and English. {cite:wikipedia-pir-ali-muhammad-rashidi-en} {cite:wikipedia-pir-ali-muhammad-rashidi-sd} {cite:wikipedia-pir-ali-muhammad-rashidi-ur}

Journalism came first. He was writing for newspapers in his teens: a correspondent of *Sindh News* from 1924, founder the same year of the monthly *Al-Rashid* from his own village, modelled on Abul Kalam Azad's journals; editor of Shikarpur's *Al-Hizb* in 1926; secretary to Mohammad Ayub Khuhro in 1927; and then, in quick succession, *Al Amin*, *Sitara-e-Sindh* (1934, which his brother edited in 1934–37), *Subah Sindh* (1936), the weekly *Muslim Voice* (1940–46, from Sukkur and then Karachi) and *Qurbani* (1945). After Partition he edited the *Sindh Observer* (c. 1949), where his paper's feud with Altaf Husain's *Dawn* became a famous episode of Pakistani press history; he was president of the All Pakistan Newspaper Editors' Conference in 1949 and of the joint Indo-Pak editors' commission in 1951, launched the *Statesman* in 1952 and the *Eastern Express* in 1954–55, and finally settled into the column: *Maktoob Mashriq-e-Ba'eed* from Hong Kong and then *Mashriq-o-Maghrib* in *Jang* from 1963, and the Sindhi column *Rand ain Pandh* in *Ibrat* in 1967–69. {cite:wikipedia-pir-ali-muhammad-rashidi-en} {cite:wikipedia-pir-ali-muhammad-rashidi-ur}

His political life ran alongside. He joined the Sindh Muhammadan Association in 1926 and campaigned in print for Sindh's separation from Bombay; passed through Shahnawaz Bhutto's Peoples Party (1934) and Abdullah Haroon's Sindh Ittehad Party (1936); and in 1938 joined the Muslim League, becoming secretary of its Sindh branch and helping to organise Jinnah's Karachi conference of October 1938, after which Jinnah made him secretary of the League's foreign committee. When the Manzilgah dispute at Sukkur went before a tribunal, Rashidi presented the League's case. Urdu accounts count him among the drafters of the 1940 Lahore Resolution, and Yusuf Abdullah Haroon later credited him with a drafting role; what is documented beyond dispute is the organisational work around that session. He lost the 1946 central election to Yusuf Haroon, and after 1947 — by the Urdu account — used his influence with Governor Din Muhammad to have Pir Pagaro's gaddi restored and the Frontier Regulation regime lifted from the Hurs. Elected unopposed to the Sindh Assembly in 1953, he held the revenue, health and information portfolios under Khuhro: as revenue minister he abolished Sindh's jagirs in 1954, and as health minister he founded Liaquat Medical College at Jamshoro and civil hospitals across the province. The same year he played a key part in steering the Sindh Assembly's resolution accepting the One Unit scheme — the English article calls it the most controversial act of his career. In 1955 he moved to the federal legislature and became Federal Minister for Information and Broadcasting under Prime Minister Chaudhry Muhammad Ali. {cite:wikipedia-pir-ali-muhammad-rashidi-en} {cite:wikipedia-pir-ali-muhammad-rashidi-ur}

In 1957 he resigned to enter diplomacy: ambassador to the Philippines from 21 September 1957, where Manila conferred the Order of Sikatuna on him, and then ambassador to China in 1961, where he concluded the boundary negotiations that Zulfikar Ali Bhutto later signed into an agreement. After a Hong Kong interval came the long *Jang* years; he lost the 1970 election in Sehwan–Kotri, worked against One Unit alongside G. M. Syed, and from 1972 served as a special advisor in the Federal Information Ministry under Bhutto, besides chairing the Pakistan National Centre. His books, in three languages, made his second reputation: the three-volume memoirs *Uhee Denhin Uhee Shenih* — parts of them translated into German by Annemarie Schimmel — *Faryad Sindh*, *Cheen Jee Diary*, *Roodad-e-Chaman*, *One Unit Ki Kahani*, a life of Ubaidullah Sindhi, and the posthumous English *Sindh: Ways and Days*, edited by his wife Begum Mumtaz Rashdi (1934–2004), herself a writer; his own English account of Sindh's separation struggle had appeared under Khuhro's name as *Story of the Sufferings of Sindh*. He died at Karachi on 14 March 1987, aged eighty-one, and was buried in the Sindhi Muslim Jamaat graveyard there; his son Pir Hussain Shah Rashdi later sat in the Senate. {cite:wikipedia-pir-ali-muhammad-rashidi-en} {cite:wikipedia-pir-ali-muhammad-rashidi-sd} {cite:wikipedia-pir-ali-muhammad-rashidi-ur} {cite:wikidata-pir-ali-muhammad-rashidi}
