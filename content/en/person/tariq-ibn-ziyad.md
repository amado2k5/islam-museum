## Overview
Tariq ibn Ziyad was the Berber commander of the Muslim troops who undertook the conquest of the Iberian Peninsula in 92 AH, 711 CE. The Encyclopaedia of Islam says that his dates of birth and death are uncertain. At the time of the expedition he was living in [[place/tanja|Tangier]], where he acted as governor on behalf of [[person/musa-ibn-nusayr|Musa ibn Nusayr]], the governor of [[place/ifriqiya|Ifriqiya]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 257} The mountain where he landed still carries his name in Gibraltar, Jabal Tariq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 258}

The article stresses that every account of the early Muslim presence in [[place/al-andalus|al-Andalus]] rests on contradictory data, so both his personality and the exact circumstances of his entry into the country are hard to fix. The museum's own sources are set out below next to the article's account. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 257}

## The state of the sources
The article explains why the history of the conquest is difficult. There are no contemporary Muslim texts, and not even texts from a period reasonably close to the events. The only near-contemporary witnesses are two very brief Christian chronicles, the Chronica byzantia-arabica of 741 and the Continuatio Hispana of 754. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 257}

The earliest surviving Muslim works are the Futuh Misr of Ibn Abd al-Hakam and the Tarikh of Ibn Habib, both from the third Islamic century, ninth century CE. The article says that they readily accept legendary accounts and exaggerate the booty that was later gathered. Most other sources are late compilations that join material of uneven worth, among them the anonymous Akhbar majmua and Fath al-Andalus, about which modern scholars disagree. Some take them as an early written form of old oral tradition, others as late compilations from summaries of earlier texts. The article nevertheless holds that the general lines of the record are clearly established. It also notes that J. Vallvé proposed a landing at [[place/cartagena|Cartagena]]; the article finds this hard to accept in full but sees in it proof that some received ideas need revision. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 257}

## Background and the contact with Julian
According to the opinion most widely held among the chroniclers, Tariq was a Berber client of Musa ibn Nusayr and took part in the conquest of the Maghrib under his orders as commander of the vanguard. When Musa went back to Ifriqiya he left Tariq in charge of a mostly Berber contingent stationed at Tangier. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 257}

From Tangier Tariq made contact with the governor of [[place/ceuta|Ceuta]], whom the article calls the legendary Count Julian, see [[person/julian-count-of-ceuta|Julian]]. The article judges him probably a Visigoth and a dependant of the Visigothic king at Toledo, and says he urged Tariq to invade. Some versions make Julian's envoy Musa himself and say Musa obtained the caliph's authorisation in Damascus. The article considers it more likely, given what followed, that Tariq acted on his own initiative without waiting for his superior's explicit consent. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 257}

Before Tariq there had been one or several small raids on the coast. The most significant, and for many authors the only one, was led by another Berber, Tarif, with about five hundred men in 91 AH, 710 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 258}

## The crossing
In the spring of the following year, Rajab 92 AH, April 711 CE, Tariq crossed the strait on boats supplied by Julian. His force was almost entirely Berber, and he landed at the foot of the mountain that came to bear his name. Many Arab sources give four ships and a landing force of twelve thousand men, in one or several waves. The article says these figures cannot be verified but are probably not far from the truth. It concludes that the crossing was slow, with dozens of trips per boat over several days and no serious resistance from most local inhabitants, who could easily have stopped it. It reads the landing as taking place with the connivance or the indifference of the local people. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 258}

## The battle against Roderic
The Visigothic king [[person/roderick|Roderic]], busy at the time fighting the Basques, led his army out in haste. Meanwhile the Muslims had built fortresses at Gibraltar and made small raids for supplies. The encounter came at the end of Ramadan and the start of Shawwal, in July, near a river that can be identified with the Guadalete or the Barbate, not far from the landing place. [[place/guadalete|The Guadalete]] has its own record. The article observes that in about three months the Muslims had hardly moved, which let the Spanish forces gather into a strong army. It calls this decision ill-advised from a strategic point of view. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 258}

The article judges that the defeat of Roderic's army was largely due to a large contingent, traditionally identified with supporters of the family of the earlier king Witiza, which helped bring about the rout and Roderic's death, actively or passively. It adds that none of the actors could have imagined that the whole Peninsula would fall. After this unexpected victory, it says, Tariq saw how weak the cohesion of the Visigothic state was, and understood that he could master the country or turn a coastal raid into a profitable campaign against the rich interior cities. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 258}

## The advance to Toledo
Tariq next went to [[place/istija|Ecija]], where the remnants of the Visigothic army, Witiza's supporters among them, had taken refuge. The battle was as hard fought as the first, and again the Muslims won, helped according to some sources by Julian. The defeated army took refuge in the town and soon capitulated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 258}

From then nothing stood in his way. The article says that he divided his army into four groups, sending them to targets including [[place/granada|Granada]] and [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]], the latter under [[person/mughith-al-rumi|Mughith al-Rumi]], while he himself went to [[place/tulaytula|Toledo]]. The Visigothic capital, abandoned by its dignitaries, fell to him without resistance. Several sources add that he marched on north, reaching Guadalajara and then [[place/astorga|Astorga]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 258} See [[event/conquest-of-al-andalus|the conquest of al-Andalus]] and [[event/conquest-of-tulaytula|the conquest of Toledo]].

## The meeting with Musa and his later life
Musa ibn Nusayr had meanwhile come to the Peninsula with a mostly Arab army. He and Tariq met at or near Toledo. The article says Musa seems to have intended a severe punishment but in the end limited himself to a harsh reprimand. From then on, it says, Tariq fades into obscurity, overshadowed by his patron, with whom he went back east in 95 AH. His last known act, according to the article, was joining the accusers in a case against Musa, still resentful of the humiliation at Toledo. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 258}

## Other reports in the museum's sources
Other sources in the museum's collection report him differently on several points, including the date and the treatment he received.

al-Tabari reports that Musa became angry with him, dismissed him, and sent him to Toledo in the year 93 AH {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.6 p.481}.

al-Baladhuri reports that he was an agent of Musa and the first to raid al-Andalus, in the year 92 AH. He conquered it and then conquered Toledo. al-Maqqari reports that Musa sent him across with seven thousand men, most of them Berbers, in four ships, and that he landed at the mountain named after him, Jabal Tariq, in Sha'ban of the year 92 AH {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari p.267}.

al-Maqqari also reports that when Lodharic advanced on him, Musa sent him reinforcements of five thousand, so that they became twelve thousand {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari p.278}.

al-Salabi's book records his crossing in Rajab of the year 92 AH and his landing near a mountain known by his name. It treats the sermon attributed to him as a point of stylistic and historical doubt, and the report of the burning of the ships is confused {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 p.68} {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 p.70}.

## In Ibn Abd al-Hakam
Ibn Abd al-Hakam calls him Tariq ibn Ziyad in one place and Tariq ibn Amr in two. {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 275} {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 276} {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 283} Musa put him over Tangier in the year 92; he had with him a slave-girl called Umm Hakim, whom he left on an island thereafter called the Island of Umm Hakim. {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 276} {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 278} He took Julian's two daughters as hostages. {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 277} Musa later wrote to him not to go beyond Cordoba, and when they met at Cordoba Tariq told him, "I am only your client, and this conquest is yours." {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 280} Ibn Abd al-Hakam reports that Musa later imprisoned him and meant to kill him; Tariq sent word to [[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik]], who wrote threatening Musa if he harmed him, and Musa released him. {cite:futuh-misr-amir-1999 p. 283} See [[object/table-of-solomon|the Table of Solomon]].
