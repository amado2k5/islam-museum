## Overview
Abu Mihjan 'Abd Allah ibn Habib al-Thaqafi, whose given name is also reported as Malik or 'Amr, was an Arab poet of the [[group/thaqif|Thaqif]] tribe. The Encyclopaedia of Islam classes him among the mukhadramun, poets whose lives spanned the time before and after the coming of Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 159}

## Before and after Islam
The article says he took part in the defence of al-Ta'if against Muhammad in 8/630, when he wounded a son of Abu Bakr with an arrow. He entered Islam in 9/631-2. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 159}

## Al-Qadisiyya
He fought at the battle of [[event/battle-of-al-qadisiyya|al-Qadisiyya]]. The article relays a story that, in order to join the battle, he first escaped from his escort, since [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|'Umar]] had banished him to Hadawda, and then obtained provisional freedom through the wife of [[person/saad-ibn-abi-waqqas|Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas]]. Sa'd had imprisoned him for drunkenness, but the poet's conduct in the battle won him the general's pardon. The article adds that historians have somewhat embroidered his conduct there, and that he possibly also took part in the battle of Vologesias, called Ullays. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 159}

## Wine poems and banishment
The surviving fragments of his poetry, the article judges, show no originality. His reputation rests mainly on his bacchanalian songs, and a famous line asking to be buried at the foot of a vine is attributed to him. A group of poems in which he openly challenges the Qur'anic prohibition of wine must, the article says, be taken seriously, and this attitude led 'Umar to banish him several times. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 159} The article on Massawa' adds that, because of his love of wine, 'Umar banished him in 14/634 to Badi'. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 668} In 16/637 he was exiled again by 'Umar and died shortly afterwards; it is said that his tomb could be seen on the frontier of Adharbayjan or of Jurjan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 159}

## Editions and sources
His diwan was edited by C. Landberg in Leiden in 1886, with another edition in Cairo with a commentary by al-'Askari, and by Abel in Leiden in 1887 with a biography and a Latin translation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 159} Accounts of him are found, among others, in al-Jumahi, Ibn Qutayba, al-Mas'udi, the Aghani and Ibn Hajar's Isaba. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 159}

## Not to be confused
The article warns against confusing him with a namesake, Abu Mihjan Nusayb ibn Rabah, treated in a separate article. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 159}

## Doubts
The sources give two different places and years of banishment, 14/634 in the Massawa' article and 16/637 in the article on the poet himself, and they may refer to separate episodes or to one disputed event; the museum does not decide. The OCR text of the full name's last element is damaged and is read as Habib. No birth date is recorded.

## The account in al-Mas'udi
Abu Mahjan al-Thaqafi, a poet held by Sa'd at the foot of the fort of al-Udhayb. Al-Mas'udi says he was imprisoned for his poetry about wine. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 610-612}

## The day of al-Qadisiyya
When the fighting grew fierce he heard the clash of iron and asked Sa'd's permission to go out, but Sa'd refused. He asked Salma daughter of Hafsa, Sa'd's wife, to unfasten his shackles and lend him Sa'd's grey mare, and promised her that he would return and put his leg back in the shackles. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 610-612}

He rode out, took the takbir, charged the right wing of the enemy, then the left, then turned them. The report says that people thought he was al-Khadir, and that Sa'd, looking down from the top of the palace, said that were it not for the imprisonment of Abu Mahjan he would have thought it was him. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 610-612}

## His return
He returned to his prison at midnight and put his leg back in the shackles. When Salma told Sa'd, he released him and said he would not hold him to account for anything he said. Abu Mahjan replied that his tongue would not speak any ugliness after that. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 610-612}
