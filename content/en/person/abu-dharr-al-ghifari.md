## Brief
Abu Dharr al-Ghifari, a man of the desert and one of the earliest Muslims — and the sharpest conscience in the tradition on wealth, whom a caliph exiled for saying it openly.

## Life
A man of the [[group/banu-ghifar|Banu Ghifar]], a tribe of the Hijaz desert, he went to Mecca and became one of the first Muslims around 614 CE {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}. He called his own people to Islam, then lived in ascetic simplicity in Medina and later in Damascus.

There, the sources record, he publicly condemned the governors who gathered gold and silver, citing the Book (the Qur'an) on hoarding. When he was sent back to Medina he did not stop, so the caliph [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]] exiled him to al-Rabadha, where he died in 652 CE {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

## Legacy
Later generations — Sufis, social critics and contemporary writers on Islamic economics — all return to Abu Dharr: the man who asked from the start what the community owes to the poor.

## For young visitors
Abu Dharr believed that someone who grows rich while others are poor is doing wrong, and he kept saying so to the most powerful men of his time — even when it cost him his home.

## His conversion in Mecca (The Sealed Nectar p.133)
He came to Mecca and asked about the man who claimed to be a prophet. He embraced Islam after the Prophet presented it to him. Then he declared his testimony in the mosque, and the pagans beat him until he nearly died; al-Abbas rescued him. The next day he returned, and they did the same to him again {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.133} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/545}.

## Encyclopaedia of Islam account
The Encyclopaedia of Islam (2nd ed.) entry gives his name as commonly Jundub b. Junada, with other names also recorded. Before his conversion he is said to have worshipped one God. When news of Muhammad reached him, he sent his brother to Mecca, and then went himself, and he became a believer at once. The article puts him among the first five believers, or even the fourth. He went to Medina after the battle of the Ditch (5/627), later lived in Syria, and was recalled by 'Uthman after a complaint from Mu'awiya; he died at al-Rabadha in 32/652-3 or 31. The article notes his humility and asceticism, and credits him with 281 traditions, of which al-Bukhari and Muslim gave thirty-one between them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 133}

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lectures on Sharh Kitab al-Fawa'id

The Sheikh narrates that Abu Dharr recited Sura al-Zalzala to a Bedouin until its end. The Bedouin wept and asked whether God would not hold them to account for the weight of an atom on the Day of Resurrection, and Abu Dharr said yes (needs_source: narration with no source given in the lecture). {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep05 00:21:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep05 00:22:00}

The Sheikh holds that knowledge is of benefit only when it is put into practice. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep05 00:22:00}

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lecture series Al Dinu Al Qayem

Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy recounts that Abu Dharr and Abu al-Darda bought a house outside Medina, and that the seller, a Christian woman, told him to purify his heart and pray wherever he wished (needs_source: the story as the sheikh tells it) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep02 00:44:30}.

The sheikh describes Abu Dharr's hot temper in a dispute held in the mosque, and mentions a remark alluding to the origins of one of the disputants, which the sheikh counts as an insult; the words are not repeated here (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep03 00:21:00}. He recounts that the Prophet told Abu Dharr that he was weak in leadership, though not in character, because of his fiery temper (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep03 00:21:00}. Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy holds, as his own opinion, that hot temper ruins an institution that is led by it, and that a preacher must not be hot-tempered (speaker's view, opinion) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep03 00:21:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep03 00:21:30}.

The sheikh describes Abu Dharr's earlier life as including highway robbery, and says that no one is free from error except the Prophet and no one is sanctified (needs_source: the description is the sheikh's) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep05 00:31:00}. He cites a saying that Abu Dharr walks alone, dies alone, and will be raised alone as a community (needs_source: the saying is paraphrased and its source is not given in this episode) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep06 00:35:30}.

## For Scholars
Abu Dharr al-Ghifari was a man of Banu Ghifar, a tribe of the Hijazi desert, who walked to Mecca and became one of the first Muslims about 614 CE; al-Raheeq records that he came asking about the man claiming prophecy, entered Islam after it was presented, then proclaimed his testimony in the mosque and was beaten nearly to death until al-Abbas rescued him — returning the next day to receive the same — and he called his people. He lived as an ascetic in Medina then Damascus, where the sources preserve his public rebuke of the officials for amassing gold and silver, citing the Book on hoarded treasure; sent back to Medina he did not stop, and Uthman exiled him to al-Rabadha where he died in 652 CE — the sternest conscience about wealth in the tradition. Later generations — Sufis, social critics, contemporary writers on Islamic economics — all return to Abu Dharr: the man who asked from the start what the community owes its poor.

## From Omar Abdelkafy's programme Wa Nafsin wa ma Sawwaha
The Sheikh relates that a man embraced Islam and asked to be taught it, and the Prophet told Abu Dharr to teach him; Abu Dharr recited [[passage/quran-99-7|Surat al-Zalzala]] to him, and when he reached its end, on an atom's weight of good and of evil, the Bedouin wept and said that this was teaching enough for him.
