## Overview
Aristotle, son of Nicomachus, who took from Plato and succeeded him. Ibn al-Nadim lists his pupils, and says that his books on logic, natural science and theology were translated into Arabic. They were interpreted by Alexander of Aphrodisias, Themistius, Yahya al-Nahwi, al-Farabi, al-Kindi and others. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.343-357}

## In al-Jahiz's Kitab al-Hayawan
In [[work/kitab-al-hayawan|Kitab al-Hayawan]], [[person/al-jahiz|al-Jahiz]] often quotes Aristotle, whom he calls "the master of logic" (sahib al-mantiq), naming his book "which is called The Animals". {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v1 p. 283} {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v2 p. 70} Discussing the lion, he takes from him that the marks left by its claws are the size of a cupper's incisions or a little larger and that the cure for its bite is the cure for a dog's bite, and builds on this the points of likeness between dog and lion. {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v1 p. 283} On hens he reports from him that one hen laid eighteen eggs, each with two yolks, and that two chicks hatched from each egg. {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v2 p. 89} He does not always defer to him: where Aristotle claims that seeing an eagle's chick is a hard thing, al-Jahiz says "I am not able to judge between them" and turns to what he knows of the crows of Basra. {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v1 p. 420} In the first part he notes that the books of Aristotle and his teacher Plato preceded the beginning of Arabic poetry by ages, and asks when Aristotle's translators were ever his equals. {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v1 p. 52} {cite:hayawan-jahiz-ilmiyya-2003-v1 p. 54}
