## Summary

Elmedina Muftić is a Bosnian journalist, columnist and author whose body of work — more than five hundred published columns — returns persistently to three subjects: the Bosniak genocide of the 1990s, the place of women in Bosnian society, and the uses and abuses of collective memory. {cite:muslim500-2026 p. 191}

This record rests on the single listed source — her entry in The Muslim 500 — and it says so plainly, as the museum does wherever public documentation is thin.

## Context: memory work in post-war Bosnia

Bosnia's postwar public sphere is crowded with competing narratives of the 1992–1995 war and of the genocide at Srebrenica in particular, which a Bosniak-majority public memory holds as its defining wound and which revisionist currents in the region continue to contest. Journalism and the newspaper column in that setting are not commentary from a distance but a front-line instrument of memory: the columnist who writes about the genocide, about women and about remembrance is doing the work of a society deciding what it will teach its children. {cite:muslim500-2026 p. 191}

## The work

Her Muslim 500 entry credits her with over five hundred columns treating the Bosniak genocide, women's roles in society and collective memory. Five hundred columns — a decade or more of weekly witness — is itself the datum: in the Bosnian press, columnists of that persistence become reference points, their archives read as a running chronicle of how the country processed its catastrophe. {cite:muslim500-2026 p. 191}

The pairing of subjects is characteristic of a strand of Bosniak public writing: remembrance of the genocide bound to the question of the living — how women who survived the war, and the daughters of those who did not, are to occupy public and religious life. {cite:muslim500-2026 p. 191}

The museum lists her among the edition's social-issues voices: a writer whose influence is measured in the accumulation of testimony rather than in office, audience figures or institutional power.
