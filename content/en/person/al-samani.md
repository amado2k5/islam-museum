## Overview
The great hafiz Abu Sa'd Taj al-Islam 'Abd al-Karim ibn Muhammad ibn Mansur al-Sam'ani, al-Tamimi al-Marwazi al-Shafi'i, known as Ibn al-Sam'ani — traditionist of the East and prolific author. He was born at Marw on Monday 21 Sha'ban 506 and died there on Monday 1 Rabi' I 562, aged fifty-six; his son Abu al-Muzaffar 'Abd al-Rahim recorded the date. {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi pp. 119, 123} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1024}

## Raised in a house of learning
He was born into scholarship: his grandfather Abu al-Muzaffar Mansur authored a Qur'an commentary, and his father, the hafiz Taj al-Islam Abu Bakr, was an authority in Shafi'i law, hadith and preaching. His father took him, a two-year-old, into the hadith sessions, and carried him to Nishapur in 509 to sit before 'Abd al-Ghaffar al-Shiruti, Abu al-'Ala' 'Ubayd ibn Muhammad al-Qushayri and others. His father died at the beginning of 510, having entrusted him to his two learned brothers; he grew up among his uncles and, as a youth, turned to Qur'an, fiqh and study, and came to love hadith and audition. {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi pp. 119-120} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1024}

## The journeys
Not quite twenty, he set out on the talab al-'ilm: first to Nishapur again for special training in the Sahih of Muslim, then to Tus and beyond. From Marw he visited the centres of learning of his time on three great journeys — 529-538, 540-546, and 549-552 with his son 'Abd al-Rahim — covering Isfahan, Hamadan, Baghdad, Mecca, Medina, Damascus and Jerusalem (in Crusader hands for forty-two years when he visited in 536), plus Khwarazm, Samarqand, Bukhara, Balkh and Herat, performing the pilgrimage twice and revisiting many towns more than once, even at the cost of detours. Ibn al-Najjar reported hearing that his teachers numbered seven thousand — something no one else attained. He returned home in 538, married, had 'Abd al-Rahim, took him on the eastern circuit and compiled an eighteen-part mu'jam for him, then cast down the staff of travel at Marw and devoted himself to composition, dictation, preaching and teaching at the Amidiyya madrasa. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 pp. 1024-1025} {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi pp. 120-121} {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 75}

## His works
His most famous book is [[work/al-ansab-samani|al-Ansab]], in 350 folios — the alphabetical dictionary of nisbas gathering thousands of biographies; he finished the clean copy years before his death yet kept adding to it. Ibn al-Athir epitomised it as al-Lubab, and al-Suyuti further as Lubb al-Lubab. Among his others: [[work/al-tahbir|al-Tahbir fi al-Mujam al-Kabir]], over 1,200 biographies of contemporary scholars, men and women; a Dhayl to al-Khatib al-Baghdadi's Tarikh Baghdad in 400 folios; Tarikh Marw in 500; Mujam al-Buldan of the towns he heard in, 50 folios; Mujam al-Shuyukh, 80; Tiraz al-Dhahab fi Adab al-Talab, 150; and Adab al-Imla' wa-l-Istimla', a handbook of dictation as a method of transmission and instruction. {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi pp. 120-122} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1025} {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 653}

## Dates
His birth and death dates are transmitted in Hijri years from al-Dhahabi's Tarikh al-Islam; the Julian equivalents are derived following the Encyclopaedia of Islam's conversion. The Muntazam lists his death among those of 563. {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi pp. 118, 123}
