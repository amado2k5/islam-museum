## Overview
Sufyan al-Thawri, imam of the people of Kufa in hadith, who was classified in the first era of codification. al-Suyuti reports that he said: "The caliphs are five: Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, Ali and Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz" {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.374}.

## With al-Mansur and al-Mahdi
al-Mansur ordered his deputy in Mecca to imprison him together with Abbad ibn Kathir in the year 158 AH. People feared that they would be killed when the pilgrims arrived, but al-Mansur died before that {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.426}.

al-Mas'udi reports that he entered upon al-Mahdi and greeted him with the greeting of the common people, and did not greet him as caliph. al-Mahdi asked him: "Are you not afraid that we will judge you by our whims?" He answered that if a powerful king judged him, then a powerful king would judge al-Mahdi too. al-Mahdi wrote him a commission to the judgeship of Kufa; he took it, went out and threw it into the Tigris, and fled {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.312-313}.

## In the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam presents Sufyan ibn Said ibn Masruq al-Thawri, Abu Abd Allah al-Kufi, who lived from 97 to 161 AH, 716 to 778 CE, as a prominent representative of early Islamic law, tradition and Qur'an interpretation. It calls him the founder of the Thawriyya law school and an important link in many hadith chains on legal, religious and doctrinal subjects. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 795}

## Education, travels and flight
He was born in 97/715-16 in [[place/kufa|Kufa]]. Early on he belonged to the exclusive Kufan legal circles around [[person/hammad-ibn-abi-sulayman|Hammad ibn Abi Sulayman]], [[person/abu-ishaq-al-sabii|Abu Ishaq al-Sabii]], Mansur ibn al-Mutamir and [[person/al-amash-sulayman-ibn-mihran|Sulayman al-Amash]], and quickly gained a reputation as a legal scholar oriented toward hadith. The article contrasts this with the generally speculative Iraqi system of personal reasoning, whose most effective exponent it names as [[person/abu-hanifa|Abu Hanifa]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 795}

From between 115/732 and 120/737 he began a period of more than three decades of travel and hadith transmission in [[place/khurasan|Khurasan]], the [[place/hijaz|Hijaz]] and above all [[place/basra|Basra]]. The article says Basra became the most formative influence on his intellectual development. Basran scholars such as [[person/ayyub-al-sakhtiyani|Ayyub al-Sakhtiyani]] and Abd Allah ibn Awn, it says, steered him away from the Shii leaning that was common among Kufan jurists of that century, and he made contact with Syria, above all with [[person/al-awzai-abd-al-rahman|al-Awzai]], which began a lasting pro-Umayyad influence on his legal and political reasoning. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 795} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 796}

The article adds that frequent contact with early Mutazilis, namely Khalid al-Hadhdha, [[person/amr-ibn-ubayd|Amr ibn Ubayd]] and [[person/wasil-ibn-ata|Wasil ibn Ata]], the last of them a critic of the Abbasid caliphate, gave his religious thinking a rational and independent direction. In 153/769 the post of judge in Kufa was offered to him, which the article calls a tactical offer; he refused it and escaped capture by going to [[place/sanaa|Sanaa]]. There he kept up a productive hadith circle with Mamar ibn Rashid and [[person/abd-al-razzaq-al-sanani|Abd al-Razzaq al-Sanani]], and the article says the latter's Musannaf partly arose from this exchange. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 796}

Between 153/769 and 158/774 he seems to have left his Yemeni exile for pilgrimages to [[place/mecca|Mecca]] and frequent visits to Syria, Lebanon and Palestine, where he repeatedly met al-Awzai and their shared disciple [[person/abu-ishaq-al-fazari|Abu Ishaq al-Fazari]]. Al-Fazari later transmitted a selection of their legal statements, which appears in al-Tabari's Ikhtilaf al-fuqaha. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 796}

During the pilgrimage of 158/774 he avoided the repeated attempts of the caliph [[person/al-mansur|al-Mansur]] to arrest him by escaping again to Basra. There he continued to transmit hadith, mainly to [[person/yahya-ibn-said-al-qattan|Yahya ibn Said al-Qattan]], [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-mahdi|Abd al-Rahman ibn Mahdi]] and Abu Hudhayfa Musa ibn Masud al-Nahdi, who received parts of his Qur'an commentary. Late in life, worn out by the constant pursuit, he agreed to an official reconciliation with the new authorities under [[person/al-mahdi|al-Mahdi]], but a planned meeting in Baghdad did not take place. The article says he died at the age of 64 in Shaban 161, May 778, and was buried among renowned Basran scholars such as [[person/al-hasan-al-basri|al-Hasan al-Basri]] and Ayyub al-Sakhtiyani. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 796}

## Works
The article counts him among the first literary generation in Islam. His works, it says, are partly lost and partly preserved. They comprise two lost hadith collections, al-Jami al-kabir and al-Jami al-saghir; a compendium of inheritance law, the Kitab al-Faraid, edited and commented on by Raddatz in 1971; a fragmentary Qur'an commentary, the Tafsir al-Quran al-kabir, edited by Arshi at Rampur in 1965; and several religious treatises. These include a creed called al-Itiqad, revised by Ibn Taymiyya, a letter to Abbad ibn Abbad al-Arsufi and a testament addressed to Ali al-Sulami. The article also notes scattered references to an interest in natural sciences and unusual subjects, with only very fragmentary literary traces. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 796}

## Legal thought and the Thawriyya
Considerable parts of his legal thought survive in al-Tabari's Ikhtilaf al-fuqaha, edited by Schacht in 1933 for military law and by Kern in 1902 for civil law. There his opinions on points of practice, in the typically Iraqi form of speculative deduction, are compared with the schools of al-Awzai, Abu Hanifa, Malik and [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafii]]. The article notes that he is cited as an essential authority throughout the hadith, jurisprudence and biographical literature, which it takes to show his formative position as a systematic developer of hadith, though methodologically criticised, within the Kufan school. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 796}

In the article's account, his views and methods form an independent body of legal and religious statements, often resting on Companion or Successor reports and more rarely on Prophetic ones, which in its words prepared, if not anticipated, a thoroughly hadith-oriented legal system like al-Shafii's. It adds that his theoretical creativity and strongly political, anti-Abbasid preferences won his school wide acceptance in his time and helped it spread as far as Umayyad Cordoba. The Thawriyya, it says, passed through Syria-based disciples such as Muhammad ibn Yusuf al-Firyabi and Abu Ishaq al-Fazari, and represented the law as practised in North Africa and Spain until the Maliki school absorbed it in the 4th/9th century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 796}

## Religious thought, as the article reads it
The article draws his religious and doctrinal statements from the [[work/hilyat-al-awliya|Hilyat al-Awliya]] of Abu Nuaym and the Taqdima of [[person/ibn-abi-hatim-al-razi|Ibn Abi Hatim]]. It describes them as basically orthodox with a rational emphasis: steady urging of diligent study, sound intention and practical action, and trust in God's unfailing justice, together with awareness of God's predestining power. On this basis it places his theology in an intermediate position between the Kharijites on one side and the [[group/qadariyya|Qadariyya]] and the Sifatiyya on the other. It also calls his moderately rational Qur'an commentary an important source for al-Tabari. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 796}

The article reads his affirmation of worldly life, as preparation for the Last Judgement, together with his hadith-based legal codification, as a rational modification of the conservative sunna and a gradual anticipation of Mutazili ideas. It judges that his thought may have influenced ascetics such as [[person/al-harith-al-muhasibi|al-Muhasibi]] and al-Junayd, whom it calls an adherent of al-Thawri, but it sets many Sufi claims aside as tendentious, holding that the Sufi demand for complete renunciation of worldly effort is incompatible with his constructive belief. It explains his known opposition to the Abbasid-leaning [[group/murjia|Murjia]] both by his pro-Umayyad political commitment and by his demanding orientation toward the Last Judgement. Occasional suspicion that he sympathised with Shii circles, Zaydi ones included, is explained as possibly political and as pointing to an early, typically Kufan inclination without lasting effect on his tenets. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 796}

## The author's assessment
The article's author concludes that al-Thawri appears as a progressive element in the 2nd/8th century, through his theoretical development and written codification of hadith-based law and of a reason-based sunna, as stepping stones toward al-Shafii's jurisprudence and, to a lesser degree, toward Mutazili rationalism, leaving little justification for Shii or Sufi claims upon him. The author adds that his conservative, pro-Umayyad political and religious stance is an unusual feature that supports his intellectual independence and helped his school expand. These are the author's judgements, set beside the ascetic and hadith-scholar portrait given in the sections drawn from Abu Nuaym and others on this page. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 796}

## His lineage and death
According to Ibn al-Nadim, he was Sufyan ibn Sa'id ibn Masruq al-Thawri, of the descendants of Thawr ibn Abd Manat. He died in Basra, hidden from the sultan, and was buried at night {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.314-315}. Among the books attributed to him, the Fihrist mentions: al-Jami' al-Kabir, al-Jami' al-Saghir, and al-Fara'id {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.314-315}.

## The two dates of birth and death
The sources differ on his age. al-Mas'udi reports that he died in the year 161 AH at the age of sixty-three, while Ibn al-Nadim says sixty-four, and his birth in the year 97 AH. al-Shirazi says he was born in the year 96 AH, or it is said 97, and died in the year 161 AH {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.312-313} {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.314-315} {cite:tabaqat-fuqaha-abbas-1981}.

## In Abdul Hamid Kishk's sermons
[[person/abdul-hamid-kishk|Abdul Hamid Kishk]] tells that a man came to Sufyan complaining of a sickness of the heart, and Sufyan prescribed a remedy whose ingredients are virtues: humility, awe, watchfulness and seeking forgiveness; he gives it in his talk on remembrance and the work of the heart (needs_source: he tells the report without a chain, and some of its words are garbled in the machine transcript). {cite:assabile-abdul-hamid-kishk shorut-kobol-assalat/ep04 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-abdul-hamid-kishk shorut-kobol-assalat/ep04 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-abdul-hamid-kishk shorut-kobol-assalat/ep04 00:19:30}

## In Abu Ishaq al-Heweny's lessons "Madrasat al-Hayat"
[[person/abu-ishaq-al-heweny|Abu Ishaq al-Heweny]] tells of Sufyan al-Thawri's death as a witness to the friends of God's fear of a bad end. Sufyan was hiding from the caliph and staying in Basra, and he died in the house of his student [[person/yahya-ibn-said-al-qattan|Yahya ibn Sa'id al-Qattan]], with his head in Yahya's lap and his feet in the lap of [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-mahdi|Abd al-Rahman ibn Mahdi]]. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny madrassat-al-hayat/ep04 00:28:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny madrassat-al-hayat/ep04 00:29:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny madrassat-al-hayat/ep04 00:29:30} He says the visitor at his sickbed was [[person/hammad-ibn-salama|Hammad ibn Salama]] in one report and another man in a second; the visitor saw him weeping and asked whether he wept for his sins, and Sufyan took up some earth and said his sins weighed less on him than this: what he feared was a bad end. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny madrassat-al-hayat/ep04 00:29:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny madrassat-al-hayat/ep04 00:30:00} Al-Heweny says that fear grows with faith, and that only the heedless feel safe from God's plan. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny madrassat-al-hayat/ep04 00:30:30} Ibn al-Nadim too says that he died in Basra, hiding from the sultan. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.314-315}

## In Abu Ishaq al-Heweny's «Difaf al-Kawthar»
[[person/abu-ishaq-al-heweny|Abu Ishaq al-Heweny]] describes Sufyan al-Thawri as an imam, hadith scholar, jurist, ascetic and fighter in God's path, whose stature only one who reads his biography and what his teachers, peers and students said of him can know. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep15 00:11:30} He relates that a caliph wanted to bring him close, while Sufyan disliked going near caliphs and princes and used to say that if the prince calls you to recite Surat al-Ikhlas to him, do not go. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep15 00:12:00} The caliph offered ten thousand dirhams to whoever brought him in, so Sufyan left Kufa for Basra and hid there as the keeper of an orchard of fresh dates. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep15 00:12:30}

He relates that two men asked him for dates, and one asked whether the dates of Basra were sweeter than those of Kufa; Sufyan said he had not tasted the dates of Basra, though he was guarding its orchard. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep15 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep15 00:13:30} One whispered to the other that this was Sufyan al-Thawri; Sufyan heard it and fled, leaving the orchard, and because he kept looking round he was suspected, seized and brought to the governor of the town, [[person/man-ibn-zaida|Ma'n ibn Za'ida]], proverbial for generosity. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep15 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep15 00:14:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep15 00:15:00} Ma'n asked his name and he said "Abd Allah ibn Abd al-Rahman", which was true; Ma'n then sent out those with him, adjured him to give his lineage, and when he admitted he was Sufyan, told him to go wherever he wished, swearing that were he under his foot he would not lift it. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep15 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep15 00:15:30} Al-Heweny repeats here that Sufyan died in the house of his student [[person/yahya-ibn-said-al-qattan|Yahya ibn Sa'id al-Qattan]], as he said in «Madrasat al-Hayat». {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep15 00:16:00} He tells the whole story as evidence that one who emigrates or flees with his religion lives in constant fear and watchfulness. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny dhifaf-al-kawtar/ep15 00:16:00}

## In Hilyat al-Awliya
His notice in *Hilyat al-Awliya* (volume 6, no. 387, pp. 356-393, continued in volume 7, pp. 3-144) opens: "Among them is the imam, the approved, the ascetic of rank, Abu Abd Allah Sufyan ibn Sa'id al-Thawri… he had brilliant sallies and matchless sayings, acknowledged in imamate and matchless in archery; knowledge was his familiar and renunciation his beloved." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 6/356} Ibn al-Mubarak counts him among the imams he met; [[person/shuba-ibn-al-hajjaj|Shu'ba]] says "Sufyan al-Thawri is the commander of the faithful in hadith," and Sufyan ibn Uyayna says: "The firmest three men after the Messenger of God's companions are Ibn al-Mubarak in his time, al-Sha'bi in his time, and Sufyan al-Thawri in his time." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 6/356}

In his letter to Ubbad ibn Abbad he warns him of the age into which he and the Prophet's companions had arrived: "How then are we, reaching it with little knowledge, little patience, few helpers for good, corruption among people and a turbid world? Hold to the first matter and cling to it… beware of the princes: do not approach them or mix with them… beware of loving leadership, for leadership may be dearer to a man than gold and silver — it is a dark door only the seeing scholars of brokers perceive… and know that a matter has drawn near to people in which a man wishes to die." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 6/376-377}

In the continuation of the notice (volume 7), Abu Nu'aym recounts his flight from the sultan: sought in al-Mahdi's days, he fled to al-Mada'in, lodged in a quarter, took refuge in its mosque, and when a theft occurred in that quarter he was accused; brought before its governor [[person/man-ibn-zaida|Ma'n ibn Za'ida]] he was asked his name and refused to have his lineage reviled, saying "I am Sufyan ibn Sa'id ibn Masruq al-Thawri"; when recognized he was called and told "You are the quarry sought by the commander of the faithful," he said "Yes," then bowed his head for a while and said "Do as you wish; come and go when you wish — by God, were I under my own foot I would not lift it." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 7/4-5} And he himself warns: "If you see me changed from the state I am in today, know that I have been altered." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 7/4}

He tells of entering upon the worshipper daughter of Umm Hassan al-Asadiyya and seeing the mark of prostration on her brow; he offered her from the zakat of Ibn Shihab's wealth what would lift her state, and she refused: "You bid me ask the world of Him who does not own it? I am ashamed to ask the world of Him while He owns it," and when night fell she entered her prayer-niche and called: "My God, let every lover be left to his beloved while I am left with You, O Beloved"; then she taught him the degrees of gratitude — whoever says praise be to God has confessed gratitude, and whoever knows gratitude owes a gratitude without end — so Sufyan said: "By God, my knowledge fell short and my tongue failed." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 7/9-10}

Of his sayings: asked what renunciation of the world was, he said "the falling of one's station"; when Abu Hanifa's death was announced he said "Praise be to God — he used to undo the knots of Islam knot by knot"; and when Ata' ibn Muslim asked how his love for Abu Bakr was, he said "strong," then grew angry and made him see his love for Umar, Ali and Uthman. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 7/16,31} He is explicit on the order of imamate: "Whoever says Ali has more right to rule than Abu Bakr and Umar has wronged Abu Bakr, Umar, Ali, the emigrants and the helpers," and of al-Mahdi he said: "If he comes to your door, have nothing to do with him until the people gather to him." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 7/31}

He mentions circling the House, praying two rak'as behind the Maqam, then raising his head to heaven and fainting until they poured zamzam water over him and he revived; [[person/abu-sulayman-al-darani|Abu Sulayman al-Darani]] asked him and he said: "It was not his sight that moved his heart — it was thought," and he wept at maghrib until his recitation broke at [[passage/quran-1-5|"You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help"]]. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 7/17}

Dying at al-Abdan while hiding from the sultan, he drew out a purse from under his head and threw it to a woman speaking behind a curtain, saying: "This woman I married; thirty dirhams of her dower remain with me — if she has left them, clothe me in them; if not, clothe me in my clothes." When he was washed, a slip was found in his waist-wrapper containing the end of the report. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 7/62} An old man with white hair and beard stood over his grave and said: "O Sufyan, you are safe from whom you feared and have come to whom you worshipped; by God, we would not wish anyone but God to hold our reckoning" — and he was not seen again, though they used to see him as al-Khidr. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 7/62} Abu Nu'aym closes the notice: "He was copious in knowledge and his transmissions were like the sea; we have confined ourselves to a portion of the gems of his hadith." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 7/125}
