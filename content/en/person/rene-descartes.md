## Overview
René Descartes was a seventeenth-century French philosopher, and the book "Min al-Falsafah al-Yunaniyyah ila al-Falsafah al-Hadithah" (From Greek Philosophy to Modern Philosophy) presents him as the father of modern philosophy because he rebelled against ancient philosophy, especially the philosophy of Aristotle {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 359}.

## Life
He was born in La Haye, a village in the province of Touraine in France. At the age of eight, he entered the Jesuit college of La Flèche, and he earned a master's degree in arts and a licentiate in law before reaching twenty. He then declared that the sciences and branches of knowledge, except mathematics, are not built on clear ideas or certain information {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 359}.

## Philosophy and Method
Descartes divided philosophical studies into logic, physics, and metaphysics. His philosophy relied on mathematics because, in the book's view, mathematics is based on order and the interconnection of ideas, so he sought to apply a similar method to the rest of the sciences {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 359}{cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 360}.

Descartes distinguishes between two mental operations: intuition, by which the mind clearly grasps fixed ideas, and deduction, which is proving a proposition from clear general principles. The book holds that his deduction differs from Aristotelian syllogism because it rests on clear intuition and accepts only certain propositions {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 360}{cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 361}.

He established four rules for this method: that the researcher accept nothing as true unless he knows it to be so; that he divide the problem as much as possible; that he arrange his thoughts from the simplest to the most complex; and that he make a comprehensive enumeration so as not to overlook anything relevant to the problem {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 361}{cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 362}.

## Doubt and Certainty
Descartes began by doubting all the knowledge he had received, even sensory and rational knowledge, in order to begin inquiry anew on a certain foundation. His doubt led to an indubitable truth: that the doubter is thinking, and that every thought requires a thinking self, making his statement "I think, therefore I am" the first certainty he reached {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 362}{cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 363}.

From this certainty he moved to proving the existence of God: an imperfect human being finds within himself the idea of the perfect, which could not have been placed in him by an imperfect being, so God must exist as a perfect being who does not deceive. Thus the soul is assured of clear and distinct ideas, the possibility of deception imagined by doubt is dispelled, and trust is restored in the existence of external things corresponding to our ideas {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 363}{cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 364}.

## For Young Visitors
Imagine a man who decided not to believe anything unless he was sure of it, so he doubted everything he had learned, and then found that his very doubt was proof that someone was thinking. From this simple idea, he built a new way of thinking.
