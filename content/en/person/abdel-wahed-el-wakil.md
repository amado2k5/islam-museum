## Summary
Abdel-Wahed El-Wakil is an Egyptian architect regarded as one of the foremost living masters of Islamic architecture — a designer who builds mosques and houses in traditional materials and forms while arguing that tradition is a living method, not a costume. The Muslim 500 lists him in Arts & Culture as a two-time Aga Khan Award winner with more than fifty buildings; Wikipedia calls him a leading contemporary authority in Islamic architecture. {cite:muslim500-abdel-wahed-el-wakil} {cite:wikipedia-abdel-wahed-el-wakil}

## Formation under Hassan Fathy
El-Wakil was born in Cairo in 1943 and trained as an architect there, coming under the decisive influence of Hassan Fathy — the Egyptian pioneer of vernacular mud-brick building — for whom he worked as a young man. From Fathy he absorbed the conviction that the old building crafts of Egypt held solutions modern concrete had forgotten: vault and dome, wind-catcher and courtyard, built of earth and brick by hands that still knew how. {cite:wikipedia-abdel-wahed-el-wakil}

## The mosques of Saudi Arabia
His reputation rests above all on more than a dozen mosques built in Saudi Arabia from the 1970s onward, mostly in Jeddah and Medina: the Corniche Mosque in Jeddah, the King Saud Mosque, the Sulaiman Mosque, and his work on the historic mosques of Medina including the Quba Mosque and the Qiblatayn Mosque — buildings that revived traditional Saudi and Ottoman-derived forms in load-bearing brick and stone at a moment when the kingdom's architecture was turning to imported styles. The Corniche Mosque won him the Aga Khan Award for Architecture; he received the award a second time for the Halawa House at Agami near Alexandria. {cite:wikipedia-abdel-wahed-el-wakil} {cite:muslim500-abdel-wahed-el-wakil}

## Practice and teaching
Beyond mosques he has designed houses, institutions and urban projects across the Arab world and taught the next generation — at the Prince of Wales's school of traditional architecture among others — standing inside the New Classical movement that argues buildings should arise from the craft traditions of their place. {cite:wikipedia-abdel-wahed-el-wakil}

## Significance
In the museum's record, El-Wakil is the architect who kept Islamic building tradition alive as practice rather than museum piece: the Egyptian who, in the oil-era Gulf, insisted that a mosque could still be raised in brick and vault by inherited craft — and whose Medina mosques let worshippers pray inside the argument. {cite:muslim500-abdel-wahed-el-wakil} {cite:wikipedia-abdel-wahed-el-wakil}

## The two masters and the city
El-Wakil's formation joins two very different teachers: the Egyptian architect Hassan Fathy, champion of mud-brick vernacular, and the British critic Peter Eisenman-adjacent formalists of the AA — and then the years in Saudi Arabia, where he translated those lessons into the mosques of Jeddah and Medina for which he became famous. The mosques are the record: small, load-bearing, domed, traditional in structure and contemporary in their restraint, they are buildings a museum of Islamic architecture can point to as the late-twentieth-century answer to a question Fathy had posed: what does a modern mosque look like when it refuses both the imported modernist block and the nostalgic replica? {cite:wikipedia-abdel-wahed-el-wakil} {cite:muslim500-abdel-wahed-el-wakil}

## The architect as translator
El-Wakil's record is the museum's example of the architect who treats building as translation: the Egyptian who learned from Fathy and from the West and spent a career finding the form that would carry both lessons into the mosques of the Hejaz. The search is the story; the mosques are the answer; and the result — a Muslim architect whose buildings are a record of what tradition can still teach — is the profile his Muslim 500 entry records. {cite:muslim500-abdel-wahed-el-wakil}
