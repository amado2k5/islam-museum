## Overview
Al-Musta'sim billah, Abd Allah ibn al-Mustansir, the last caliph of Baghdad, who held the caliphate between 640 and 656 AH. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p. 703,710-711}

## His accession
Al-Suyuti says that al-Duwaydar and al-Sharabi set him up after his father's death and put him before his uncle al-Khafaji, for his softness and weak judgement, so that the affair would be theirs. His father had multiplied the troops and nevertheless flattered and placated the Tatars. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p. 703,710-711}

## The vizier and the Tatars
Al-Suyuti says that the vizier al-Alqami advised him to cut down most of the troops, and that placating the Tatars would achieve the aim, and he did so. He adds that al-Alqami was eager to transfer the state to the Alids, that messages passed between him and the Tatars in secret, while the caliph was occupied with his pleasures. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p. 703,710-711}

Ibn Khaldun relates that the vizier went out to Hulagu and sought safe conduct for himself, then returned, promising the caliph that he would leave him in the caliphate. Al-Musta'sim went out with his household and the men of his state, and they were killed. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p. 900-901}

## His death
He was born in 609 AH. Ibn al-Imad describes him as forbearing, generous, sound in his inner self, of little judgement and good in religion. The Tatars trampled him to death at the end of Muharram 656 AH. Ibn al-Imad says that the affair was too occupied for any historian to record his death or the burial of his body. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 278-279}

Al-Suyuti reports al-Dhahabi's view that he was not buried, and describes the event as a calamity such as Islam had not suffered. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p. 715-716}

Ibn Khaldun gives a description of the disorder in Baghdad before the fall: a lasting strife between the Shi'a and the people of the Sunna, and between the Hanbalis and the other schools. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p. 900-901}

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "History Taught Me"
[[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] discusses the caliph al-Musta'sim bi'llah in the episodes on the fall of Baghdad (fourteen to seventeen) of his programme [[work/allamani-al-tarikh-program|"History Taught Me"]], and makes him an example of what he calls "poor leadership", which he counts among the causes of defeat. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:16:30}

### The caliph and the vizier
Al-Suwaidan regards it as "poor judgement and bad planning" on al-Musta'sim's part that he left the vizier [[person/al-alqami|Mu'ayyad al-Din Ibn al-Alqami]] in office, and says the caliph was taken up with his parties, luxury and play and ran nothing of the state, so that its whole administration was in the vizier's hands. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:05:30} He says that "delegation does not cancel responsibility": the ruler must keep his eyes open and choose his inner circle, and he cites a hadith narrated by al-Bukhari from Abu Sa'id al-Khudri that every caliph has two inner circles, one urging him to good and one urging him to evil. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:06:30} He takes the vizier's fourteen years in office, from 642 to 656 AH, without the caliph grasping the danger, as proof of the caliph's "lightness of mind". {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep14 00:07:00}

### Weakening the army
Al-Suwaidan relates that the caliph believed the vizier and issued a decision cutting the arms budget and reducing the number of soldiers, so that the army fell from a hundred thousand in the last days of [[person/al-mustansir-billah|al-Mustansir]] to ten thousand horsemen in 654 AH. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:05:30} He reports that [[person/ibn-kathir|Ibn Kathir]] in "al-Bidaya wa-l-Nihaya" blames Ibn al-Alqami for his advice but lays greater blame on the caliph himself, who accepted humiliation and forgot that among his first duties were to secure his people, defend his land and strengthen his army. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:07:30} He adds that Hulagu came to know everything about the caliph: his means, his strength and his weak points. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep15 00:08:30}

### The letters and the counsel
Al-Suwaidan says that when Hulagu's first letter arrived the caliph was still deluded by his power, thinking he ruled the greatest state in the world and that the caliph's capital had never fallen, so he answered harshly, telling Hulagu to go back to Khurasan and that he had thousands upon thousands of horsemen and foot ready to fight. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:08:00} Al-Suwaidan thinks he was deceiving himself, since the Muslim world was fragmented: the Ayyubids in Syria and the Mamluks in Egypt were in fierce conflict, and neither Turks nor Persians would move to help him. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:09:00} When the second letter came, fear took hold of him and he consulted his court; the vizier Ibn al-Alqami advised him to placate Hulagu with money and treasures, apologise to him, and put his name in the Friday sermon and on the coinage, and at first the caliph leaned to this view. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:10:00} But Mujahid al-Din Aybak, known as the Lesser Dawatdar, the army commander, refused and insisted on fighting, accusing Ibn al-Alqami of treason and collusion with Hulagu, and the caliph changed his mind and turned to resistance. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:11:00} During the siege the caliph announced large money rewards for those who stood firm, without success, and then sent to Hulagu to ask for peace. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep16 00:18:30}

### Surrender and death
Al-Suwaidan relates that when the eastern walls fell the caliph sent a delegation with precious gifts and jewels to ask for safe-conduct, and Hulagu gave no answer. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:06:00} On Tuesday 29 Muharram 656 AH he sent a delegation led by his middle son Abu al-Fadl Abd al-Rahman carrying great sums, which Hulagu did not accept. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:06:00} On Sunday 4 Safar the caliph went out in person with his sons and three thousand of the notables, imams, judges and leading men of Baghdad, and ordered the people to lay down their arms, which they did; Hulagu then ordered those who had come killed. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:08:00} The caliph was later brought before Hulagu in his palace and made to reveal his possessions and jewels, visible and hidden, and he handed over all the gold, silver and precious stones he owned. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:09:30} On 10 Safar 656 AH Hulagu ordered him killed with his sons, uncles and relatives. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:11:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:12:00} Al-Suwaidan says, in strong words, that he was killed "unlamented", because by his weakness and by handing power to an unworthy inner circle he ended the rule of his forebears. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:13:00}

### As Ibn Aybak al-Dawadari describes him
Al-Suwaidan says the caliph was not equal to the event: weak of resolve, hesitant, easily led, inclined to amusement and luxury, and blind to where things were heading. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:17:00} He stresses that this is how men of his own time described him, not his own description, quoting [[person/ibn-aybak-al-dawadari|Ibn Aybak al-Dawadari]] in "Kanz al-Durar" that "there was rashness, levity and injustice in him", and "simple-mindedness, weakness and a readiness to be led by frivolous men", that he played with pigeons and rode Egyptian donkeys, heedless of the Muslims' interests. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:17:30} He relates that shortly before the Mongol attack the caliph wrote to [[person/badr-al-din-lulu|Badr al-Din Lu'lu']], lord of Mosul, asking for a troupe of singers, at the very time Hulagu's envoy was asking Mosul for mangonels, and that Badr al-Din, as he tells it, pointed those around him to the contrast between the two requests; al-Suwaidan remarks that victory does not come through singing and amusement. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:18:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:18:30}

This portrait stands against what Ibn al-Imad reports in "Shadharat al-Dhahab", that he was forbearing, generous, sound at heart and of little judgement (see above); the page gives each account under its own name. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep17 00:17:00} {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 278-279}
