## Overview
Raymond III was count of Tripoli and, through his wife, lord of Tiberias. The Muslim chronicler Ibn al-Athir calls him "the count, lord of Tripoli", by name Raymond son of Raymond of Saint-Gilles, and gives him a prominent place in the story of the Frankish quarrel of 582 and the battle of [[event/battle-of-hattin-583|Hattin]]. The Encyclopaedia of Islam has no article on him; it mentions him in the articles on Tiberias and on Nur al-Din. This record follows those mentions and Ibn al-Athir's chronicle. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 141} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 29} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 142}

## Capture in 1165
The Encyclopaedia of Islam refers in passing to the capture of Raymond III of Tripoli in the year it dates 561/1165, in its account of Nur al-Din's campaigns: the ruler used the moment to cross the Biqa' and win back the fortress of Munaytira. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 142}

## Guardian of the boy king
According to Ibn al-Athir, the count had married the countess who held Tiberias and moved to live with her there. When the leprous king of the Franks in Syria died and left the kingdom to a young nephew, the count took the boy in charge and ran the state, because, the chronicler says, no Frank of that time was greater in standing, braver or sounder in counsel. He therefore hoped for the throne through the boy. When the boy died, the kingdom went to his mother, who married a newcomer named Kay, [[person/guy-de-lusignan|Guy de Lusignan]], and crowned him, which ended Raymond's hopes. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 141}

Raymond was asked to account for the revenues collected while he governed, and replied that he had spent them on the boy; this deepened his resentment. He broke openly with the king and wrote to [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Saladin]], who was glad, promised him help and, in Ibn al-Athir's words, undertook to make him independent king of all the Franks. Saladin held some of the count's knights and released them. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 141-142} Ibn al-Athir treats this division as one of the greatest causes of the later Muslim recovery of Frankish lands and of Jerusalem. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 142} The episode has its own record, [[event/frank-split-raymond-582|the Frankish schism]].

## Return to the Franks
In 583 Saladin's raiders returned from the Acre region through Tiberias, where the count was, and he did not object. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 144} The Franks then sent the patriarch, priests, monks and knights to him; they reproached him for siding with Saladin, and the patriarch threatened to excommunicate him and annul his marriage. Ibn al-Athir says the count became afraid, apologised and repented; they accepted his excuse and he went with them to the king. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 145}

## Counsel before Hattin
After Saladin took the town of Tiberias while its citadel, holding its lady and her children, held out, the Frankish leaders met. Ibn al-Athir reports Raymond arguing against advancing: Tiberias belonged to him and his wife, he was content for Saladin to take the citadel, and he had never seen a Muslim army as large and strong as Saladin's. If Saladin stayed, his troops would be unable to endure the long absence from home, and the Franks could retake the town. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 145} {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 146} [[person/raynald-de-chatillon|Raynald of Kerak]] answered that he had spoken at length in fear of the Muslims and must lean towards them; Raymond replied that he was one of them and would go forward if they did. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 146} The Encyclopaedia of Islam, summarising the same episode, says the Templars' Grand Master persuaded the Frankish troops encamped at Saffuriyya to move to rescue the town despite Raymond's warnings, and that this led to the disaster of Hattin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 29}

## Hattin and death
During the fighting, Ibn al-Athir says, the count and a group with him charged the Muslims facing them; the commander on that side, Taqi al-Din Umar, ordered his men to open a way for them, and the count broke through. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 147} He reached Tyre, then Tripoli, and died within a few days, in the chronicler's words of rage and grief at what had befallen the Franks and the Christian cause. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 148} {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 152} Since he left no son, Ibn al-Athir adds, the Franks handed Tripoli and its lands to the prince of Antioch. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 175}

## Dates and doubts
Neither the chronicle nor the Encyclopaedia gives his birth or death date. Wikidata gives 1140 for birth, and 1 September 1187 or simply 1187 for death; they are marked as tradition here. The cause of death given above is Ibn al-Athir's characterisation, not a medical record.
