## Summary
Professor Winai Dahlan is a Thai food scientist who founded and directs the Halal Science Center at Chulalongkorn University in Bangkok — widely described as the world's first scientific institution dedicated to halal assurance. The Muslim 500 lists him in Science & Technology; Wikipedia records his professorship in the Faculty of Allied Health Sciences and his leadership of lipid and fat science research and the international graduate programme in food and nutrition. {cite:muslim500-winai-dahlan} {cite:wikipedia-winai-dahlan}

## Halal as a science
Dahlan's field is the laboratory infrastructure of the halal market: how to detect pork DNA, alcohol and non-halal additives in food, cosmetics and pharmaceuticals; how to certify supply chains; and how to give religious compliance a measurable, reproducible scientific basis. At the Halal Science Center he built laboratories for halal analysis, trained inspectors and industry auditors, and developed information systems that let manufacturers and consumers trace the halal status of products. {cite:wikipedia-winai-dahlan} {cite:muslim500-winai-dahlan}

## Serving a Muslim minority
Thailand's Muslims are a minority of some millions in a Buddhist-majority kingdom, and the centre Dahlan directs serves them with the tools of a modern university: analytical chemistry, molecular biology and databases. The model — a national university housing halal science — has been studied and emulated in other countries where Muslim minorities rely on state and university laboratories for certification. {cite:wikipedia-winai-dahlan}

## Research and honours
Beyond halal science, his research covers lipids, fats and food safety; he has served on Thai national committees on food standards and received national honours for his service to science and to Thailand's Muslim community, which regards him as among its most prominent scholars. {cite:wikipedia-winai-dahlan}

## Significance
In the museum's record, Dahlan stands for an unusual kind of Islamic institution: the university laboratory that certifies what millions eat. His career links a Muslim minority's daily religious observance to the analytical machinery of a national university — halal made measurable. {cite:muslim500-winai-dahlan} {cite:wikipedia-winai-dahlan}

## Halal as a laboratory question
Dahlan's most quoted argument is deceptively simple: halal certification is a science problem before it is a religious-administrative one. Whether a gelatin is pork-derived, whether a processing aid is alcohol-based, whether a supply chain is clean — these are questions for chromatography and DNA testing, not only for a committee's stamp. By building a halal-science centre inside a major Thai university, he gave the certificate an evidentiary backbone, and gave Thailand — a mostly Buddhist country that became one of the world's halal-food exporters — a scientific instrument for a religious market. His public lectures on halal and on science in Muslim life are the reason he appears in the Muslim 500's science pages rather than in a trade directory. {cite:muslim500-winai-dahlan} {cite:wikipedia-winai-dahlan}

## Science as service
Dahlan's record is the museum's example of the scientist who treats his discipline as a form of service: the chemist who built a halal-science centre not because it was a fashionable research area but because the Muslim consumers of his country needed it. The pattern — scientific expertise directed at a community's practical need, in a university that had no obligation to serve it — is the one his Muslim 500 entry records; the museum keeps it as the profile of a researcher who made his laboratory answer to a market that had never been asked. {cite:muslim500-winai-dahlan}
