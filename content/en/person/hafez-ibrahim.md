## Summary
Egyptian poet (1872–1932), surnamed 'Poet of the Nile', a pillar of modern neoclassical Arabic verse alongside Ahmad Shawqi. His poetry is collected in the Diwan Hafez; he treated the cares of the Egyptian people, social justice and women's questions, and translated Victor Hugo's Les Miserables from French. {cite:wikipedia-hafez-ibrahim}

## Life and career

Hafez Ibrahim was born in 1872 in Dayrut, Upper Egypt, and raised an orphan in poverty, coming to know the hardships of the popular classes that later marked his verse; he entered the military schools and served as an officer in the Egyptian army for years. {cite:wikipedia-hafez-ibrahim}

Pensioned early, he turned to literature and journalism, worked in libraries and the Egyptian National Library, and joined Ahmad Shawqi to form the twin pillar of modern Egyptian poetry. {cite:wikipedia-hafez-ibrahim}

## Poetry

He published the Diwan Hafez in 1901 containing panegyric, patriotic, political and social poems; among the famous are his poems on Egyptian events such as the Dinshaway incident, besides poems calling for women's education and fair treatment. {cite:wikipedia-hafez-ibrahim}

His style was marked by emotional honesty and closeness to the people's cares — he was called 'poet of the people' against Shawqi the court poet — and his other works include a prose translation of Hugo's Les Miserables. {cite:wikipedia-hafez-ibrahim}

## Legacy

With Ahmad Shawqi he forms the twin of Egyptian poetry in the Nahda age; his poems entered the Egyptian conscience so deeply that he became the model of the 'social poet' before the term existed. {cite:wikipedia-hafez-ibrahim}

He died in Cairo in 1932; his verses — especially the socially tuned ones — are still recited and taught in Arab schools and universities. {cite:wikipedia-hafez-ibrahim}
