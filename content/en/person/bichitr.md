## Summary
The earliest works securely by Bichitr are in a fully mature, if experimental, style, followed almost at once by the brilliant portraits for which he is best known. A self-portrait in [[person/jahangir-mughal|Jahangir]] Preferring a Sufi Shaikh to Kings (Freer Gallery) shows a man of no more than thirty; Milo Cleveland Beach argues for a date of about 1615–20, since Shaikh Husain Ajmeri died about 1620 and the portrait of James I in it was probably copied from a painting brought by Sir Thomas Roe. {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 100} {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 104} Beach infers from his dress that he was a Hindu. {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 100}

## Style
In the portraits of the 1630s — among them [[person/shah-jahan-mughal|Shah Jahan]] inscribed by the emperor "a good portrait of me in my fortieth year" (1631) and Shah Shuja Enthroned with Maharaja Gaj Singh (1638) — Bichitr paints with a brilliant but hard line and bold patterns, to an effect of cold formality, unlike the soft [[person/balchand-painter|Balchand]]. {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 100} {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 101} He drew on European prints and paintings for shading and copied motifs from Dürer and John de Critz's portrait of James I. {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 101} His Asaf Khan in the [[object/minto-album|Minto Album]], about 1628–30, marks for Beach the transition from his softer, more romantic early style to the immaculate, restrained works after 1630; he was still painting in the 1640s. {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 72} {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 101}
