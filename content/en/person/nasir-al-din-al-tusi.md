## Overview
Nasir al-Din Muhammad ibn Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Tusi died in 672 AH, about 1274 CE. Haji Khalifa describes him as "the learned verifier" {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p.268}. The Encyclopaedia of Islam presents him as an extraordinarily prolific scholar of the Mongol age, whose works range over astronomy, astrology, geomancy, mathematics, physics, mineralogy, medicine, jurisprudence, philosophy including logic, and theology {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 798}. Its article on him has three parts: his life, his work as theologian and philosopher, and his work as scientist.

## Life
He was born in 1201 at [[place/tus|Tus]], near Mashhad, into a family that, according to the Shi'i biographer al-Khwansari, came originally from Jahrud Sawa near Qumm. He received his Imami education in Tus and died at [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] on 18 Dhu l-Hijja 672/25 June 1274 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 797}. The article's second author describes his schooling in more detail. He first studied philosophy and mathematics under Kamal al-Din Muhammad al-Hasib in Tus, then travelled to nearby [[place/nishapur|Nishapur]] to study with the physician Qutb al-Din al-Misri and the polymath Farid al-Din Damadh, reading there the works of [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]], who was one of his most important formative influences. Later he went to Iraq to read under the Shi'i jurist Mu'in al-Din Salim ibn Badran al-Misri and to study with [[person/kamal-al-din-ibn-yunus|Kamal al-Din ibn Yunus]], a renowned Shafi'i jurist who was also expert in astronomy and mathematics; the article stresses that being a Shi'i did not prevent al-Tusi from studying with him {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 802}.

## Years with the Isma'ili courts
After his formal education al-Tusi found patrons at the Isma'ili courts of Persia, beginning in the early 630s/1230s. He spent some twenty-five years at Qa'in in [[place/quhistan|Quhistan]] and at [[place/alamut|Alamut]], until the fall of Alamut to the Mongols in 654/1256. The article records a dispute over whether he was a willing convert to Isma'ilism, as his early autobiography Sayr wa-suluk states, or whether this was a pretence made necessary by his need of patronage and protection. It adds that in these years he found refuge from the upheavals of the Mongol invasions and produced some of his most important work {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 802}.

His first patron, the Isma'ili governor Muhtasham Nasir al-Din Abd al-Rahim, was especially interested in ethics. For him al-Tusi translated into Persian a short treatise on the behaviour of children attributed to [[person/ibn-al-muqaffa|Ibn al-Muqaffa]], whose authenticity the article calls doubtful. Muhtasham then asked him to complete a draft of practical ethics that he had himself begun. The result, the Akhlaq-i Muhtashami, was composed around 630/1233 and issued under al-Tusi's name; it survives in an Arabic version with a Persian translation attached, in forty chapters that move from knowledge of God, prophecy and the imamate to the virtues of the pious and the Sufi, illustrated from the Qur'an, hadith, poetry and the sayings of sages and Isma'ili propagandists {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 798}.

## Akhlaq-i Nasiri
He wrote his Persian work "Akhlaq-i Nasiri" in Quhistan for its ruler, Nasir al-Din Abd al-Rahim al-Muhtasham {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p.268}. See [[work/akhlaq-i-nasiri|Akhlaq-i Nasiri]]. The Encyclopaedia article places this work, written for the same patron, among the ethical writings of his Isma'ili period, and notes that he returned to ethics a last time under the Mongols, in a Sufi spirit, with the treatise [[work/awsaf-al-ashraf|Awsaf al-ashraf]], written at the request of the vizier Shams al-Din Muhammad Juwayni as a guide for the Sufi from belief to union and the extinction of the self in God {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 798} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 799}.

## With the Mongols and the Maragha observatory
After Alamut fell, al-Tusi, already famous, joined the entourage of [[person/hulagu|Hulagu]] as court astrologer and director of religious endowments. In the first capacity he accompanied the Il-Khan on the campaign against Baghdad and witnessed the end of the [[polity/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid caliphate]]. He then oversaw the building of an observatory and its instruments at [[place/maragha|Maragha]] in Azerbaijan, the Mongol headquarters, and became its first director {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 802}. He also prepared the astronomical tables known as [[work/zij-i-ilkhani|al-Zij al-Ilkhani]], finished at about the age of seventy under Hulagu's successor [[person/abaga-ibn-hulagu|Abaqa]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 798}.

The article describes the observatory, with its large library and school, as one of the most ambitious scientific institutions founded up to that time. It drew many scholars and students, reportedly including Chinese astronomers, and although it lasted only about fifty years its intellectual legacy had repercussions from China to Europe {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 802}. Among those said to have visited Maragha were Barhebraeus, Ibn al-Fuwati and the logician [[person/najm-al-din-al-katibi|Najm al-Din al-Katibi]], with whom al-Tusi held a scholarly exchange on metaphysics and logic. The name of Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi, the main commentator on al-Tusi's astronomical Tadhkira, is absent from the lists; the article suggests this may be because he criticised al-Tusi's work and is said to have denounced him to Hulagu. For a reason unknown, al-Tusi left Maragha shortly before his death with many pupils and followers, went to Baghdad, and died there some months later; he is said to have been buried there according to his own testament {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 798}.

## Theologian and philosopher
The article's author on this side of his work, H. Daiber, describes Ibn Sina's metaphysics as the base of al-Tusi's theology. Al-Tusi defended Ibn Sina against [[person/fakhr-al-din-al-razi|Fakhr al-Din al-Razi]] in his Hall mushkilat al-Isharat, composed around 644/1246 for Muhtasham Shihab al-Din, and the Neo-Platonic doctrine of emanation underlies his reply to the mystic Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi on how many things can come from one. Later, after he had turned away from the Isma'ilis, he wrote Masari al-musari, a refutation of the criticisms of Ibn Sina by [[person/al-shahrastani|al-Shahrastani]]. His Tajrid al-i'tiqad, a theological work that became authoritative in Twelver Shi'i circles and has been commented upon many times, belongs to this phase {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 799}. See [[work/tajrid-al-itiqad|Tajrid al-i'tiqad]].

On his confessional position the article is careful. It says that, despite a growing sympathy for the [[group/imamiyya|Twelver Shi'a]], he remained faithful to many Isma'ili doctrines, and that even his last work, the Talkhis al-muhassal of 669/1271, defends the Isma'ili doctrine of authoritative teaching and rejects the Shi'i doctrine that the Imam's designation can be cancelled by a change in God's will. It adds that the Isma'ili and Shi'i inheritance does not obscure his role as a pioneer of Ibn Sina's philosophy {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 799}.

The article also records a critical Sunni reception. The Hanbali theologian [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] criticised the correspondence between al-Tusi and al-Qunawi, and his pupil [[person/ibn-qayyim-al-jawziyya|Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya]] followed that criticism, naming al-Tusi in his Ighathat al-lahfan as a concealed unbeliever who held that the Qur'an was scripture for the masses and Ibn Sina's works scripture for the elite. This is the judgement of those authors, reported by the article, and it is not the museum's own {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 799}.

## Scientist
F. J. Ragep, the author of the third part, notes that al-Tusi wrote over 150 prose works in Arabic and Persian, on religious and on secular subjects, and was especially drawn to the exact mathematical sciences, whose certainty he and others valued as a means to the truth of God's creation {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 802}. His recensions of Greek and early Islamic scientific works, among them Euclid's Elements, Ptolemy's Almagest and the "intermediate books", gave generations of later students a way to study the Greek tradition with or without a teacher. His attempt to prove Euclid's parallels postulate was, in the article's words, one of the more sophisticated of a long line of such attempts, and his treatise al-Shakl al-qatta' marked a step in treating trigonometry as a discipline apart from astronomy {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 802}.

He won his greatest fame in [[concept/astronomy|astronomy]]. The article calls his most original achievement his planetary theory: troubled by the departure of the Ptolemaic system from uniform circular motion, he devised a model of two spheres, the smaller rotating twice as fast as the larger and in the opposite direction, so that a point on it moves along a straight line. Used in his lunar and planetary models, it kept Ptolemaic accuracy while preserving uniform circular motion. The models first appeared in a Persian treatise for his Isma'ili patrons and were developed in his Arabic Tadhkira fi ilm al-hay'a. The article adds that the device, now called the "Tusi couple", influenced astronomers for several centuries and reached Sanskrit and Byzantine texts and Renaissance astronomers including Copernicus {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 802}.
