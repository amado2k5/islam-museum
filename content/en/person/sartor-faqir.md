## The faqir of Buner

Sartor Faqir — born Saidullah Khan — came from Rega in the Buner Valley, of the Yusufzai tribe, and left home young in search of religious education, travelling as far as Mazar-i-Sharif, where he settled for some ten years before returning about 1895.{cite:wikipedia-sartor-faqir-en} Edwards, who studied his following, describes a mendicant who had abandoned home and family and was believed by the tribesmen to work miracles — raising the dead, healing sickness, flying from place to place.{cite:edwards-heroes-of-the-age-1996} The name Sartor itself, "bare-headed", marks the ascetic who goes uncovered, and his followers called him Mullah Mastan: the God-intoxicated.{cite:wikipedia-sartor-faqir-en}

## July 1897

In the summer of 1897 he proclaimed jihad and, on the night of 26–27 July, led thousands of tribesmen up to Malakand, the first blow of the great frontier uprising of that year; the assault on Chakdara followed.{cite:wikipedia-sartor-faqir-en} Edwards treats the rising's suddenness as inseparable from the faqir himself — a figure whose very unpredictability the settled authorities, British and tribal alike, could neither predict nor buy.{cite:edwards-heroes-of-the-age-1996}

## After the rising

The movement waned after 1897, though the name continued to draw followers in Buner for years.{cite:wikipedia-sartor-faqir-en} He died in 1917 — the article's infobox also records 1918 — and on his death the following broke up.{cite:wikipedia-sartor-faqir-en} British writers called him the "Mad Mullah" and the "Mad Faqir"; those names are recorded here as what they were, colonial labels for a man the Raj could not otherwise explain, not as descriptions this museum adopts.{cite:wikipedia-sartor-faqir-en}

## Identity and faith

No source consulted names a school or a Sufi order for Sartor Faqir. Edwards presents an independent mendicant, with no teacher, initiation or order attested; his followers' belief in his powers is a public role, not an affiliation. A single unattributed modern magazine claim of affinity with the Wahhabi movement restates colonial framing without a source and is not evidence; it is preserved in his record. His faith is therefore recorded as unknown.
