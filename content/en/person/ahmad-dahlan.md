## Summary
Ahmad Dahlan (1 August 1868 – 23 February 1923), born Muhammad Darwis, was an Indonesian reformer and religious leader from Yogyakarta who founded the Muhammadiyah in 1912 as an instrument of religious and educational reform. It became one of the largest Muslim organisations in Indonesia, and he was posthumously declared a National Hero. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-ar}

His place in this record rests on what the Muhammadiyah built: a wide network of schools, universities, hospitals and mosques, carrying a reformist message through its institutions to tens of millions of Indonesian Muslims across generations. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en}

## Early life and education
He was born as Muhammad Darwis in Kauman, the Muslim quarter of Yogyakarta behind its Great Mosque, the fourth of seven children of Kyai Haji Abu Bakr, a courtier of the Yogyakarta Sultanate who served as imam and preacher at the Kauman Great Mosque. He was first taught by his parents at home, then sent to a pesantren (Islamic boarding school) to further his knowledge. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-ar}

Both articles trace his descent to the twelfth generation from Maulana Malik Ibrahim (Sunan Gresik), believed to be among the first who brought Islam to the island of Java. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-ar}

## Pilgrimage and study in Mecca
In 1883, aged fifteen, he travelled to Mecca, performed the Hajj and remained five years studying the religious sciences — recitation, exegesis, theology, jurisprudence and Sufism — before returning to Indonesia in 1888. During this pilgrimage his name was changed from Muhammad Darwis to Ahmad Dahlan, a name he received from Sayid Bakri Syatha, a teacher of the Shafi'i school in Mecca; he also became a student of Ahmad Khatib Al-Minangkabawi, an imam of the Grand Mosque. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-ar}

In Mecca he associated with fellow Indonesians from Sulawesi, West Java, West Sumatra, Aceh and other areas, which helped them conceive a common interest against the Dutch colonial authorities and the need to revive Islam in Indonesia. He went to Mecca again in 1903 to deepen his knowledge and returned in 1905. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-ar}

## Founding the Muhammadiyah
After returning to Java he married the daughter of the imam of the Great Mosque of Yogyakarta. As one of a growing group of self-described modernists, he was concerned at the many Javanese practices not justified by Islamic scripture, and argued for a renewed, purer Islam more in step with the modern world; the efforts of Western Christian missionaries also concerned him. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-ar}

He joined Budi Utomo in 1909 hoping to preach reform to its members, but his supporters urged him to create his own organisation. In 1912 he created Muhammadiyah as an educational organisation realising his reformist ideals; it was quickly joined by merchants and craftsmen, and in 1917 he added a women's section, Aisyiyah, which played a significant role in modernising the life of Indonesian women. Spreading to the outer islands, Muhammadiyah established a strong base in Sulawesi only a decade after its founding. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-ar}

It was one of several indigenous Indonesian organisations founded in the first three decades of the twentieth century, the period known as the Indonesian National Awakening, key in establishing Indonesian nationalism and ultimately independence. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-ar}

## Positions and criticism
This section should be read as a presentation of differing positions reported in the sources, not as the museum's verdict on them.

Dahlan's project rested on criticising Javanese practices he held to lack justification in scripture, and calling for a renewed, "purer" Islam in step with the modern world; countering Western missionary activity was among his motives. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-ar}

The two articles differ on the Muhammadiyah's present size: the English article gives more than 60 million members, the Arabic article 20 million; both make it the second-largest Muslim organisation in Indonesia after Nahdlatul Ulama. These are the articles' own figures at their revision dates, not a neutral fact recorded by the museum. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-ar}

## Personal life and death
After his first return from Mecca he married the daughter of the imam of the Great Mosque of Yogyakarta; this record does not detail his family further. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en}

Throughout the last year of his life he suffered several health issues; on his doctor's advice he rested at Mount Tretes, Malang, then returned to Yogyakarta for the annual Muhammadiyah meeting. His health deteriorated until he died on 23 February 1923, aged 54, and he was buried at Karangkajen cemetery. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en}

## Impact and audience
Ahmad Dahlan was inaugurated as a National Hero of Indonesia: the English article gives Presidential Decree No. 657 of 1961, while the Arabic article gives No. 157 of the same year; the two numbers stand in conflict in the sources and await a third source. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-ar}

The Muhammadiyah he founded still works in education, health and preaching across Indonesia, so his institutional impact long outlived his short life. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-dahlan-en}
