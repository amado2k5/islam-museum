## Summary
Amjad Masad is a Jordanian-American entrepreneur and software engineer, founder and chief executive of Replit, an online development environment that lets anyone write, run and deploy software from a browser and, increasingly, by describing what they want to an AI agent. The Muslim 500 places him in Science & Technology; Wikipedia records his path from Amman through Codecademy and Facebook to founding Replit. {cite:muslim500-amjad-masad} {cite:wikipedia-amjad-masad}

## From Amman to Silicon Valley
Masad was born in 1987 in Amman, Jordan, and taught himself to code on an old family computer, later describing how he reverse-engineered software he could not afford to buy. He studied computer science at the Princess Sumaya University for Technology in Amman, then moved to the United States, where he became a founding engineer at Codecademy, the online learn-to-code platform, and afterwards led work on the JavaScript infrastructure team at Facebook. {cite:wikipedia-amjad-masad}

## Replit
Masad had been building versions of a browser-based coding environment for years — an open-source project called JSRepl was used inside early versions of Codecademy — and in 2016 he founded Replit with Haya Odeh and Faris Masad. The company's premise is that the friction of setting up a programming environment keeps people from creating software, so it puts the whole toolchain in the browser: editor, runtime, hosting, collaboration. Replit passed through Y Combinator and grew into one of the most-used development platforms in the world, valued in the billions of dollars by the mid-2020s. {cite:wikipedia-amjad-masad}

## The AI turn
From 2022 Replit moved aggressively into artificial intelligence, shipping code-completion and, later, an agent that builds entire applications from natural-language prompts — the wave the industry calls "vibe coding". Masad became a prominent public advocate for the idea that AI will multiply the number of people who can make software, framing Replit's mission as giving everyone the power to program. {cite:wikipedia-amjad-masad} {cite:muslim500-amjad-masad}

## Recognition and voice
The Muslim 500 cites him as the co-founder and CEO of the AI-powered development platform. Beyond product, Masad is a visible spokesman for the Arab world's engineering talent and for the thesis that the next billion programmers will come from places like the Jordan where he started. {cite:muslim500-amjad-masad}

## Significance
In the museum's record, Masad represents the Levantine diaspora's software generation: self-taught in Amman, formed inside Silicon Valley's best-known companies, and building a tool whose stated purpose — to remove the gatekeeping around making software — carries an egalitarian echo that his own biography underwrites. {cite:muslim500-amjad-masad} {cite:wikipedia-amjad-masad}

## The pitch he keeps repeating
Masad's story, retold by him in interviews, is deliberately a parable: a boy in Amman learns computing on borrowed machines, teaches the world to code, then builds the machine that lets machines code. The narrative is also a recruiting tool — Replit's marketing and Masad's own advocacy are inseparable — but the underlying wager is real: that programming is moving from a craft of specialists toward something closer to literacy, and that the company holding the browser-based toolchain will matter in that transition. {cite:wikipedia-amjad-masad} {cite:muslim500-amjad-masad}

## The Arab founder
For the Arab world's engineers he is a proof: the founder-CEO of a multibillion-dollar American platform who came out of a Jordanian classroom. He speaks about the region's talent directly, and his hiring and public comments keep Amman and Cairo on the map of Replit's story — the reason his Muslim 500 entry resonates beyond a business listing. {cite:muslim500-amjad-masad} {cite:wikipedia-amjad-masad}

## The founder as teacher
Masad's public persona is built on a single pedagogy: that anyone can code, that the tools are now good enough to make the promise real, and that the only thing standing between a person and software is the idea. The argument is self-serving — it is also the argument of his company — but it is also, the sources agree, sincerely held. For the museum's record the point is the position it marks: a Muslim founder at the centre of the argument about whether artificial intelligence will finally make the promise of universal literacy in code true. {cite:muslim500-amjad-masad}
