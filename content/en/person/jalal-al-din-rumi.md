## Overview
Jalal al-Din Muhammad al-Balkhi al-Rumi, known by the honorific Mawlana, the greatest Sufi mystic and poet in Persian; born at [[place/balkh|Balkh]] about 30 September 604/1207 and died at [[place/konya|Konya]] on 17 December 672/1273. He is famous for his lyrics and for his didactic epic [[work/mathnawi-rumi|Mathnavi-yi Ma'navi]], which influenced mystical thought and literature throughout the Muslim world; after his death his disciples formed the [[group/mawlawiyya|Mawlawiyya]] order. {cite:britannica-rumi}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam article on him opens by calling him a Persian poet and the founder of the order of dervishes named after him. It gives his birth in Balkh on Rabi I 604/30 September 1207 and his death at Konya in Jumada II 672/1273, and judges unconvincing the arguments that have been raised against the birth date. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 418}

## The road from Balkh to Konya
His father [[person/baha-al-din-walad|Baha al-Din Walad]] was a noted mystical theologian, author and teacher. The family left Balkh about 615/1218 — over a quarrel with the ruler or ahead of the Mongols — passed through Nishapur where [[person/farid-al-din-attar|Farid al-Din Attar]] blessed them in the report, made the pilgrimage to Mecca and journeyed through Syria and Iraq, until they were called to Konya in 625/1228 where Baha al-Din taught. When he died in 628/1231 Jalal al-Din succeeded him, and Burhan al-Din Muhaqqiq completed his spiritual formation until about 638/1240. {cite:britannica-rumi}

## Family and the emigration, as the Encyclopaedia of Islam tells it
The article gives the father's name in full as Sultan al-Ulama Baha al-Din Walad, son of Husayn al-Khatibi, a preacher at Balkh whose sermons survive in print. It examines and rejects two family claims found in the sources, that the lineage reaches back to Abu Bakr and that the mother was a daughter of the Khwarazmshah [[person/muhammad-khwarazmshah|Ala al-Din Muhammad]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 418}

The biographical sources say Baha al-Din left Balkh after a dispute with that ruler and with his protege [[person/fakhr-al-din-al-razi|Fakhr al-Din al-Razi]], when his son was five, which would be 609/1212-3. The article notes that the sermons do attack both men, but also place Baha al-Din at [[place/vakhsh|Wakhsh]] between 600/1203 and 607/1211 and at [[place/samarqand|Samarkand]] in 609/1212-3, so he must have gone back to Balkh before leaving. It finds 609/1212-3 too early and suggests 614/1217 or the year before, since the hagiographer Aflaki has the family reach Malatya in 614/1217. Whether the quarrel was tied to the ruler's hostility to the caliph in Baghdad, it says, cannot be settled. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 418}

In 616/1219 Baha al-Din was in [[place/sivas|Sivas]]; he spent about four years at [[place/aksehir|Akshehir]] near Erzincan, then moved to Larende, now [[place/karaman|Karaman]], probably in 619/1222, and stayed seven years. The tomb of Rumi's mother, Mu'mina Khatun, is there, and Baha al-Din married his son at Larende to Jawhar Khatun, daughter of Sharaf al-Din Lala. In 626/1228, at the request of the Seljuk prince [[person/kayqubad-i|Ala al-Din Kaykubad]], the family moved to Konya, where Baha al-Din died on 18 Rabi II 628/1231. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 418}

## Burhan al-Din and the years of study
A year after Baha al-Din's death his former pupil Sayyid Burhan al-Din Muhaqqiq came to Konya to visit him and found him dead. Jalal al-Din became Burhan al-Din's disciple until the latter's death nine years on. Burhan al-Din withdrew to Kayseri after a time and died there, probably in 637/1239-40; his tomb is in [[place/kayseri|Kayseri]]. According to [[person/shams-al-din-al-aflaki|Aflaki]], Jalal al-Din went to [[place/aleppo|Aleppo]] and [[place/damascus|Damascus]] after the Sayyid arrived, to complete his studies, and Burhan al-Din is reported to have made him aware that his father held, besides outward learning, a knowledge reached through inner experience rather than study. After Burhan al-Din's death Jalal al-Din was alone for five years. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 418} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 419}

## Shams al-Din and the poetry
On 30 November 642/1244 he met Shams al-Din of Tabriz at Konya, and his state was transformed. After Shams's final disappearance in 645/1247 — now established as a murder — love, longing and loss became poetry in the ghazals of the [[work/diwan-shams-tabrizi|Divan-e Shams]] and the quatrains; he then found in the goldsmith Salah al-Din Zarkub and his disciple [[person/husam-al-din-chalabi|Husam al-Din Chalabi]] new manifestations of Shams, and under Husam al-Din's inspiration composed nearly 26,000 couplets of the Mathnavi on the model of Attar and [[person/sanai|Sana'i]], dictating them even in the bath or on the road while Husam al-Din wrote them down. {cite:britannica-rumi}

## Shams-i Tabrizi and the crisis with the disciples
The Encyclopaedia of Islam dates the first meeting to 26 Jumada II 642/1244, when the wandering dervish [[person/shams-tabrizi|Shams al-Din Muhammad Tabrizi]] lodged at the sugar merchants' khan in Konya. He asked Jalal al-Din the meaning of a saying of [[person/bayazid-al-bistami|Bayazid Bistami]] and was answered; Aflaki adds that they had met once in Damascus. The article calls the encounter a decisive change: Jalal al-Din fell in love with the dervish in the Sufi manner and took him into his home. It describes Shams as a man who always wore a black cap, was nicknamed the flier for his restless wandering, held the usual theology of his day, tried to keep Rumi from books and was by his sayings rather blunt. The sources call him the prince of the loved ones. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 419}

Rumi's son [[person/sultan-walad|Sultan Walad]] sets out in his Ibtidanama the idea that beyond the perfect saints stand lovers who have arrived, and beyond them the stage of the beloved, which nobody had known before Shams reached it. The article says Shams showed Rumi this way of love, that it made him a poet, and that it also led him to neglect his disciples. They grew angry and are said to have threatened Shams, who left for Damascus on 21 Shawwal 643/11 March 1246. Rumi sent Sultan Walad after him; Shams returned on foot, but the murmuring resumed. On 5 Shaban 645/5 December 1247, the article says, Shams was killed with the participation of Sultan Walad's brother Ala al-Din, or at his instigation, and his body was thrown into a well, later found and buried by Sultan Walad. It reads the silence of the Ibtidanama as reluctance to publish a family scandal. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 419}

The article adds that the death seems to have been kept from Rumi, who went to Damascus twice to look for him; that Sultan Walad depicts his father's state in moving verses; that he gave himself to music and dancing to a degree his own son felt was excessive; and that he found the lost Shams within himself, which is why the pen-name in most of his ghazals is Shams rather than his own name. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 419}

## Salah al-Din, Husam al-Din and the succession
In 647/1249 Rumi announced that Shams had appeared again in one of his disciples, the Konya goldsmith Salah al-Din Zarkub, unlettered but handsome and pleasant. He made him his deputy and the superior of the other disciples, wishing to withdraw from the offices of shaykh and preacher. The disciples, who had known him since childhood, found the first Shams easier to bear, and a second crisis followed until Rumi's threat to abandon them brought their apology; the article credits Sultan Walad's loyalty and Salah al-Din's modest character. Salah al-Din served as deputy for ten years and died, by the inscription on his sarcophagus, on 1 Muharram 657/29 December 1258. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 419}

His successor Chelebi Husam al-Din Hasan, whose family came from Urmiya and whose father headed the akhis of Konya, is called the inspirer of the Mathnavi. He lived with Rumi ten years, to his death on 6 Jumada II 672/18 December 1273, so the article dates his appointment to about 662/1263-4. Husam al-Din offered the succession to Sultan Walad, who declined; Husam al-Din died in 683/1283, after which Sultan Walad accepted the title of shaykh at the people's insistence and held it until 10 Rajab 712/1312. His son [[person/ulu-arif-celebi|Ulu Arif Chelebi]] followed, d. 719/1319, then Abid Chelebi, then Wajid Chelebi, d. 742/1341-2. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 419}

## The Mawlawiyya order
The article says the real history of the order begins with Sultan Walad, who founded its first branches and won it respect. Members were called Mawlawi already in Rumi's lifetime and were at first drawn from artisans, which caused offence. Listening to music and dancing stood at the centre of its devotions, as in other orders but with greater weight; the regular solemn form of the whirling ceremony, it says, was first introduced by Pir Adil Chelebi, d. 864/1460. See [[practice/sama-mawlawiyya|the Mawlawi sama]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 419}

It reports the view of the Turkish scholar Golpinarli, himself once a Mawlawi dervish, that the Mawlawis consider theirs a Sufi order in the strict sense, and his inclination to link it with the [[group/malamatiyya|Malamatiyya]] of Khurasan. It notes a gladness in Baha al-Din's sermons reminiscent of the [[group/qalandariyya|Qalandars]], and that some Chelebis lived like Qalandar dervishes, but holds that this proves nothing about Rumi himself, whom it describes as strongly emotional, easily inflamed and inclined to release excitement in dance. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 420}

## Works
The article stresses that Rumi's piety and thought have not been thoroughly studied, warns against relying on Mathnavi commentaries that read their own times into it, and notes that the Divan had only just become available in a critical edition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 420}

The Divan holds ghazals and quatrains, with some Greek and Turkish verses that the article takes as a sign of contact with common people and the non-Muslim population of Konya. His own pen-name there is Khamush, usually replaced by Shams-i Tabriz and sometimes by Salah al-Din. The Mathnavi is a didactic poem in rhyming couplets in six books; the seventh book is judged spurious. Husam al-Din asked for a work like the religious mathnavis of Sana'i and Attar. The eighteen opening verses are said to have been produced already written from Rumi's turban and the rest dictated to Husam al-Din, whenever they came, dancing, in the bath or walking, sometimes through the night, and read back for correction. A two-year pause followed Husam al-Din's wife's death, and the second book was begun in 662/1263-4, as the poet says. The article calls the structure informal, held together by free association, with stories broken off and resumed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 420}

A third work, [[work/fihi-ma-fihi|Fihi ma Fihi]], collects his sayings; the article says the title comes from a verse of [[person/ibn-arabi-muhyi-al-din|Ibn al-Arabi]]. A collection of his letters, the Maktubat, also exists. The chief biographical sources it names are Sultan Walad's Ibtidanama, the Risala of Faridun b. Ahmad Sipahsalar and Aflaki's Manaqib al-arifin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 420} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 421}

## Thought, as the article outlines it
In a second part by A. Bausani, the article says Rumi was no philosopher, protested against purely intellectual philosophy and disclaimed being a classical poet; above all he was a passionate lover of God who expressed himself in an unorthodox, volcanic manner, creating a style unique in Persian literature. It lists traces of [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]], Ibn al-Arabi, Sana'i and Attar, and thinks Ibn al-Arabi's influence perhaps exaggerated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 421}

On God, it finds absolute transcendence, spatial, intellectual and moral, with good and evil relative to God; reality is ordered in four realms, of nothingness, imagination, existence, and senses and colours, and God works in nothingness as His workshop. It judges real pantheism hard to attribute to him, since immanentism is foreign to his mind. On creation he seems to accept the [[concept/ashariyya|Ashari]] view that God creates and destroys in discontinuous atoms of time. Nature is a hint of God; a famous passage sketches a mystical progression, not scientific evolution, in which the rise of humanity from the animal kingdom is a first step towards the realms of angels and the Godhead. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 421}

Man is body, a deeper soul, a more hidden intellect and, deepest, a spirit of revelation found only in saints and prophets. Outward worship binds all, and the article gives his reason that rites are like a lover's gifts to the Beloved. On freedom and destiny he separates God's act from its product and says that, with spiritual eyes open, a person sees himself as wholly moved by God and wholly free, a freedom reached only through effort. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 421}

On the afterlife, the article says he never describes [[concept/fana-sufi|annihilation]] as absorption without remainder, comparing it to a candle before the sun or red-hot iron in fire, which takes on fire's qualities yet remains iron. In one story a lover dies on reaching the Beloved, and a few lines later the Beloved revives him; the highest state, he says, is not attainment but infinite aspiration after having attained. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 421} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 422}

## Style
The ghazals were often sung, or meant to be; tradition shows him improvising odes while dancing round a pillar of his school, and another story has him find Salah al-Din in the street, listening enraptured to the beat of a goldsmith's hammer. His strong sense of rhythm does not always keep to the classical metres, of which he complains, and irregularities appear in both major works. The Divan has a singing and a didactic style; the Mathnavi, one continuous discourse, has narrative, didactic and ecstatic styles. The narrative and didactic parts are remarkably simple and colloquial, with some verses containing colloquial Greek. The article says he found almost no imitators but is highly valued by modern Persians. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 422}
