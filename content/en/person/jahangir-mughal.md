## Overview
Jahangir was the fourth Mughal emperor of India in the line of Babur and the first of Akbar's children to survive. He was born on 17 Rabi' I 977 AH, 31 August 1569 CE, at Fathpur Sikri near Agra, in the hermitage of Shaykh Salim Chishti, to whose intercession the birth was attributed, to a Rajput queen called Maryam al-Zamani {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 404}. The Encyclopaedia of Islam article says he was named Salim after the shaykh, and that Akbar always called him Shaykhu Baba {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 404}.

## The quarrel with his father
The article says Akbar fell ill in 1001 AH, 1591 CE, and accused Salim of plotting to poison him, which began their estrangement; it reached a climax in 1008 AH, 1599 CE, when Salim rebelled and declared his independence at Allahabad {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 404}. In 1010 AH, 1601 CE, he marched on Agra with a large army, then withdrew to Allahabad, took the royal title and set up a court; Salima Sultan Begam, the widow of Bayram Khan, helped to bring about a reconciliation {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 405}. The article holds that the story of his love for the palace girl Anarkali finds no corroboration in history {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 405}. It says he believed Akbar's minister Abu al-Fadl was poisoning his father against him, so in 1011 AH, 1602 CE, he had Bir Singh Dev Bundela attack Abu al-Fadl on his way back from the Deccan; the minister was killed and his head sent to Salim, a killing the article calls unjustifiable {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 405}.

## Accession and his sons
After Akbar's death in 1013 AH, 1605 CE, he took the throne as Abu al-Muzaffar Muhammad Jahangir Ghazi, a title that also appears on some of his coins {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 405}. His eldest son Khusraw rebelled in 1015 AH, 1606 CE; they were reconciled but the emperor never forgave him. The article says a Sikh guru who had sheltered Khusraw was punished, that the punishment was seen as an atrocity, and that it laid the foundation of a long hostility between Indian Muslims and the Sikhs {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 405}. In 1016 AH, 1607 CE, a plot to kill him at Kabul was crushed; four ringleaders were executed and Khusraw was partly blinded by the emperor's order {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 405}.

## Marriage to Nur Jahan
In 1020 AH, 1611 CE, he married [[person/nur-jahan|Nur Jahan]], daughter of Ghiyath Beg, known as I'timad al-Dawla, which the article treats as the start of a new phase of his rule {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 405}. It notes that contemporary sources do not mention the popular story of his passion for her and of his arranging the murder of her first husband, Ali Quli Khan Istajlu, called Sher Afkan, and that after the marriage she gradually took all power, her name inscribed with his on gold coins {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 405}.

## Scholars in his reign
The article reports that [[person/nur-allah-al-shustari|Nur Allah al-Shushtari]], a Shi'i scholar Akbar had made qadi of Lahore and who had hidden his faith by taqiyya, began to give judgments that raised doubts among the Sunni majority once Nur Jahan, herself a Shi'i according to the article, rose to power; he was accused of professing the Shi'i faith while acting as a Sunni qadi and was executed by the emperor's order {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 405}. It also says Jahangir called Shaykh [[person/ahmad-sirhindi|Ahmad Sirhindi]] an impostor and had him imprisoned in the fort of Gwalior, then changed his opinion after about a year {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 405}.

## Khurram's revolt and the last crisis
In 1032 AH, 1623 CE, Prince Khurram, later Shah Jahan, rebelled, driven by the machinations of Nur Jahan, who wanted her son-in-law Shahryar to succeed. The revolt amounted to a civil war that weakened imperial prestige and drained the treasury, and Khurram surrendered to Mahabat Khan in Jumada II 1035 AH, March 1626 CE {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 406}. In the same year Mahabat Khan seized Jahangir to remove him from the influence of Nur Jahan and her brother Asaf Khan, and at first succeeded {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 406}.

## His death
He died on 27 Safar 1037 AH, 28 October 1627 CE, on the way from Rajawri to Bhimbar, in the 58th solar year of his age and the 22nd of his reign. His body was taken to Lahore and buried at a spot Nur Jahan chose, over which she built a magnificent mausoleum at her own expense {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 406}.

## The article's portrait
The article calls him a cultured man, a patron of literature and art and a keen observer, a generous ruler who hated oppression; it says he ordered a gold chain hung with bells from the palace at Agra so that anyone wronged could shake it for justice {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 406}. It says his memoirs, the Tuzuk-i Jahangiri, describe the scenery, plants and animals of Kashmir and compares them with Babur's memoirs, with more frankness, while he admits to his drinking and sometimes to cruelty {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 406}. The article judges that his reign was generally one of peace and prosperity, with industry, commerce, architecture, painting and literature flourishing, marred by a few wars in Mewar and the Deccan and minor disturbances in Bengal {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 406}.
