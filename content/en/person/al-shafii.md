## Overview
Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i al-Muttalibi, imam of the Shafi'i school, who died in the days of al-Ma'mun. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.518}

## Name, birth and lineage
The article in the Encyclopaedia of Islam gives his full name as Muhammad ibn Idris ibn al-Abbas ibn Uthman ibn Shafi, of the Quraysh, and calls him the eponym of the Shafi'i school rather than its founder. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 190} All his biographers agree on a birth date of 150/767, the year Abu Hanifa died, and one tradition, often disputed, says the two events fell on the same day. The oldest source places his birth either at [[place/askalan|Ashkelon]] or in [[place/yemen|Yemen]], while most biographers prefer [[place/gaza|Gaza]]; Mina near Mecca is also mentioned less often. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 190}

His genealogy was reckoned among the most prestigious: a man of Quraysh, he was a Muttalibi on his father's side, a distant relative of the Prophet through al-Muttalib, brother of [[person/hashim-ibn-abd-manaf|Hashim]]. His mother is said by different sources to have belonged to the Yemeni tribe of Azd or to have descended from [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]]. The second view was disputed by [[person/fakhr-al-din-al-razi|Fakhr al-Din al-Razi]] and kept by al-Subki, and the article suggests it may partly explain his attitude at the time of his crisis. Later writers cite this lineage with hadiths about the Imams being from Quraysh, in order to stress the standing of the school, and he is often treated as the renewer of religion for the second century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 190}

## Childhood in Mecca
Orphaned of his father at the age of two, or ten by the source that places his birth in Yemen, he was taken by his mother, apparently without means, to [[place/mecca|Mecca]], where the family had relatives. He lived modestly and took to poetry and archery. His eloquence and command of Arabic are said to come from long stays with [[group/hudhayl|Hudhayl]], and are said to have been praised by [[person/al-jahiz|al-Jahiz]]; a collection of poems is attributed to him, and a passage on archery appears in the Umm. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 190} A biographers' anecdote tells how a spectator, Amr ibn Sawwad, told him he was a better scholar than archer, which apparently persuaded him to give himself wholly to learning. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 190} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 191}

## Teachers in Mecca and Medina
His chief masters in Mecca were [[person/muslim-ibn-khalid-al-zanji|Muslim ibn Khalid al-Zanji]], the city's mufti, and [[person/sufyan-ibn-uyayna|Sufyan ibn Uyayna]], who later also taught Ibn Hanbal. At fifteen or eighteen he is said to have received permission to issue fatwas. He then turned to [[person/malik-ibn-anas|Malik ibn Anas]] of [[place/medina|Medina]]. According to Ibn Abi Hatim, he had memorised Malik's [[work/muwatta-malik|Muwatta]] in Mecca and presented himself to Malik about 170/786, asking persistently to recite it; Malik agreed and was pleased with his eloquence. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 191} He remained with Malik until Malik's death, about ten years by al-Razi's account, and always counted him his supreme master. Yet he later allowed himself a critical book, Ikhtilaf Malik wa-l-Shafi'i, which in its surviving form is the work of his Egyptian pupil [[person/al-rabi-ibn-sulayman-al-muradi|al-Rabi al-Muradi]]; the [[group/maliki-madhhab|Maliki school]], especially in Egypt, did not forgive him for it and produced polemic directed at him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 191} In Medina he also studied under Ibrahim ibn Abi Yahya, whom the heresiographers call a follower of the [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazila]]; al-Razi says this teacher taught him only law and hadith, not theology. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 191}

## The crisis and the years in Iraq
The article warns that the biographers contradict one another, so that the order of his later life cannot be fixed precisely and the dates given are hypothetical, except that of his death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 191} It holds as certain that he was called to an official task at [[place/najran|Najran]] in northern Yemen and compromised himself there by joining partisans of the Hasanid Yahya ibn Abd Allah. [[person/ibn-al-nadim|Ibn al-Nadim]] calls him a fervent Shi'i, which the article says can only be understood politically. This episode, which the biographers call his trial or crisis, ended with an appearance before the caliph [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]] at [[place/raqqa|Raqqa]]. Through the intervention of [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-hasan-al-shaybani|Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Shaybani]] he was pardoned, perhaps after a spell in prison, while nine co-defendants were executed. The hagiographic version credits the strength of his own argument. He later held no official post and refused the caliph's offer of the judgeship of Yemen. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 191}

He spent a first period in Iraq, perhaps two years, before or just after this crisis, and became acquainted with the legal school that had grown there from Abu Hanifa through his disciples Abu Yusuf and al-Shaybani. He attended al-Shaybani's circle, held disputations with him and later wrote a refutation of him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 191}

## Mecca, Baghdad and Egypt
He then returned to Mecca for some nine years, passing gradually from pupil to master. About 195/811 he was in [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] for roughly two years, where he composed the first, lost version of the Risala and texts that the Shafi'is call his old doctrine. He was again in Baghdad in 198/813 for a few months, and probably met [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ibn Hanbal]] around then; the article doubts that the two were closely acquainted, despite many anecdotes linking them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 191} The reasons for his settling at [[place/al-fustat|Fustat]] in Egypt are little known. He was probably invited by the governor, but the article thinks the isolation imposed on him by Maliki dominance in the Hijaz and Hanafi dominance in Baghdad also pushed him to found a school elsewhere. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 191}

In Fustat the leading Maliki family, the Banu Abd al-Hakam, first welcomed him, and [[person/muhammad-ibn-abd-allah-ibn-abd-al-hakam|Muhammad ibn Abd Allah ibn Abd al-Hakam]] was among his keenest pupils before returning to the Malikis and writing against him. Egyptian Malikis soon criticised him and tried unsuccessfully to have him banished. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 191} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 192} He taught in the mosque of Amr, his main pupils were Egyptians, and there he wrote the surviving version of the Risala and most of the texts collected in the Umm. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 192}

## Last days, tomb and family
He died aged fifty-four on the last day of Rajab 204/20 January 820. The circumstances remain uncertain: some say a violent assault by a fanatical Maliki, others illness. He was buried in the tomb of the Banu Abd al-Hakam at the foot of the Muqattam hills. The complex around [[building/mausoleum-of-imam-al-shafii|his mausoleum]], often altered and restored, was built under the Ayyubids. The tomb is an object of veneration, and his mawlid is among the most important dates in Cairo. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 192} He married twice and had four children, two sons, one of whom, Abu Uthman, became judge of Aleppo, and two daughters. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 192}

## His theological views
The article says much ink has been spent on his theology, but that later interest in the question was in inverse proportion to his own; his few mentions of the people of kalam concern legal matters. It urges caution toward the creeds attributed to him, some of which suggest a forerunner of [[concept/ashariyya|Ash'arism]] and others of Hanbalism, and calls one of them clearly apocryphal and another doubtful. It also treats as largely retrospective the accounts, by Ash'ari Shafi'is such as al-Razi and al-Subki, that he favoured kalam and those by traditionalist Shafi'is that he opposed it. One recent reading, which the article finds open to objection, presents him as a traditionalist aiming to oppose rationalist theology. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 192} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 193}

## Legal theory and the Risala
According to the account the article gives, he composed the [[work/al-risala-shafii|Risala]] at the request of the Basran traditionist [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-mahdi|Abd al-Rahman ibn Mahdi]], and so instituted the science of usul al-fiqh; the article regards denials of this credit as polemical. The surviving text, in two manuscripts, was probably written in Egypt and reflects his final thinking; an earlier, substantially different version was composed in Iraq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 192} Modern scholarship following Goldziher and Schacht usually sees his contribution as a synthesis between the approach of Malik and that of Abu Hanifa as carried on by al-Shaybani, closer in spirit to the former and placed under traditionalism. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 193}

The core idea is that every act of a person bound by the law has a corresponding ruling, given either in the scriptural sources, the Qur'an and the [[concept/sunna|Sunna]], which he calls the foundation, or inferred from them by [[concept/qiyas|analogical reasoning]]. His originality, the article says, lay in defining, critically establishing and ranking these sources. He identified the Sunna strictly with the sayings, acts and tacit approval of the Prophet in firmly established reports, an argument aimed chiefly at Malik's followers, who tended to equate the practice of Medina with that of the Prophet. His critique of [[concept/taqlid|conformism]] belongs with this. He distinguished two kinds of analogy, by cause and the weaker analogy by resemblance, both requiring a legal proof. Against Abu Hanifa's partisans, who were reputed to rely on opinion and [[concept/istihsan|istihsan]], he argued that such reasoning brings arbitrariness into understanding the law; al-Ghazali attributes to him the maxim that whoever applies istihsan has legislated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 193} The article states that he held his distance from both camps with the message to return to the proof, and that in this sense he was anything but a traditionalist, since he changed the notion that the community remained in immediate contact with revelation. It adds that the Risala long remained a dead letter and the science of usul developed fully only after the fourth/tenth century, but that it is not certain the work's traditional importance is exaggerated, a thesis the article notes was put by W. B. Hallaq. Two further works on legal theory, Ibtal al-istihsan and Jima al-ilm, survive. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 193}

## Works of fiqh and hadith
The article notes that no monograph exists on his practical law and that biographers are confused about his fiqh works. It again separates two periods. The first, in the Hijaz and Iraq, produced the lost Hujja, best transmitted by the Baghdadi pupil al-Hasan al-Zafarani. The new doctrine was elaborated in Egypt and is recorded in the monumental [[work/kitab-al-umm|Umm]], whose edition also holds other texts, some from the Iraqi period according to Schacht. The Mabsut mentioned by al-Bayhaqi is probably the same book as the Umm. The surviving Ahkam al-Qur'an is a compilation by [[person/al-bayhaqi-abu-bakr-ahmad-ibn-al-husayn|al-Bayhaqi]] from his texts, not the lost original. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 193}

As a traditionist he wrote a Musnad and the Ikhtilaf al-hadith. He was criticised by Malikis and later by followers of Ibn Hanbal: for being an unreliable transmitter, since neither [[person/al-bukhari|al-Bukhari]] nor [[person/muslim-ibn-al-hajjaj|Muslim]] accepted reports transmitted by him, for resting some points on doubtful traditions, and for trusting unacceptable transmitters such as Ibrahim ibn Abi Yahya. Al-Bayhaqi wrote a book to clear him of these charges. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 193} The biographers also mention his wide knowledge of medicine and physiognomy and an interest in astrology that he later gave up. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 193}

## His death
Al-Mas'udi says he died on the night of Friday in Rajab 204 AH, and was buried the next morning at the age of fifty-four. [[person/al-sari-ibn-al-hakam|Al-Sari ibn al-Hakam]], the governor of Egypt, prayed over him. He was buried in the cemetery of the Banu Abd al-Hakam. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.417-418}

## In al-Suyuti
Al-Suyuti relates that al-Mutawakkil regretted not having reached his time, and mentioned a dream he related from the Prophet about Ibn Idris. Al-Suyuti considers that al-Mutawakkil was a follower of the Shafi'i school, and that he was the first of the caliphs to adopt it. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.545-546}

## For Sprouts
Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i was a great scholar who lived more than twelve hundred years ago; he gathered learning in Mecca, Medina, Iraq and Egypt and wrote the foundations of fiqh. Many Muslims follow his school today, and among his books are al-Risala and al-Umm.

## For Explorers
Abu Abdullah Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i (150–204 AH), the imam of the Shafi'i school, was born in Gaza and raised in Mecca, memorizing the Qur'an and the Muwatta young. He travelled for hadith to Mecca, Medina, Iraq and Yemen, was among Malik's foremost students, then settled in Baghdad and then Egypt where he completed his school and wrote most of his works. He authored al-Risala in legal theory — the first known book on the discipline — and al-Umm in fiqh, and many imams followed his school. He died in Egypt where his tomb is.

## For Scholars
Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i al-Qurashi al-Muttalibi (150–204 AH) is the imam of the Shafi'i school and the first to compose a separate book on legal theory. Born in Gaza, he was brought to Mecca as a child, memorized the Qur'an and the Muwatta, and travelled for hadith between the Hijaz, Iraq and Yemen; he kept close to Malik in Medina until his death, and argued with the Hanbalis, Muhammad ibn al-Hasan and the Iraqis, taking and replying. He settled in Baghdad then Egypt, where his school took its final form and he dictated the 'new' versions of his books. His works include al-Risala — the first known written treatise on usul al-fiqh — al-Umm, the Mukhtasar of al-Muzani and the Sunan, and fine verses on asceticism are told of him. He laid a method joining the people of hadith and opinion, building on Book, Sunna, consensus and analogy; among his students are al-Rabi' al-Muzani and al-Buwayti. He died in Fustat in 204 AH and is buried there, and his school spread through Egypt, Syria, Yemen and Southeast Asia.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lectures: Knowledge and fairness

He describes Imam al-Shafi'i as having filled the earth with knowledge, as the founder of the principles of jurisprudence, as from the Quraysh, and as a student of Malik, who was himself a teacher of Ahmad ibn Hanbal, as the Sheikh puts it (needs_source). {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-naba-al-safi/ep18 00:02:30}

He mentions illnesses that families conceal, and recounts the story of Imam al-Shafi'i who disclosed a defect in goods he sold, and he gives a figure for the sale price (needs_source: the figure is not clear in the transcript). {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-naba-al-safi/ep17 00:25:30}

He asks listeners to write down "the eight" that he mentions, and he praises the wisdom of al-Shafi'i's sayings (needs_source: what "the eight" are is not clear in the transcript). {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-naba-al-safi/ep18 00:05:30}

## From Omar Abdelkafy's programme Wa Nafsin wa ma Sawwaha
The Sheikh relates that al-Shafi'i, past seventy, still had white, strong teeth, and when asked said they were God's trusts that he guarded in youth, so God guarded them for him in old age; the Sheikh explains that he guarded them from eating the unlawful and from attacking people's honour and slandering them (needs_source: the story is the Sheikh's narration). {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy wa-nafsun-ma-sawaha/ep08 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy wa-nafsun-ma-sawaha/ep08 00:08:00} See: [[concept/iftira|slandering people]].
