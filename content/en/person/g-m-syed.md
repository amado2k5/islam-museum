G. M. Syed — Ghulam Murtaza Syed (17 January 1904 – 25 April 1995), known to his followers as "Saeen", was the founder of modern Sindhi nationalism: a politician who began inside the Pakistan movement, broke with it, and spent his last decades demanding a separate Sindhi homeland, Sindhudesh, from prison cells and house arrest. He was born at Sann, in what was then Karachi District of the Bombay Presidency, into a family of Sayyids; his father, Syed Mohammed Shah Kazmi, was killed in a family feud on 1 November 1905, and the boy was raised as a ward of the Court of Wards. After five years of primary schooling in Sindhi he was educated at home, in Persian and English. {cite:wikipedia-g-m-syed-en} {cite:wikipedia-g-m-syed-ar} {cite:wikidata-g-m-syed}

His public life began at sixteen, organising the Khilafat Conference at Sann on 17 March 1920; the next year he met Gandhi at Sann railway station. He founded a school at Sann — the educationist Ibrahim Joyo was among its pupils — chaired the district school board from 1919, became president of the Karachi District Local Board in 1929, and in 1930 turned to the peasantry with the Sindh Hari Conference. Elected to the Sindh Legislative Assembly in 1937, he joined the Muslim League in 1938, served as Sindh's education minister from March 1940 to March 1941, sat on the All-India Muslim League's central committee from 1941 and became president of the Sindh Muslim League in 1943. The break came early: he resigned his assembly seat and his League membership in 1946 and founded the Progressive Muslim League in opposition. {cite:wikipedia-g-m-syed-en} {cite:wikipedia-g-m-syed-ar} {cite:wikipedia-g-m-syed-ur}

In Pakistan he became the state's most persistent internal dissenter. He opposed the One Unit merger of the provinces, chaired the Sindhi Adabi Board from 1954, and founded in turn the Bazm-e-Soofia-e Sindh (1966), the Sindh United Front (1969) and the Jeay Sindh Mahaz (1972), whose programme was an independent Sindhudesh. The price was some thirty years of prison and house arrest; Amnesty International named him a prisoner of conscience in 1995, his house in Karachi having been declared a sub-jail in January 1992, and he died there on 25 April 1995 — a body passage in the English article, following Suranjan Das, and the Arabic article's body give 26 April instead — aged ninety-one, the oldest political prisoner in the country. Sindh mourned him as it had followed him: thousands attended his burial at Sann. {cite:wikipedia-g-m-syed-en} {cite:wikipedia-g-m-syed-ar} {cite:wikipedia-g-m-syed-ur}

His nationalism was a reader's and writer's nationalism. Across more than sixty books — *Janab Guzarium Jin Sein*, his lives of Sindhi heroes; *Sindh Ja Soorma*; *Religion and Reality*; *Shah Latif's Message*; *A Nation in Chains* — he fused Sindhi identity with the poetry of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai and with his own reading of religion. The English article describes his politics as a blend of Sindhi nationalism, Communism and Sufism, and quotes Oskar Verkaaik's verdict that Syed, drawing on classical Sufism, theosophy, Marxism and German idealism, was "creating his own personal interpretation of Islam". His books were banned from time to time, and his movement's demand for Sindhudesh has outlived him in Sindhi politics. {cite:wikipedia-g-m-syed-en} {cite:wikipedia-g-m-syed-ur} {cite:wikidata-g-m-syed}
