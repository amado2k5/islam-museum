## Overview
Abu Ali al-Husayn ibn Abd Allah ibn Sina was a physician and philosopher, author of many works in philosophy and medicine; Golius, in his Latin notes on al-Farghani's book, says he is commonly called "Avicenna" {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 234} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 420}. He was born around 980 CE in a village near [[place/bukhara|Bukhara]], and Tareq al-Suwaidan says his family was of Persian origin {cite:gohlman-1974} {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 234} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:02:00}. Ibn al-Imad relates from Ibn Khallikan that by the age of ten he had mastered the Qur'an and literature, then read logic with [[person/abu-abdallah-al-natili|Abu Abd Allah al-Natili]], then turned to medicine until leading physicians read under him when he was about sixteen {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 234} {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 235}. He treated the Samanid amir Nuh until he recovered and so entered his library, then served Shams al-Dawla as vizier in [[place/hamadan|Hamadan]], then went to [[place/isfahan|Isfahan]], where Ala al-Dawla treated him well {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 235}. He died in Hamadan in June 1037 CE (Ramadan 428 AH) {cite:gutas-2014} {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 234}. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, says that he followed the old encyclopaedic ideal of joining philosophy with the study of nature, and that he saw human perfection in both knowledge and action; it calls him as illustrious a physician as he was a philosopher {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 973}.

Among his books Ibn al-Imad lists [[work/kitab-al-shifa|al-Shifa']], al-Najat, [[work/al-isharat-wal-tanbihat|al-Isharat]] and [[work/al-qanun-fi-al-tibb|al-Qanun]], out of nearly a hundred works {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 236}. The Qanun is an encyclopaedia of medicine and the Shifa' a broad philosophical compendium, and he wrote mostly in Arabic and also in Persian {cite:gutas-2014}. Al-Suwaidan says the Qanun became one of the basic references of Europe's universities and was taught in several of them until the end of the seventeenth century {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:17:00}. Sunni scholars judged him differently: [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]] held that three of his teachings led to unbelief, while his medical works were widely used, and Ibn al-Imad reports that Ibn Khallikan praised him {cite:gutas-2014} {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 237}.

## Life
Ibn Sina was born in the village of Afshana near [[place/bukhara|Bukhara]] around 980 CE, and he grew up in a city ruled by the Samanid dynasty. In his autobiography, which his student al-Juzjani completed, he describes a boy who mastered the Qur'an and the first sciences at an early age, and then turned to logic, mathematics and medicine {cite:gohlman-1974}.

He left Bukhara after about 1002 CE and worked as a physician, and at times as a vizier, at several courts in Iran. In his last years he served [[person/ala-al-dawla-ibn-kakuya|Ala al-Dawla]] in [[place/isfahan|Isfahan]]. He died in [[place/hamadan|Hamadan]] in June 1037 CE (Ramadan 428 AH) {cite:gutas-2014}.

## Life as the Encyclopaedia of Islam tells it
The article says that his life is well documented. His autobiography covers his first thirty years, and the rest is recorded by his disciple al-Juzjani, who was also his secretary and companion. He was born in 370/980 at Afshana, near Bukhara, and Persian was his first language {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 973}. His father was an official of the [[polity/samanid-dynasty|Samanid]] administration and had him carefully educated at Bukhara. The article reports that his father and brother were drawn to Ismaili propaganda; Ibn Sina knew its tenets but refused to adopt them {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 973}. The article credits his intellectual independence to an exceptional intelligence and memory, which let him overtake his teachers at fourteen.

According to the account, he explained logic to his own master [[person/abu-abdallah-al-natili|al-Natili]]. He had no teacher in the natural sciences or in medicine, and by sixteen famous physicians were already working under his direction. He found Aristotle's Metaphysics hard and understood it only with the help of a commentary by [[person/al-farabi|al-Farabi]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 973}. After he cured the amir of Khurasan of a severe illness, he was allowed to use the rich library of the Samanid princes. By eighteen he had mastered all the sciences then known, and the article says his later progress rested on his own judgement. He wrote his first philosophical book at twenty-one {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 973}.

The death of his father the following year obliged him to enter the administration to earn a living. The princes who consulted him on medicine turned to him on politics too, and he served as a minister several times. The article adds that this made him an object of envy: he was sometimes persecuted by his enemies and sometimes sought by princes opposed to those he wished to stay loyal to. He fled and hid on several occasions, earning his living by medical consultations, and he was imprisoned and escaped {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 973}. He then lived for fourteen years in relative peace at the court in Isfahan, and died at Hamadan in 428/1037, during an expedition of the prince Ala al-Dawla. He was buried there, and a monument was later raised to him on the thousandth anniversary of his birth by the Hijri count {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 973}.

## How he worked
The article asks readers not to picture a philosopher who lived among his books. He spent his days on affairs of state and wrote his large works at night, with astonishing speed. He was often forced to move, wrote on horseback and sometimes in prison, and had only his memory to consult {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 973}.

The article explains why his writings sometimes differ from Aristotle: he quoted him without rereading him, and his independence of mind led him to set out his own thought rather than repeat earlier works. It adds that his training was practical, that of a physician dealing with particular cases, and that Aristotle's logic seemed to him too distant from life. Of the many modern controversies about his personality, the article considers the most plausible view to be that of a scientific man trying to bring Greek theories to the level of concrete reality. It also notes that his development cannot be fully understood while the *Book of Impartial Judgement*, of which only fragments survive, and his *Eastern Philosophy* are missing {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 973}.

## Works
Among his best-known books are [[work/al-qanun-fi-al-tibb|The Canon of Medicine]], an encyclopaedia of medicine, and [[work/kitab-al-shifa|The Healing]], a broad philosophical compendium. He wrote mostly in Arabic and also wrote in Persian {cite:gutas-2014}.

## The corpus as the article surveys it
The article calls the surviving corpus considerable but incomplete. He answered many questions in haste and did not always keep his texts; al-Juzjani saved several, others circulate under different titles, and others are lost. The manuscript of the *Impartial Judgement* vanished in the sack of Isfahan during his own lifetime. The article reports that G. C. Anawati listed 276 works in 1950, counting doubtful and apocryphal ones, and that Mahdavi in 1954 listed 131 authentic and 110 doubtful works {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 973}. Besides philosophy and medicine he wrote on natural history, physics, chemistry, astronomy, mathematics, economics and politics, and produced shorter writings on Qur'anic exegesis and on Sufi doctrine and conduct. He versified summaries of logic and medicine for teaching, and the article also sees in him the gifts of a true poet, who put his philosophy into images, as in his poem on the soul, and into symbolic tales whose meaning is disputed {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 973} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 974}.

## The Canon of Medicine and the medical writings
Medicine has its own work, while natural history and mathematics were treated as parts of philosophy and sit inside the *Healing*. The article judges the [[work/al-qanun-fi-al-tibb|Canon]] to be a more consciously coherent whole than the philosophical works: an ordered summary of the medical knowledge of his day, enriched by his own observations, which stayed authoritative until experimental science began and was easier to use than Hippocrates or Galen. On this basis it served as the foundation of medical teaching and practice for seven centuries. The article adds that a Tehran clinician, Dr Abd Allah Ahmadieh, was said to use Avicenna's therapeutics with good results, particularly against rheumatism {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 974}.

The Canon has five books. The first covers the human body, sickness, health and general treatment. The second is the materia medica and pharmacology of herbs, and contains a page on experimentation in medicine; the article says this passage sets out three methods, agreement, difference and concomitant variations, which are usually regarded as characteristic of modern science. The third treats special pathology by organs or systems. The fourth opens with the famous treatise on fevers and goes on to signs, symptoms, diagnosis and prognosis, minor surgery, tumours, wounds, fractures and bites, and poisons. The fifth is the pharmacopoeia {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 974}.

Several shorter treatises took single topics from the Canon, and their size gave them wide circulation: a pharmacopoeia, advice on preserving health, a study of diarrhoea, and monographs on remedies such as oxymel and balsam, on bleeding, and on the virtues of wine. The article also names his medical poem, the *Urjuza fi al-tibb*, a verse memory aid covering principles, observations, therapeutic and dietary advice and simple surgery, which was translated into Latin several times between the thirteenth and seventeenth centuries as the *Cantica Avicennae* {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 974}.

## Philosophical works
The article reports that his philosophical works survive in damaged form. The *Healing* is complete, and the *Najat*, a selection made by the author himself from it, is not an independent composition, as was thought until 1937. The [[work/al-isharat-wa-al-tanbihat|Book of Directives and Remarks]] is complete, as is the *Danishnama-i Ala'i*, a summary of his doctrine written at the request of Ala al-Dawla. Only fragments remain of the *Book of Impartial Judgement between the Easterners and the Westerners*, and a small part of the *Logic of the Easterners*, the logic of his *Eastern Philosophy*; the rest is lost. Many minor writings survive but do not fill these gaps {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 974}.

## Logic and metaphysics
The article says that his mind was too searching to stay within the separate sciences. He looked for their principle and guarantee, and so placed above them the science of being, metaphysics, and logic, which the philosophers called the instrumental science {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 974}. As far as can be judged without his lost works, the article sees him as an innovator in logic, who corrected Aristotle's excess of abstraction by attending to change and to the difference between a strict meaning and one shaped by the conditions in which a thing is realised. As a physician he admitted a sign as the middle term of a syllogism and gave it the force of proof, as a symptom has in diagnosis {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 974}.

In metaphysics the article finds his doctrine most individual. It names three formative teachers: Aristotle, al-Farabi, and Plotinus, who reached him under the name of Aristotle through the so-called *Theology of Aristotle*. The article says that this error of attribution runs through his whole work. It calls him a philosopher of being and also of essence, since his metaphysics is organised around the origin of being and its passage to individually realised essence {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 974} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 975}.

The article states that Ibn Sina was a believer who accepted God as Creator and tried to bring doctrine and philosophy together, and that it judges him not very successful in this, though he kept working at it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 975}. In its account, the first certainty of the human mind is being. The second is that being in any existing thing is not necessary to its essence, so existence is given by a Being different from all of them, whose essence is its very being. The proof of God's existence therefore belongs to metaphysics rather than physics. The article notes a Western debate over whether he held an analogy of being, and says that Thomas Aquinas drew on his teaching on this point {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 975}.

The article then says that his scheme of creation follows Plotinus: the necessary Being produces a first Intelligence, from which come another Intelligence, a celestial soul and a celestial body, and so down the spheres of Ptolemy's system to a tenth Intelligence, the Active Intellect, which governs the terrestrial world of corruptible matter. It points out that the Qur'an and the earlier scriptures explain creation by God's free act of will, whereas in this scheme emanation is not free. In its reading, he makes the Active Intellect a separate Intelligence which gives the human soul its intelligibles and the substantial forms of things, and the article judges that this removes from the soul the active function Aristotle gave it. The human soul, on this account, reaches sensation, imagination and estimation by itself and receives intelligibles from outside, intuition arising when it joins the Active Intellect. Beings are also said to ascend by love and desire for their source; the article reads his *Epistle on Love* as mainly a metaphysical explanation of every being's tendency toward its good {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 975} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 976}.

## The soul, resurrection and mysticism
The article says that Ibn Sina held firmly to the immortality of the soul, which is immaterial and so cannot be corrupted. On the resurrection of the body he was much more hesitant, according to the article: it reports that he accepted it at first in the *Healing* and the *Najat*, denied it in the *Adhawiyya* epistle, and appears finally to have understood it symbolically {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 976}. Other views on this question, among them the judgement of later Sunni scholars, are set out in the perspectives block of this record.

Whether he should be counted a mystic has caused fierce debate. The article reads the stages of the Sufis in the *Directives and Remarks* as observations by a respectful, curious mind that does not take part, and it insists that Ibn Sina was a believer, against those who portray him as a pleasure-lover or a near hypocrite or reduce him to occult gnosis. It doubts that he was a mystic in the strict sense of one whose whole life is a love of God. Yet it suggests he may have glimpsed true mystic apprehension in what he calls the secret of the heart, and notes a doubt, since his terms there could equally refer to a link with the Active Intellect rather than with God. The article says the loss of the *Eastern Philosophy* prevents a firm conclusion {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 976}.

## Reception in medieval and Renaissance Europe
The article connects his fame in Europe to the translation of Arab learning into Latin. The *Healing* and the *Canon* made him an undisputed master of medicine, sciences and philosophy. From the twelfth to the sixteenth century, it says, the teaching and practice of medicine rested on him. The article credits [[person/al-razi-abu-bakr|al-Razi]] with a better reputation as a clinician, but the Canon gave an irreplaceable teaching corpus, since the *Kulliyyat* of [[person/ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd]] matched only its first part {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 976}.

Gerard of Cremona translated the Canon in full between 1150 and 1187, and in all eighty-seven translations were made, some partial, mostly into Latin and some into Hebrew. The article judges the medical translations weaker than the philosophical ones, since some Greek-derived terms were misunderstood and some Arabic terms were merely transcribed. The Canon appears in the syllabus of the medical school at Montpellier from a bull of 1309 until 1557, after which Galen was preferred, but Ibn Sina was taught until the seventeenth century, and the Arabic text was printed at Rome in 1593. The article adds that Chaucer's Prologue to the Canterbury Tales names him among authorities no doctor should ignore {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 976}.

Among Arab physicians from the seventh/thirteenth to the tenth/sixteenth century, the article says, all depend on him even when they question him; it names [[person/ibn-al-nafis|Ibn al-Nafis]], who augmented and corrected him and recorded his discovery of the pulmonary circulation in his commentary on the Canon. In the West several physicians learned Arabic for his sake. The article cites Henrik Harpestraeng, who died in 1244, as the first known case, and lists Arnold of Villeneuve and the surgeons William of Saliceto, Lanfranc and Guy of Chauliac as users of his work. Anatomy at Bologna was still taught in Arabic terms in the fourteenth century {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 976} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 977}.

The Renaissance brought a sharp reaction in the article's telling: Leonardo da Vinci rejected his anatomy while still using its Arabic terms, Paracelsus burned the Canon at Basle, and William Harvey's account of the circulation in 1628 dealt it a severe blow. In natural science, the article says, medieval encyclopaedists used the *Healing* widely, Michael Scot translated his treatise on animals, and Albertus Magnus used his mineralogy. It describes him as an Aristotelian in physics, who nevertheless opposed the transmutation of metals and so alchemy {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 977}.

In philosophy, the article judges his influence to have been less absolute and more disputed, but longer lasting, because Thomas Aquinas built some of his proofs into Catholic theology. When the *Healing* was translated, Aristotle was known in the Latin West only through a few logical works, and Ibn Sina was admired for a century and set among the Neoplatonist and Augustinian traditions. The article says the decrees of 1210 and 1215 against Aristotle and his followers banned him from the Sorbonne, though his role in private discussion continued {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 977}.

## Legacy
Later Muslim thinkers engaged with him in two directions. [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|Al-Ghazali]] criticised some of his views, while many others built on his system. Latin translations carried his medicine and philosophy into medieval Europe.

A wise physician and scholar who wrote a medical encyclopedia that helped doctors care for patients and protect their health.

## For Explorers
Ibn Sina was a physician and polymath born near Bukhara. He authored The Canon of Medicine, which served as the primary medical textbook in universities across the world for centuries, describing illnesses and treatments, as well as The Book of Healing covering natural sciences, astronomy, and philosophy.

## For Scholars
Abu Ali al-Husayn ibn Abd Allah ibn Sina (c. 370–428 AH / 980–1037 CE), the polymath philosopher and physician known to the West as Avicenna. Author of an immense corpus across diverse disciplines, his medical masterwork 'The Canon of Medicine' synthesized classical medicine with systematic clinical observation, anatomy, and pharmacology, remaining a standard university textbook in both East and West for centuries. In philosophy, his encyclopedic 'Kitab al-Shifa' (The Book of Healing) articulated a comprehensive framework uniting logic, natural philosophy, and metaphysics.

## In the 'Al-Mubdi'un' programme
Tareq al-Suwaidan presents Ibn Sina — 'al-Shaykh al-Ra'is' — as the model of concentration on one field, philosophy his means and medicine his end {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:06:30}.
Of his upbringing: born Safar 370 AH to a Persian family, Qur'an and grammar by ten; raised among an Isma'ili father and brother, he rejected the doctrine — 'I hear them and know what they say and my soul does not accept it' (correcting the charge of Isma'ilism) {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:02:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:03:00}.
He began medicine at sixteen saying 'medicine is not among the hard sciences, so no wonder I excelled in the shortest time'; he read philosophy under al-Natili then left his shallowness to read alone — Aristotle's Metaphysics defeated him through forty readings until al-Farabi's book on its aims unlocked it {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:06:30}.
Of his discipline: 'I never slept a whole night nor spent a day but in learning'; when perplexed he would repair to the mosque praying 'until the closed opened to me and the hard eased'; and when sleep overcame him he turned to a drink and back to reading {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:08:30}.
Of the princes' patronage: he cured Mansur ibn Nuh al-Samani where every physician had failed, who opened his library of treasures to him, and Nuh ibn Mansur continued the care — 'inner and material stability turn into output' (the speaker's lesson) {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:10:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:10:30}.
Imprisoned in the political turmoil, he emerged into hiding at Ibn Ghalib the perfumer's, where in that enforced retreat he composed most of his works in the natural and divine sciences {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:11:30}.
His contest with Abu Mansur al-Jubba'i: worsted in language, he bent three years over it until he composed odes in the rarest words, presented them to the caliph aged and weathered, and al-Jubba'i could not explain them {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:14:30}.
Of his medical firsts: first describer of meningitis, anthrax and stroke, discoverer of the roundworm eight hundred years before the Italian physician, cardiac stimulants and pre-surgical anaesthetics, psychotherapy and the diseases of passionate love {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:16:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:16:30}.
Of his physics: light outruns sound, hearing relates to air's undulation, and an instrument measuring minute lengths precisely {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:16:45}.
Of his educational theory in Kitab al-Siyasa: upbringing from birth to marriage, the first seven years building character, individual differences, moral reward and punishment without vengeance {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:20:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:20:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:21:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:21:30}.
Of his works: over a hundred books; the Qanun a base reference of European universities to the seventeenth century; al-Shifa' in eighteen volumes with a complete original at Oxford; al-Insaf in astronomical observations; and al-Mukhtasar al-Awsat in logic {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:23:00}.
Of his death: illness recurring weekly until he said 'the manager who has failed to manage my body profits nothing from treatment — I who taught you medicine'; he abandoned treatment and died in 428 AH {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:23:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:24:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep27 00:24:30}.

## Drug-assessment conditions: addition from the inspected text
The [[invention/ibn-sina-seven-drug-assessment-conditions|seven-conditions record]] presents a specific Book 2 contribution: on pages 318–319 Ibn Sina distinguishes learning drug powers through experience from learning them by analogy, then orders conditions for interpreting observations {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319}. They concern the substance's condition, disease complexity, contrary conditions, relative strength, timing, usual consistency and different bodies; this is neither a patient list nor evidence of implementing a modern trial {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319}. Context retains humoral theory and the difference between intrinsic and incidental benefit instead of reducing the passage to the title “inventor of the clinical trial” {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 pp.79–80}.

The inspected 1999 Arabic page images were compared with Tibi's translation in the modern study, which uses a Rome 1593 witness; the two editions and the translation's bracketed dosage gloss remain distinct despite similar ideas {cite:canon-ibn-sina-print-v1 pp.318–319} {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 pp.79–80}. This enrichment adds to the older biography above, not newly verifying every priority claim or lecture anecdote it contains, or revising its date variants {cite:nasser-tibi-savage-smith-canon-rules-2009 pp.78–80}.
