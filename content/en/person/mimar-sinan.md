## Overview
Sinan was the chief court architect of the [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottoman empire]] from 945/1538 until his death in 996/1588. The Encyclopaedia of Islam gives his birth as 895/1490. The names of several other Ottoman court architects are known, but none matches his fame: a long life, joined to the resources of the empire at its height, let him produce a body of work that the article's author, B. O'Kane, judges unmatched in quantity and quality in Islamic architecture as a whole. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 644}

## From the levy to the court
Sinan was of Christian Greek origin and was taken in the [[concept/devshirme|devshirme]] levy in the reign of [[person/selim-i-uthmani|Selim I]]. He first went on campaign at [[place/belgrade|Belgrade]] in 1521 under [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Suleyman]], and before his appointment as court architect served in places as far apart as [[place/vienna|Vienna]] in 1529 and [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] in 1535. O'Kane supposes that he learned his profession by repairing and raising military works such as bridges and citadels, and in the early 1530s by building mosques in [[place/istanbul|Istanbul]] between campaigns. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 644}

## How many buildings
Three manuscript sources of the late sixteenth century set out his work: the Tadhkirat al-bunyan and Tadhkirat al-abniya of Mustafa Sa'i, and the anonymous but partly autobiographical Tuhfat al-mi'marin. Between them they list some 477 buildings: congregational and neighbourhood mosques, colleges, infant schools, mausoleums, hospitals, aqueducts, bridges, caravanserais, soup kitchens, palaces and baths. O'Kane takes the number itself as proof that Sinan could not have overseen each project. For works far from Istanbul he can hardly have done more than send a plan with instructions; the stonework and ornament of the Suleyman complex at [[place/damascus|Damascus]] of 962/1554-5 belong entirely to the local style. Even some Istanbul buildings on the lists, such as the [[building/kilic-ali-pasha-mosque|Kilic Ali Pasha mosque]] of 988/1580, a small copy of [[building/hagia-sophia|Hagia Sophia]], and the [[building/piyale-pasha-mosque|Piyale Pasha mosque]] of 981/1573-4, have been questioned on grounds of quality. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 644}

## The Shehzade
His three largest complexes, the Shehzade, the Suleymaniye and the Selimiye, each mark a step in his maturity. In all three he kept the form that had been standard for great Ottoman mosques since the [[building/uc-serefeli-mosque|Uc Serefeli mosque]] at [[place/edirne|Edirne]]: a large domed chamber joined to a square or rectangular courtyard. The death of Suleyman's son Mehmed in 1543 brought him his first major commission, the [[building/sehzade-mosque|Shehzade complex]] in Istanbul, built in 950-5/1543-8, with a central dome surrounded by four half-domes. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 644}

O'Kane points to two differences from the earlier [[building/bayezid-ii-mosque|mosque of Bayezid II]] that return in the later works. The first is the windows: Sinan nearly doubled their number by thickening the supporting walls with buttresses at regular intervals and opening the spaces between. The second is the roofline. The square block under the dome, awkwardly plain in the older mosque, is all but hidden here by taller and wider corner turrets and by a stepped profile between turrets and dome, which gives the whole a pyramidal outline. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 644}

## The Suleymaniye
The [[building/sulaymaniyya-mosque|Suleymaniye]] in Istanbul, built in 957-64/1550-7, was the most ambitious single Ottoman complex, with some fourteen buildings of different uses fitted onto the sloping ground around the mosque. A garden sets the mosque apart from them. It stands on a hill above the harbour and still dominates the skyline of the city. Sinan took up the challenge of Hagia Sophia, the largest dome in Istanbul, by repeating its vaulting scheme along the main axis, while at ground level the great interior is kept as open as possible so that worshippers can pray in unbroken rows. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 644}

## The Selimiye
In the [[building/selimiye-mosque|complex built for Selim II]] at Edirne the dome as built is about the same diameter as that of Hagia Sophia; it is lower measured from the ground, but its steeper profile makes it higher measured from the base of the dome. The plan differs sharply from the two earlier mosques. It rests on an octagon, so that eight piers rather than four carry the load, and the curtain walls could be reduced to let light pour in through more windows than in any of his earlier mosques. O'Kane finds one drawback: the mihrab had to be set in a deep recess between two great piers, where few windows reach it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 644} The four minarets at the corners of the domed chamber are the tallest in Ottoman architecture, and their slenderness answers the mass of the dome between them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 645}

## Smaller works
Among the many smaller projects O'Kane singles out four. The [[building/rustem-pasha-mosque|mosque of the grand vizier Rustem Pasha]] in Istanbul is raised on a vaulted substructure so that it stands above its market quarter, and is famous for its lavish covering of Iznik tiles, which he judges superb close up but unsettling to the structure as a whole. The [[building/mihrimah-edirnekapi-mosque|mosque of Mihrimah Sultan]] at Edirnekapi, to which a teacher was first appointed in 976/1568-9, shows from outside a dome on pendentives whose four great arches rise above the roofline and are filled with windows. The [[building/sokollu-mehmed-pasha-mosque|complex of Sokollu Mehmed Pasha]] at Kadirga, of 979/1571-2, is fitted onto an awkward slope below the old hippodrome; inside, a hexagonal base carries the dome over a rectangular hall without columns. The fourth is the aqueduct at Maglova, built in 1553-64, which O'Kane calls strikingly modern in its joining of form and function. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 645}

## What is lost
None of the painted interiors of Sinan's buildings has survived without restoration. O'Kane counts this a major loss, one that might have tempered the view of his buildings as works of structure alone. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 645}
