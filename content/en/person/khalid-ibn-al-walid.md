## Brief
Khalid ibn al-Walid was the most dreaded cavalry commander in the Arabian Peninsula: first for Mecca against the Muslims, then for Islam against two empires. Few figures in this museum fought on as many sides of the story as he did.

## Life
A son of [[person/al-walid-ibn-al-mughira|al-Walid ibn al-Mughira]] of [[group/banu-makhzum|Banu Makhzum]], Khalid led the cavalry of Mecca at [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]]. His cavalry's counter-attack tipped the battle against the Muslims that day in 625 CE {cite:ibn-ishaq-guillaume}. In 629 CE he rode to Medina and embraced Islam. Within months the Prophet gave him command, and at [[event/battle-of-mutah|Mu'ta]] he rescued the army after its three commanders had fallen {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

Under the caliphs he led the army at [[event/battle-of-yamama|al-Yamama]] in the wars of apostasy (Ridda), and at [[event/battle-of-yarmuk|al-Yarmuk]] against the Byzantines {cite:tabari-donner-1993}. The sources record, in the wars of apostasy, the case of Malik ibn Nuwayra, whom his men killed. It is reported that the Prophet himself cleared Khalid of hasty action in that affair {cite:tabari-donner-1993}. Umar removed him from command around 638 CE. He died in 642 CE in Homs or Medina {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

## Career in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, describes Khalid as an Arab commander of the early conquests and sets out his career as Muslim tradition gives it. He fought against the Prophet at Uhud and was converted in 6/627 or 8/629. He took part in the expedition to Mu'ta and in the [[event/conquest-of-mecca|conquest of Mecca]], both in 8 AH. The Prophet charged him with destroying the idol of al-Uzza at Nakhla, which the museum treats under the [[event/demolition-of-al-uzza-and-idols|demolition of al-Uzza and the major idols]], and later sent him to the Banu Jadhima, whom, the article says, he attacked wrongfully. In 9/630 the Prophet sent him from [[place/tabuk|Tabuk]] to [[place/dumat-al-jandal|Dumat al-Jandal]], where he captured the ruler [[person/ukaydir-ibn-abd-al-malik|al-Ukaydir]] and sent him to Medina. In 10/631 he was sent to invite the Banu al-Harith of Yemen to Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 966}

After the Prophet's death in 11/632, [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] sent him against the rebels of the [[event/ridda-wars|ridda]], or apostasy. The article says that tradition ascribes two further misdeeds to him on that occasion: the killing of Muslims through a misunderstanding, and the marriage of a widow. Abu Bakr nevertheless forgave him, and Khalid commanded the Muslims against [[person/musaylima|Musaylima]] in the Yamama. In 12/633, the article continues, he was sent either directly from the Yamama or by way of Medina to [[place/iraq|Iraq]], where he conquered [[place/al-hira|al-Hira]], Dumat al-Jandal and other places before crossing the desert to [[place/syria|Syria]] to assist the armies there. He stayed in Syria, though his troops returned to Iraq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 966}

When Abu Bakr died in 13/634, [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar ibn al-Khattab]] immediately dismissed him from the high command, according to the tradition the article reports. Khalid still fought in northern Syria under [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda]] and conducted a number of campaigns on the Byzantine border before he died in [[place/homs|Homs]], or in Medina, in 21/642. His son [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-khalid|Abd al-Rahman]] was governor of Homs and the Jazira for [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Muawiya]], who is said to have had him killed because he feared his prestige. A grandson, Khalid ibn Abd al-Rahman, who avenged his father, is found as a commander. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 966}

## How the article reads the tradition
The article's author points to two matters of major interest in Khalid's career, and these are the author's own readings, set beside the tradition above. First, the author finds that Muslim tradition is eager to discredit him. The title Sayf Allah, the Sword of God, is reported as earned in battle against the Romans at Mu'ta, with al-Tabari giving the report in one place and a different account in another. The author argues that the title belongs with the designation of the armies of Syria as the armies of God, sent to take revenge on the Romans for their oppression of the Jews, and with the name of Umar as a redeemer. On this reading, later tradition could have reduced Khalid to a sword of the Messenger of God, but instead reduced his contribution, partly by depicting him as self-willed, as in the episodes above, and partly by having Umar dismiss him. The author adds that De Goeje had already pointed out that Umar must on the contrary have appointed him to the high command. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 966}

Second, Muslim tradition has Khalid begin the conquests in Iraq, a point that the author says non-Muslim sources contradict. Sebeos, writing around 661 CE, has the Arabs divide their armies after their first successes in Syria and send one division to Iraq. The Khuzistani chronicle, around 680 CE, knows Khalid only as the conqueror of Syria. The author also notes that al-Tabari has Abu Bakr instruct Khalid to go to Syria. The author reads the shift to Iraq as setting aside the status of Syria as the promised land for Jews and Arabs alike. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 966}

The author then proposes a reconstruction, offered as a judgement and not as the museum's own. Non-Muslim sources of about 634 CE show the Prophet at the head of the first invasions of Syria, which tradition reduced to the expedition to Tabuk. It was from Tabuk that the Prophet sent Khalid to Dumat al-Jandal, a city vital to the control of Syria and one that Khalid is supposed to have conquered a second time on the Iraq campaign, although that would have been an unlikely detour from al-Hira. The author concludes that Khalid went from Dumat al-Jandal to join the armies in Syria, a desert crossing that the author calls more plausible, if less romantic, than the one tradition preserves. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 966}

## Legacy
Military histories still study his campaigns, and Muslim memory still debates whether genius excuses anything. He wished to die in battle and died in his bed, and the reports say he regarded that as an injustice.

## For young visitors
Khalid was the most skilled commander of his age, and he fought on both sides. People still argue about whether he was a hero or went too far.

## For Sprouts
A brave commander titled the Drawn Sword of God by the Prophet, skilled in leadership and devoted to justice.

## For Explorers
Khalid ibn al-Walid was a brilliant military strategist and commander renowned for his tactical genius and courage. Embracing Islam before the opening of Mecca in 7 AH, the Prophet honored him with the title 'The Drawn Sword of God' after the battle of Mut'ah for his skillful leadership, subsequently leading campaigns in Iraq and Syria.

## For Scholars
Abu Sulayman Khalid ibn al-Walid (d. 21 AH / 642 CE), celebrated as the 'Drawn Sword of God' (Sayf Allah al-Maslul), stands among the most brilliant military commanders in history. Accepting Islam in 8 AH prior to the conquest of Mecca alongside Amr ibn al-As, the Prophet bestowed his honorific title following his tactical withdrawal of the Muslim army at Mut'ah. Entrusted with key commands during the Ridda campaigns and early expeditions in Iraq and Syria, his maneuvers at the decisive Battle of Yarmouk (15 AH) demonstrated supreme strategic mastery. Undefeated in battle, he spent his final years in Homs, embodying military discipline upon accepting Caliph Umar's administrative decisions.

He recounts that at Uhud the archers left the gap which Khalid exploited, and that the Prophet was then wounded (needs_source: the account is garbled in the automatic transcript) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep11 00:45:30}. He says Khalid entered a hundred battles in jihad and Islam without being defeated in any of them, and that the figures for Mu'ta need checking (needs_source: the figures have not been checked) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep11 00:46:30}.

He recounts that after the three commanders fell at Mu'ta, Khalid, who had recently become Muslim, was chosen to lead; he said he was a recent convert, and then took up the banner (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep11 00:47:00}. He recounts that Khalid converted at about twenty-one, with Ikrima, and the Prophet welcomed them; the sheikh also says Khalid was in his thirties when he converted, and that the Prophet remarked that Mecca had given him its precious men, among them Khalid and Ikrima (needs_source: the sheikh gives two ages) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep13 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep13 00:10:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep13 00:10:30}.

## In Ahmad ibn Hanbal's Fada'il al-Sahaba
The chapter on Khalid in [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]]'s [[work/fadail-al-sahaba-ahmad|Fada'il al-Sahaba]] opens with Qays ibn Abi Hazim's report that he heard Khalid tell people at al-Hira that on the day of [[event/battle-of-mutah|Mu'ta]] nine swords broke in his hand and only a Yemeni blade held out — a sound chain according to the editor {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1024}. From Qays it also gives Khalid's saying that no night on which a bride he loved was brought to him, or on which he was given news of a son, was dearer to him than a night of hard frost in a detachment of Muhajirun at dawn upon the enemy {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1025}. From Abu al-Safar it narrates that Khalid camped at [[place/al-hira|al-Hira]], was warned that the Persians might give him poison, called for it and drank it saying "In the name of God", and it did him no harm {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1026} {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1028}. It narrates Wahshi ibn Harb's report that [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] gave Khalid command against the people of the Ridda and said he had heard the Prophet say: "What an excellent servant of God and brother of the clan is Khalid ibn al-Walid, a sword of God's swords that God has drawn against the unbelievers and the hypocrites" — graded good by the editor {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1027-1028}. At the start of the book there is also al-Sha'bi's report that Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf complained of Khalid to the Prophet, who told Khalid that even if he spent the like of Uhud in gold he would not reach the deeds of a man of Badr, and then said: "Do not harm Khalid, for he is a sword of God's swords" {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.65-66}.
