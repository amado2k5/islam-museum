# Muhammad Ali Jinnah

## Summary
Muhammad Ali Jinnah (born Mahomedali Jinnahbhai; 25 December 1876 – 11 September 1948) was a barrister, politician and statesman, and the founder of Pakistan. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-ar} He served as the leader of the All-India Muslim League from 1913 until the independence of Pakistan on 14 August 1947, and then as the first Governor-General of Pakistan until his death a year later. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} In Pakistan he is revered as the Quaid-e-Azam ("Great Leader") and Baba-e-Qaum ("Father of the Nation"), and his birthday is observed as a national holiday. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-ur}

## Early life and education
Jinnah was born, likely on 25 December 1876, to Jinnahbhai Poonja and his wife Mithibai, in a rented apartment on the second floor of Wazir Mansion near Karachi, then in the Bombay Presidency of British India. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} His father was a merchant born to a family of textile weavers in the village of Paneli in the princely state of Gondal, in Kathiawar; his parents had moved to Karachi in 1875, before his birth. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-ar} He was the eldest of seven children; his younger sister Fatima Jinnah became his closest associate. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} In Karachi he attended the Sindh Madressatul Islam and the Christian Missionary Society High School, and in Bombay the Cathedral and John Connon School, completing his matriculation from Bombay University. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en}

In 1892 Sir Frederick Leigh Croft, a business associate of his father, offered him a London apprenticeship with Graham's Shipping and Trading Company; before he left, his mother had him marry his cousin Emibai, and both his mother and his first wife died during his absence in England. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} He soon gave up the apprenticeship to study law at Lincoln's Inn, shortened his name to Muhammad Ali Jinnah, and in 1895, at the age of nineteen, became the youngest British Indian to be called to the bar in England. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en}

## The barrister
At twenty Jinnah began his practice in Bombay, the only Muslim barrister in the city, and English became and remained his principal language. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} After lean first years he was invited to work from the chambers of the acting Advocate General, John Molesworth MacPherson, and in 1900 he held a presidency magistrate's post on an interim basis, declining the permanent appointment at 1,500 rupees a month with the remark that he planned to earn that sum in a day — which he eventually did. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} His skilled handling of the 1908 "Caucus Case" for Sir Pherozeshah Mehta made his reputation for advocacy and legal logic, and in 1916 he obtained an acquittal for Bal Gangadhar Tilak on a renewed sedition charge. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} As Governor-General he would later fix his own salary at one rupee a month. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en}

## Politics: from unity to the League
Jinnah rose to prominence in the Indian National Congress in the first two decades of the twentieth century, advocating Hindu–Muslim unity; he helped shape the 1916 Lucknow Pact between Congress and the All-India Muslim League, was a key leader in the Home Rule League, and proposed a fourteen-point constitutional reform plan to safeguard the political rights of Muslims in the subcontinent. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-ur} In 1920 he resigned from Congress when it agreed to follow Gandhi's campaign of satyagraha, which he regarded as political anarchy. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en}

He had joined the All-India Muslim League in 1913 and led it thereafter, working for the rights of Muslims in the subcontinent. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} In 1940 the League under his leadership passed the Lahore Resolution demanding a separate state; during the Second World War the League gained strength while Congress leaders were imprisoned, and in the provincial elections held shortly after the war it won most of the seats reserved for Muslims. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} When no power-sharing formula for a united India proved possible, all parties agreed to the independence of a Hindu-majority India and of a predominantly Muslim state of Pakistan. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en}

## Governor-General and death
As the first Governor-General of Pakistan, Jinnah worked to establish the government and policies of the new nation and to aid the millions of Muslim migrants who had moved to Pakistan after independence, personally supervising the establishment of refugee camps. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} In his first address to the Constituent Assembly of Pakistan, on 11 August 1947, he declared that people of all religions were free to go to their places of worship and that religion was not the business of the state. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} Ill with tuberculosis and advanced lung cancer, he died at Karachi at 10:20 pm on 11 September 1948, aged seventy-one, just over a year after Pakistan's creation. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-ar} He was buried the next day amid official mourning in both India and Pakistan; a million people gathered for his funeral, led by Shabbir Ahmad Usmani, and he rests in the mausoleum of Mazar-e-Quaid in Karachi. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} His biographer Stanley Wolpert judged that Jinnah remains Pakistan's greatest leader. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en}

## The question of his religious affiliation
What follows are the reported positions, as the sources give them; the museum records them and decides nothing between them. Jinnah's family was from a Gujarati Khoja Shi'a Muslim background; the English article's text says that he later followed the Twelver Shi'a teachings, and that after his death his relatives and other witnesses claimed he had converted in later life to the Sunni sect, his religion at the time of his death being disputed in multiple court cases. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} Vali Nasr described him as "an Ismaili by birth and a Twelver Shia by confession, though not a religiously observant man". {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} After his death his sister Fatima asked the court to execute his will under Shia Islamic law, and Fatima and Prime Minister Liaquat Ali Khan jointly filed an affidavit describing him as Shia. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} Against this, Syed Sharifuddin Pirzada testified in court that Jinnah converted to Sunni Islam in 1901, when his sisters married Sunnis; an eminent Bombay High Court lawyer testified that he used to pray as an orthodox Sunni; and Akbar Ahmed concluded that by the end of his life he had become a firm Sunni Muslim. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} The courts themselves never settled it: the Shia affidavit was rejected in 1970, a 1976 ruling rejected the Sunni-conversion claim — effectively implying he was a Shia — and a 1984 bench reversed 1976 and maintained that "the Quaid was definitely not a Shia". {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} His grandnephew Liaquat H. Merchant writes that "the Quaid was not a Shia; he was also not a Sunni, he was simply a Muslim", and the journalist Khaled Ahmed records that publicly Jinnah "was at pains to gather the Muslims of India under the banner of a general Muslim faith and not under a divisive sectarian identity". {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-en} The Urdu article asserts, for its part, that he adopted the Twelver school, while its infobox gives his religion simply as Islam. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-ali-jinnah-ur} The museum's record accordingly leaves his affiliation unknown and preserves each reading in full.
