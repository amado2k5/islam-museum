## Overview
Abu Ali Isa ibn Ishaq ibn Zur'a, usually called Ibn Zur'a, was a Jacobite Christian philosopher, apologist and translator who lived in Baghdad in the second half of the tenth century. The Encyclopaedia of Islam gives his birth as Dhu l-Hijja 331, August 943, and his death as 6 Sha'ban 398, 16 April 1008. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1011} He was a pupil of [[person/yahya-ibn-adi|Yahya ibn Adi]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1011}

## Dates
The article insists on the dates above and says that the alternative dates of 371/981 and 448/1056, which Ibn Abi Usaybi'a gives, should not be accepted. Its reasons are that Ibn Zur'a is already mentioned by Ibn al-Nadim, and that Ibn Abi Usaybi'a himself speaks of Ibn Zur'a's relations with Yahya ibn Adi, who died in 364/975 according to this article. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1011} The museum's record of [[person/yahya-ibn-adi|Yahya ibn Adi]] follows a different article that gives 363/974 for his death, so the sources differ by a year on that date.

## Studies and livelihood
Ibn Zur'a studied literature, physics and mathematics, and then philosophy under the direction of Yahya ibn Adi. He seems also to have studied medicine, because Ibn Abi Usaybi'a counts him among the famous physicians. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1011} Nevertheless he had to earn his living through commerce, trading in particular with Byzantium. [[person/abu-hayyan-al-tawhidi|Abu Hayyan al-Tawhidi]] is cited as holding that this trade greatly hindered his philosophical work. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1011}

## Trouble with the authorities
The article reports that his competitors denounced him to the authorities, accusing him of secret intrigues with Byzantium. He was arrested and sentenced and his possessions were confiscated. These misfortunes ruined his already poor health and hastened his death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1011}

## Translations
Ibn Zur'a translated or abridged several works of Aristotle, probably from Syriac, and above all the Historia Animalium, and for this reason he is esteemed as a translator. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1011} Of the translations listed by Ibn al-Nadim and completed by al-Qifti and Ibn Abi Usaybi'a, only one has survived, the Sophistical Refutations of Aristotle, preserved in a Paris manuscript. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1011}

## Writings
His fame rests on treatises of philosophy, theology and apologetics, most of which are lost. Only about ten survive. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1011} Four were published by P. Sbath in Cairo in 1929, among them a treatise on the intellect and a letter to a Muslim friend on the existence of God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1011} The article's list continues on the next page with a work on the nature of the union of the Christians, here attributed to a Christian author named Yumn, and a fragment of the Kitab al-Majami' of Ibn al-Muqaffa' (Severus). The OCR text of this passage is damaged, so the museum records the details with caution. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1012}

## Method and approach
The article refers to an unpublished Sorbonne thesis of 1952 by Cyrille Haddad on Ibn Zur'a's surviving works. According to this thesis, as the article summarises it, Ibn Zur'a follows Yahya ibn Adi but departs from him on points of detail. He makes great use of Aristotelian logic, of Platonic or Plotinian doctrine, of the Bible and of the Fathers of the Church. His style is rather heavy, and the result is a cool but scholarly and rational apologetic that only rarely uses arguments aimed at the opponent personally. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 1012}

## What is not known
The OCR text read for this record does not preserve the end of his full name or any detail of his family. The article gives no place of burial and no precise date for his arrest. His surviving works are not summarised here beyond what the article states.
