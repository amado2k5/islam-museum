## Summary
Aisha bint Abi Bakr was the daughter of the first caliph and a wife of the Prophet. Sunni tradition counts her among the greatest transmitters of hadith, and among the most learned women of the first Islamic century.

## Life
She was born in Mecca around 613–614 CE, and the date of her birth is disputed. She migrated to Medina with the [[event/hijra|Hijra]], and married [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] around 623 CE {cite:watt-1956-medina}. She grew up in the Prophet's household, and her contemporaries recorded her sharp memory and intelligence {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

In 627 CE came the episode of the slander. She fell behind the caravan on the way back from a raid, and a young Companion brought her back to the people, while the rabble gossiped with malice. The tradition of tafsir links her innocence to verses revealed on that matter {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

The Prophet died when she was about eighteen. She lived in Medina for nearly five decades and related more than two thousand hadith in the Sunni collections. She corrected the Companions in matters of fiqh and worship. She had a part in the first civil strife, when she led the side that met [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] at the Battle of the Camel in 656 CE {cite:watt-1956-medina}. She died in Medina in July 678 CE.

## Legacy
Her voice runs through Sunni fiqh, creed and tafsir. Whenever the community asked what the Prophet had said or done, Aisha's answer carried a particular weight.

## In Ahmad ibn Hanbal's Fada'il al-Sahaba
[[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]] devotes a chapter to A'isha's merits, including her report that the Prophet told her, "This is Gabriel greeting you with peace", and she replied, "And upon him be peace, God's mercy and blessings — you see what we do not see" (1627, sound according to the editor [[person/wasi-allah-abbas|Wasi Allah Abbas]]), and the hadith "A'isha's excellence over other women is like that of *tharid* over other food" (1628, sound). {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1098} {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1099} It has Ammar's words to a man who disparaged A'isha in Ali's presence — that she was the Messenger of God's beloved and his wife in Paradise, while Ali stayed silent (1625, 1631; the editor discusses Abu Ishaq al-Sabi'i's late confusion and notes that her being his wife in Paradise is narrated by al-Bukhari from Ammar). {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1097} {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1100} It reports that al-Zuhri, at the court of al-Walid when the caliph nearly spoke against A'isha, told him of Abu Muslim al-Khawlani's words to the Syrians: that they and their mother were like two eyes in a head that pain their owner, who can only treat them with what is good for them — and al-Walid fell silent. {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1099} {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1100} In his note the editor calls her the most learned in law of the Muslim women, to whom the Companion jurists referred difficult questions, and gives her death as 57 AH, or 58. {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1097}

## For young visitors
Aisha had one of the strongest memories of her generation. People came from far away to ask her what the Prophet had said and done, and her answers are among the things the tradition was written down from.

## A slave of the descendants of Isma'il
Ibn Ishaq, from Asim ibn Umar ibn Qatada, relates that Aisha said to the Prophet that she owed the freeing of a slave from the descendants of Isma'il. He said to her: "The captives of Banu al-Anbar are coming now, and we will give you one of them to free." See [[event/sariyya-of-uyayna-to-banu-tamim|the raid on Banu al-Anbar]] {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/269}.

## Her marriage (The Sealed Nectar, p.136)
The Prophet married her in Shawwal of the eleventh year of the mission, when she was six years old. He consummated the marriage in Medina in Shawwal of the first year of the Hijra, when she was nine {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.136} {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 1/551}.

## For Explorers
Aisha bint Abi Bakr, Mother of the Believers and wife of the Prophet, was one of the greatest scholars in Islamic history. Renowned for her sharp intellect, she served as a key authority for senior companions in Qur'anic exegesis, jurisprudence, medicine, and Arabic poetry, narrating hundreds of traditions depicting the Prophet's household life and character.

## For Scholars
Aisha bint Abi Bakr al-Siddiq (c. 9 BH – 58 AH / 613–678 CE), Mother of the Believers, regarded in Islamic scholarship as the foremost female jurist and one of the highest authorities among the companions. Raised in Abu Bakr's household and married to the Prophet in Medina, she possessed remarkable insight into prophetic household life and private devotion. Highly esteemed for her expertise in Qur'anic interpretation, legal inheritance, poetry, and medicine, senior companions sought her legal verdicts, and her critical corrections of transmitted traditions (istidrak) shaped the discipline of Hadith verification.

## Encyclopaedia of Islam account
The Encyclopaedia of Islam (2nd ed.), article signed Ch. Pellat, calls 'A'isha the third and favourite wife of the Prophet, born at Mecca about 614, daughter of Abu Bakr and of Umm Ruman of the Kinana. The article gives the marriage as proposed through Khawla bint Hakim, the wife of 'Uthman b. Maz'un, after Khadija's death; an earlier betrothal of 'A'isha to Jubayr b. Mut'im was set aside, and the marriage was consummated some months after the Hijra, when she was nine or so. The article notes that Muhammad's marriages had political purposes and that this one strengthened his tie with Abu Bakr. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 329}

In 5/627, on the return from the expedition against Banu l-Mustaliq, she was left behind when she went to find a necklace. A young man, Safwan b. al-Mu'attal al-Sulami, escorted her back to Medina, which was improper in the circumstances. The gossip was magnified by 'Abd Allah b. Ubayy, the leader of the hypocrites, and when no solid evidence was found, a revelation (Qur'an 24) implied her innocence and rebuked the gossips, and Ibn Ubayy was publicly humiliated. The article notes stories of Muhammad's affection for her and hers for him, but judges that they do not show her engaged in political intrigue; there were two factions among the wives, one led by 'A'isha and Hafsa, which supported the policy of their fathers, and another led by Umm Salama, but their rivalry had little political effect. She nursed him in his last illness, and he was buried in her chamber, where Abu Bakr and 'Umar were also buried. She was a childless widow of about eighteen, and was not to remarry. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 329}

After the murder of 'Uthman in Dhu l-Hijja 35/June 656 she left Medina for Mecca and then, with about a thousand men of Quraysh, for Basra, with Talha and al-Zubayr, professing to avenge 'Uthman. At the battle of the Camel (Jumada II 36/December 656), around the camel carrying her litter, 'Ali was victorious; 'A'isha was treated with respect, while Talha and al-Zubayr were killed. She then lived quietly in Medina for over twenty years, became reconciled with 'Ali, and did not oppose Mu'awiya. She died in Ramadan 58/July 678. The article notes that more than two thousand traditions were related on her authority, of which far fewer were retained by al-Bukhari and Muslim; she was known for poetry, eloquence and history. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 330}
