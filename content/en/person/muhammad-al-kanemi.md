## Summary
Muhammad al-Amin al-Kanemi (1776 – 8 June 1837), called Laminu, was a Kanembu Islamic scholar who saved the Kanem–Bornu Empire from the Fulani jihad and became its shehu and de facto ruler, supplanting the thousand-year Sayfawa order while keeping its forms. He fought Sokoto with armies and with arguments, and the dynasty he founded ruled Bornu for the rest of the century. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-al-kanemi-en}

## Early life and studies
He was born in 1776 at Murzuk. His father Muhammad Ninka was a Kanembu teacher from Fahi, a village near Mao in Kanem, and his mother was the daughter of a wealthy Arab trader. Taught the Qur'an at Murzuk, he travelled to Tripoli and elsewhere to study, and in the 1790s accompanied his father on the pilgrimage to Mecca; his father died at Medina, and al-Kanemi remained in the Middle East for about a decade of further study before returning to settle at Ngala in Bornu, where students gathered around him. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-al-kanemi-en}

## The defence of Bornu
When the Sokoto jihad reached Bornu and the Fulani captured the capital Ngazargamu in 1808, the mai Dunama IX called on al-Kanemi, whose small force of students and followers had already defended Ngala. Al-Kanemi led the recovery of the capital and the campaigns that followed, and his standing rose with each success until the mais depended on him. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-al-kanemi-en}

His war was also fought on paper. He carried on a series of theological, legal and political debates by letter with Usman dan Fodio, and later with Muhammad Bello, challenging the right of Sokoto to strike at a state that had been Muslim for at least 800 years; the exchanges, on the nature of jihad and Muslim rule, remain points of contention in modern Nigeria. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-al-kanemi-en}

## Shehu of Bornu
Rewarded with fiefs and followers, he built the new city of Kukawa in 1814 and took the title shehu, "sheikh", backdating his rule to 1809; attempts by the court to remove him failed, and later mais reigned as his titular sovereigns while power lay with him. In the 1820s he drove the Fulani out of Bornu and extended his authority over the Kotoko city-states. His rule worked through the empire's existing framework of fiefs, and the written administration he and his successors produced — more than a hundred preserved diplomatic letters — shows an established chancery. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-al-kanemi-en} {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-al-kanemi-ar}

The British traveller Dixon Denham, presented to him in 1823, described a commander of sound judgment and gentle, conciliating demeanour, who had refused an offer of the sultanate itself. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-al-kanemi-en}

## Faith
No source consulted names a legal school or a Sufi order for al-Kanemi himself. His learning was formed in Bornu, Tripoli and the Hijaz, and his public cause was the defence of an old Muslim land's right to its own Islam; that is context, not attestation of a school, and Wikidata gives his religion as Islam only. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-al-kanemi-en} {cite:wikidata-muhammad-al-kanemi}

## Death and succession
He died on 8 June 1837 and was succeeded as shehu by his son Umar, under whom the al-Kanemi dynasty in time replaced the Sayfawa mais altogether. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-al-kanemi-en}

## Relationships
His historic relationships are his correspondences: the letter debates with Usman dan Fodio and with Muhammad Bello, the two men whose jihad he resisted and whose statecraft he answered in kind. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-al-kanemi-en}
