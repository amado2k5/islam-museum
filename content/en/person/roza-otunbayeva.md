## Summary
Roza Otunbayeva (born 23 August 1950) is a Kyrgyz politician and diplomat who led her country's interim government after the April 2010 revolution and served as President of Kyrgyzstan from 3 July 2010 to 1 December 2011, the first woman head of state in Central Asia. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en} {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-ur}

A philosopher by training, she had already been her country's foreign minister three times over, its first ambassador to the United States, its ambassador to Britain and a UN envoy to Georgia before she took charge of the transition. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en}

Her nineteen months in office carried Kyrgyzstan from revolution through ethnic violence and a constitutional referendum to what Britannica calls its first peaceful transfer of power. {cite:britannica-roza-otunbayeva}

## Early life and education
Roza Isakovna Otunbayeva was born on 23 August 1950 at Frunze, the Kyrgyz capital now called Bishkek, to Isak Otunbayev, a member of the Kirghiz SSR's Supreme Court from 1967 to 1992, and Salika Daniyarova, a teacher. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en} {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-ur}

Britannica gives her birthplace as Osh; the three Wikipedia articles and Wikidata give Frunze, which this record follows, and the variant is recorded. {cite:britannica-roza-otunbayeva} {cite:wikidata-roza-otunbayeva}

She graduated from the philosophy faculty of Moscow State University in 1972, and in 1975 defended her Candidate of Sciences dissertation, a critique of the Frankfurt School's treatments of Marxist-Leninist dialectics. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en}

She taught philosophy at Kyrgyz State National University from 1975 to 1981, rising to head of department, before entering Communist Party work in 1981. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en} {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-ur}

## Soviet and diplomatic career
In 1986 she became deputy chair of the Kirghiz SSR's Council of Ministers and concurrently its foreign minister, and from 1989 to 1992 she chaired the USSR's UNESCO committee and served as a vice-president of UNESCO's Executive Board. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en}

Britannica's shorter account of these years instead describes her Soviet service as including an ambassadorship to Malaysia, a detail the longer English article does not carry; the difference is recorded rather than resolved. {cite:britannica-roza-otunbayeva}

After independence she served as Kyrgyzstan's foreign minister and deputy prime minister in 1992, then as its first ambassador to the United States and Canada from 1992 to 1994, foreign minister again from 1994 to 1997, and ambassador to the United Kingdom from 1997 to 2002. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en}

From 2002 to 2004 she was the UN Secretary-General's Deputy Special Representative for Georgia. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en}

## Opposition and the Tulip Revolution
In December 2004 she co-founded the Ata-Jurt movement and broke with President Askar Akayev, accusing him of corruption and nepotism; his government blocked her movement from the 2005 elections. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en} {cite:britannica-roza-otunbayeva}

After Akayev's fall in the Tulip Revolution of March 2005 she served as acting foreign minister, but parliament refused to approve her appointment and she lost the post within months. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en} {cite:britannica-roza-otunbayeva}

She won a seat in the Jogorku Kenesh in December 2007 at the head of the Social Democratic bloc and led the parliamentary opposition from 2008 to 2010. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en}

## The April Revolution and the interim presidency
On 7 April 2010, after bloody unrest in which the Arabic article counts more than sixty dead, the opposition chose Otunbayeva to head an interim government; President Kurmanbek Bakiyev fled and resigned, leaving via Kazakhstan for Minsk. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en} {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-ar}

She announced that her government would dissolve parliament, prepare a new constitution and hold democratic elections, vowing not to repeat Bakiyev's mistakes. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-ar}

A constitutional referendum, passed with more than 90 per cent support, turned Kyrgyzstan into a parliamentary republic, and on 3 July 2010 she was sworn in as president for the remainder of the transition, barred by the new constitution from standing in the 2011 election. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en}

Her term ended on 1 December 2011 — one passage in the English article's body gives 31 December, but its lead, the Urdu article and Britannica all support 1 December — and her stepping down after Almazbek Atambayev's election was, in Britannica's words, Kyrgyzstan's first peaceful transfer of power. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en} {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-ur} {cite:britannica-roza-otunbayeva}

## The June 2010 violence and its inquiries
Weeks before her swearing-in, ethnic violence between Kyrgyz and Uzbeks in the south killed more than 400 people and destroyed thousands of homes. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en}

The international inquiry commission led by Kimmo Kiljunen condemned her provisional government for inaction and for failing to protect the population, and Amnesty International criticised the authorities' response; in May 2011 her government rejected the commission's conclusion that crimes against humanity had been committed. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en}

The OSCE recorded statements describing systematic violence against Uzbek neighbourhoods, and the case of the activist Azimjon Askarov drew sustained criticism: Askarov claimed in court that he had been convicted on Otunbayeva's direct order, the former prosecutor general Kubatbek Baibolov told investigators the case was politically motivated and directed by her, and on 31 March 2016 the UN Human Rights Committee found violations of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights in his case, including torture and fair-trial breaches — all of it carried here as the article attributes it. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en}

The article likewise records the arrests of the Baryktabasov supporters on 5 August 2010 with attributed reports of torture, and Kamchybek Tashiev's accusation against her security chief over an attack on his house. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en}

Against this, Britannica credits her with having largely quelled the unrest and brought stability to the country. {cite:britannica-roza-otunbayeva}

## After the presidency
In January 2012 she founded the Roza Otunbayeva Initiative, a foundation working on education and development. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en}

In 2022 she became the UN Secretary-General's Special Representative for Afghanistan and head of the UN Assistance Mission in Afghanistan, a service the English article dates to 2025; the Urdu article still speaks of it in the present tense. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en} {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-ur}

Her honours include the United States' International Women of Courage Award in 2011, commandership of the French Légion d'honneur, Mongolia's Order of the Polar Star, and a place in Newsweek and The Daily Beast's 2011 list of 150 influential women. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en}

## Personal life
Otunbayeva is divorced and has two children, and she is fluent in Kyrgyz, Russian, English, German and French. {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-en} {cite:wikipedia-roza-otunbayeva-ur}
