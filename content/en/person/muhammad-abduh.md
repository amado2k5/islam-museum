## Overview
Shaykh Muhammad Abduh was an Egyptian theologian and reformer, and the founder of what the Encyclopaedia of Islam calls the Egyptian modernist school. He was a scholar trained at the Azhar who became a journalist, a judge, the state mufti and a member of the governing body of the Azhar itself. His life's work, in the article's account, was an attempt to keep Islam standing as a religion in the face of the pressure of the West, by returning it to what he took to be its original principles. He died in 1905. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 470} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 471}

## Birth, schooling and the Azhar
Abduh came from an Egyptian peasant family and was born in 1849 in Lower Egypt. He spent his childhood in the small village of Mahallat Nasr in the province of Buhayra, where his father had gone back to his land. After he had learned the Qur'an by heart he was sent, in 1862, to the theological school at [[place/tanta|Tanta]], but he left after a year and a half, discouraged. A grand-uncle who stirred his interest in mysticism brought him back to his studies, and in 1865 he returned to Tanta. The next year he went on to the Azhar mosque in [[place/cairo|Cairo]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 470}

The article describes the [[building/al-azhar|Azhar]] of those years as a place where a new spirit was just beginning to show: a return to the classics and a growing interest in natural science and history. Abduh at first gave himself wholly to mysticism, practised asceticism and withdrew from the world, until his grand-uncle persuaded him to give this up. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 470}

## Al-Afghani and the turn to journalism
Around 1872 Abduh came into contact with [[person/jamal-al-din-al-afghani|Jamal al-Din al-Afghani]], who had just arrived in Egypt. According to the article, al-Afghani showed him traditional learning in a new light, drew his attention to European works available in translation, and turned his interest to the problems of Egypt and of the Muslims of the day. Abduh became his most ardent disciple. His first work, a mystical treatise, Risalat al-Waridat of 1290/1874, praised al-Afghani as his spiritual guide, and the influence is even clearer in his second work, notes on a commentary on a creed, the Hashiya ala Sharh al-Dawani li-l-Aqa'id al-Adudiyya of 1292/1876. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 470}

Al-Afghani's influence, together with developments in Egypt at the end of the reign of Khedive Ismail, led Abduh to take up journalism in 1876, and he kept it up afterwards. After finishing his studies at the Azhar and obtaining the certificate of an alim, he first gave private lessons. In 1879 he was appointed a teacher at the Dar al-Ulum, which had been founded a few years earlier to modernise religious instruction. In the same year, soon after [[person/muhammad-tawfiq-pasha|Khedive Tawfiq]] came to the throne, Abduh was dismissed for reasons the article says have not been clearly explained and was sent to his native village, while al-Afghani was banished from Egypt. A liberal ministry recalled him in 1880 and made him chief editor of the official gazette al-Waqa'i al-Misriyya, which under his hand became the mouthpiece of the liberal party. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 470}

The article stresses a difference between the two men despite a shared aim, the liberation of Muslim peoples and a renaissance of Islam by its own strength. Al-Afghani, it says, was a revolutionary seeking a complete upheaval. Abduh held that only gradual reform could succeed, that no political revolution could replace a gradual change of mentality, and that the reform of education, above all moral and religious education, was the first step towards progress. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 470}

## The Urabi rebellion and exile
The rebellion of [[person/ahmad-urabi|Urabi Pasha]] ended this phase of his activity. The article says his part in the movement has not been sufficiently clarified. It judges it certain that he neither shared the optimism of the military circles nor approved of their use of force, that he sided with the nationalist opponents of absolutism, and that he tried to exert a moderating influence on the leaders. After the rebellion was suppressed he was sentenced to banishment from Egypt at the end of 1882. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 470} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 471}

He went first to Beirut and then to [[place/paris|Paris]], where he met al-Afghani at the beginning of 1884. The two founded a society called al-Urwa al-Wuthqa and published a paper of the same name, [[work/urwa-al-wuthqa|al-Urwa al-Wuthqa]], which had to stop after eight months but, the article says, had a very deep influence on the growth of nationalism in the Muslim East. Abduh continued to promote the society in [[place/tunis|Tunis]], then cut himself off from it and settled in Beirut at the beginning of 1885. The article states that the paper expressed al-Afghani's views entirely. In Beirut he pursued Islamic and Arabic studies and produced a translation from the Persian of al-Afghani's Risalat al-Radd ala al-Dahriyyin, the only considerable work of al-Afghani, together with two philological works, a commentary on the Nahj al-Balagha and a commentary on the Maqamat of Badi al-Zaman al-Hamadhani. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 471}

## Return to Egypt and public office
In 1889 Abduh was allowed to return and went straight to Cairo. His wish to teach again was not granted at once. Instead he entered the judiciary and was appointed a judge on the Native Tribunals, and two years later a counsellor at the Court of Appeal. In 1899 he reached the highest religious post in Egypt, that of state mufti. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 471}

His court work produced the publication of his verdicts in Takrir fi Islah al-Mahakim al-Shar'iyya of 1318/1900, which, the article says, gave the stimulus to important reforms in the administration of the sharia, and the founding of the college for qadis goes back chiefly to his efforts. In 1899 he also became a member of the Legislative Council, which the article calls the first stage in the representation of the Egyptian people. In 1894 he had already become a member of the governing body of the Azhar, set up at his suggestion. There he gained wide renown for his reforms in the university and himself took part in teaching. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 471}

## Writings
In the fifteen years after his return he published a number of works. The article names, as the most important, the Risalat al-Tawhid of 1315/1897, his chief theological work, based on his lectures in Beirut; the [[work/risalat-al-tawhid|Risalat al-Tawhid]] is also a record in this museum. He also published a work on logic of 1316/1898, a commentary on a text by al-Qadi Zayn al-Din, and a defence of Islam against Christianity in the field of knowledge and civilisation, of 1320/1902, first printed in al-Manar. He did not finish his commentary on the Qur'an, to which he attached great importance and of which portions had appeared in al-Manar. His disciple and friend [[person/rashid-rida|Rashid Rida]] revised it and published it first in al-Manar; see [[work/tafsir-al-manar|Tafsir al-Manar]]. Of his many articles, two of 1900 were published in French translation as L'Europe et l'Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 471}

## Reception
The article says that his advanced ideas drew sharp hostility from orthodox and conservative circles, in serious refutations and also in attacks and intrigues against him, as a whole literature of lampoons shows. It adds that his teaching found remarkable support among all serious-minded Muslims. The main organ of his views was the monthly al-Manar, which had appeared since 1897 under the editorship of Rashid Rida, who also produced a large literary monument to his master; the article cautions that Rida's views and the tendencies of his periodical must not be identified uncritically with Abduh's. According to the article, his teaching has kept its influence steadily to the present day. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 471}

## His programme
In the article's summary of his own statement of aims, his programme included the reform of the Muslim religion by bringing it back to its original condition, the reform of the Arabic language, and the recognition of the rights of the people in relation to the government. His political activity was dominated by patriotism, which the article says he was the first to champion in Egypt. It describes him as opposed both to European political control and to oriental despotism in Muslim lands, and as favouring an inner assimilation of Western civilisation without giving up the fundamental Muslim ideas, a synthesis of the two. The article judges that this programme gives him an important place among the founders of modern Egypt. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 471}

## Theological outlook
The article distinguishes the programme from his efforts in theology, where it calls him first of all a theologian. It reports that the foundations of his teaching came primarily from the school of [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] and [[person/ibn-qayyim-al-jawziyya|Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya]], which favoured reform on conservative lines, and from [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]]'s ethical conception of religion, whatever stimulus he received from Western thought. Convinced of the superiority of true Islam, he wished to remove the abuses that, in his view, falsified the religion and made it out of step with the times, and to adapt Islam to real progress by going back to its true principles. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 471}

On this basis, the article says, he attacked the [[concept/madhhab|schools of law]] and [[concept/taqlid|taqlid]], and called for the restoration of [[concept/ijtihad|ijtihad]] and for a new consensus suited to modern conditions, grounded in the Qur'an and the true [[concept/sunna|sunna]], for which he laid down strict criteria. He rejected the hairsplitting of the jurists, the worship of saints and all [[concept/bidah|innovation]], and sought a deeper, more ethical religion in place of mechanical formalism. For the old system of jurisprudence he claimed full freedom, and wanted it replaced by new, developing laws in which regard for the common good, [[concept/al-maslaha-al-mursala|maslaha]], and for the times could take precedence over the literal text of revelation, and in which reason should prevail in any conflict between reason and tradition on matters of religion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 471}

He was also convinced that knowledge and religion, rightly understood, cannot conflict, so that reason need not accept a logical impossibility as a religious truth. Religion was given to humanity as a thread guiding it against the errors of reason, and reason, once it has tested the proofs of religion, should accept its dogmas. In doctrine, says the article, he adopted the most rational conception still reconcilable with orthodoxy. Revelation, for him, was intuitive knowledge caused by God and accompanied by awareness of its source, an experience limited to the prophets. The task of prophecy was the moral education of the masses, so religious teaching and commandments were meant for the masses and not for an elite. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 471} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 472}

The article adds that he kept the saints within his system but was sceptical about belief in miracles; that he found a basis for explaining nature by causal laws; that he kept the four main duties, prayer, the alms tax, fasting and pilgrimage, but moved them, as mystics usually do, from the sphere of worship to that of religion and morals; and that on free will he decided for indeterminism, opening the way to an ethic that excludes fatalism and preaches active effort and mutual support. He defended Islam against Christianity through a kind of philosophy of the history of religion: the sending of prophets was a gradual education of humanity whose last and highest stage was the mission of Muhammad, and present Muslims fall short of the ideal only because they lost the old purity of the teaching. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 472}

## Assessments of his reform
The article's own author offers a mixed assessment, which is reported here as that author's view. The author calls the primitive Islam of Abduh's thought not the historical Islam but a very idealised one, and judges that his rationalism, genuinely part of early Islam, became for him the main weapon of defence and took the place of a deepening of religion, so that his theology had the character of an apologetic compromise. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 472}

Other assessments appear in the article's account of the reception: conservative critics met his ideas with refutations, as noted above, while serious-minded Muslims supported them. The museum records these as positions held by the persons concerned, not as findings of its own, and does not decide between them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 471}

## His portrait
Shakib Arslan, in his commentary on *Hadir al-Alam al-Islami*, describes him as his teacher. He says that he got to know him in Beirut in 1886 during his exile after the Urabi revolt, and that he saw in his understanding of Islamic doctrine the path to the renaissance of Islam. He says that his pupil Rashid Rida wrote his biography in two volumes. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923}

The source of this section is a single witness who admired the shaykh, and for that reason the dates of his exile and death need an additional source.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lecture series Al Dinu Al Qayem

Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy recalls Muhammad Abduh's visit to Europe and his return. He says that Abduh found in their scripture the teaching to offer the other cheek, while the same people possessed a hundred fleets, and the speaker remarks, as his own opinion, on this contrast between word and deed {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep01 00:54:00}.
