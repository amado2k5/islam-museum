## Summary
Mubarak Al-Sabah (c. 1837 – 28 November 1915), called "the Great" and nicknamed "The lion of the peninsula", was the seventh ruler of the Sheikhdom of Kuwait, from 1896 until his death, as the English article describes him; he ascended the throne after assassinating his predecessor and half-brother, Muhammad Al-Sabah, and with the 1899 agreement with Britain and the guarantee it represented he is regarded as the founder of modern-day Kuwait. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en} {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-ar} The Arabic article, which dates his birth to 1844 against the English article's c. 1837, calls him Kuwait's seventh ruler and her "true founder", under whom Kuwait grew in building, security, wealth and trade until its pearl-diving fleet reached some eight hundred ships and its first regular schools and hospitals were built. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-ar}

## Early life
Mubarak was born into Kuwait's ruling al-Sabah family, the third son of Sheikh Sabah II Al-Sabah (r. 1859–1866) by Lulwa bint Muhammad al-Thaqib, daughter of the sheikh of al-Zubayr; from the age of five his grandfather Jaber had him taught reading, writing and memorisation of the Quran, and from twelve he trained in shooting, horsemanship and fighting on campaigns under his elder brothers Abdullah and then Muhammad, as the Arabic article recounts. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-ar} {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en} His birth year is unsettled, and the museum states the readings rather than merging them: the Arabic article gives 1844 in its lead and biography; the English article gives "c. 1837", as do the Urdu stub and the harvest queue behind this record; Wikidata carries both 1838 and 1844. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-ar} {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en} {cite:wikidata-mubarak-al-sabah} He served primarily as cavalry commander of Kuwait's military in many operations, including Ottoman campaigns of 1871, 1892 and 1894 into Hasa, Qatar and southern Iraq, receiving the Medjidie order in 1871 and, in August 1879, the rank of Grand Equerry of the Sultan for a campaign into Qatif and southern Iraq, and he had dealings with the British as early as 1863, when he met Sir Lewis Pelly. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en} {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-ar}

## Career
The brothers' quarrel was over money: Muhammad and Jarrah, who between them ran the town and its finances, withheld from Mubarak the funds his desert expeditions and household required, Yusuf al-Ibrahim intervened to block him, and repeated mediation by Kuwait's notables failed, as the Arabic article recounts at length. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-ar} On the night of 25 Dhu al-Qa'da 1313 AH / 7 May 1896 — also given as 8 May — Mubarak, supported by his sons Jaber and Salim and a party of servants and guards, killed Muhammad in his bedchamber while Jaber killed their uncle Jarrah after a struggle, and Mubarak was acclaimed ruler, the first time in the emirate's history that power was taken by bloodshed. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-ar} The English article dates the killing to 8 May 1896 but its lead dates his reign from 18 May 1896, an internal inconsistency the museum states. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en} Yusuf al-Ibrahim then fought him for a decade with Ottoman and Rashidi allies and naval expeditions against Kuwait, until his death in 1906, while Mubarak courted Istanbul with lavish gifts until the Ottomans appointed him kaymakam of Kuwait in December 1897. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-ar} {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en} On 18 January 1899 he signed the secret agreement with Major M. J. Meade, the British political resident, guarding Kuwait against outside aggression and pledging himself and his successors not to receive foreign agents or cede or sell territory without British approval. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en} In 1901 he invaded Najd hoping to claim the southern Rashidi dominions, but at the Battle of Sarif on 17 March 1901 most of his force was destroyed, his brother and two nephews killed, and his rule nearly lost; the Status Quo Agreement of September 1901 between Mubarak, the British and the Ottomans then preserved nominal Ottoman jurisdiction while leaving him free. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en} In 1904–1906 he gave Abdulaziz Al Saud "strong moral and material" support against the Ottomans in al-Qasim and mediated between the Saudis and the Ottomans in 1905; in October 1907 he sold the railway-terminus rights to Britain, compromising the Berlin–Baghdad Railway's extension to Kuwait's port; Britain made him an honorary Knight Commander of the Order of the Indian Empire in 1911 and of the Order of the Star of India in 1914; and the Anglo-Ottoman Convention of 1913 recognised Kuwait as an autonomous kaza of the Ottoman Empire and an independent entity. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en} When the First World War began he sided closely with Britain, sending a force in November 1914 to Umm Qasr, Safwan, Bubiyan and Basra to expel the Ottomans, in exchange for which Britain recognised Kuwait as "an independent government under British protection", and he removed the Ottoman symbol from the Kuwaiti flag and replaced it with "Kuwait" in Arabic script. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en}

## Death and legacy
In his later years Mubarak wrestled with bouts of illness, and he died on 28 November 1915 of an attack of malaria aggravated by a bad heart — the date of the English article's death section, its lead parenthesis, the Arabic article, the Urdu stub and Wikidata — while the English lead's narrative line says "18 November 1915", an inconsistency the museum states; his son Jaber's accession on 28 November 1915 in both Jaber articles confirms the 28th. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en} {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-ar} {cite:wikidata-mubarak-al-sabah} He was the father of two rulers who succeeded him, Jaber and Salim, from whom the Al-Jaber and Al-Salim branches of the family originate, the paternal ancestor of all successive rulers and prime ministers of Kuwait, and the ancestor from whom Kuwait's constitution requires every Emir to descend. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en}

## Positions and criticism
This section is read as a presentation of positions reported from the sources, not as a verdict of the museum.

The assassination itself is contested among scholars, as the English article reports: most believe Mubarak killed his half-brothers — Jill Crystal with his sons Jabir and Salim and loyal supporters in secret during the night, Frederick Anscombe with "his men" in the early hours — while B. J. Slot, "not even convinced that Mubarak was the assassin", holds that "the widely divergent stories and interpretations… make it impossible to reach a firm conclusion about what happened in Kuwait in 1896", and the theories of motive range from resentment at constant desert expeditions and inadequate funding to Muhammad's alleged indolence and Mubarak's denied share of the family wealth. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en} The Arabic article, drawing on Kuwaiti historiography, gives its own judgement in passing: that in his first ten years he followed his ancestors in humility and freedom from injustice and subjected the Al-Sabah to strict rule, but that his later intimacy with Khaz'al of Mohammerah changed him and he imitated him in many injustices; it also preserves his answer when asked why he never built Kuwait a new wall — "I am the wall" — and Ameen Rihani's portrait of a man of sharp temper and ambition with "something of the lion and things of the chameleon". {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-ar} The sources differ in details the museum states rather than merges: his birth year (c. 1837 in English and Urdu, 1844 in Arabic, 1838 and 1844 in Wikidata), the opening of his reign (the night of 7/8 May 1896 in Arabic, 8 May in the English body, 18 May in the English lead), and his death day (28 November everywhere but one line of the English lead). {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-ar} {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en} {cite:wikidata-mubarak-al-sabah} His faith is recorded as unknown as to school in his data record: none of the three articles states a religion or school for him — the Arabic article's note that he was taught memorisation of the Quran as a child is an upbringing, not an attestation — and Wikidata's single value is Islam generically, so the value stays unknown under the two-source rule and the doubt rule. {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-ar} {cite:wikipedia-mubarak-al-sabah-en} {cite:wikidata-mubarak-al-sabah}
