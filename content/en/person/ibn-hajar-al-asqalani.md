## Overview
The memoriser and shaykh of Islam Ahmad ibn Ali ibn Hajar al-Asqalani, author of *Inba' al-Ghumr* (Informing the Ignorant of the Age). {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.765,774,781}

## Family and origins
The Encyclopaedia of Islam describes Ibn Hajar as an Egyptian hadith scholar, judge and historian who lived from 773/1372 to 852/1449, and calls his life the final summation of the science of hadith. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 808} The article says he did not know where the name Ibn Hajar came from. Family tradition traced the nisba al-Asqalani to 587/1191, when [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Saladin]] ordered [[place/ascalon|Ascalon]] destroyed and its Muslim inhabitants resettled; his forebears went to [[place/alexandria|Alexandria]] and then to [[place/cairo|Cairo]], where he was born on 22 Sha'ban 773/28 February 1372. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 808} His grandfather seems to have been a cloth manufacturer in Alexandria. His father had received a good legal training, had to abandon an early judicial career, wrote occasional poetry and died in Rajab 777/1375, so that the boy was left an orphan, his mother having died before. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 808}

The article stresses that the family had money and connections. His mother came from a family with a Karimi merchant in it and left a considerable fortune to a son of an earlier marriage. His elder sister, Sitt al-Rakb, born on pilgrimage in 770/1369, acted as a mother to him, and she married into a wealthy Karimi merchant family. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 808} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809}

## Childhood and education
His father had named Zaki al-Din al-Kharrubi, a scholar turned wealthy merchant, as principal guardian. In 784/1382 this guardian took the eleven-year-old boy on the pilgrimage to [[place/mecca|Mecca]], brought him back in 786, and died in Muharram 787/1385. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809} The article describes a background of long-established mercantile wealth and intense, mostly non-professional interest in religious learning. The loss of his parents brought no material hardship, although it may have delayed his schooling, which began at five; he had memorised the Qur'an by nine. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809}

After his return to Egypt his studies began in earnest, and in the custom of the time he recorded them in detail, with teachers and books read, in several catalogues of his own. Another guardian and early teacher, Shams al-Din Ibn al-Qattan, introduced him to historical literature. When he chose to specialise in hadith, Zayn al-Din [[person/abd-al-rahim-al-iraqi|al-Iraqi]], who died in 806/1404, became his principal teacher. He also benefited from a scholar named Ibn Jama'a from 790/1388 until 819/1416. The article adds that none of his teachers influenced him as strongly as he later influenced his own students. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809}

## Marriage, travels and early writing
He began scholarly hadith research at twenty and committed himself wholly to it in 796/1393-4. In Sha'ban 798/May 1396 Ibn al-Qattan arranged his marriage to Uns, daughter of an army inspector; he moved into his wife's family house and lived there until he died. Later marriages did not bring another wife into that house. Uns outlived him by almost fourteen years. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809}

After months of study in Alexandria he left in Shawwal 799/July 1397 for the Hijaz and [[place/yemen|Yemen]], a journey lasting into 801/1398, and then studied in Palestine and Syria. He later made the pilgrimage several times, revisited Yemen in 806/1403, and toured Syria for lectures and study in 836-7/1432-3 in the entourage of the sultan [[person/barsbay|Barsbay]]. His student travels as such ended with his return from Syria in 803/1400. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809} His first recorded writing was a paper on prosody from 795/1392-3, much of the poetry in his Diwan dates from his youth, and many of his large later works were begun in these years. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809}

## Teaching and offices
The article describes a career that followed the usual path of lecturer, professor, head of college and finally judge, with posts as mufti, preacher and librarian besides, and moved smoothly towards growing fame despite minor annoyances and interruptions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809} His hadith lectures began in Shawwal 808/March 1406 at the Shaykhuniyya; he later taught at the Jamaliyya, opened in Rajab 811/November 1408, and the Mankutimuriyya in Jumada II 812/October 1409. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809}

His main academic association was the [[building/khanqah-of-baybars-al-gashankir|Baybarsiyya khanqah]], of which he was installed head, for both teaching and administration, on 3 Rabi' I 813/6 July 1410. He lost the post in 816/1413, regained it in Rabi' II 818/June 1415 and kept it for almost thirty-one years, until he was ousted on 20 Jumada I 849/24 August 1445. In the meantime he taught at the Dar al-Hadith al-Kamiliyya while working to regain the Baybarsiyya, which he did on 2 Rabi' II 852/6 June 1448. An alphabetical filing system for the institution's beneficiaries, introduced under his administration, was imitated by other colleges and by the army registry. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809}

He was also mufti at the Dar al-'Adl from 811/1408-9 until his death, and associate preacher and imam at the mosques of [[building/al-azhar|al-Azhar]] and [[building/masjid-amr-fustat|'Amr]]. In 826/1423 he took over the library of the Mahmudiyya, with about four thousand valuable manuscripts, and compiled two catalogues of it, one alphabetical and one by subject. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809}

## Chief judge
He had declined a judgeship offered in Yemen in his early years. His great opportunity came on 27 Muharram 827/31 December 1423, a moment the article connects with [[person/jalal-al-din-al-bulqini|Jalal al-Din al-Bulqini]]; it notes that al-Sakhawi's biography gives slightly different days. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809} He was dismissed less than eleven months later, but he held the office of chief judge of Egypt and Syria for a combined total of about twenty-one years. The article lists a series of dismissals and reinstatements between 827/1423 and 852/1448, among them his dismissal on 11 Muharram 849/19 April 1445 after the collapse of a minaret with much loss of life, when attempts were made to hold the chief judge responsible for the safety of the structure; he was reinstated on 5 Safar 850/2 May 1446. He lost the office finally on 25 Jumada II 852/26 August 1448. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809}

## Death and character
He died about an hour after the evening prayer in the night of Saturday, 28 Dhu al-Hijja 852/22 February 1449. His will, with a variety of individual bequests, has survived. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809} The article says that his pupil al-Sakhawi describes his appearance, character and religious and moral conduct as fully conforming to the ideal standards of Islam, and that few biographers disagree, al-Biqa'i being a notable exception. He was a good chess player and remained fond of poetry all his life. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 809}

## Family life
According to the article, success as a scholar and official was accompanied by great family disappointments. His wife Uns bore him five daughters and no surviving sons, and he outlived all five daughters by many years. The eldest, Zayn Khatun, married a Mamluk official, and their son Yusuf, born in 828/1425, became a scholar of some name but did not fulfil his grandfather's hope for a successor; he angered al-Sakhawi by presuming to correct supposed mistakes in the grandfather's work. The other daughters also died young, two of them as small children. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 810} His only surviving son, Badr al-Din Muhammad, born in 815/1412 and dead in 869/1465, was borne by a Tatar slave girl of Uns, and the article suspects he was not qualified for the scholarly posts his father tried to give him, nor a good administrator. A later marriage, contracted in [[place/aleppo|Aleppo]] in 836/1432, lasted until Ibn Hajar's death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 810}

## Works in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The article attributes his lasting fame to numerous works, mainly on hadith, whose sheer volume presupposes an enormous amount of labour. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 810} In his lifetime he was most admired for his work on [[work/sahih-al-bukhari|al-Bukhari's Sahih]]. He drafted *Ta'liq al-Ta'liq*, on the chains of transmission of the Sahih, in 804/1401-2 and published it three years later. The introduction to his great commentary [[work/fath-al-bari|Fath al-Bari]] was finished in 813/1410-11, and the work took shape in lecture courses from 817/1414 until its completion on 1 Rajab 842/18 December 1438. Its fame was such that in 833/1429-30 the [[polity/timurid-empire|Timurid]] ruler Shah Rukh asked Barsbay to obtain copies of the part published so far, as did the [[polity/hafsid-dynasty|Hafsid]] Abu Faris 'Abd al-'Aziz from the other end of the Muslim world. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 810}

Among his large biographical dictionaries, *al-Isaba* treats the men around Muhammad, while [[work/tahdhib-al-tahdhib|Tahdhib al-Tahdhib]], partly clean-copied by 807/1404-5, and [[work/lisan-al-mizan|Lisan al-Mizan]], drafted by 847/1443-4, treat transmitters of hadith. *Raf' al-Isr* gathers biographies of Egyptian judges and shows, in the article's reading, his literary interests and his concern with his own place in history. [[work/durar-al-kaminah|al-Durar al-Kamina]], covering notable people who died in the eighth century AH, is called the first of the all-inclusive century biographical collections; he continued an annalistic supplement to it down to 832/1428-9. His annalistic history of events from 773/1372, his birth year, to 850/1446 is [[work/inba-al-ghumr|Inba' al-Ghumr]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 810}

The article judges that most of his works are to a large degree mechanical compilations, their material outside contemporary data being excerpts from earlier compilations. It adds that he was extremely thorough, aimed at completeness, was within limits critical, and created handbooks of great scope and accuracy that summarise nearly all earlier relevant literature and remain indispensable reference works. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 810} The article names al-Sakhawi's *al-Jawahir wa-l-Durar* as its basic biographical source. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 810}

## In the History of the Caliphs
Al-Suyuti says that he composed his well-known poem in praise of the caliph al-Musta'in when he was given the oath of allegiance to the sultanate. Al-Suyuti relied on him for events up to 850 AH. He died in the days of al-Mustakfi II. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.765,774,781}

## His works
Haji Khalifa attributes to him [[work/ilam-bi-man-wala-misr|Al-I'lam bi-man Wali Misr]] (Informing about Those Who Governed Egypt), [[work/raf-al-isr-qudat-misr|Raf' al-Isr 'an Qudat Misr]] (Lifting the Burden from the Judges of Egypt) and [[work/al-isaba-fi-tamyiz-al-sahaba|al-Isaba fi Tamyiz al-Sahaba]] (The Hit: Distinguishing the Companions). {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan}

## In al-Sakhawi's al-Daw' al-Lami'
[[person/al-sakhawi|Al-Sakhawi]] gives an entry for his teacher Ahmad ibn Ali, Shihab al-Din Abu al-Fadl Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani: born in Old Cairo on 22 Sha'ban 773, he grew up an orphan. {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 2/36} He studied hadith with al-Iraqi, and with al-Bulqini, Ibn al-Mulaqqin, al-Haythami and others. {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 2/37} He wrote more than a hundred and fifty works, the greatest being Fath al-Bari, and became Shafi'i chief judge in al-Muharram 827. {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 2/38} He died at the end of Dhu al-Hijja 852; al-Sakhawi devoted a separate book to his life, al-Jawahir wa-l-Durar. {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 2/39} {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 2/40}
