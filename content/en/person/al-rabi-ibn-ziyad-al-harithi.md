## Overview
Al-Rabi' ibn Ziyad al-Harithi. In a report of al-Baladhuri, Abu Musa al-Ash'ari sent him to the Asawira when they sided with the Kalbaniyya. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

## The report
Al-Rabi' fought them, then they asked for safe conduct on condition that they should embrace Islam, fight the Madus, form alliances with whomever they wished, and settle where they liked. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}

## His entry in al-Isaba
He is al-Rabi' ibn Ziyad ibn Abd Allah ibn Sufyan ibn Nashib ibn Hidm ibn Awadh ibn Ghalib ibn Qutay'a ibn Abs al-Absi, a famous horseman, known as al-Harithi {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.455}. The entry reports that Abd Allah ibn Amir appointed him over Sijistan in the year 29 AH and it was conquered at his hands, and that his brother al-Muhajir ibn Ziyad was killed at Manadhir {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.455}. Al-Bukhari, Ibn Abi Hatim and Ibn Hibban mention him among the Successors, and Ibn al-Kalbi reports on his life and poetry in the Jahiliyya {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.455}.

## For younger visitors
Al-Rabi' ibn Ziyad al-Harithi was one of the horsemen of the Khurasan conquests; Abd Allah ibn Amir appointed him over Sijistan in 29 AH and it was conquered at his hands, and he lived long into the days of Umar and after {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.455}.

## Notes for researchers
- al-Isaba fi Tamyiz al-Sahaba, entries 2615-2616 (al-Maktaba al-Asriyya scan, p. 455); the entry lists him among the Successors and reports doubt about his having reached Islam except in al-Rishati's account — his Companionship is unsettled.
- The report of fighting the Asawira in al-Baladhuri stands on a single account and needs corroboration.

## In Suwar Min Hayat al-Sahaba by Abd al-Rahman Rafat al-Basha
[[person/abd-al-rahman-rafat-al-basha|Al-Basha]] opens his chapter on him in [[work/suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba|Suwar Min Hayat al-Sahaba]] with the saying attributed to Umar ibn al-Khattab — "no one has been truer to me since I was made caliph than al-Rabi' ibn Ziyad." He tells that the provinces' delegations came daily to Medina to pledge Umar hearing and obedience, and when the Bahrain delegation arrived with another party of delegations, and Umar bade those present speak and they said nothing worthwhile, he turned to a man in whom he sensed good, gestured to him and said "speak what you have," and the man praised God then said: "Commander of the Faithful, you were only made ruler of this umma as a trial from God, Who tried you with it — fear God in what you have been given, and know that were a single sheep to stray on the bank of the Euphrates you would be asked about it on the Day of Resurrection." Umar burst into tears and said: "no one has been truer to me since I was made caliph than you — who are you?" "Al-Rabi' ibn Ziyad al-Harithi." "Brother of al-Muhajir ibn Ziyad?" "Yes." {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.419} {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.420}
Umar bade Abu Musa al-Ash'ari write him of his affair, and set al-Rabi' in Abu Musa's army with his brother al-Muhajir for the conquest of [[place/manadhir|Manadhir]] in al-Ahwaz; the Muslims were then fighting while fasting Ramadan, and when al-Muhajir saw the slaughter mounting in Muslim ranks he resolved to sell his life seeking God's pleasure — he anointed and shrouded himself and commended his brother — whereupon al-Rabi' went to Abu Musa and said: al-Muhajir has resolved to sell his life while fasting, and the Muslims are worn down by the battle's weight and the fasting's severity yet refuse to break fast — do what you see fit. Abu Musa stood and cried to the army: "Muslims, I adjure every fasters either to break his fast or quit the fighting," and drank from a ewer he carried that the people might drink of his draught; when al-Muhajir heard it he took a draught of water and said: "by God I did not drink it from thirst but to fulfil my commander's oath," then drew his sword and began cutting through the ranks; when he plunged deep into the enemy host they closed on him from every side and their swords passed over him front and back until he fell slain — they cut off his head and set it on a ledge overlooking the battlefield. Al-Rabi' looked at it and said: "blessedness and a goodly end be yours; by God I will surely take vengeance for you and for the Muslim dead, God willing." Seeing what grief al-Rabi' showed for his brother and sensing the grudge he harboured against God's enemies, Abu Musa released him from army command and went on to [[place/al-sus|al-Sus]] to conquer it. {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.420} {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.421} {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.422}
Al-Rabi' and his soldiers then swept down on the polytheists like a whirlwind and poured upon their strongholds; God opened Manadhir to al-Rabi' by force — he killed the fighters, took captives and gained what God willed him to gain — and al-Rabi's star blazed after Manadhir and his name spread on every tongue; when the Muslims resolved to conquer [[place/sijistan|Sijistan]] they entrusted him with the army's command and hoped on his hands for victory. {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.422}
Al-Basha tells that he marched with his army across a seventy-five-farsakh wilderness that the desert's swiftest beasts could scarce cross; the first thing to meet him was Rustaq Zaliq on Sijistan's borders — a thriving district of stately palaces ringed by towering forts. He sent his scouts ahead before arriving and learned the people would soon hold their festival, so he lay in wait and fell on them in the festival night unawares, taking twenty thousand captive and capturing their dehqan; among the captives was a slave of the dehqan's who had gathered three hundred thousand to carry to his master. Al-Rabi' asked where such wealth came from; "from one of my master's villages." "Does a single village give such wealth every year?" "Yes." "How?" "With our foreheads, our shovels and our sweat." {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.422} {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.423} {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.424}
The dehqan offered to ransom himself and his family; al-Rabi' said: I will ransom you if you lavish a ransom on the Muslims. "How much do you want?" "Plant this spear in the ground, then pour gold and silver upon it until it is covered." He agreed, and brought out what his treasuries held of yellow and white and poured it on the spear until it was covered. {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.424}
Al-Rabi' prowled over Sijistan until its cities and villages came out to him seeking aman and submitting before the sword was drawn in their faces, until he reached Zaranj, Sijistan's capital; war raged between him and its enemies, neither side sparing what it demanded of victims. When the first token of Muslim victory appeared, the people's marzban, called Barwiz, resolved to seek al-Rabi's peace while strength remained in him — perhaps to win better terms for himself and his people — and sent him a messenger asking for a meeting to negotiate peace. {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.424} {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.425}
Al-Rabi' ordered his men to heap around the council mounds of Persian slain and to strew other corpses in disorder along the road by which Barwiz would pass; al-Rabi' was tall of stature, mighty of brow, dark-complexioned, massive of frame, striking awe into whoever saw him. When Barwiz entered, his limbs trembled with dread of him and his heart leapt out of him at the sight of the dead; he dared not draw near or advance to grasp his hand, spoke in a stammering tongue, and made peace on terms of a thousand pages for him, a gold cup on each page's head — and al-Rabi' accepted Barwiz's peace on that. {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.425} {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.426}
The next day al-Rabi' entered the city with the pages' procession about him, between the Muslims' cries of "there is no god but God" and "God is greatest" — a day to be witnessed among God's days. Al-Rabi' remained a drawn sword in Muslim hands by which they routed God's enemies, opening cities for them and governing provinces for them until the affair came to the Umayyads and Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan made him governor of Khurasan. {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.426}
Al-Basha tells he was not at ease in that governorship, and that Ziyad ibn Abih — one of the Umayyads' great governors — wrote him a letter saying the Commander of the Faithful Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan commanded him to reserve the "yellow and white" (gold and silver) of war spoils for the Muslims' treasury and divide the rest among the fighters; al-Rabi' wrote back: "I have found God's Book, mighty and glorious, commanding other than what the Commander of the Faithful commanded me by word of mouth" — then cried to the people: "set out for your spoils and take them," and sent the fifth to the caliph's seat in Damascus. {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.426} {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.427}
On the Friday following the letter's arrival al-Rabi' went out to prayer in white garments, preached the Friday sermon to the people, then said: "People, I have grown weary of life, and I call a call — say 'amen' to my call," then said: "O God, if You will good by me, take me to You soon, not late" — and the people said amen to his prayer; the sun of that day had not set before al-Rabi' ibn Ziyad joined his Lord. {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.426} {cite:suwar-min-hayat-al-sahaba-nafais-1992 p.427}
