## Overview
Al-Malik al-Nasir Nasir al-Din Abu al-Ma'ali Hasan was the nineteenth Mamluk sultan of Egypt in the line the chroniclers call the Turkish dynasty. The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls him the most prominent of the eight sons of al-Nasir Muhammad ibn Qalawun who ruled in turn between 741/1340 and 763/1362. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 259} Ibn Taghribirdi counts him as the seventh of that sultan's sons to rule. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/187} He is remembered above all for the great mosque and madrasa that bears his name below the Citadel of Cairo. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1047}

## Birth and name
Ibn Hajar gives his birth year as 735 AH and says that he was first called Qumari. {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 2/38} Ibn Taghribirdi adds that his mother was a concubine who died while he was small, and that a lady of the household, Khawand Urdu, brought him up. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/187} When he was seated on the throne the amirs gave him the name Qumari; the boy objected that his name was Hasan, and the vice-regent Arqutay answered that Hasan was by God's choice. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/187} The sources do not agree on his age: Ibn Taghribirdi says he was eleven at his accession, while Ibn Hajar's date would make him about thirteen. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/187} {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 2/38}

## First reign, 748-752
Hasan was enthroned on 14 Ramadan 748/18 December 1347, after his brother al-Muzaffar Hajji had been overthrown and killed by the senior amirs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1047} A council of regency governed in his name. The Encyclopaedia of Islam names Baybugha Urus, Manjak al-Yusufi and Shaykhu al-Umari as the men who held real power, and Ibn Taghribirdi lists nine amirs of counsel, among them [[person/shaykhu-al-umari|Shaykhu]] and [[person/taz-al-nasiri|Taz]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1047} {cite:nujum-zahira 10/188} The councillors removed the Circassian mamluks whom Hajji had favoured. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/188} The plague of 749/1348 fell in these years and devastated Egypt and Syria. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 260}

On 24 Shawwal 751 Hasan used the absence of Baybugha on pilgrimage and of Shaykhu on a hunt to declare himself of age and take the powers of a sovereign. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1047} Ibn Hajar says he had the vizier seized that day and later had Shaykhu arrested too. {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 2/39} The amirs deposed him in Jumada II 752/August 1351 and installed his older brother [[person/al-salih-salih|al-Salih Salih]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1047}

## Second reign, 755-762
Another revolt of the amirs deposed al-Salih, and Hasan was restored on 2 Shawwal 755/20 October 1354. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1047} Ibn Taghribirdi says that [[person/sarghitmish|Sarghitmish]] and Shaykhu agreed to bring him back; the amirs took him from his confinement in the Citadel, made him accept conditions, and he was sworn in again before the caliph and the judges, keeping the regnal name al-Nasir. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/302} At first he was dominated by Shaykhu and Sarghitmish. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1047}

Shaykhu was struck down by a mamluk in the Dar al-Adl in Shaban 758 and died of the wounds. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/305} Ibn Taghribirdi says his death relieved the sultan of a heavy weight, because Hasan had done nothing without consulting him; the sultan then turned to raising his own mamluks to the rank of amir. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/305} Early in 758 he had already arrested Taz, then governor of Aleppo, on Sarghitmish's urging. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/307} On 20 Ramadan 759/26 August 1358 he arrested Sarghitmish himself, who was said to be plotting his fall; the sultan's mamluks defeated those of Sarghitmish in a day of fighting, and Sarghitmish was sent to Alexandria. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1047} {cite:nujum-zahira 10/308-309}

Ruling alone, Hasan promoted his own mamluks, among them Yalbugha and Taybugha al-Tawil, and also, unusually, the sons of mamluks, the awlad al-nas. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1047} Ibn Taghribirdi says he did this not out of love for them but because he thought them safe and dependent on him. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/309-310} The Encyclopaedia of Islam notes that his preference for the awlad al-nas was exceptional among the sultans. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 814} Documents show his dealings with the Byzantine Empire, the monks of Mount Sinai and Venice. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 260}

## The madrasa
Hasan began his mosque-madrasa in Cairo in 757/1356 according to the Encyclopaedia of Islam, and in 758 according to Ibn Taghribirdi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 260} {cite:nujum-zahira 10/306} It stands on the Rumayla square opposite the Citadel, on the site of the houses of Altunbugha al-Maridani and Yalbugha al-Yahyawi, which he bought and demolished. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/306} Ibn Taghribirdi, writing in the fifteenth century, called it the finest building of its kind in the world. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/306} Ibn Hajar says the sultan died before it was finished. {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 2/39} The building is described in [[building/masjid-sultan-hasan|Mosque-Madrasa of Sultan Hasan]]. A note by the editor of the Nujum, citing al-Sakhawi, says Hasan himself was not buried in it; a descendant was. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/315}

## Character and charity
Ibn Taghribirdi describes him as brave, resolute, generous, with dignity and high ambition, fond of charity and good works. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/315} He adds that Hasan was devoted to women and kept more eunuchs than earlier Turkish sultans, and that he left ten sons and six daughters. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/315} {cite:nujum-zahira 10/316} He credits him with works in Mecca: the door of the Kaaba and the covering that hangs inside it date from his time, and his name is written on the east side of the sanctuary. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/316} Ibn Hajar is harsher: he says the sultan was greedy, took over lands of the treasury and shed much blood. {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 2/39} The same author records that in prison Hasan turned to study and copied al-Bayhaqi's Dala'il al-Nubuwwa in his own hand. {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 2/40}

## His fall
By 762 the mamluk [[person/yalbugha-al-umari|Yalbugha al-Umari]] had become the most powerful of Hasan's men. Ibn Taghribirdi says Yalbugha objected to the large land grants made to women and to the influence of the eunuchs, and that the sultan, hearing of it, decided to seize him. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/311} On Tuesday 8 Jumada I the sultan went out hunting near the pyramids of Giza with his amirs; he moved against Yalbugha, whose men fought back, and Hasan, with few followers, was beaten and fled to the Citadel at night. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/312} Yalbugha followed, and the sultan, dressed as a bedouin, tried to slip away towards Syria with the dawadar Aydamur; mamluks recognised them and brought them to the house of the steward Musa ibn al-Arkushi, who handed them over, and Yalbugha had them killed before sunrise. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/313} The Encyclopaedia of Islam says he was put to death by Yalbugha on 9 Jumada I 762/17 March 1361. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 1047} Another Encyclopaedia article gives 12 Jumada I 763/9 March 1362, and the reasons for the difference are not stated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 259} Ibn Taghribirdi puts his age at a little over thirty and the length of his second reign at six years and seven months. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/313}

Ibn Taghribirdi reflects that the men who killed him were the mamluks he had bought and raised, whereas the old amirs who had earlier deposed him had kept him in honour. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/314} Yalbugha took over the Citadel and the treasury and set up Hasan's nephew al-Mansur Muhammad, son of Hajji. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/314} {cite:nujum-zahira 11/3} Hasan's widow Tulubiya later married Yalbugha. {cite:nujum-zahira 11/6}
