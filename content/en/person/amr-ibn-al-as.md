## Brief
Amr ibn al-As was the shrewdest diplomat of Mecca. Sent to argue on behalf of the polytheists, he embraced Islam in the last hour of the old order, and then became the commander who brought Egypt to Islam.

## Life
A Qurayshi of Banu Sahm, Amr appears in the sources first as Mecca's cunning envoy. He argued at the court of [[person/negus-of-aksum|the Negus]] for the return of the Abyssinia migrants, and failed {cite:ibn-ishaq-guillaume}. Between [[event/treaty-of-hudaybiyya|Hudaybiyya]] and the conquest he read the wind, rode to Medina and embraced Islam around 629–630 CE {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

The Prophet made use of his shrewdness and sent him to Oman. Under Umar he led in Syria; then he asked for permission to conquer Egypt, and it was granted. He took Fustat in 641 CE and Alexandria after it {cite:tabari-donner-1993}. In the first civil war he sided with [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya]], and at the arbitration of Siffin he outmanoeuvred Ali's party, a famous ruse that the sources recount with grudging admiration {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}. He died as governor of Egypt around 663 CE {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

## Legacy
Egypt became Islamic through his hand, and it stayed so. Politically he is the tradition's emblem of the brilliant pragmatist: the man who always landed on the winning side, including, last of all, the caliphate.

## For young visitors
Amr was the cleverest of those sent to argue with the Muslims. Then he joined them, conquered Egypt, and remained its governor for the rest of his life.

## Dhat al-Salasil in Ibn Hisham's Sira
The Prophet sent him to rouse the Arabs for Syria, because his father al-As ibn Wa'il's mother was from Bali. When he feared for the water of al-Salsal in the land of Judham, he asked the Prophet for reinforcements. Abu Ubayda came with the earliest emigrants, and they disputed the command until Abu Ubayda said: "It is yours." So Amr led the people in prayer. See [[event/expedition-of-dhat-al-salasil|the expedition of Dhat al-Salasil]]. {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/272}

## Encyclopaedia of Islam account
The Encyclopaedia of Islam (2nd ed.), in the entry AL-SAHMI by A. J. Wensinck, calls 'Amr one of the most wily politicians of his time. After the failed siege of Medina the more far-sighted Meccans already saw that he was the turning point of Muhammad's career; he converted before the capture of Mecca. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 473}

Sent on a small expedition to 'Uman, he negotiated with its two rulers, Jayfar and 'Abbad ibn Julanda, who accepted Islam; he returned to Medina on news of the Prophet's death. In 12/633 Abu Bakr sent him with an army into Palestine, where he subjected the country west of the Jordan and took part in the battles of Ajnadayn and the Yarmuk and the capture of Damascus. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 473}

His real fame rests on the conquest of Egypt, where he also regulated government, justice and taxation and founded Fustat. Uthman recalled him in favour of Abd Allah ibn Sa'd, and he withdrew in disgust, secretly inciting 'Ali, Talha and al-Zubayr against the caliph. After the Battle of the Camel he joined Mu'awiya and commanded the Syrian cavalry at Siffin, where he devised the ruse of raising leaves of the Qur'an on the lances. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 473}

Before the arbitration he secured Egypt for Mu'awiya, defeating and executing the 'Alid governor Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr early in 38/658. At Adhruh, Abu Musa declared both 'Ali and Mu'awiya unworthy of the office, so 'Ali lost the caliphal title while Mu'awiya lost nothing. 'Amr governed Egypt until his death. On 15 Ramadan 40 (22 January 661) he escaped assassination by chance: he was unwell that day and had left the leading of the prayer to Kharija ibn Hudhafa, who was mortally wounded instead. The assassin is reported to have said: "I meant 'Amr, but God meant Kharija." {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 473}

## For Sprouts
Amr ibn al-As was a clever man of Quraysh who was first an opponent of the Muslims and then accepted Islam before the conquest of Mecca. He later became one of the great commanders of the Muslim armies; he conquered Egypt and founded the city of Fustat and the mosque that carries his name.

## For Explorers
Amr ibn al-As is among the most famous diplomats of Quraysh: they sent him to negotiate for them, and he was in the delegation that tried to persuade the Negus to hand over the emigrants. He accepted Islam shortly before the conquest of Mecca, together with Khalid ibn al-Walid and Uthman ibn Talha, and the Prophet employed him. The Prophet sent him in command to Dhat al-Salasil, and then he was among the commanders of the conquests: he opened Palestine and then led the conquest of Egypt under the caliph Umar, founded the city of Fustat with the Mosque of Amr that still stands, was dismissed and returned as governor, stood with Mu'awiya in the first fitna and was his arbiter at Siffin, and died at Fustat.

## For Scholars
Amr ibn al-As ibn Wa'il al-Sahmi of Quraysh was among the shrewdest political men of his generation. He first appears in the polytheists' camp: the Quraysh sent him with Abdullah ibn Abi Rabi'a to turn the emigrants back from Abyssinia, and the Negus refused them; the sira books record his cunning in negotiations. He accepted Islam in year 8 AH shortly before the conquest of Mecca, with Khalid ibn al-Walid and Uthman ibn Talha, seeing no other way out, and the Prophet sent him to Dhat al-Salasil late in his life. In the conquests he held a front in Syria, then set out for Egypt and took it around 20 AH after the siege of Babylon and the peaceful entry of Alexandria, founding Fustat and its mosque. Uthman dismissed him and he returned; in the first fitna he stood with Mu'awiya as his arbiter at Siffin opposite Abu Musa al-Ash'ari — the sources preserve the affair of raising the masahif there. He governed Egypt under Mu'awiya and died at Fustat around 43 AH; his tomb is there. Historians read him as an example of political ability moving from the opposition camp into state-building.
