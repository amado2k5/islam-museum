# Muhammadu Attahiru I

## Summary

Muhammadu Attahiru I (died 1903) was the twelfth Sultan of the Sokoto Caliphate, reigning from October 1902 until March 1903. He was the last independent Sultan of Sokoto before the Caliphate was taken over by the British. No source consulted records the year of his birth; the English article files him under "Year of birth missing", and the museum does not carry the unsupported year given by Wikidata.{cite:wikipedia-muhammadu-attahiru-i-en}{cite:wikidata-muhammadu-attahiru-i}

## Accession

Attahiru came to the throne on the death of his predecessor, Abdur Rahman Atiku, in October 1902, at a moment when British forces had already taken over parts of the Caliphate. In the last year of his predecessor's reign, General Frederick Lugard had used rivalries between the southern emirs and Sokoto to prevent a coherent defence. As a British-led force approached the city of Sokoto with the clear intention of taking it, Attahiru organised a quick defence and chose to fight the advancing army outside the city. The battle ended quickly in the British favour, their superior firepower causing heavy casualties among his men.{cite:wikipedia-muhammadu-attahiru-i-en}

## The hijra and Mbormi

Attahiru and many followers then fled Sokoto on what he described as a hijra, a withdrawal to prepare for the coming of the Mahdi. The British entered the largely depopulated city and appointed Muhammadu Attahiru II as the new Caliph on 21 March 1903, while Lugard retained the title of Sultan only as a symbolic position in the new Northern Nigeria Protectorate. Attahiru travelled through the rural regions of the Caliphate gathering supporters; the British and the emirs working with them were taken aback by the numbers who joined him, and his force grew to thousands. Marching through Zamfara and Kano, he alarmed the colonial authorities, who attacked his force at the Mbormi battle ground, near present-day Gombe, on 29 July 1903. Attahiru was among those killed. The article's infobox gives August 1903 as his death date; the text's dated account, citing Falola's Historical Dictionary of Nigeria and a Daily Trust report, is the one carried in the data record. The British then displayed photographs of his beheaded body across Northern Nigeria to discourage further resistance. His son, Muhammad Bello bin Attahiru, known as Mai Wurno, led the remaining members of the movement onward and eventually settled in Sudan, where many of their descendants still live.{cite:wikipedia-muhammadu-attahiru-i-en}

## What the sources do not say

The sources consulted describe Attahiru I through his office and his resistance: he is Sultan and Caliph, and his flight is framed as a hijra in expectation of the Mahdi. Neither the English article nor Wikidata names a school, madhhab or branch for him personally, and Wikidata carries no religion value for him at all. His faith is therefore recorded as unknown in the data record: descent in the caliphal line and the caliphal office itself are not attestations of a school.{cite:wikipedia-muhammadu-attahiru-i-en}{cite:wikidata-muhammadu-attahiru-i}
