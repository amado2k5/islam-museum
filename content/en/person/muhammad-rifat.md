# Muhammad Rifat

## Summary

Muhammad bin Mahmud Rif'at (9 May 1882 – 9 May 1950) was an Egyptian Qur'an reciter, the first to recite the Qur'an on Egyptian Radio when it began broadcasting in 1934. Blinded in early childhood, he became reciter of Cairo's Fadil Pasha Mosque in 1918 and held the post, out of loyalty to the mosque where his career began, until a throat illness ended his public recitation in 1943. His admirers called him Qitharat al-Sama, the "Harp of Heaven".{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-en}{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-ar}

## Early life and education

He was born on Monday 9 May 1882 at Darb al-Aghawat in the al-Maghreblin quarter of Cairo, and lost his sight at about the age of two, the English article says from conjunctivitis. His father Mahmud Rif'at, a police officer in the Gammaliya district, enrolled him at five in the Bushtak kuttab attached to Fadil Pasha Mosque in Sayyida Zeinab, where his first teacher, Muhammad Hamida, took him through the memorisation of the Qur'an and a body of hadith over six years. He then studied the science of the recitations and tajwid for two years under Abd al-Fattah al-Hinidi, holder of the highest isnad of his time, and received his ijaza.{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-en}{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-ar}

His father died when he was nine, leaving him the sole provider for his mother, aunt, sister and brother. Plans to send him to Al-Azhar were dropped, and from the age of fourteen he recited at gatherings in Cairo and then in the provinces.{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-ar}

## Fadil Pasha Mosque and Egyptian Radio

In 1918 he became reciter of the sura at Fadil Pasha Mosque — the Arabic article notes the anomaly that he was described as appointed at fifteen, an error for the fame he had already won as a boy — and crowds came to hear him, among them Prime Minister Mustafa el-Nahas and King Farouk. He recited there until his retirement, refusing to leave the mosque that had witnessed his beginnings, and in summer he recited at the Abu al-Abbas al-Mursi Mosque in Alexandria.{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-en}{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-ar}

On 31 May 1934 Egyptian Radio opened its broadcasts with his recitation, from the opening of Surat al-Fath, after he had sought and received a fatwa from the Shaykh al-Azhar Muhammad al-Ahmadi al-Zawahiri that broadcasting the Qur'an was permissible. When the BBC Arabic Service asked him to record, he first refused, thinking it impermissible because they were not Muslims; the Grand Imam Muhammad Mustafa al-Maraghi explained that it was not forbidden, and he recorded Surat Maryam for them. The Paris and London radios competed for him, the Arabic article adds. Egyptian Radio broadcast his live recitations twice daily except Sundays, and contracted with him from its first day.{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-en}{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-ar}

## Voice and character

The sources dwell on the character of his voice: beautiful, with an unrepeatable stamp, rising from a quiet opening to a full, controlled height, exact in the articulation of the letters so that the meaning would reach the listener's heart. He reached, it was said, more than three thousand listeners in open places without amplification, and moved between recitations and maqams without strain. He guarded his voice, avoiding colds, spiced food, smoking and evening meals.{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-ar}

The Arabic article's Personality section describes him as generous to the poor, weeping during recitation, and — in its words — ascetic, of Sufi inclination and Naqshbandi in tariqa, preferring the company of the simple poor to that of the rich; it tells how he once chose to recite at his poor neighbour's occasion over the anniversary of King Fuad's death. The English article's Personal life section likewise describes him as a follower of the Naqshbandi Sufi tradition.{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-ar}{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-en}

Of the judgements passed on him: al-Sha'rawi said that for the rules of recitation there is Al-Hussary, for sweetness of voice Abdul Basit, for long breath with sweetness Mustafa Ismail, "and for all of these together, Muhammad Rifat". The composer Mohammed Abdel Wahab called his voice angelic, come from heaven for the first time, and the writer Muhammad al-Sayyid al-Muwaylihi called him "master of the reciters of this age, a musician by nature".{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-ar}

## Preservation of his recitations

Most of his live broadcasts were not recorded by Egyptian Radio. Almost his whole surviving legacy comes from one admirer, Zakariyya Pasha Mahran, a notable of Qusiya in Asyut and a member of the Egyptian Senate, who loved him without ever meeting him and bought two gramophones from Germany to record the broadcasts. When Rifat fell ill, Mahran took a disc to the radio and asked for a lifelong pension for him; the radio assigned ten pounds a month, but Rifat died before receiving it. After Mahran's death his widow gave the discs to Rifat's heirs, and the family has spent decades restoring them, donating thirty hours of his voice to the radio without charge, with some thirty further hours still unreleased. In December 2025 Al-Azhar announced an initiative to restore one hundred rare, never-broadcast discs of his recitation.{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-ar}{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-en}

When his recordings ran out at the radio, the Egyptian station would call on Abu al-Aynayn Shuaysha, whose voice closely resembled his, to fill the gaps; and the Palestinian reciter Muhammad Rashad al-Sharif, reciter of Al-Aqsa Mosque, was formed by his recitation — Rifat heard him in 1943 and wrote to him in 1944 calling him "the second Muhammad Rifat".{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-ar}

## Family

He married Zaynab, from the village of Al-Far'uniyya in Monufia, and had four children: Muhammad (born 1909), who accompanied him as secretary and manager; Ahmad (born 1911), who followed his path, memorised the Qur'an, studied the recitations and took his ijaza in them; Bahiyya (born 1914); and Husayn (born 1920), who read books aloud to his blind father.{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-en}{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-ar}

## Illness and death

In 1943 a hiccup-like affliction seized his throat — believed to be a tumour, thought to be throat cancer — cutting off his recitation. He spent what he had on treatment until he was poor, but would not ask anyone for help, and declined a public subscription of some fifty thousand pounds raised for his treatment, with his famous words: "A reciter of the Qur'an must not be humiliated." He died on Tuesday 9 May 1950, his sixty-eighth birthday, and was buried, as he had wished, on land granted beside the Sayyida Nafisa Mosque in Cairo, where he used to go every Monday to recite before his prepared tomb. Egyptian Radio announced him to its listeners as "a banner of the banners of Islam".{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-ar}{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-en}

## Faith record

The museum records him as sufi, documented. Two independent article texts attest his personal affiliation to the Naqshbandi Sufi order: the English article's Personal life section states that he is described as a follower of the Naqshbandi Sufi tradition, citing Ahram Online, and the Arabic article's Personality section describes him in its own words as of Sufi inclination, Naqshbandi in tariqa, citing an interview with his granddaughter. Sunni stands only in the two infoboxes, one structured tradition, so no sunni segment is recorded, and no madhhab is named in any text.{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-en}{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-rifat-ar}
