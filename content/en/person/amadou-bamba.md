## Overview

Amadou Bamba (1853 – 19 July 1927), whose full name was Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn Habiballah and whom his followers call Khadim al-Rasul, "Servant of the Messenger", and Serigne Touba, "Sheikh of Touba", was a Senegalese Sufi sheikh, the founder of the Mouride Brotherhood (the Muridiyya) and of the city of Touba.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en}{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr} The Arabic article calls him one of the prominent figures of Sufi Islam in West Africa through that founding.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-ar} He taught that salvation comes through complete submission to God and hard work, and he led a pacifist spiritual struggle against French colonialism rather than a war.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en} The French exiled him to Gabon and Mauritania and held him under house arrest for fifteen years, yet his brotherhood grew into one of the largest in Africa, with an estimated following of more than three million and an annual pilgrimage, the Magal, that draws millions to Touba.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en}

## Early life and the founding of the Muridiyya

He was born around 1853 — the English article dates it to the outskirts of 1270 AH — at Mbacké in the kingdom of Baol, the second son of Maam Mor Anta Saly Mbacke and Maam Diaara Bousso.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en} His father was a marabout of the Qadiriyya, the oldest Sufi order in Senegal, and qadi to kings, and the young Bamba is said to have pressed him to give up a judgeship that obliged him to render rulings inspired by power rather than by Islamic law.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en} The French article follows his studies: entrusted at seven to his uncle Thierno Mboussobé for the Qur'an, then to Tafsir Mbacké Ndoumbé, then to his father's own school, where he versified al-Sanusi's theological manual Umm al-Barahin as Mawahib al-Quddus, a poem of some six hundred verses that his father adopted into the school's programme.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr} His father died at Mbacké Cayor in 1881, and after two years teaching his father's disciples Bamba announced that he would no longer educate them by study alone but by spiritual training, tarbiya: those who wanted only study could go to the country's many masters, and those who wanted to draw near to God should stay — the French article calls this the birth of the Mouridiyya.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr} The English article dates the brotherhood's founding to 1883.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en} He founded Touba in 1887 according to the English, Arabic and Urdu articles, in 1888 according to the French article; in his Matlabul Fawzeyni he described the city's purpose as reconciling the spiritual and the temporal.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en}{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-ar}{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr}

## Exile and the greater jihad

As his following grew, the French colonial administration feared his power and, in its own later-quoted justification, could hold against him no act of preaching holy war, only a suspect attitude and suspect disciples.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr} Arrested on the road at Djéwol, he was brought before the Privy Council at Saint-Louis on 5 September 1895; his disciples testify that he prayed two rak'as in the Governor's office and declared his intention to be subject to God alone, a testimony the English article notes is not in the colonial archives.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en}{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr} He was deported to Gabon, where he remained seven years (the English article: seven years and nine months), at Mayumba and Lambaréné, and returned to Dakar on 11 November 1902 to crowds who had thought him dead.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr}{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en} Arrested again, he was exiled to Mauritania from 1903 to 1907, in the keeping of the Moorish scholar Sidia Baba, then held under surveillance at Thiéyène and, from January 1912, at Diourbel.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr} In all, the English article reckons thirty-two years of exile and restriction.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en} He taught instead what he called the jihad al-akbar, the greater struggle, fought not through weapons but through learning and the fear of God, and his brotherhood built a large economic organisation on his teaching of work, settling young followers on marginal lands in eastern Senegal to found communities and groundnut plantations whose proceeds partly returned to Touba.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en} The French, concluding he did not seek war, moved to work with him; in January 1919 the governor awarded him the Legion of Honor for his followers' enlistment in the First World War — the English article says he refused it, while the French article says he accepted the diploma but refused to wear the cross, since his religious principles forbade it.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en}{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr} In 1921 he first publicly organised the anniversary of his departure into exile and told the Mourides to mark it yearly with worship, Qur'an reading and feeding people — the origin of the Magal.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr} In 1926 he began work on the Great Mosque of Touba.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en}

## Death and succession

He died at his home in Diourbel on 19 July 1927 and was buried the next day at Touba, in his house beside the Great Mosque, at a site he had chosen; his tomb became a place of pilgrimage.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr}{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en} His eldest son, Mouhamadou Moustapha Mbacké, succeeded him at the head of the brotherhood, whose leaders since have been his descendants, holding absolute authority over the followers.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr}{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en}

## Writings

Bamba was a prolific author in Arabic, and most of his manuscripts are held at the library of the Great Mosque of Touba; his biographer counted some thousands of writings, with forty-one works commonly published in Senegal.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en}{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr} The French article names Masalik al-Jinan (The Itineraries of Paradise), a treatise on Sufism, among his greatest books, alongside the Tazawwud works for the young, Jawhar al-Nafis on jurisprudence, Mawahib al-Quddus on theology, and Munawwir al-Sudur and Maghaliq al-Niran on the purification of the heart.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr} The English article lists in addition qasidas in honour of the Prophet, among them Muqaddimat al-Amdah and Jazb al-Qulub.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en}

## Positions and criticism

This section reports what the sources attribute to Bamba and how they assess him; it is not the museum's own judgement.

**His confession.** The French article describes him in its lead as "a Sunni Muslim, an Ash'ari theologian, a Maliki jurist, founder of his own Sufi path", and its doctrine section quotes the opening of his Mawahib al-Quddus on God's attributes and calls his belief traditional Sunni Islam, framed by fiqh.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr} No other fetched source names a school of law or theology for him, and the museum accordingly records his faith as sufi — on the three articles' attestation of his founding and heading the Muridiyya — and states the French description here without adopting it as a second attestation.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en}{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-ar}

**Believers' claims.** His third caliph and son, Abdoul Ahad Mbacke, declared that Bamba had met the Prophet in dreams, and that during Ramadan 1895 the Prophet and his companions appeared to him at Touba and conferred on him the rank of mujaddid, renewer of his age, and of "Servant of the Prophet" — a tale the English article presents as an article of faith for Mouride believers.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en} His followers likewise call him a mujaddid and hold that salvation comes through submission to the sheikh and hard work.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en}

**The colonial record.** The administration's files, as quoted in the French article, admitted no provable act of war-preaching against him even as they deported him, and the English article stresses that his alleged crime was persisting in preaching Islam; the same administration later decorated him and sought his followers' labour and enlistment, a reversal both articles record.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-fr}{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en} His single surviving photograph from 1913, in a white robe with his face mostly covered by a scarf, is venerated and reproduced on walls, buses and taxis across Senegal.{cite:source/wikipedia-amadou-bamba-en}
