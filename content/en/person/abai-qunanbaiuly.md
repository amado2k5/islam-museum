# Abai Qunanbaiuly

## Summary
Abai Qunanbaiuly (10 August 1845 – 6 July 1904) was a Kazakh poet, composer, philosopher and translator, the founder of written Kazakh literature and its first classic. Born Ibrahim, the son of the clan chief Qunanbai, he was schooled in a madrasa and in a Russian school at Semipalatinsk, drew Kazakh poetry out of its oral tradition, translated Pushkin, Lermontov, Goethe, Byron and Krylov into Kazakh, and left in The Book of Words a prose testament urging his people toward education, literacy and moral character. {cite:wikipedia-abai-en} {cite:wikipedia-abai-ru}

## Origins and education
He was born at Karauyl village in the Chingiz volost of Semipalatinsk uyezd, the son of Qunanbai and of Uljan, Qunanbai's second wife; the family was Muslim and named him Ibrahim. He first studied at a local madrasa under Mullah Ahmed Ryza, where he received the nickname "Abai" — "careful" — which remained with him for life. His father's wealth then sent him to a Russian secondary school in Semipalatinsk, where he read Lermontov and Pushkin and the Eastern classics, including the Shahnameh and the Thousand and One Nights. {cite:wikipedia-abai-en}

## Work
Before Abai, Kazakh poetry was almost entirely oral, echoing the nomadic life of the steppe; his verse, rooted in Kazakh folk culture and carrying a new national consciousness, fixed it in writing. He composed songs as well as poems, and he translated into Kazakh — mostly for the first time — works by Pushkin (including Eugene Onegin), Lermontov, Goethe, Byron and Krylov. His major work, The Book of Words (Qara sözder), is a theological and philosophical treatise in forty-five "words" of edification, calling his fellow Kazakhs to embrace education, literacy and good character to escape poverty, enslavement and corruption; in Word Twenty Five he argues the importance of Russian culture for his people. The American explorer George Kennan, visiting Semipalatinsk in 1885, was struck by Kazakhs borrowing books from its public library and mentioned Abai in Siberia and the Exile System, among the earliest Western references to him. {cite:wikipedia-abai-en}

## Legacy
Abai died at Zhidebay on 6 July 1904 (23 June, Old Style). The leaders of the Alash Orda movement a decade later claimed him as their inspiration and spiritual predecessor, and his nephew and student Shakarim Qudayberdiuli carried his line forward. Today his name is borne by Kazakhstan's national pedagogical university, by the Abai Region, and by streets, theatres and schools across the country; his statues stand from Almaty to Moscow, Tehran, Cairo and Berlin, UNESCO marked his 150th anniversary in 1995 as the Year of Abai, and his 175th anniversary was celebrated nationwide in 2020. {cite:wikipedia-abai-en} {cite:wikipedia-abai-ru}
