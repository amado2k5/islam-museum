## Summary

Zeb-un-Nissa (15 February 1638 – 26 May 1702) was a Mughal princess, the eldest child of Emperor Aurangzeb and his chief consort Dilras Banu Begum {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-en} {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-ar}. She was a poet who wrote under the pen name Makhfi, "the Hidden One", and her writings were collected after her death as the Diwan-i-Makhfi {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-en}. The sources divide on her death year — the articles and Wikidata give 1702, while other sources noted in the English article give 1701 — and the record preserves both {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-en} {cite:wikidata-zeb-un-nissa}.

## Education and patronage

Aurangzeb charged Hafiza Mariam with her education, and she memorised the Quran in three years, becoming a hafiza at the age of seven, an occasion her father marked with a feast, a public holiday and gifts of gold {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-en} {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-ur}. She then studied philosophy, mathematics, astronomy and literature with the Persian poet Ashraf Mazandarani, and became a mistress of Persian, Arabic and Urdu with a reputation in calligraphy {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-en} {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-ur}. Her library surpassed every other private collection of her time, and she employed scholars on liberal salaries to write works at her bidding and to copy manuscripts for her {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-en}. Under her patronage the scholar Safi-ud-din Ardabili translated a great Arabic commentary on the Quran into Persian and named it Zeb-ul-Tafasir after her {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-en} {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-ar}. She supported widows and orphans and each year sent pilgrims to Mecca and Medina at her own expense {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-en}.

## Poetry and imprisonment

Zeb-un-Nissa never married, though she was betrothed in childhood to her cousin Sulaiman Shikoh and had many suitors {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-en}. Her verse, in the Indian school of Persian poetry and under the influence of Hafez, is steeped in the Sufi language of divine love, and a later account links her fall to her Sufi sympathies {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-en}. That colouring is a matter of poetry and inclination; no source read attests an initiation, a discipleship or an order membership for her, and her faith is recorded accordingly {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-ur}. In 1681 her correspondence with her rebellious brother Muhammad Akbar was discovered, her wealth and pension were confiscated, and Aurangzeb imprisoned her in Salimgarh Fort at Delhi {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-en}. She remained a prisoner for twenty years and died there on 26 May 1702; in 1724 her scattered poems were gathered as the Diwan-i-Makhfi, four hundred and twenty-one ghazals and several ruba'is {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-en} {cite:wikipedia-zeb-un-nissa-ar}.
