## Overview
Nur al-Din Mahmud, son of [[person/imad-al-din-zangi|Zangi]], was the second son and successor in Syria of the Zangid ruler murdered in 541/1146, and the head of the [[polity/zengid-dynasty|Zangid]] house for nearly three decades. From [[place/aleppo|Aleppo]] he gathered much of Syria into a single federation, took [[place/damascus|Damascus]] in 549/1154, pursued the jihad against the Franks, sent forces to Egypt, and in 566/1171 took over [[place/mosul|Mosul]] and the Jazira. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose account this section follows, gives him the title al-Malik al-Adil and describes his policy as the building of a united Syria. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 138} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 139}

## The inheritance of 541/1146
When his father was killed during the siege of Qal'at Ja'bar in Rabi' I 541/September 1146, there were four heirs. The eldest, [[person/sayf-al-din-ghazi-i|Sayf al-Din Ghazi]], represented his father at Mosul; Nur al-Din, the second son, had accompanied his father on most of his campaigns; the third, Nusrat al-Din, was to govern Harran; and the fourth, [[person/qutb-al-din-mawdud|Qutb al-Din Mawdud]], was to follow the eldest at Mosul. Nur al-Din went to Aleppo on the advice of [[person/asad-al-din-shirkuh|Shirkuh]], the Kurdish amir and friend of his father. The governor of the city accepted Zangid rule, Hama rallied to him, and at Mosul the pro-Zangid amirs brought Sayf al-Din Ghazi from Kurdistan and had him appointed ruler. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 138}

Raymond of Poitiers, prince of Antioch, learned of the murder seven days after Nur al-Din was established in Aleppo and sent two forces, one against Aleppo and one against [[place/hama|Hama]]; the Muslims made them withdraw to [[place/antioch|Antioch]]. In [[place/al-ruha|Edessa]], the Armenian majority called on Joscelin, but Nur al-Din arrived first with siege machinery after six days of forced marches, the Armenians left, and Edessa was added to his domain. Relations with his elder brother grew strained until Nur al-Din sent an act of homage recognising Sayf al-Din's primacy, and in return he obtained guarantees for his eastern frontier, where Harran replaced Edessa, together with charge of the [[concept/jihad|jihad]] against the enemy from the west. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 138} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 139}

## The aim of Damascus
Reviving his father's policy, Nur al-Din meant to bring Damascus into a Syrian federation. The article gives political reasons, the kingdom of Jerusalem to the south, and economic ones: cut off from the Jazira, Syria needed the Biqa' and the Hawran for grain. In the spring of 541/1147 he and Mu'in al-Din Unur of Damascus confronted the Franks together in the Hawran. In July 1148 the Franks mustered at Tiberias and came before Damascus; Mu'in al-Din appealed to Mosul and Aleppo, used the Zangid threat to push the Franks back, and they raised the [[event/siege-of-damascus-1148|siege]] on 28 July. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 139}

In 1149 Nur al-Din besieged Inab and, on 20 Safar 544/29 June 1149, defeated the Franks at 'Ard al-Hatim, as the [[event/battle-of-inab-544|battle of Inab]] is described here, then took Afamiya, Qal'at al-Mudiq and Harim. When Unur died on 23 Rabi' II 544/28 August 1149 and Mujir al-Din Aybak took charge in Damascus, Nur al-Din camped southwest of the city in 544/1150 and declared that he had come to protect the Damascenes from their Frankish allies, but his supporters were too few and he went back to Aleppo after Joscelin was captured by Turkmen. Over the next years he strengthened his northern position, moved the frontier of the Dar al-Islam from the Euphrates to the Orontes, and in 1151 and 1152 pressed Damascus again, taking Tartus in 1152. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 139}

## Damascus in 549/1154
Mujir al-Din preferred a Frankish protectorate to Zangid ascendancy. According to the article, Nur al-Din's agents conducted propaganda in the city, he cut off the food supplies arriving from the south so that prices rose, and he dealt with the heads of the [[concept/ahdath|ahdath]] and with the recruited lower ranks of the markets. When his troops entered, the middle classes barricaded their homes and the crowd rioted, but within hours he restored order, distributed provisions and promised to respect the people. Mujir al-Din, isolated in the citadel, accepted Homs in exchange for surrendering. Nur al-Din then met the notables, among them Najm al-Din [[person/najm-al-din-ayyub|Ayyub]], the judges and the jurists, and announced the abolition of the taxes on the markets. The amir [[person/usama-ibn-munqidh|Usama ibn Munqidh]], who had left the city ten years earlier, returned in Rabi' II 549/June 1154. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 139}

The article reports that in eight years he reached by stages the aim of a united Syria. It says he revived, with greater vigour, his father's measures against the Shi'a at Aleppo, and judges that the imposition of Sunni Islam was one of the major objectives of his policy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 139}

## Wars with the Franks and the Byzantine factor
After Damascus he settled the revolt of the amir of Ba'labakk in 1155 and aided his brother-in-law in a Seljuk-Danishmendid dispute. In 1157 his troops defeated the Franks at Baniyas, and he later set an ambush near the Jisr Banat Ya'qub and took King Baldwin's men prisoner. He fell gravely ill at Sarmin in Ramadan 552/October 1157 and left instructions for the succession; recovered, he occupied Shayzar, so that the whole course of the Orontes lay in Zangid hands. He fell ill again in Damascus at the end of 553/early 1159, and then named his brother Qutb al-Din Mawdud of Mosul as his successor. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 140}

In 554/1159 a Franco-Byzantine coalition threatened Aleppo. He called his amirs to holy war, but agreed with the emperor Manuel in Safar 554/May 1159, promising support against Kilij Arslan II of Rum. The article judges that fear of a [[polity/byzantine-empire|Byzantine]] intervention long prevented him from exploiting his successes against the Franks. Afterwards he took territory from the [[polity/rum-seljuk-sultanate|Seljuks of Rum]] in the north, including Marash and Ra'ban. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 140} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 141}

## Pilgrimage, defeat and a changed style of rule
In 556/1161 he made the [[practice/hajj|pilgrimage]] with Shirkuh, was generous to the people of the holy cities and improved the wells; at [[place/medina|Medina]] he restored the defences and had a second wall with towers built. In May 558/1163, camped in the Bukay'a plain, he was surprised in his siesta by Frankish cavalry, routed, and did not halt until he reached the lake of Qadesh. The article judges that this defeat profoundly affected his personality and policy: he embraced a life of piety and religious observance, which earned him the respect of the religious classes and the public, though the amirs accepted it with reservations. He assigned iqta's to the orphans of fighters and paid subsidies from the public treasury to scholars, Sufis and Qur'an readers. Inscriptions after 560/1165 give him the titles "Helper of the Truth by means of proofs" and "Protector of the Oppressed against the Oppressors", which the article reads as part of a programme of presenting himself as champion of the disadvantaged. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 141}

On 20 Ramadan 559/11 August 1164 he beat the Franks near Artah by withdrawal and counter-attack, and Harim fell the next day; he then took Baniyas. In 561/1165 he freed Bohemond III for a ransom of 100,000 gold pieces, avoiding anything that might anger the Byzantine emperor. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 142}

## Egypt
When the vizier [[person/shawar|Shawar]] came to him in 559/1164 and offered a third of Egypt's revenues and recognition of his sovereignty, Nur al-Din sent Shirkuh, with a diversion at Baniyas to cover the march. Back in power, Shawar reneged on his promises. After Shirkuh's second campaign, Nur al-Din rewarded him with the fief of Homs. In Muharram 564/October 1168 the Franks attacked Cairo, and Shawar warned the caliph [[person/al-adid-fatimid|al-Adid]] that appealing to Nur al-Din was the only hope, since Sunnis were preferable to a Christian protectorate. A third expedition went out under Shirkuh; when he died on 22 Jumada II 564/23 March 1169, his nephew [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Salah al-Din]] became vizier under al-Adid and commander of the Syrian forces under Nur al-Din. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 141} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 142}

After the repulse of the Franks at Damietta, al-Adid asked that the Syrian reinforcements be recalled. The article says Nur al-Din seemed worried by Salah al-Din's attitude and ambitions, and sent Najm al-Din Ayyub to remind his son that the war against the infidels was the first duty and that the Abbasid khutba must be adopted in Cairo. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 142}

## Mosul and the Jazira
On 1 Muharram 566/14 September 1170 he took [[place/raqqa|Raqqa]], then laid siege to Sinjar, and on 13 Jumada I 566/22 January 1171 entered Mosul, confident, the article says, of the support of the caliph and of the people of Mosul. He suppressed the market taxes and other abuses, applied to the Jazira the regime in force in Syria and Egypt, and confirmed his nephew Sayf al-Din Ghazi in Mosul. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 142}

## Nur al-Din and Salah al-Din
Until 567/1171-2 the relations were those of chief and subordinate; afterwards they came into conflict over how to fight the Franks, which the article calls a clash between two generations and two temperaments, Turkish and Kurdish. Citing Gibb, it says that for Nur al-Din Syria was the main field of battle against the Crusaders and Egypt only a source of revenue for the jihad. When Salah al-Din, ordered to besiege [[place/al-karak|al-Karak]], withdrew to Cairo with the excuse of unrest in Egypt, Nur al-Din announced he would depose him, then relented when Salah al-Din reaffirmed his loyalty. In 568/1173 the same thing happened again, Salah al-Din citing his father's illness. The article judges that through this gesture the kingdom of Jerusalem gained a reprieve of forty years, and that Nur al-Din never saw in [[building/al-aqsa-mosque|al-Aqsa]] the minbar he had had made in Aleppo. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 142} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 143}

## Investiture and death
Nur al-Din sent his adviser al-Shahrazuri to Baghdad to ask the caliph for a document conferring all the territories he held; in granting it, the article says, the caliph removed the successors of the Great Seljuks from any authority west of the Euphrates. Preparing an expedition to Egypt, he fell ill after the feast of Fitr and died on Wednesday 11 Shawwal 569/15 May 1174, according to Ibn al-Athir attended by two physicians. He was first buried in the citadel, and later moved to the funerary [[building/nur-al-din-madrasa-damascus|madrasa]] being built southwest of the [[building/umayyad-mosque|Umayyad Mosque]]. The article notes that the tomb is still venerated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 143}

## Early notices from Arabic histories
Nur al-Din Mahmud ibn Zangi, lord of Aleppo and then of Syria. In 543 AH, together with his brother Ghazi, lord of Mosul, he lifted the Frankish siege of Damascus, and he kept up the fight against the Franks. He took Damascus in Safar 549 AH and took several fortresses and strongholds. The caliph al-Muqtafi sent him a commission to govern Egypt and gave him the title "al-Malik al-Adil" {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.673-675}.

## Death and legacy
He died in 569 AH while lord of Damascus, and his son al-Malik al-Salih Isma'il succeeded him as a boy. The Franks then moved against the coasts, and were pacified with money {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.684}.

The author of "Shadharat al-Dhahab" describes him as the finest, most just, most pious and most active in jihad of the kings of his time. He says that he conquered more than fifty fortresses in Frankish lands, abolished unlawful taxes, and built schools, the wall of Damascus, houses for orphans, hospices and a hospital, and a house of justice {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.708-709}.

He is also reported to have been approached by a man who made a claim against him while he was playing polo. He went to the judge's session, and when the man's claim could not be proven, he gave him the thing he had claimed. Ibn al-Athir is quoted as saying that after the rightly guided caliphs and Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz he had not seen a ruler of better conduct {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.708-709}.

## For Sprouts

## For Scholars
Nur al-Din Mahmud ibn Zangi, lord of Aleppo then Syria. With his brother Ghazi, lord of Mosul, he lifted the Franks' siege of Damascus in 543 AH and kept fighting them; he took Damascus in Safar 549 AH and several castles and strongholds, and the caliph al-Muqtafi sent him a writ for the governorship of Egypt styling him al-Malik al-Adil. He died in 569 AH still lord of Damascus, succeeded by his son al-Malik al-Salih Isma'il, a boy — the Franks moved on the coasts and were bought off with money. The Shadharat al-Dhahab calls him the greatest, most just, most devout and most striving of the kings of his age, and records that he opened more than fifty of the Franks' forts, dropped the tolls, and built schools, Damascus's wall, orphanages, ribats, the hospital and the house of justice. It relates that a man claimed against him while he played polo and he went before the judge; when no claim stood for the man, the king made him a gift. And it quotes Ibn al-Athir: after the Rightly-Guided caliphs and Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz he saw no king of better conduct than Nur al-Din.

## In the 'Al-Mubdi'un' programme (part 1 of 2)
Tareq al-Suwaidan presents Nur al-Din Zengi as 'the builder of Islamic unity', declaring — in his judgement — greater than Salah al-Din: after the rightly-guided caliphs and Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz only Yusuf ibn Tashfin rivals him {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:12:30}.
He sketches the context: an ummah in darkness torn by princes' ambitions and Crusader occupiers, conditions worse than today's — yet hope rose {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:04:00}.
Of his father: Imad al-Din Zengi stretched his rule from Mosul to Nisibis and Harran, spent seven years fighting Muslim princes before turning to the Crusaders, took al-Ruha in 538 AH and annihilated three quarters of the army that attacked him — the first clear Muslim victory — then the Assassins murdered him on 6 Rabi' II 541 AH {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:09:00}.
Nur al-Din was born 17 Shawwal 511 AH, Imad al-Din's second son; the brothers split the state without quarrel — Sayf al-Din Ghazi settling in Mosul, Nur al-Din alone in Aleppo — Sayf the man of politics, Nur al-Din the lover of jihad {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:10:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:11:30}.
He describes him: hard upon Islam's enemies yet innately tender and fine of feeling, so he became beloved of the masses across the ummah, not only in Aleppo; and his field of creativity was jihad {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:14:30}.
Of his campaigns: he attacked the principality of Antioch taking its dependent fortresses, and destroyed Joscelin II's attempt to retake al-Ruha — the Crusaders' second defeat harsher than the first — the first news of victory spreading through the ummah after years of defeats {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:16:00}.
Of his uniting of the ranks: he struck a treaty with Mu'in al-Din Unur of Damascus in 541 AH and married his daughter, and when the Crusaders betrayed their ally Unur and threatened Damascus, Nur al-Din marched out and stood before them, taking Busra and Sarkhad before they could fall, then immediately left Damascus to reassure its ruler {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:18:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:19:00}.
In 542 AH the Second Crusade under Louis VII and Conrad III failed before Damascus through the unity of the armies of Damascus, Aleppo and Mosul; and in 544 AH he defeated Raymond of Poitiers, prince of Antioch, in a battle that annihilated the Crusaders and killed Raymond — the first decisive reversal of the Crusader tide since the loss of Palestine — when he was about twenty-nine {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:20:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:20:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:21:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:21:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:22:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:22:30}.
Unur's successor Abaq continued the alliance until humiliation reached the point that Damascus' ruler agreed to pay the Crusaders a yearly tribute in return for protecting the city from Nur al-Din {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:24:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep18 00:24:30}.

## In the 'Al-Mubdi'un' programme (part 2)
Al-Suwaidan continues: Nur al-Din united Syria in 549 AH when Damascus' scholars and notables wrote asking him to annex it and expel Mujir al-Din Abaq, under whom the Crusaders levied tribute on Muslims — 'when the ummah senses a sincere ruler it surrenders to him' (the speaker's remark) — so the Islamic front equalled the Crusader one from al-Ruha to Hawran {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:04:30}.
Then the race for Egypt: the Crusaders took Ascalon signalling invasion of the tottering Fatimid state, so Nur al-Din launched continuous raids in Syria to occupy them, and sent Asad al-Din Shirkuh with Salah al-Din to ally Egypt against them — five years of campaigns from 559 AH {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:08:00}.
The battle of al-Buqay'a 558 AH: taken by surprise he was defeated and escaped at the last moment, and said his famous words 'by God I will not shade under a roof until I take my revenge and Islam's' — when they offered peace he refused, then met them at Harim where ten thousand were killed and ten thousand captured {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:10:00}.
In Egypt: Shirkuh became vizier to al-Adid, the last Fatimid caliph, and when al-Adid betrayed him secretly negotiating with the Crusaders, Salah al-Din killed him in his palace and Egypt was declared subject to Nur al-Din; Shirkuh died two months later and Nur al-Din made Salah al-Din his governor — completing the united front from the Euphrates to the Nile {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:11:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:12:00}.
Of his conduct: he fought the Crusaders not as Christians but as foreign occupiers — 'he harmed no Christian in his lands, demolished no church, injured no priest or monk' (from the episode) {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:15:30}.
Of his faith: his saying 'had I a thousand horsemen I would never shade under a wall', and at Harim he was seen on a hill prostrating, smearing his face with dust, crying 'O God, grant victory to Your religion, not to Mahmud — who is Mahmud that he should triumph?' {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:17:30}.
He led the armies fighting at their head, and mourned that martyrdom was not granted him despite seeking it repeatedly {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:18:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:18:30}.
Of his civilisational building: hundreds of madrasas and mosques across Syria with generous endowments devoted to Qur'an and hadith — until he himself became a muhaddith licensed to transmit — free hospitals for the poor in every town, traveller khans on the roads, the House of Justice in Damascus, and the Nuri hospital and Nuri hadith college built with a captured Frankish king's three-hundred-thousand-dinar ransom {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:20:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:21:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:22:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:22:30}.
Of his reforms: he abolished 'hayya ala khayr al-amal' the Fatimids had added to the adhan, banned wine throughout his lands, lifted uncanonical taxes, and cared for the zakat {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:20:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:21:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:21:30}.
He fell ill preparing the march on Egypt toward Palestine and died 11 Shawwal 569 AH at fifty-nine; the whole Muslim world shook — and the speaker draws the lesson 'whenever a leader leaves the field another rises', Salah al-Din following his path {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:22:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:23:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:23:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep19 00:24:00}.
