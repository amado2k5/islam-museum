## Overview
Abu Hamid Muhammad ibn Muhammad al-Ghazali al-Shafi'i, titled "the Proof of Islam", is the author of [[work/ihya-ulum-al-din|Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din]] {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p.221}. He was born in [[place/tus|Tus]] in 450 AH (1058 CE); his father spun wool and sold it, and on his deathbed entrusted him and his brother Ahmad to a Sufi friend {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.491} {cite:britannica-al-ghazali}. He then travelled to [[place/nishapur|Nishapur]] and stayed with [[person/imam-al-haramayn-al-juwayni|Imam al-Haramayn]], and was appointed to teach at the Nizamiyya of [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] in 484 AH {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.491} {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.492}. He then renounced the world and turned to worship and wandering: he left for the Hijaz in 488 AH and settled in [[place/damascus|Damascus]] for ten years, writing books there, the Ihya' reportedly among them {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.492}. He then visited [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]] and [[place/alexandria|Alexandria]] and returned to Tus, taught for a time at the Nizamiyya of Nishapur, then left it and returned home, and built nearby a lodge for Sufis and a school for students {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.492}. He died on 14 Jumada al-Akhira 505 AH at al-Tabaran in the lands of Tus {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.490}.

He says of himself that the thirst to grasp the realities of things was his habit from the start, until the bond of imitation fell away from him while he was still close to boyhood {cite:mustasfa-hafiz-v1 p.2}. Among his works listed by Ibn al-Imad are [[work/al-mustasfa|al-Mustasfa]] on legal theory, [[work/tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahafut al-Falasifa]] and [[work/al-munqidh-min-al-dalal|al-Munqidh min al-Dalal]] {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.493}. Hajji Khalifa relates from [[person/al-nawawi|al-Nawawi]] that if the books of Islam were lost and the Ihya' remained, it would suffice for what was lost {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p.221}.

## His life
He was born in Tus in 450 AH. His father spun wool and sold it, and on his deathbed he entrusted him and his brother Ahmad to a Sufi friend. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.491-492}

He moved to Nishapur and attended the lessons of the Imam al-Haramayn, and was appointed to teach at the Nizamiyya in Baghdad in 484 AH. He then left the world and turned to worship and wandering, and went out to the Hijaz in 488 AH. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.491-492}

He stayed in Damascus for ten years and wrote books there, and it is said that the "Ihya" was among them. He then visited Jerusalem and Alexandria and returned to Tus. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.491-492}

He died on the fourteenth of Jumada al-Akhira 505 AH at al-Tabaran in the lands of Tus. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p.491-492}

Among his works, in the museum's records, are [[work/iljam-al-awam|Iljam al-Awamm]] and [[work/al-iqtisad-fil-itiqad|al-Iqtisad fi al-I'tiqad]]. {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan}

## The life according to the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, describes al-Ghazali as an outstanding theologian, jurist, original thinker, mystic and religious reformer, and notes an old discussion over whether his name ending should be read Ghazali or Ghazzali, preferring the first {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1087}
It places his birth at [[place/tus|Tus]] in Khurasan, near modern Meshhed, in 450 AH/1058 CE. He and his brother [[person/ahmad-al-ghazali|Ahmad]] were orphaned young, and their schooling began in Tus. Al-Ghazali then went to Jurjan and, after another period in Tus, to [[place/nishapur|Nishapur]], where he studied under [[person/imam-al-haramayn-al-juwayni|al-Juwayni, Imam al-Haramayn]] until the latter's death in 478 AH/1085 CE. Other teachers are named, mostly obscure, the best known being Abu Ali al-Farmadhi {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1087}
From Nishapur in 478 AH/1085 CE he went to the camp of [[person/nizam-al-mulk|Nizam al-Mulk]], which had drawn many scholars, and was received there with honour {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1087} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088}
At a date he does not give, which cannot be much later than his move to [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] and may be earlier, he passed through a phase of scepticism and came out of it to search for a more satisfying intellectual position and way of life. In 484 AH/1091 CE Nizam al-Mulk sent him as professor to the [[building/al-madrasa-al-nizamiyya-baghdad|Nizamiyya of Baghdad]], the madrasa he had founded. Al-Ghazali became one of the most prominent men of the city and for four years lectured to more than three hundred students, while also pursuing philosophy by private reading and writing several books {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088}

## The break of 488 AH and the years away from teaching
In 488 AH/1095 CE al-Ghazali fell ill with a nervous disorder that made lecturing physically impossible. After some months he left Baghdad on the pretext of making the pilgrimage, while in fact giving up his chair and his whole career as jurist and theologian {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088}
The article reports that the motives for this renunciation have been debated from his own time to the present. He himself says he feared that he was going to Hell, and he voices many criticisms of the corruption of the scholars of his day, for instance in the first volume of the Ihya'. The article therefore suggests that he may have felt the organised legal profession so corrupt that an upright life, as he conceived it, required leaving it altogether {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088}
It also mentions two other proposals and weighs them. F. Jabre proposed that he chiefly feared the Ismailis, who had murdered Nizam al-Mulk in 485 AH/1092 CE; the article holds that this stresses what can at most have been one factor. D. B. Macdonald proposed that political events made him anxious: shortly before he left Baghdad the Seljuk sultan [[person/barkyaruq|Barkiyaruq]] had executed his uncle Tutush, who had been supported by the caliph and presumably by al-Ghazali, and al-Ghazali returned to teaching soon after Barkiyaruq's death in 498 AH/1105 CE {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088}
The article corrects a common figure. Some early biographical notices say that he spent ten of the eleven years between leaving Baghdad and resuming teaching in Syria, but a careful reading of the Munqidh and of small details in other sources makes it certain, the article says, that he was in Syria only about two years {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088}
His route was as follows. He left Baghdad in Dhu al-Qa'da 488 AH/November 1095 CE, spent time in [[place/damascus|Damascus]], then went by [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]] and Hebron to [[place/medina|Medina]] and [[place/mecca|Mecca]] for the pilgrimage of 489 AH/November-December 1096 CE. He returned briefly to Damascus. He is reported seen in Baghdad in Jumada II 490 AH/May-June 1097 CE, which the article reads as a brief stay on the way home to Tus. A report that he visited [[place/alexandria|Alexandria]] is, the article says, now mostly rejected by scholars, and if he went to Egypt it can only have been briefly {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088}
In these years in Damascus and Tus he lived as a poor Sufi, often alone, given to meditation and spiritual exercises. The article says he composed his greatest work, the [[work/ihya-ulum-al-din|Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din]], in this period and may have lectured on it to select audiences, and that by the end of it he had advanced far along the mystic path and was convinced it was the highest way of life for a person {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089}

## The return to teaching and the last years
In 499 AH/1105-6 CE Fakhr al-Mulk, son of Nizam al-Mulk and vizier of Sanjar, the Seljuk ruler of Khurasan, pressed him to return to academic work. He yielded, the article says, partly from a belief that he was destined to be the reviver of religion at the start of the new century, in line with a well-known Tradition. In Dhu al-Qa'da 499 AH/July-August 1106 CE he began lecturing at the Nizamiyya of Nishapur and not long afterwards wrote his account of his religious development, [[work/al-munqidh-min-al-dalal|al-Munqidh min al-Dalal]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089}
Before his death he again gave up teaching and withdrew to Tus in Jumada II 505 AH/December 1111 CE. There he had set up, probably before going to Nishapur, a khanqah or hermitage where he trained young disciples in the theory and practice of the Sufi path, and several of his pupils there are known by name {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089}

## Authenticity and esoteric teaching
The article calls the study of his thought difficult because many works have been attributed to him that he did not write. P. Bouyges's chronology lists 404 titles, many known only from lists of his works with no surviving copy, while in other cases one book circulates under several titles, so much manuscript work remains. Since at least Ibn al-Arabi, who died in 638 AH/1240 CE, it has been alleged that books were falsely attributed to al-Ghazali. The works whose authenticity is doubted mostly express advanced Sufi and philosophical views that differ from his teaching in the works generally accepted as authentic {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089}
[[person/ibn-tufayl|Ibn Tufayl]], who died in 581 AH/1185 CE, drew attention to contradictions and suggested that al-Ghazali wrote differently for ordinary people and for an elite, that is, that he held esoteric views not shared with everyone. The article answers that, even if he taught on different levels, there is no reason to think the higher levels directly contradicted the lower. It also sets aside the idea that he took up extreme philosophical Sufism late in life, because [[work/iljam-al-awam|Iljam al-Awamm]], which takes a position like that of the Ihya', was finished only days before his death. Its recommended method is to concentrate on the main works of undoubted authenticity and to accept others only where their views do not conflict with those {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1088}

## The works, by field
The Munqidh was written a year or two before his death. The article describes it as an account of how his religious opinions developed, arranged by theme and not by date, and not an autobiography; most of the life details above come from it. In it he also defends himself against accusations about his conduct and views. A small work answering criticisms of the Ihya' is the Imla' {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089}
Law. His training was in jurisprudence, and the article judges that he probably gave special attention to kalam only under al-Juwayni. His earliest writings include the Basit and the Wasit; the Wajiz is dated 495 AH/1101 CE; and [[work/al-mustasfa|al-Mustasfa]], on the sources of law, was written in 503 AH/1109 CE while he taught at Nishapur. The article says it shows the influence of his earlier philosophical studies yet lies wholly within the juristic tradition. Biographical notices report that at his death he was deepening his knowledge of Tradition {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089}
Philosophy and logic. After the sceptical phase described in the Munqidh he made a thorough study of philosophy, to which al-Juwayni had introduced him, mainly the Arabic Neoplatonism of [[person/al-farabi|al-Farabi]] and [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]]. His final aim was to show where their doctrines were incompatible with Sunni Islam, but he first wrote an uncritical exposition, [[work/maqasid-al-falasifa|Maqasid al-Falasifa]], which the article says was much appreciated in Spain and the rest of Europe in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. He followed it with [[work/tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahafut al-Falasifa]], finished at the beginning of 488 AH/1095 CE. In it he notes twenty points on which the philosophers' views are objectionable to Sunnis or inconsistent with their own claims, and on three of them he judges them to be unbelievers. The book exposes inconsistencies and argues for no positive views of his own, which led some to accuse him of remaining something of a sceptic. The article answers that the Tahafut was written just before the crisis that made him leave Baghdad, so he may then have been unsure of his positive beliefs, whereas when he wrote the Ihya' he was in no doubt {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089}
Logic impressed him most among the philosophical disciplines, especially the Aristotelian syllogism. For Sunni jurists and theologians, to whom philosophical books were hard to reach or understand, he wrote two books on Aristotelian logic, Mi'yar al-Ilm and Mihakk al-Nazar. [[work/al-qistas-al-mustaqim|al-Qistas al-Mustaqim]] justifies the use of this logic in religion and was apparently written for simpler believers drawn to Batini, that is Ismaili, teachings. His ethics work, Mizan al-Amal, comes from his period of enthusiasm for philosophy; whether the whole extant text is his has been questioned, and the article thinks he may have rejected much of it as he grew critical of philosophical ethics {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089}
Dogmatic theology. His chief work is [[work/al-iqtisad-fil-itiqad|al-Iqtisad fi al-I'tiqad]], probably composed shortly before or after he left Baghdad. It follows roughly the Irshad of al-Juwayni but makes full use of Aristotelian logic. The article agrees with [[person/ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]] that this makes him the founder of a new tendency in theology, though it finds no striking novelty in his doctrines. In his later Arba'in he says the Iqtisad prepares for the Sufi's gnosis better than the usual dogmatic works. The article concludes from this that he never ceased to be an Ash'ari in dogmatics, while coming to hold that religious discussion should range far beyond dogmatics and that detailed dogmatic argument had no practical value {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089}
[[work/faysal-al-tafriqa|Faysal al-Tafriqa]] is partly aimed at the Batiniyya but is mainly a defence of his views on how far interpretation, ta'wil, is justified and on the relative places of tawatur and ijma' as sources of religious knowledge. Iljam al-Awamm, which appears to be his last work, warns the little educated of the dangers of studying kalam {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089}
Polemics. The Mustazhiri, edited in abridged form by Goldziher, is a searching theological critique of the Nizari Ismailis. A Persian work edited by O. Pretzl attacks the antinomianism of certain mystics. The authenticity of an anti-Christian polemic, al-Radd al-Jamil, is doubted by Bouyges and defended by Louis Massignon {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089}
Sufi practice. The Ihya' is, the article says, his greatest work in size and importance, in four volumes. It has four quarters, on acts of worship, social customs, vices that lead to perdition, and virtues that lead to salvation, each with ten books, and the first two books set out the necessary minimum of intellectual knowledge. The article calls it a complete guide to every aspect of the religious life: worship, daily conduct, purification of the heart and progress along the mystic way. It arises from his feeling that in the hands of the scholars of his day religious knowledge had become a means of worldly advancement, whereas he was convinced it served to attain salvation in the world to come. While describing the prescriptions of the Shari'a in some detail, he therefore tries to show how they contribute to salvation {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1089} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1090}
Related works. [[work/bidayat-al-hidaya|Bidayat al-Hidaya]] is a brief rule of daily life for the devout, with counsel on avoiding sins. Kitab al-Arba'in is a short summary of the Ihya', though its forty sections do not fully match the forty books. al-Maqsad al-Asna discusses in what sense people may imitate the names or attributes of God. Kimiya' al-Sa'ada is mainly a Persian abridgement of the Ihya', also translated wholly or partly into Urdu, Arabic and other languages, with differences not yet fully studied {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1090}
Sufi theory. Most cases of false or doubtful attribution fall here. [[work/mishkat-al-anwar|Mishkat al-Anwar]] is genuine, except possibly its last section. The authenticity of al-Risala al-Laduniyya, on the nature of knowledge of divine things, has been doubted because of its closeness to a work of Ibn al-Arabi and its Neoplatonism, and Minhaj al-Abidin is the most important of many others in this category. The article adds that a false attribution, if proved, would not destroy their value as illustrations of Sufi thought of his time and the following half-century {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1090}

## Assessments of his influence
The article says a balanced account of his influence will need much more study of the religious movements of later centuries, so its assessments are provisional. It offers five {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1090}
First, his criticism of the Batiniyya may have lowered the movement's intellectual appeal, but its comparative failure after capturing Alamut had many other causes. Second, the eastern Islamic world produced no further great philosophers after his criticism of the philosophers, yet it is unclear how far this was due to him, since philosophy continued in the West, where the Tahafut was also known. Third, his studies led to the incorporation of aspects of philosophy, above all logic, into Islamic theology, so that theologians in time gave far more space to philosophical preliminaries than to theology proper {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1090}
Fourth, he did a great service to devout Muslims of every level of education by presenting obedience to the Shari'a as a meaningful way of life. His khanqah at Tus, where he lived with his disciples, was not unlike a Christian monastery, and he may have given an impetus to the movement from which the dervish orders came, though this needs further investigation. Fifth, his example may have encouraged forms of Sufism close to or wholly within Sunnism, although the article notes that there was much more Sufism among Sunni scholars before him than is commonly realised, and his influence on the Sufi movement as a whole needs further study {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1090}

## In the 'Al-Mubdi'un' programme (part 1 — formation)
Tareq al-Suwaidan chooses Abu Hamid al-Ghazali as the programme's closing figure 'not because he is the greatest but because he is unique, gathering many fields' — modelling the maker-quartets on him {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:01:30}.
The son his father prayed for: born 450 AH — of the five factors in his making: a pious illiterate father who spun cloth in Tus, sat weeping in the jurists' circles at his own ignorance, and prayed with a sincere heart for a son who would be a jurist and a preacher — answered with Abu Hamid and his preacher brother Ahmad {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:05:30}.
The father died with his sons still small, entrusting them to a Sufi friend — 'this is my inheritance: educate my sons with my money, spend it all' — and the Sufi, having spent it, seeing their keenness, advised them to a school that maintains its students {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:07:30}.
The travel factor: among them a lesson from a highwayman — who looted the caravan including al-Ghazali's satchel of notes; begged to return them, the robber laughed 'how do you call it your knowledge when you do not know? if the notes are lost your knowledge is lost' and threw it back; al-Ghazali said 'a man God made speak to enlighten me about my affair', went home and memorized all he had written over three years — memorization becoming his method {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:10:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:10:30}.
Of his teachers: al-Radhkani in fiqh at Tus, Abu Nasr al-Isma'ili at Jurjan, then Nishapur's Nizamiyya — among the schools of Nizam al-Mulk, 'the greatest minister in the nation's history' (the speaker's view) — where he clung to Imam al-Haramayn al-Juwayni, who would leave him teaching in his place and call him 'a drowning sea' {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:14:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:15:30}.
He left Nishapur at al-Juwayni's death aged twenty-eight, debated in Nizam al-Mulk's assembly until opponents conceded, and was appointed to Baghdad's Nizamiyya — 'the world's greatest university' — at thirty-four {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:16:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:18:00}.
Of his quest for truth: he studied the four contending groups — philosophers, theologians, Batinis, Sufis — until he had absorbed and answered each, recording it in al-Munqidh min al-Dalal; emerging 'with a heavy dose of doubt' for six months, then the decision to leave Baghdad on subsistence money {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:20:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:20:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:21:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:21:30}.
The seclusion: two years in Damascus at the Umayyad Mosque's western minaret, then Jerusalem shutting the Dome's door on himself — where he began Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din — then Hajj in 489 AH, and ten years of retreat {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:22:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:22:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:23:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:23:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:24:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep28 00:24:30}.

## In the 'Al-Mubdi'un' programme (part 2 — crisis and return)
Al-Suwaidan narrates the turning in al-Ghazali's own words: he read al-Makki's Qut al-Qulub and the works of al-Muhasibi and al-Junayd — 'I knew with certainty they are masters of states, not masters of sayings' — and 'what could be acquired by study I acquired; what remained cannot come by hearing or teaching, only by taste and wayfaring' {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:02:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:03:30}.
His reckoning with himself: he examined his deeds and found the best of them teaching, then probed its motive — 'my intention was not pure for God but sought rank and fame; I knew I was on the brink of a collapsing bank' — and six months between 'the appetites of the world dragging me by their chains' and 'the herald of faith crying: depart, depart' {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:07:30}.
From choice to compulsion: 'God sealed my tongue until it was bound from teaching' — he strove to lecture a single day and could not utter a word; 'I resorted to God with the resort of the desperate, and He who answers the desperate answered me' {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:10:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:11:00}.
Eleven years of seclusion — ten between Syria, Jerusalem, Mecca and Medina and one in Tus — neither teaching nor learning, only contemplating his self: 'that is why none has spoken of the soul's depths like al-Ghazali — he studied his own for eleven years' (the speaker's conclusion) {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:12:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:13:00}.
The return: Fakhr al-Mulk Ali ibn Nizam al-Mulk pressed him, and he taught at Nishapur's Nizamiyya two years until Fakhr al-Mulk's assassination, then retired to Tus building a lodge for students and a khanqah for Sufis, leaving it only in death {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:14:30}.
Of his traits: bearer of a systematic call since youth; he united — rarely joined — Sufi spirituality and purity of worship with scientific method, freedom of opinion and courage; and he led the intellectual stand against the Isma'ili-Batini Fatimid bid — who assassinated Nizam al-Mulk and his son — writing Fada'ih al-Batiniyya at Caliph al-Mustazhir's commission in 487 AH, Tahafut al-Falasifa, and al-Munqidh min al-Dalal {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:18:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:20:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:21:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:21:30}.
His farthest aim: to be 'the renewer of religion in the fifth century' — visible in the very title Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:19:00}.
His method of mind: doubt everything from zero, then intuition and contemplation — 'certain knowledge is that in which the known is unveiled so no doubt remains' — doubting even sense-data and axioms until he reached sincere faith {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:22:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:23:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:23:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep29 00:24:00}.

## In the introduction to the edition of al-Mustasfa

Hamza ibn Zuhayr Hafiz, editor of [[work/al-mustasfa|al-Mustasfa]], opens his edition with al-Ghazali's words that the thirst to grasp the realities of things was his habit from the start, an instinct and nature placed in him not by his own choice, until the bond of imitation fell away and inherited beliefs broke for him while he was still close to boyhood. {cite:mustasfa-hafiz-v1 p.2} The editor quotes Ibn al-Jawzi's Kitab al-Thabat inda al-Mamat, from al-Ghazali's brother Ahmad: on Monday at dawn he performed ablution and prayed, asked for his shroud, kissed it and put it to his eyes, saying "hearing and obeying, to enter upon the King", then stretched out his legs, faced the qibla and died before daybreak, on 14 Jumada al-Akhira 505 AH. {cite:mustasfa-hafiz-v1 p.57} He wrote al-Mustasfa after returning to teaching, as he says in its preface. {cite:mustasfa-hafiz-v1 p.71}
