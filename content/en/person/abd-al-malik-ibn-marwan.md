## Overview
Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan, an Umayyad caliph whose governor was al-Hajjaj, and who died in 86 AH. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.6 p.338} {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.6 p.418}

## Origins and early years in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The article on Abd al-Malik in the second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam, by H. A. R. Gibb, counts him as the fifth caliph of the Umayyad line, reigning 65-86/685-705. By general report he was born in 26/646-7, the son of [[person/marwan-ibn-al-hakam|Marwan ibn al-Hakam]] and of Aisha bint Muawiya ibn al-Mughira. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 95}

The article says that at the age of ten he witnessed the storming of the house of [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]], and that at sixteen [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Muawiya]] appointed him to command the troops of [[place/medina|Medina]] against the Byzantines. He stayed in the city until the revolt against [[person/yazid-ibn-muawiya|Yazid I]] in 62-3/682-3. When the rebels expelled the Umayyads he left, but on meeting the Syrian army under [[person/muslim-ibn-uqba-al-murri|Muslim ibn Uqba]] he turned back and gave Muslim information about the town and its defences. The battle of [[event/waqat-al-harra|al-Harra]] followed, and the Medinans were wholly defeated on 27 Dhu l-Hijja 63/27 August 683. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 95}

## Taking up the caliphate in 65/685
According to the article, after the assassination of his father in Ramadan 65/April-May 685, Abd al-Malik was recognised as caliph by the Umayyad partisans but faced serious difficulties. The battle of [[event/battle-of-marj-rahit|Marj Rahit]] had reaffirmed Umayyad control of [[place/syria|Syria]], and Egypt had been recovered and was firmly held by his brother [[person/abd-al-aziz-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Aziz ibn Marwan]]. Yet [[person/zufar-ibn-al-harith-al-kilabi|Zufar ibn al-Harith]] held out in the north at Qarqisiya with Kays support until 71/690-1, and the [[polity/byzantine-empire|Byzantines]] pressed the frontiers, reoccupying [[place/antioch|Antioch]] in 68/688 and aiding the Mardaites inside Syria. At [[place/mecca|Mecca]], [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr]] had been proclaimed caliph and was at least nominally recognised in most provinces. The author judges that Abd al-Malik showed himself equal to the task and that within a few years he restored the unity of the Arabs under Syrian leadership. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 95}

## Iraq, the truce and the fall of the Zubayrids
The article reports that [[place/iraq|Iraq]] and the East had at first to be abandoned. [[person/ubayd-allah-ibn-ziyad|Ubayd Allah ibn Ziyad]], driven out after the death of Yazid, defeated a Kufan force in [[place/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] in Ramadan 65/May 685 but could not reoccupy [[place/kufa|Kufa]] or [[place/basra|Basra]]. Kufa was seized by the Shiite leader [[person/al-mukhtar-ibn-abi-ubayd|al-Mukhtar]], whose partisans, after an indecisive clash with the Syrians in Dhu l-Hijja 66/July 686, defeated Ubayd Allah on the Khazir river the following month under Ibrahim ibn al-Ashtar. For the next five years Iraq stayed under [[person/musab-ibn-al-zubayr|Musab ibn al-Zubayr]], whose general [[person/al-muhallab-ibn-abi-sufra|al-Muhallab ibn Abi Sufra]], with the Basran troops, defeated al-Mukhtar's forces at Harura in Ramadan 67/April 687 and reoccupied Kufa.

To free his hands for Iraq, Abd al-Malik made a ten-year truce with the Greek emperor in 69/689, under which the emperor, in return for an annual tribute, removed the Mardaites from Syria into Greek territory. He then set out from Damascus against Musab but had to return to face a revolt in the capital led by his kinsman [[person/amr-ibn-said-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn Said al-Ashdaq]]. Amr fortified himself in the residence and surrendered on a promise of life and liberty when the caliph arrived. The article says Abd al-Malik could not trust him and soon had him seized and executed, according to the general statement with his own hand. The campaign against Musab was renewed in 70/690, with the two armies facing each other in Mesopotamia without result. In the third year Abd al-Malik besieged Zufar at Qarqisiya for some months, then reoccupied Upper Mesopotamia and, reinforced by the Kays, marched into Iraq. At [[event/battle-of-dayr-al-jathaliq|Dayr al-Jathaliq]], near Maskin, Musab and Ibn al-Ashtar were defeated and killed in Jumada I or II 72/October-November 691. Al-Muhallab was occupied with the Kharijites, and most Iraqis, the article says, were weary of a conflict that had brought them little but hardship and loss. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 95}

Immediately after the caliph entered Kufa and received the homage of the province, a force of 2000 Syrians was sent under [[person/al-hajjaj-ibn-yusuf|al-Hajjaj]] against Ibn al-Zubayr at Mecca. After a halt at [[place/taif|Taif]], al-Hajjaj laid siege to Mecca on 1 Dhu l-Qada 72/25 March 692, and a little more than six months later Ibn al-Zubayr was killed in the field and the city surrendered, on 17 Jumada I or II 73/4 October or 3 November 692. Al-Hajjaj was rewarded with the governorship of the Hijaz. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 95} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 96}

## The Kharijites, al-Hajjaj and the revolt of Ibn al-Ashath
The recovery of Iraq, in the article's account, obliged Abd al-Malik to take measures against the [[group/khawarij|Kharijites]]. After an initial failure, the combined forces of Kufa and Basra defeated the Najdiyya of Yamama at Mushahhar in 73/692-3, but the [[group/azariqa|Azariqa]] in Persia posed a harder problem. Even under al-Muhallab the war-weary fighters showed little appetite for it until Abd al-Malik moved al-Hajjaj to the government of Kufa in 75/694. With his backing al-Muhallab hunted down the Azariqa in a campaign of three years. Meanwhile a fresh Kharijite rising among the Rabia tribesmen of Mesopotamia, under Shabib, swept down on the territory of Kufa and seized Mada'in in 76-7/695-6. Al-Hajjaj obtained 4000 Syrian troops, who drove off the attackers and killed Shabib at the end of 77, or the beginning of 697, and went on to break up the Arab section of the Azariqa in Tabaristan. After disorder in [[place/khurasan|Khurasan]] in 78/697, Abd al-Malik added that province to al-Hajjaj's government, and al-Hajjaj named al-Muhallab as his deputy. Al-Muhallab reopened the campaigns towards Central Asia, though the article records few positive gains before his death in 82/701-2, when his son Yazid succeeded him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 96}

The article continues that [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-muhammad-ibn-al-ashath|Abd al-Rahman ibn Muhammad ibn al-Ashath]], who had been appointed to [[place/sijistan|Sijistan]], was campaigning in Afghanistan with the troops of Kufa and Basra. Angered by criticism from the viceroy, the nobles revolted in 81/700-1 and marched back into Iraq. The small Syrian force and its supporters could not withstand the united forces of the province, and for a time the situation was critical. With reinforcements from Syria the rebels were beaten at [[event/battle-of-dayr-al-jamajim|Dayr al-Jamajim]] in Jumada II 82/July 701 and again at Maskin on the Dujayl in Shaban 82/October 701, and the remnants were pursued into Sijistan and Khurasan, where [[person/yazid-ibn-al-muhallab|Yazid ibn al-Muhallab]] dispersed them in 83/702. In the same year al-Hajjaj built a new garrison city for the Syrian troops at [[place/wasit|Wasit]]. The author calls this episode a turning-point in the history of the caliphate and the Arab empire: from then on a permanent Syrian army held Iraq, and the fighters of Kufa and Basra were never again called out on a war footing. For twelve further years, he says, al-Hajjaj's firm rule kept order and security and laid the foundations of later economic prosperity in Iraq, at the cost of much bitter resentment among the tribesmen, especially in Kufa. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 96}

## The frontiers
The article reports that war with the Byzantines was renewed in 73/692 because the emperor refused to accept the new Muslim gold currency struck by Abd al-Malik. Despite early successes in raids into Anatolia and Armenia, the Syrian troops under the caliph's brother Muhammad gained little territory, though they prepared the way for the expeditions of the next reign. In North Africa the fighters of Egypt under [[person/hassan-ibn-al-numan|Hassan ibn al-Numan]], having regained southern Ifriqiya, advanced on [[place/carthage|Carthage]] with naval support. A Greek relief fleet was defeated, Carthage was occupied, and a secure base was set up at [[place/qayrawan|Qayrawan]] for further conquests. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 96}

## Centralisation, coinage, the Quranic text and the Dome of the Rock
In the midst of internal conflict and foreign war, the article says, Abd al-Malik found time to develop the administrative efficiency of his empire. The answer to the disintegrating pull of tribalism was centralisation. The most important measure was the substitution of Arabic for Greek and Persian in the financial offices, a first step towards reorganising and unifying the differing provincial tax systems and towards a more definitely Muslim administration. The author sees the same direction in the decision to issue an Islamic gold coinage, replacing the Byzantine denarius with its imperial image by a Muslim dinar bearing Quranic texts. He holds that, whatever hostility later tradition showed towards the Umayyads and al-Hajjaj in particular, the influence of Islam was strongly felt in this first generation of rulers raised in the faith from childhood. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 96}

Another far-reaching measure, in the article, was a new edition of the Uthmanic text of the Quran with vowel-marking, generally attributed to al-Hajjaj, which angered the pietists of Kufa who held to the reading of Ibn Masud. The article also names Abd al-Malik as the builder of the [[building/dome-of-the-rock|Dome of the Rock]] at Jerusalem. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 96}

## The succession and his death
The article describes the last years of the reign as a time of prosperity and peaceful consolidation, troubled only by anxiety over the succession. Marwan had named Abd al-Malik's brother Abd al-Aziz as his successor, but Abd al-Malik wished to exclude him in favour of his own sons [[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|al-Walid]] and [[person/sulayman-ibn-abd-al-malik|Sulayman]]. A split was avoided only just, by the death of Abd al-Aziz in Egypt in Jumada I 86/May 705, five months before the death of Abd al-Malik in Shawwal 86/October 705. He was succeeded by his eldest son al-Walid. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 96}

## Accession and death
According to al-Mas'udi, he was given the oath of allegiance on the night of Sunday, the first of Ramadan 65 AH, and he died in Damascus on Saturday, fourteen days into Shawwal 86 AH, aged sixty-six. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.86-87} Al-Salabi says that al-Walid led the funeral prayer and that he was buried between the Jabiya Gate and the Small Gate. {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 p.115-128}

## His character as al-Mas'udi describes it
Al-Mas'udi says that he loved poetry, boasting and panegyric, that stinginess predominated in him, and that he was bold in shedding blood. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.86-87}

## Al-Hajjaj and Ibn al-Ash'ath
Al-Tabari reports that the followers of Ibn al-Ash'ath deposed him, saying that deposing al-Hajjaj meant deposing Abd al-Malik. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.6 p.338} Al-Mas'udi reports that he wrote to al-Hajjaj asking him to describe the rebellion to him, and that when news of Ibn al-Ash'ath's deposition reached him, he went up the pulpit and prayed that God would give the sword of the people of al-Sham power over the people of Iraq. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.111}

## The killing of Amr ibn Sa'id al-Ashdaq
Al-Mas'udi reports that Amr ibn Sa'id al-Ashdaq was killed in 70 AH after correspondence between them, each seeking power, and that the reports differ on how he was killed. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.95-97}

## His sermon in Medina
Al-Mas'udi reports that when he went on pilgrimage he ordered the distribution of gifts, and a purse was brought out marked as alms. The people of Medina refused to accept it, because their allowances came from the fay' (property taken without battle). He then addressed them, reminding them of the governorship of Umar and Uthman and of the day of al-Harra. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.113}

## In his administration
Al-Salabi says that he was the first to set aside a day for hearing complaints of injustice, that he fixed the dowry at four hundred dinars as an upper limit, and that he upheld the judgements of Abdallah ibn al-Zubayr and did not overturn them. {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 p.27-28} He would instruct his commanders to beware of night raids and to send scouts ahead of every army, and he consulted people before sending an army to the land of the Romans. {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 p.115-128}

## For Sprouts
Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan was an Umayyad caliph who ruled about twenty years in the seventh century. He reunited the caliphate after years of civil war, built the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem, and made the coinage and records Arabic. He died in 705 CE.

## For Explorers
Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan, fifth Umayyad caliph (65–86 AH / 685–705 CE), inherited a broken caliphate: he retook Iraq and the Hijaz after Ibn al-Zubayr's defeat by al-Hajjaj in 73 AH and reunited the empire a second time. His reign built the state: he Arabized the government registers from Persian and Greek, struck the first imageless Islamic coins and then the famous gold dinar around 77 AH, built the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem in 72 AH, and created the postal and road systems. His strongest governor was al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf in Iraq and the east, who founded Wasit in his day. Abd al-Malik died in 86 AH (705 CE) and was succeeded by his son al-Walid.

## For Scholars
Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan ibn al-Hakam (65–86 AH / 685–705 CE), fifth Umayyad caliph and founder of the dynasty's second state. He came to power after Marwan ibn al-Hakam's death with the caliphate torn: Ibn al-Zubayr in Mecca, Kharijites and Shia in Iraq, the Romans on the front. He retook Iraq with Mus'ab ibn al-Zubayr killed in 71 AH, then al-Hajjaj took Mecca in 73 AH and the caliphate was reunited; al-Hajjaj was made governor of Iraq and the east, founding Wasit and crushing revolts by force. His policies made the model state: Arabization of the registers — records switched from Persian and Greek to Arabic — striking the imageless Islamic dinar and dirham around 77 AH, building the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem 69–72 AH which historians read as both a rival sanctuary to Ibn al-Zubayr's and a proclamation of Islam, and organizing the post, caravans and the administration that led to his son al-Walid's era of peak conquests. He died in 86 AH; historians treat his reign as the foundation of the Marwanid state.

## Epigraphic dinar standard: reform, not invention of money
[[invention/abd-al-malik-epigraphic-dinar|The epigraphic dinar standard]] credits Abd al-Malik with commissioning a design/weight change, not inventing money or personally cutting every die. MWNF describes British Museum1874.7-6.1, dated77AH/696–7, comparing4.25g with the Byzantine4.55g standard; Damascus is a probable mint, not a name inscribed on the piece {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton}. Byzantine/Sasanian precedents and the transitional standing-caliph type prevent reducing the history to an empty space followed by one invention {cite:tdv-abdulmelik-yildiz} {cite:ashmolean-standing-caliph}. This administrative attribution does not replace the political and personal assessments reported above. A monetary reform does not erase accounts of violence or disagreements in the biography's sources.

## Narrative dates and specimen dates differ
Suyuti transmits al-Askari's papyrus/threat/consultation story and gives75AH for coinage; this is not turned into a contemporary decree or sole explanation for every type {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.359}. MWNF describes a76AH reform alongside its77AH object: both dates remain attributed to their respective statements {cite:mwnf-coin-77ah-shovelton}. Met99.35.2386 is catalogued79AH/698–99, gold, Syria,2.1cm, Durkee1898bequest, not the British first-issue specimen {cite:met-dinar-453447}. Its photograph shows one epigraphic face, not an independent reading of the other side's date or measurement of purity/weight. Quranic wording in a source or image is not transcribed.

## Individual identity evidence and interpretive limits
Suyuti356 reports personal worship and jurisprudence in Medina before accession; TDV describes his own Quran, hadith and fiqh studies. These are individual biographical grounds, not an inference from name or ancestry {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.356} {cite:tdv-abdulmelik-yildiz}. The recorded Sunni frame remains the museum's frame, not membership in a later sectarian institution. For transitional coinage the Ashmolean notes recent research questioning a simple response-to-Justinian explanation. Preserving limits on that causal story makes attribution more precise without denying political commissioning {cite:ashmolean-standing-caliph}.

## Arabic fiscal registers: commissioning is not translation
The [[invention/arabic-fiscal-register-reform|fiscal-register language contribution]] differs from dinars and tiraz: Baladhuri/Hitti reports Abd al-Malik's81AHcommission and Sulaiman ibn-Sa'd's Syrian transfer, while Sarjun receives the change and Greek clerks' job anxiety appears {cite:baladhuri-hitti-1916 p.301}. The commissioner is not every entry's copyist, nor is Sarjun the translator in this narrative. Jordan's annual tax assigned as assistance,180000dinars in the text, is not Sulaiman's wage or an independently audited project cost. Division of work distinguishes commissioning from accounting/language expertise without inferring clerks' religion from their writing.

## Regional stages and staff interests
Duri lists78AHIraq,81AHSyria,87AHEgypt and124AHKhurasan, with persistent local languages and staff of different backgrounds {cite:tdv-divan-duri Arabicization}. Baladhuri465–466reports Salih's proposal, work alongside Zadan and al-Hajjaj's commission, linking Zadan's death with Ibn al-Ash'ath's unrest {cite:baladhuri-hitti-1916 p.465–466}. A synthesis year and a narrative sequence are not fused into a dated decree never seen. Continuity and job loss preserve human costs rather than reducing all employees to complete replacement or one collective response.

## Personal frame and an earlier witness
The personal basis remains Suyuti's pre-accession worship/legal-learning reports, alongside Yildiz's religious studies and commissioner/translator distinction. Explicit Sunni presentation is not inference from rulership or erasure of the political assessments preserved here {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.356} {cite:tdv-abdulmelik-yildiz}. The contextual PERF558image shows a Greek/Arabic receipt dated643in metadata, preceding the reform rather than Abd al-Malik's register {cite:commons-perf-558-recto}. Narrative and image coexist without filling each other's gaps; no unseen decree, register text or clerk's belief is invented.
