## Summary
[[person/amr-ibn-al-jamuh|Amr ibn al-Jamuh]] (jim unvowelled-fatha, mim lightened) ibn Zayd ibn Haram al-Salami al-Ansari, "one of the Ansar's chiefs, martyred at Uhud" {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p. 1091}. He is of [[group/banu-salima|Banu Salama]] {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p. 1091}.

## His idol and his Islam
Ibn Ishaq relates in al-Maghazi that he was a chief among Banu Salama's chiefs and a noble of their nobles, and had set up in his house a wooden idol he venerated; when the lads of Banu Salama — his son [[person/muadh-ibn-amr-ibn-al-jamuh|Mu'adh]] and Mu'adh ibn Jabal among them — became Muslim, they would go in on his idol and throw it into one of Banu Salama's pits. Amr would find it face-down in the filth, wash and perfume it, then hang his sword on it saying: "if there is good in you, defend yourself" — so they took a dead dog, bound it round its neck and took the sword; he rose to find it so, "saw his right guidance and became Muslim" {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p. 1091}. Ibn al-Kalbi said Amr ibn al-Jamuh was the last of the Ansar to become Muslim {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p. 1091}.

## Chief of Banu Salama
Bukhari in al-Adab al-Mufrad, al-Sirraj and Abu Nu'aym relate through Abu al-Zubayr from Jabir that the Prophet said to Banu Salama: "who is your chief?" They said: al-Jadd ibn Qays — though we think him a miser. He said: "and what disease is worse than miserliness? Nay, your chief is Amr ibn al-Jamuh." Jabir said: and Amr would feast for the Prophet whenever he married {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p. 1091}. Ibn Hajar then gives the report's other chains through Ibn Uyayna, Abu Hurayra, Anas, al-Sha'bi and Habib ibn Abi Thabit (mursal), and verses some Ansar spoke on it {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p. 1092}.


## In Rijal Hawl al-Rasul
[[person/khalid-muhammad-khalid|Khalid Muhammad Khalid]] heads his chapter on him in [[work/rijal-hawl-al-rasul|Rijal Hawl al-Rasul]] "I want to step into Paradise on this lame foot of mine," recording that he was the brother-in-law of [[person/abdullah-ibn-amr-ibn-haram|Abdullah ibn Amr ibn Haram]] — husband of his sister [[person/hind-bint-amr-ibn-haram|Hind bint Amr]] — one of Medina's chiefs and a lord of Banu Salama, whose son [[person/muadh-ibn-amr-ibn-al-jamuh|Mu'adh ibn Amr]] was among the seventy of the Aqaba pledge, and that Mu'adh ibn Amr and his friend [[person/muadh-ibn-jabal|Mu'adh ibn Jabal]] were calling people to Islam in Medina. {cite:rijal-hawl-al-rasul-muqattam-1994 p.483} {cite:rijal-hawl-al-rasul-muqattam-1994 p.484}

He records that Amr had set up a wooden idol in his house he named "Manaf"; the two Mu'adhs would slip in at night and throw it into a pit where people cast their refuse. Amr would cry out, find it nowhere, search until he found it fallen in that pit — then wash, purify and perfume it and lay his sword on its neck saying "if there is good in you, defend yourself"; on the next morning he would find the idol in the very pit bound fast to a dead dog on a stout rope. {cite:rijal-hawl-al-rasul-muqattam-1994 p.484} {cite:rijal-hawl-al-rasul-muqattam-1994 p.485}

The book relates that chiefs who had already embraced Islam addressed his father's mind and guidance toward the True High God and toward Muhammad the truthful, faithful Messenger who came to life to guide, not to mislead — so he purified his garment and body, perfumed and dressed himself, and went to pledge to the Seal of the Messengers; the book pauses on how men of such rank with such minds could worship idols, answering with an age when even Athens and its philosophers were polytheists. {cite:rijal-hawl-al-rasul-muqattam-1994 p.485} {cite:rijal-hawl-al-rasul-muqattam-1994 p.486}

He had a severe lameness in his foot, and four sons, all Muslim, all men like lions who went out raiding with the Prophet; when he went out at Badr his sons begged the Prophet to convince him to stay — or to command him if he would not be convinced — and the Prophet told him Islam exempted him from jihad as an obligation because of his lameness, ordering him to remain in Medina. {cite:rijal-hawl-al-rasul-muqattam-1994 p.486} {cite:rijal-hawl-al-rasul-muqattam-1994 p.487}

Then came Uhud and he went to the Prophet begging leave, saying "by God, I hope to step into Paradise on this lame foot of mine"; permission was granted, he took up his weapon and set out in joy and gladness praying "O God, grant me martyrdom and do not return me to my family," and he and his four sons went out striking with their swords at the army of darkness and polytheism — with every step his sword reaped a head from the heads of paganism, until a sword-stroke flashed announcing the hour of his wedding to the gardens of eternity and the paradise of the Merciful. {cite:rijal-hawl-al-rasul-muqattam-1994 p.487} {cite:rijal-hawl-al-rasul-muqattam-1994 p.488}

He was martyred at Uhud and buried in one grave with [[person/abdullah-ibn-amr-ibn-haram|Abdullah ibn Amr ibn Haram]] "for they were two loving, kindred friends in this world"; forty-six years later a heavy flood — caused by a spring Mu'awiya had channelled there — struck the cemetery ground, and the Muslims hurried to move the martyrs' remains, finding their bodies soft and pliant; [[person/jabir-ibn-abdullah|Jabir ibn Abdullah]] went with his family to move his father's remains and those of his aunt's husband Amr ibn al-Jamuh, and found them in their grave as if asleep, the earth having taken nothing of them, the smile of contentment and gladness that had been on their lips the day they were called to meet God still there. {cite:rijal-hawl-al-rasul-muqattam-1994 p.488} {cite:rijal-hawl-al-rasul-muqattam-1994 p.489}
