## Overview
Ibrahim ibn Sayyaba, Abu Ishaq the secretary, was a witty poet who kept company with the caliphs al-Mahdi and al-Rashid. Al-Safadi gives him two entries in al-Wafi bi-l-Wafayat, the first headed Abu Ishaq the secretary and the second headed Ibn Sayyaba. In the first he reports from Ibn al-Najjar that he died in 198 AH, about 814 CE by derived conversion {cite:wafi-bi-l-wafayat-safadi-dar-ihya-2000 vol. 6 p. 11}.

## First entry
Al-Safadi says he was a client of Thaqif, of Hijazi origin and from Kufa, a graceful poet who kept company with al-Mahdi and al-Rashid. Al-Awfi mentioned that his father Sayyaba was a cupper, and Utba al-A'war satirised him on that account in verses that al-Safadi quotes {cite:wafi-bi-l-wafayat-safadi-dar-ihya-2000 vol. 6 p. 10}. Al-Safadi comments that he knows no better satire among the early poets, since it is a heavy attack cast in the form of praise {cite:wafi-bi-l-wafayat-safadi-dar-ihya-2000 vol. 6 p. 11}.

Ibrahim was accused of heresy. Al-Mahdi had him seized and his books brought, found nothing of the kind in them and reassured him; Ibrahim wrote in al-Mahdi's presence at his sessions and was among the most eloquent of men. Later al-Mahdi became convinced that there was something in the charge, dismissed and sidelined him, and his condition worsened until he had to beg. Al-Safadi calls him a naturally gifted poet and a skilled arguer, and quotes verses he spoke when accused of heresy, verses asking a brother for a dirham, two or three in his straitened state, and verses on forbearance and ignorance {cite:wafi-bi-l-wafayat-safadi-dar-ihya-2000 vol. 6 p. 11}.

Al-Safadi reports al-Marzubani as thinking he lived into the time of al-Ma'mun, and Ibn al-Najjar as giving his death in 198 AH. He then explains the spelling and pointing of the name Sayyaba, saying it comes from the word for the unripe date and that the man was named after it {cite:wafi-bi-l-wafayat-safadi-dar-ihya-2000 vol. 6 p. 11}.

## Second entry
In the second entry al-Safadi reports from the author of al-Aghani that he was among the clients of the Banu Hashim, with no noble poetry or distinction, but that he leaned in affection to Ibrahim al-Mawsili and his son, who sang his verses and mentioned him to caliphs and viziers; he was licentious and had a pleasant wit. It is told that he fell in love with a dark-skinned woman; her family blamed him, and he answered in two verses that a mole on a plain face clothes it with charm {cite:wafi-bi-l-wafayat-safadi-dar-ihya-2000 vol. 6 p. 11}.

Among his anecdotes, he wrote to a friend asking to borrow something; the friend excused himself and swore he had nothing, and Ibrahim replied that if the friend was lying, may God make him truthful, and if he was blameworthy, may God make him excused. He went to Bashshar ibn Burd and told him he had never seen a blind man whom God had not compensated with memory, intelligence or a good voice, and asked what Bashshar had been given; Bashshar answered that he would never see the like of him, then asked who he was and, hearing Ibn Sayyaba, answered with verses of sharp ridicule that al-Safadi quotes. Al-Safadi closes the entry with an incident involving the son of the judge Ibn Sura and verses Ibrahim composed in apology {cite:wafi-bi-l-wafayat-safadi-dar-ihya-2000 vol. 6 p. 12}.
