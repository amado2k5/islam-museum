## Overview
Abu Abd Allah Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, a scholar of mathematics and astronomy. Al-Mas'udi counts him among those who engaged in the sciences of astronomy after the appearance of Islam, together with [[person/al-kindi|al-Kindi]], Abu Ma'shar, al-Farghani, Thabit ibn Qurra, al-Battani and others. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 519}

The museum's data give his birth as about 164 AH (780 CE) and his death as about 235 AH (850 CE). These dates are given as reported, rest on one source (the "Encyclopaedia of Notable Scholars and Men of Letters"), and need an additional source. {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba}

## Life and dates in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam presents him as a mathematician, astronomer and geographer who wrote in Arabic, and gives his kunya as Abu Ja'far, a form different from the Abu Abd Allah used above. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} It places his life in the first half of the 3rd/9th century, about 184 to about 232 AH, that is about 800 to 847 CE, and warns against confusing him with two other important men who bear the same nisba and have their own articles. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} These dates differ from those in the museum's data, and the 1939 Cairo editors treat his dates as unknown; the museum does not choose between them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111}

The article says that in his youth, under the caliphate of [[person/abd-allah-al-mamun|al-Ma'mun]], he worked in the [[building/bayt-al-hikma|House of Wisdom]] of [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]], and that very few other biographical details are known. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} His main works, by contrast, are well known, because many were translated into Latin in Spain and strongly influenced mediaeval thought. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} If the datings proposed by C. J. Toomer are correct, almost all of them were composed during al-Ma'mun's reign. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111}

## The Algebra and its Latin versions
His algebra is called *al-Mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa'l-muqabala*; it was edited with an English translation by F. Rosen in London in 1831 and edited again in Cairo in 1939. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} Robert of Chester translated part of it into Latin as *Liber algebrae et almucabola*, and shortly afterwards Gerard of Cremona made a second version. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} The article says these translations brought into Europe a science wholly unknown until then, together with an already fully developed terminology. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} The discipline was known by the two terms of the Latin titles until Canacci, in the 14th century, began to use only the first, algebra; two centuries later that usage prevailed and the word *al-muqabala* fell out of use. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} See also [[invention/algebra|the museum's record on algebra]] and [[work/al-jabr-wal-muqabala|the work itself]].

On the origin of the word *jabr*, the article reports a proposal by Gandz that it comes from Assyrian *gabru*, and notes that about a hundred mathematical tablets from the second millennium BCE contain algebraic problems parallel to al-Khwarizmi's; Bruins concluded from them that the Assyrians knew the six model equations he used. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} The article adds a difficulty: the word is not attested in any intervening language such as Greek, and it is hard to hold that it survived only in Aramaic until al-Khwarizmi's time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} It judges it more likely that the word comes from medical vocabulary, where *jabr* means setting a dislocated limb, and points out that in a Spanish dictionary *algebrista* still means a specialist in wounds and broken limbs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111}

## Arithmetic, the Indian numerals and the word algorithm
His Arithmetic is lost in Arabic. About the same time as the Algebra was translated, John of Seville made known a Latin version of an adaptation of it, made either by himself or by a Muslim author. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} The article says the Arabic original must have borne a title such as *Kitab al-jam' wa'l-tafriq bi-hisab al-Hind*, and that the Latin work was called *Liber alghoarismi*. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} The connections among the various mediaeval works and al-Khwarizmi's own are not well established, but all explain how to calculate with the numerals now called "Arabic", which were known in the Iberian peninsula in the 3rd/9th century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} See also [[work/kitab-al-hisab-al-khwarizmi|the Book of Arithmetic]] and [[invention/algorism|the record on algorism]].

Mediaeval scholars invented many etymologies for the word algorithm, deriving it from a hypothetical king or philosopher called Algor, or from the Arabic article joined to the Greek *arithmos*. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} The article states that these technical terms in fact come from the name al-Khwarizmi, as Reinaud showed in 1849. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} It also notes that the idea of zero as an empty space, put forward by Gaspar of Tejada, appears much earlier in the *Mafatih al-ulum*, where a line is drawn in a calculation to mean "nothing". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111}

The form of the numerals does not matter for the operations in the *Liber alghoarismi*, but the use of nine or ten symbols implies knowledge of positional rules, and these were known in Spain by the 4th/10th century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} The exact shapes used then are still debated; the article summarises a suggestion by Destombes connected with the monk Vigila of Albelda. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} It also records that in 1229 the Senate of Florence forbade the use of the numerals and required numbers to be written out in letters, to avoid corruption of the figures. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111}

## Fractions
The Latin works on Indian reckoning explain addition, subtraction, multiplication and division, show how decimal and sexagesimal fractions are used, and employ the so-called Egyptian fractions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1111} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} This kind of fraction already appears in the Rhind papyrus; the article links its development to the Qur'anic inheritance rules, which gave rise to [[concept/ilm-al-faraid|the science of inheritance shares]] and called for better arithmetic with fractions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} The system reached Europe through several Spanish versions and through Fibonacci. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112}

For sexagesimal fractions, needed in astronomical computation, al-Khwarizmi gave rules, known in Latin as *Algorismus de minutiis*, which entered university teaching quickly through an adaptation called *De numero indorum* and above all through John of Seville. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} Arabic works of the 3rd/9th century contained a sexagesimal multiplication table of 3,600 cases. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} A table of this type in the lost work of [[person/kushyar-ibn-labban|Kushyar ibn Labban]] is gone, and the oldest surviving example is in the Latin version of al-Khwarizmi's astronomical tables made by Adelard of Bath; the article remarks that such tables recall cuneiform tablets. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112}

## The astronomical tables in the Latin tradition
The article counts his *Zij al-Sindhind*, astronomical tables translated into Latin by Adelard of Bath, among the works that most influenced the birth of western science; Suter edited the Latin text in 1914 and Neugebauer produced an English translation and study in 1962. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} A commentary by Ibn al-Muthanna, lost in Arabic but preserved in Latin and Hebrew, shows that the work had two recensions; Adelard used the shorter one, which [[person/maslama-al-majriti|Maslama al-Majriti]] had corrected for the position of Cordoba. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} See [[work/zij-al-sindhind-khwarizmi|the work]] and [[concept/zij|the genre of the zij]].

The article says this translation contains the first mediaeval mathematical symbols, a sign for a sum and a dot for a remainder. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} It describes the tables as of mixed character, drawing on many sources: the lunar theory comes from an intermediate source that did not know the Almagest, the methods for the true longitude of a planet come from the Indian *Surya Siddhanta* and a second Indian text, and the planetary motions come from Brahmagupta. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} A manuscript from Adelard's version mentions the to-and-fro motion explained by [[person/al-zarqali|al-Zarqali]], also from Indian sources, and the early sine tables with a radius of 150 were replaced in Maslama's adaptation by tables with a base of 60, a number of Hellenistic origin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} The methods for direct and retrograde planetary motion derive, directly or not, from the handy tables of Theon, and other elements come from the *Zij al-Ma'muni* and the *Zij al-Shah*. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} The iterative method for parallax parallels one used later by Kepler for the eccentric anomaly. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112}

## Geography and other works
The article says his other works were not known in the mediaeval Latin world. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} His geography, [[work/surat-al-ard-khwarizmi|the Book of the Image of the Earth]], consists of lists of coordinates of the main towns and geographical features, in harmony with Ptolemy's. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} Its source of inspiration might have been the world map made for al-Ma'mun by a team of geographers that may have included al-Khwarizmi himself. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} It depends, if indirectly, on Ptolemy's Geography, and corrects some of Ptolemy's errors, such as the excessive length of the Mediterranean. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} Nallino studied it in detail. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112}

His work on extracting the Jewish era, dated by internal evidence to 208/823, is among the oldest pointers to the Jewish calendar that survive, and the article says it requires revising much previously written on the subject. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} He also wrote two works on the astrolabe, a historical chronicle, the *Kitab al-Tarikh*, and a book on sundials, the *Kitab al-Rukhama*; the last two are lost. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112} The article surmises that the chronicle gave an astronomical explanation of history, as [[person/abu-mashar|Abu Ma'shar]] and others did later. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1112}

## In the introduction to the Cairo edition of the Algebra
The editors of the 1939 Cairo edition of the [[work/al-jabr-wal-muqabala|Book of al-Jabr and al-Muqabala]] say that what is known of al-Khwarizmi himself goes back to Ibn al-Nadim's *Fihrist*: his name was Muhammad ibn Musa, he came from Khwarazm, he was attached to al-Ma'mun's "Treasury of Wisdom", and he was an expert in astronomy. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.22} Ibn al-Nadim lists among his books [[work/zij-al-sindhind-khwarizmi|the Book of the Zij]] in its two versions known as the Sindhind, a book on the sundial, two books on using and making the astrolabe, and a book of history. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.22}

In their view the dates of his birth and death are not known with certainty, but his attachment to the library of [[person/abd-allah-al-mamun|al-Ma'mun]], who ruled from 813 to 833 CE, and the mention of al-Ma'mun in the preface of the Algebra show that he was al-Ma'mun's contemporary. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.22} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.23} They list four surviving books of his that Ibn al-Nadim does not mention: [[work/kitab-al-hisab-al-khwarizmi|the Book of Arithmetic]], the Algebra, [[work/surat-al-ard-khwarizmi|a book on the coordinates of countries]] explaining Ptolemy's views, and a fourth combining arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.23}

They report that al-Mas'udi named him among the historians, that al-Biruni refers to his tables and wrote three works commenting on his books, and that Suter believed he was one of those whom al-Ma'mun charged with measuring a degree of the earth's circumference; they add that some Arab historians say the [[group/banu-musa|Banu Musa]] took part in that task and suggest that Muhammad, the eldest of the Banu Musa, was al-Khwarizmi himself, an inference of theirs rather than a report they quote. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.23} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.24} They hold that he "is rightly regarded as the founder of algebra" and that his name became "algorithm" in English and *Algorismus* in Latin. {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.24} {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 p.25} In the London edition he is given the kunya Abu Abd Allah and the title "al-Shaykh al-Ajall". {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-rosen-1831 p.121}

## For Sprouts
A great mathematician who invented algebra and used numbers to help people solve arithmetic problems easily.

## For Explorers
Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi was a mathematician and astronomer who lived in Baghdad and worked at the House of Wisdom during al-Ma'mun's reign. He authored the book on algebra that founded it as a distinct science, introduced decimal positional numerals to the world, and gave his name to the computer algorithm.

## For Scholars
Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi (c. 164–232 AH / 781–847 CE), an astronomer, mathematician, and geographer at the Abbasid House of Wisdom in Baghdad. Regarded as the founder of algebra through his foundational text 'The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing', he provided practical formulas for inheritance, commerce, and surveying. His treatise on Hindu-Arabic numerals introduced the decimal positional system and zero to the West, and his Latinized name gave rise to the term 'algorithm'.

## The Sindhind tables
Al-Khwarizmi's astronomical work known as the Sindhind includes a calendar and tables for positions of the Sun, Moon, and planets, alongside topics such as sines, tangents, spherical astronomy, and eclipses {cite:khwarizmi-mactutor Astronomy paragraph} {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227}. The modern biography says the work drew on Indian astronomy, so the museum record describes his contribution as an adaptation rather than the creation of the table tradition from nothing {cite:khwarizmi-mactutor Astronomy paragraph}.

## Attribution and transmission
The record [[invention/khwarizmi-sindhind-tables|Al-Khwarizmi's Sindhind tables]] credits him as an improver, not as the author of every table in the zij {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227} {cite:khwarizmi-mactutor Astronomy paragraph}. The biography reports two lost Arabic versions, a tenth-century Andalusian revision and Latin translation of the shorter version, and a surviving Latin version of the longer one {cite:khwarizmi-mactutor Astronomy paragraph}. These witnesses do not by themselves establish the Arabic originals' arrangement or a direct line to every later author {cite:tdv-harizmi pp. 224–227}.

## Faith and frame
The person record bases an individual Muslim identification on the algebra preface attributed to the author's own words, rather than his name or occupation {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 scan pp. 29–30}. The museum presents him within its Sunni default frame without assigning a personal legal school that the cited basis does not establish {cite:khwarizmi-jabr-cairo-1939 scan pp. 29–30}.
