## Summary
Yuqanna, patrician of the citadel of [[place/aleppo|Aleppo]], is one of the most prominent figures of the [[work/futuh-al-sham-waqidi|Futuh al-Sham]] ascribed to al-Waqidi, in which he turns from the fiercest opponent of the Muslims in northern Syria into a fighter in their ranks. All that follows is this book's account, and its ascription to al-Waqidi is open to question (see [[question/attribution-of-futuh-al-sham-waqidi|the attribution of Futuh al-Sham]]).

## The two brothers
The book says that Aleppo was held by two full brothers living in the citadel, Yuqanna and Yuhanna, whose father had ruled Aleppo for years and was feared by Heraclius; on his death power passed to Yuqanna, the elder, "brave, a hero, a hoarder of wealth, bold in war", while Yuhanna had renounced rule and become a monk, studied the Gospel and read the Psalms, caring only for building churches and monasteries. {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.237} When al-Awasim and Qinnasrin fell, Yuhanna urged his brother to make peace with the Arabs for a yearly payment "as long as they prevail"; Yuqanna, enraged, said: I am a king, son of a king, and between us and them there is nothing but war; Yuhanna warned him that his end was near. {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.237} {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.238}

## The killing of his brother
In the book's telling, a group of Aleppans made peace with the Muslims; Yuqanna ordered his slaves to kill them and three hundred were killed; Yuhanna cried out that Christ had forbidden killing even an enemy, let alone a fellow believer; Yuqanna seized him and drew his sword, and Yuhanna raised his head to heaven, declared himself a Muslim and spoke the testimony of faith, and Yuqanna struck off his head. The Muslims then arrived led by [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]], Yuqanna fled into the citadel, and the book reports from Muhsin ibn Itra that the Muslims killed three thousand or more of his men. {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.245}

## His conversion
After the citadel was taken, the book says, the first of its patricians to become Muslim was Yuqanna with a group of their leading men, and [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda]] returned their property and families. {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.264} When Abu Ubayda consulted the commanders on marching against Antioch, Yuqanna rose and spoke "in eloquent Arabic"; Abu Ubayda was astonished, having heard he knew no Arabic, and Yuqanna said he had seen the Prophet in a dream inviting him to Islam, had embraced it at his hand, and woke speaking Arabic, then opened his brother Yuhanna's library and found the Prophet's description there. {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.264} [[person/muadh-ibn-jabal|Mu'adh ibn Jabal]] discusses with him the meaning of verses of Surat al-Duha, and Yuqanna says: "by God, I will help this religion until I join my brother Yuhanna". {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.265}

## The stratagem at Azaz
The book relates that Abu Ubayda gave Yuqanna a hundred horsemen from various tribes dressed as Byzantines, with a leader over each ten, and sent a thousand horsemen under Malik al-Ashtar after them; Yuqanna rode to the fortress of Azaz and told his men not to speak, since their language would betray them to the Byzantines. {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.267} {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.268} But a spy from the Christian Arabs overheard the plan, wrote it on a slip tied under a bird's wing and released it to the lord of Azaz, who received Yuqanna with a show of honour, cut his saddle girth with a knife, seized him and his men, and threatened to send him to Heraclius to be crucified at the gate of Antioch. {cite:futuh-al-sham-ilmiyya-1997-v1 p.268}

## On the source
This figure and his story come from the circulating Futuh al-Sham, which an academic study holds to have been redacted between the 12th and 13th centuries CE. {cite:dehghani-farsani-futuh-al-sham-thesis abstract}
