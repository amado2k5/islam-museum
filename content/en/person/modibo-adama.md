## Overview

Modibbo Adama (1786–1847), born Adama ɓii Ardo Hassana, was a Fulani scholar who led the jihad into Fombina, "the southlands", and founded the emirate of Fombina, known as Adamawa after him.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} He received his flag from Shehu Usman dan Fodio during the Sokoto revolution, and Fombina became one of the largest and most complex emirates of the Sokoto Caliphate, spanning some forty thousand square miles across parts of today's Nigeria, Cameroon and the Central African Republic.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} The French article adds that he founded the dynasty of the emirs of Yola and gave his name to today's Adamawa State in Nigeria.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-fr} "Modibbo" itself is a Fulani honorific meaning "learned man", and he preferred it to his royal title.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en}

## Early life and education

He was born at Wuro Chekke in 1786 by the articles' leads — the English article's own early-life section says 1779, and a modern Cameroonian book gives 1780; the variants are recorded in his record rather than resolved.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en}{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-fr} His father, Modibbo Hassana, was an Ardo, a clan leader, who educated him in Islam until he was eleven.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} Sent on for further study, he learned the Qur'an under Shehu Muhammad Tahir in Baghirmi and then spent about a decade under the renowned scholar Mallam Kiari at Birnin Ngazargamo, capital of the Bornu empire.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} Returning home, he found his father had been killed in a local conflict, and he joined the Ba'en Fulbe of the Faro valley, where he and the scholar Modibbo Hamman built a reputation for piety and knowledge across the upper Benue.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} The Arabic article's lead assigns him to the Ba'en clan directly, while the English article's fuller account makes him of the Yillaga clan, joining the Ba'en only at this point.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-ar}{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en}

## The mandate and the jihad

When news of Usman dan Fodio's jihad, begun in 1804, reached Fombina, the Fulani clan leaders met at Guringa in 1809 and chose Adama — a learned man, neutral among the Ardo'en and without political ambition — to lead their delegation to Sokoto.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} Usman appointed him Lamido Fombina, "Lord of the South", gave him the flag of jihad, and charged him to propagate Islam through Fombina, to distribute flags among the clan leaders, and to watch for the signs of the Mahdi's coming, whom Usman expected from the east within the caliphate's first century.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} A Yola manuscript of March 1809, quoted in the article, preserves the appointment's terms: whoever obeys Adama obeys Usman, and fealty to Adama is fealty to him.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} The French article dates the green flag to 1806 rather than 1809; both datings are recorded here.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-fr} Adama forbade his volunteers pillage and indiscriminate killing, and offered defeated peoples conversion or tributary status; the non-Fulani Muslims of the region largely rejected his jihad, the article notes, viewing it as a vehicle of Fulani hegemony.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} From his headquarters at Gurin he struck the Bata settlements, joined Buba Yero of Gombe against the Yanguru, Hona and Kibba, and created a new territorial office, the lamido, appointing more than forty of them before his death.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} Against Mandara, the region's one major state, he took the capital Dulo after its king Bukar Djiama refused to yield sovereignty, then lost it to a counterattack from Mora and fled.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en}

## Rule at Yola

Adama retreated to Yola, which became his capital by 1841 — the French article gives the sequence Ribadu in 1838, Joboliwo in 1839, then Yola — and from there he and his lieutenants took Maroua, Garoua and Ngaoundéré and penetrated the Adamawa Plateau by 1825.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en}{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-fr} He governed, on Usman's advice, through three tiers: himself as emir al-Mu'minin at Yola, answerable only to Sokoto; the lamibe ruling districts; and village chiefs below them, with tribute paid in cattle.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} Some of the older clan leaders grew into near-independent powers in their own conquest zones and did not always answer his call, as Bouba Njida of Rai's conduct at Poli showed.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} He lived in moderate surroundings and never acquired great wealth.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en}

## Personal life and character

Adama had four wives — Astajam, Yasebo, Mammare and Yara — and the English article recounts at length his second wife Yasebo's refusal of harem hierarchy, the quarrel that it says moved his capital, and the tradition that he excluded her descendants from the succession, which the article itself judges likely a rumour fabricated by a rival's supporters in the succession crisis of 1847; two of her descendants nonetheless became Lamido in the twentieth century, as did two sons of Yara.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} He was known for an ascetic life, reportedly going a week at a time without eating, and supported himself by making and selling ropes; the explorer Hugh Clapperton, seeing him among the emirs at Caliph Muhammad Bello's court, described him as the most modestly dressed of them all.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} He was, by the article's description, lean and tall, his features sharp and marked by smallpox.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en}

## Death and legacy

He died at Yola in 1847 and was laid to rest there, his tomb surviving to this day; after a brief regency his son Muhammadu Lawal succeeded him, and three more of his sons served as Lamido in their turn.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} At his death Adamawa covered some 103,000 square kilometres and about a million and a half people.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en} His conquests made the Fulani the preeminent people of northern Cameroon, spread Islam until it became the region's dominant religion, and reshaped its ecology, as pasture replaced forest across the plateau.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en}

## Positions and criticism

This section reports what the sources attribute to Adama and how they assess him; it is not the museum's own judgement.

**His confession.** No fetched source names a school of law or theology, or a Sufi affiliation, for Adama himself, and Wikidata carries no religion statement for him at all; the museum records his faith as unknown, as his record's faith note sets out, rather than infer one from his teachers or from the Maliki character of the Sokoto world he served.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en}{cite:source/wikidata-modibo-adama} What the sources do attest is his standing as a scholar: "Modibbo" is the Fulani title of a learned man, the clan leaders chose him as a neutral scholar, and Usman's written charge treated him as a trustworthy scholar who could read the signs of the times.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en}

**The jihad as judged.** The English article records both his rules of war — no pillage, no indiscriminate killing — and the judgement of the region's non-Fulani Muslims, who rejected the jihad as an excuse to spread Fulani hegemony; it also records that many peoples fled his advance rather than fight, displacing others in turn.{cite:source/wikipedia-modibo-adama-en}
