## Overview
Malcolm X was an American preacher of African descent who moved from the Nation of Islam to Sunni Islam after his hajj in 1964, and was assassinated in New York in 1965 {cite:wikidata}. [[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] tells his life in the second episode of his programme [[work/kissa-wa-fikra-program|"A Story and an Idea"]] as an example of change, and the dates and judgements below are his {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:00:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:01:00}. He was born Malcolm Little in 1925 in [[place/omaha|Omaha]], Nebraska; his schooling ended at sixteen, and theft and drug dealing brought him to prison {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:06:30}. There, letters from his siblings told him they had joined the Nation of Islam, and he was freed after seven years {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:13:00}. Elijah Muhammad appointed him the movement's official spokesman, and he took the name "X" to reject "Little", which he regarded as a slave name {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:13:30}.

In 1964 he made the hajj and stayed twelve days in Mecca, where he saw a brotherhood of all colours and gave up the idea that the white man is a devil {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:17:30}. On his return he renounced the Nation's principles and urged Black Americans to vote and to form a bloc holding candidates to their promises {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:20:00}. Al-Suwaidan says he became a symbol of the Black struggle in America {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:21:30}. After Elijah Muhammad died in 1975, his son [[person/warith-deen-mohammed|Warith Deen Mohammed]] succeeded him and dissolved the Nation of Islam, and al-Suwaidan says more than two hundred thousand of its members turned to Sunni Islam {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:24:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:25:00}.

## In the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam deals with Malcolm X inside its article on Muslims in the Americas, in the part on the so-called Black Muslims, also known as the Nation of Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 757} The article says the organisation was founded in [[place/detroit|Detroit]] in 1931 by Wallace D. Fard, who vanished in 1934; after internal conflict among his followers, Elijah Muhammad, the son of a Baptist preacher in Georgia, led a major faction to [[place/chicago|Chicago]], which became its main headquarters. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 756} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 757} The article adds that under Elijah Muhammad the movement was consolidated and held considerable property, and that its prominence grew further when Malcolm Little, born in 1925 and also the son of a Baptist preacher, joined it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 757}

The article explains the name by which he became known: the letter X, it says, signified that he was an ex-smoker, ex-drinker, ex-Christian and ex-slave. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 757} It describes him as playing a central role in carrying Elijah Muhammad's message within the United States and abroad, and as one of the most articulate critics of racial injustice in the United States during the civil rights period. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 757} According to the article, he rejected the non-violent approach of Martin Luther King, Jr., arguing that separatism and determination were necessary if Black Americans were to achieve full equality. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 757}

## The pilgrimage, the break and the new name
The article says that after 1964, when Malcolm X had made the pilgrimage and been converted to what it calls orthodox Islam, he took the name El-Hajj Malik El-Shabazz. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 757} It explains the second element of the name as a nisba derived from the teachings of Wallace D. Fard, which held that the Black African diaspora were all of Muslim heritage, the "lost-found members of the tribe of" Shabazz. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 757} The text of the article is cut off at that point in the scanned copy, so the end of the phrase is not given here.

The article also mentions an internal scandal, which it calls the "Elijah affair" and describes as Elijah Muhammad's relations with his secretaries and the children he fathered. It says that Malcolm X left the organisation and founded his own, the Muslim Mosque, Inc., in [[place/new-york-city|New York]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 757} The scan does not make the order of these events fully clear, so the museum does not state their sequence beyond what the article says.

## Beliefs and influence, as the article presents them
The article says Malcolm X's religious beliefs were influenced by developments in the Middle East and by his experiences there in 1960 and 1964, and that they became interwoven with his political views. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 758} It gives his characteristic phrase as "God aids those who get involved and do their utmost to change their circumstances", which it sets beside Elijah Muhammad's saying that God helps those who help themselves, and it compares both with Qur'anic verses it cites. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 758} In its account, he held that Islam, nationalism and socialism were compatible, pointed to the ancient states of Ghana, Mali and Songhay as having been ruled by Muslims, and encouraged the adoption of Arabic, Hausa and Swahili names and the learning of Arabic. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 758}

The article judges that, until the leadership of Elijah Muhammad's son changed, the Black Muslims were not Muslims by any definition of the word, and it lists practices such as observing seven daily prayers and fasting in December; it records that they observed Ramadan for the first time in 1976. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 757} This is the encyclopaedia's own assessment, not the museum's, and the Suwaidan section below gives a different account of the same period. The article presents Malcolm X's ideas as the foundation on which [[person/warith-deen-mohammed|Warith Deen Muhammad]] built after 1975, when he discarded the belief in the divinity of Wallace D. Fard and the apostleship of his father, whom he described as a social reformer, and rejected the idea of the evil nature of European-Americans. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 757} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 758}

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "A Story and an Idea"
[[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] gives the second episode of his programme [[work/kissa-wa-fikra-program|"A Story and an Idea"]] to Malcolm X, as his example of change: moving from an unwanted reality to a hoped-for vision. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:00:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:01:00} The dates and judgements below are al-Suwaidan's.

He was born Malcolm Little in 1925 in Omaha, Nebraska; his father was a Baptist preacher and an activist in the largest Black organisation of the day. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:01:30} Under threats the family moved to Michigan in 1928, and months later the Ku Klux Klan burned their home. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:03:30} When Malcolm was six his father was killed, and the official report called it suicide. His mother, widowed at thirty-four with seven children, worked as a maid; in 1939 she broke down and was committed, and the children were split among different homes. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:05:30} At sixteen he left school for theft and drug dealing and was jailed. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:06:30}

In prison, letters from his siblings told him they had joined a religion for Black people: the Nation of Islam, founded by Elijah Muhammad, whose beliefs al-Suwaidan describes as deviant, such as a Black god of good and a white god of evil, prayer twice a day, and pilgrimage to Elijah's house in Chicago. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:09:00} Malcolm read in the prison library fifteen hours a day, and at night by the light from the corridor; he drew other prisoners to the movement and was released after seven years. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:12:30} He became the Nation's official spokesman and took the name "X" to reject "Little", which he called a slave name. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:13:30}

In 1964 he went on Hajj and stayed twelve days in Mecca. There he saw brotherhood across all colours, said America needed Islam to solve its racism, and gave up the idea that the white man is a devil. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:17:30} Back in America he renounced the Nation's racist principles, founded a new group, and called on Black Americans to vote, after the Nation had boycotted elections, and to form a bloc that would hold candidates to their promises; his Detroit speech of 1964 bore the title "the ballot or the bullet". {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:20:30} Members of the Nation set fire to his house, and in 1965 three of them shot him dead during a speech. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:22:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:23:00} His memoirs, written with Alex Haley, spread widely, and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 ended the literacy test for voters. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:23:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:24:00}

After Elijah Muhammad died in 1975, his son [[person/warith-deen-mohammed|Warith Deen Mohammed]], an admirer of Malcolm, dissolved the Nation; al-Suwaidan says more than two hundred thousand of its members turned to Sunni Islam. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:24:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:25:00} His lessons from the story: be ready to change when you find something better, and do not accept oppression until it falls. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep02 00:26:00}
