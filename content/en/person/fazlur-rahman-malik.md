## Summary
Pakistani-American Islamic thinker (1919–1988), among the foremost philosophers of modern Islamic reform. He directed the Central Institute of Islamic Research in Karachi then taught at the University of Chicago, proposed the 'double movement' hermeneutic for interpreting the Qur'an, and wrote Islam and Modernity, Major Themes of the Qur'an and Islamic Methodology in History. {cite:wikipedia-fazlur-rahman-malik}

## Life and education

Fazlur Rahman was born in 1919 in the Hazara region of British India (later Pakistan) into a family of religious learning: he memorised the Qur'an and studied traditional Islamic sciences under his father before entering modern university education. {cite:wikipedia-fazlur-rahman-malik}

He took a master's in Arabic at Punjab University and a doctorate in philosophy at Oxford with a thesis on Avicenna's philosophy, taught at Durham University in Britain, then returned to Pakistan. {cite:wikipedia-fazlur-rahman-malik}

## Work and method

Appointed director of the Central Institute of Islamic Research in Karachi in 1961, his reformist ideas met objections from jurists and religious groups, and he left Pakistan in 1968 for the United States, where he became professor of Islamic thought at the University of Chicago until his death. {cite:wikipedia-fazlur-rahman-malik}

His best-known contribution is the 'double movement' method for understanding the Qur'an: moving from the present situation to the context of revelation to grasp the text's intent, then returning to the Muslim present. His books include Islam, Islam and Modernity, Major Themes of the Qur'an and Revival and Reform in Islam. {cite:wikipedia-fazlur-rahman-malik}

## Legacy

Fazlur Rahman is counted among the most influential Muslim thinkers in twentieth-century Western Islamic studies; his doctoral students became professors in American universities, some later founding interpretive schools of their own. {cite:wikipedia-fazlur-rahman-malik}

He died in the United States in 1988. His theses remain debated between admirers who see a renewer of Islamic thought and critics who hold his method an overreach of tradition; the museum presents both without ruling. {cite:wikipedia-fazlur-rahman-malik}
