## Overview
Muhammad ibn Waddah ibn Bazi', Abu Abd Allah, a client of Abd al-Rahman ibn Mu'awiya ibn Hisham ibn Abd al-Malik, the Andalusi of Cordoba; he heard in his own land and then travelled twice to the East {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 179}. Quoting al-Humaydi, Ibn Asakir calls him one of the prolific transmitters and famous imams {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 181}.

## Journeys and teachers
Ibn Asakir records that he read the Qur'an to Uthman ibn Sa'id [[person/warsh|Warsh]], that a group in al-Andalus later read to him, and that in Damascus he heard Muhammad ibn al-Mubarak al-Suri, Hisham ibn Ammar, Duhaym and Hisham ibn Khalid. He also heard Abu Bakr ibn Abi Shayba, Adam ibn Abi Iyas, Sa'id ibn Mansur, Yahya ibn Ma'in, Ahmad ibn Hanbal, Muhammad ibn A'idh, Safwan ibn Salih, Sahnun, Zuhayr ibn Harb, Isma'il ibn Abi Uways, Ya'qub ibn Humayd ibn Kasib, Asbagh ibn al-Faraj, Harmala ibn Yahya, al-Harith ibn Miskin, Ishaq ibn Abi Isra'il, Shuja' ibn Makhlad, Yahya ibn Yahya al-Laythi and others among the Syrians, Egyptians and Iraqis {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 179}.

Al-Walid ibn Abi Bakr al-Andalusi says that he heard from Abu Mus'ab in Medina, was a contemporary of Yusuf ibn Umar, entered the East and heard in Iraq from Yusuf ibn Adi, Ibn Abi Shayba and their like, studied law under Sahnun and the shaykhs of the Maghrib and al-Andalus, and then took up asceticism {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 181}. In al-Humaydi's account he heard in Ifriqiya from Sahnun ibn Sa'id al-Tanukhi and in al-Andalus from [[person/yahya-ibn-yahya-al-laythi|Yahya ibn Yahya al-Laythi]], the companion of Malik ibn Anas, and some say he also heard Abu Mus'ab in Medina {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 181}.

Ibn Asakir relays from Ibn al-Faradi that he made two journeys, the first in 218 AH, on which his aim was not to seek hadith but asceticism and worship, before the journey of Baqi ibn Makhlad {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 pp. 181-182}. On the second he heard from many Baghdadis, Meccans, Syrians, Egyptians and Qayrawanis, and the men he heard from in those cities numbered one hundred and sixty-five {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 182}.

## Students and the spread of hadith
He taught in al-Andalus for a long time and much knowledge spread from him there; those who narrated from him included Wahb ibn Masarra, Ibn Abi Dulaym, Qasim ibn Asbagh, Ahmad ibn Khalid ibn Yazid, Muhammad ibn al-Musawwar, Ali ibn Abd al-Qadir ibn Abi Shayba and Ahmad ibn Ziyad Shabtun {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 181}. The opening of the entry names among his students Abu Umar Ahmad ibn Ubada al-Ru'ayni, imam of Cordoba, the jurist Ja'far ibn Yahya ibn Madin, Sulayman ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Umawi and Ubayd Allah ibn Muhammad al-Rafi'i {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 179}, followed by Isa ibn Ayyub al-Ghassani, Muhammad ibn Uzra al-Hijari, the jurist Muhammad ibn al-Musawwar, Mu'awiya ibn Sa'id, Qasim ibn Asbagh, Muhammad ibn Abd Allah ibn Abi Dulaym and Muhammad ibn Abd al-Malik ibn Ayman {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 180}. Ibn al-Faradi said that through Muhammad ibn Waddah and Baqi ibn Makhlad al-Andalus became a house of hadith {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 182}.

## How the sources describe him
Ibn al-Faradi said he was learned in hadith, expert in its routes and able to speak on its defects, full of stories of the pious, scrupulous, ascetic, poor, chaste, patient in dictating, and seeking reward in spreading his knowledge {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 182}. Al-Baji described him as famous and as one who travelled in search of knowledge {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 182}; Ibn Yunus called him a known and famous Andalusi {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 182}. The jurist Ishaq ibn Ibrahim al-Tujibi reports hearing from reliable teachers that after his last pilgrimage Ibn Waddah's tongue was bound for seven days; he prayed, his tongue was loosed, and they counted this among his marvels {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 180}.

## Where the reports differ
Ibn Asakir quotes Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn Abd al-Barr that Ahmad ibn Khalid put no one in al-Andalus ahead of Ibn Waddah and greatly honoured him, describing his virtue, intellect and scrupulousness, but objected to his frequent rejection of many hadiths, and that Ibn Waddah often said of a hadith that it was not at all among the Prophet's words. The same narrator goes on to say that many errors were preserved from him, that he mistook and misread some things, and that he had no knowledge of law or of Arabic {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 182}. This differs from al-Walid's statement that he studied law under Sahnun {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 181}; the entry does not decide between them.

## Birth and death
Ibn al-Faradi, through al-Abbas ibn Asbagh and Uthman ibn Abd al-Rahman, who knew Ibn Waddah's affairs best, says he died on the night of Saturday, four nights before the end of Muharram 287 AH, and was born in 199 AH, at its beginning or its end, though the narrator says he could not confirm this; others say he died in Dhu al-Hijja 286 AH {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 183}. Al-Humaydi gives 286 AH {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 181}, as does Ibn Yunus, who adds that al-Ala' ibn Abd al-Wahhab ibn Ahmad ibn Hazm said 287 AH {cite:tarikh-dimashq-ibn-asakir-fikr-1995 vol. 56 p. 182}. By the museum's derived conversion 287 AH falls at the beginning of 900 CE and 286 AH in 899 CE.
