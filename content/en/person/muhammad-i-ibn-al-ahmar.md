## Overview
Muhammad I ibn al-Ahmar, founder of the state of the [[group/bani-al-ahmar|Banu al-Ahmar]] in al-Andalus. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.1008-1009}

## Origins
Ibn Khaldun says that their origin is from Arjuna, one of the fortresses of Córdoba, and that they had an ancestor there among the sons of the soldiery known as the Banu Nasr, who trace their lineage to Sa'd ibn Ubada, lord of the Khazraj. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.1008-1009}

## Rise
When the power of the Almohads weakened, he rose against Ibn Hud, and was given the oath of allegiance in 629 AH, with prayers for the amir Abu Zakariyya, lord of Ifriqiya. He was known as al-Shaykh and was titled Abu Dabbus. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.1008-1009}

Hayyan and Sharish obeyed him in 630 AH, and he entered Seville in 632 AH. Then he overcame Granada in 635 AH through the collusion of its people, and settled there after Ibn Hud's death. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.1008-1009}

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "History Taught Me"
[[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] treats Ibn al-Ahmar in episodes 2 and 3 of his programme [[work/allamani-al-tarikh-program|"History Taught Me"]], part of a series on the fall of al-Andalus that he says he tells to draw out lessons and laws, not for the story alone {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:02:30}.

### Name and nickname
Al-Suwaidan says that Ibn al-Ahmar was Muhammad ibn Yusuf ibn Nasr, and that "Ibn al-Ahmar" ("son of the red one") was a nickname, not a name, given for the redness of his hair; it then passed to his descendants, so that the rule and the name of the Banu al-Ahmar lasted until the end of Muslim rule in Granada {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:08:30}.

### The treaty with Castile
He relates that Ferdinand III, king of Castile, in his account the largest of the Christian states in the north of al-Andalus, began writing to Ibn al-Ahmar, lord of Granada, and that a treaty was made between them which Ibn al-Ahmar accepted because of his weakness {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:08:30}. Among its terms he lists: Ibn al-Ahmar pays tribute (jizya) to the king of Castile; he attends Ferdinand's court as one of his vassals; and he governs Granada openly in the name of the king of Castile, so that Granada is legally and politically subject to Castile, though with no Castilian army or Castilian government inside it {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:09:30}. Further, he hands over what remained of the fortresses of [[place/jaen|Jaén]] and many other fortresses around Granada, which he did, and he helps Ferdinand in his wars when needed, even against Muslims; this last clause is, for al-Suwaidan, the gravest of all {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:10:00}.

### The siege of Seville
Al-Suwaidan relates that after the treaty the king of Castile marched on [[place/seville|Seville]] and asked Ibn al-Ahmar for help under its terms; Ibn al-Ahmar complied, Granada's horsemen rode at the front of the Castilian armies, and together they laid a long, hard siege to the city {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:12:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:12:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:13:00}. What puzzles him most, he says, is not Ibn al-Ahmar's stand alone but that the people of Granada, his strength and his army, accepted it and marched out with him {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:13:00}. He says the people of Seville appealed to Granada and to all around them and found no one, since the Maghreb was taken up with its internal revolts and with the Marinid rising against the Almohads {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:14:00}. He dates the fall of Seville to 27 Ramadan 646 AH, 1248 CE, and says 400,000 Muslims were driven out of it {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:15:00}. He adds that its great mosque, built by [[person/yaqub-al-mansur-al-muwahhidi|Ya'qub al-Mansur the Almohad]] from the spoils of [[event/battle-of-alarcos-591|the battle of Alarcos]], is still a church today {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:16:00}.

### Why Castile spared Granada
Al-Suwaidan asks why Ferdinand III made a treaty with Ibn al-Ahmar instead of taking Granada as he had taken other lands, and gives three reasons: its dense population, since the people of every fallen city fled there, until it was said they exceeded a million; its strong fortresses; and its nearness to the Maghreb, where the Marinid state had arisen, in his words a Sunni state that helped Granada from time to time {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:18:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:19:00}. Even so, he says, the Castilians broke the pact now and then and seized towns and fortresses, Ibn al-Ahmar tried in vain to recover them, and Granada shrank little by little {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:19:30}.

### The call to the Marinids, and his death
He relates that this pattern of treaties and broken treaties lasted until 671 AH, 1273 CE, when the new king of Castile, Alfonso X, openly broke the treaty and raised armies to enter Granada itself; Ibn al-Ahmar turned, as usual, to the Maghreb, and [[person/abu-yusuf-yaqub-marini|Ya'qub ibn Mansur al-Marini]] crossed over and drove the Christian attack back from Granada {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:05:00}. He says Muhammad ibn al-Ahmar I died in the very year that army was repulsed, 671 AH, 1273 CE, at nearly eighty, and was succeeded by his son Muhammad, nicknamed al-Faqih ("the jurist") for the breadth of his learning and the depth of his fiqh {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:09:30}.

### His criticism of the spending on palaces
Al-Suwaidan criticises the vast sums spent on palaces, among them [[building/nasrid-palaces-alhambra|the Alhambra]], which he calls an enduring architectural masterpiece, at a time when the country was under siege, and says Ibn al-Ahmar laid out more than 300 great gardens, each called a "janna" (garden of paradise) for its size and its abundance of fruit and trees {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:07:30}. He adds that he has no objection to great building as such; what he criticises is luxury while the community is in distress and the army is left idle, and then calling on fighters from neighbouring lands {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:07:30}.

### His verdict
Al-Suwaidan calls Ibn al-Ahmar a traitor and makes him an example of two of the obstacles to victory, treachery and the corrupt ruler, saying that he never broke his pact with the Christians while he broke his pacts with Muslims {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep02 00:20:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:09:00}. This is al-Suwaidan's own judgement in his programme and is given here as his {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep03 00:09:00}.
