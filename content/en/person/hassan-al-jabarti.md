## Summary
Hasan ibn Ibrahim al-Jabarti al-Zayla'i al-Aqili al-Hanafi was a Hanafi jurist, mathematician and astronomer among the scholars of al-Azhar, and father of the historian [[person/abd-al-rahman-al-jabarti|Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti]], who gives him a long biography among the deaths of 1188 AH in [[work/ajaib-al-athar|Aja'ib al-athar]], opening by calling him "the philosopher of Islam" and the authority of his age in Abu Hanifa's law. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.502} Everything below is his son's account.

## Lineage and the land of Jabart
His son says the land of Jabart is the land of Zayla' in Abyssinia, inhabited by the Muslims of that region, who follow the Hanafi and Shafi'i schools and trace their descent to Aqil ibn Abi Talib; they come for pilgrimage and to reside in search of learning, and have a riwaq (residential hall) in Medina, one in Mecca and one at al-Azhar. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.502}

## Birth and upbringing
He was born in 1110 AH; his father died when he was a month old, and his mother raised him in the care of his grandmother, with Shaykh Muhammad al-Nashirti as guardian, and they installed him as head of the Jabart riwaq like his forebears. He memorised the Qur'an at ten, then the standard texts: the Alfiyya, the Jawhara, Kanz al-daqa'iq, the Sullam and the Rahbiyya. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.503} At thirteen he received a licence from the son of Shaykh al-Shurunbulali after reading with him his father's Nur al-idah; the old man told him that he had studied with his grandfather. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.503}

## Calligraphy and languages
He perfected thuluth and naskh with Abd Allah Efendi al-Anis and Hasan Efendi al-Diya'i until licensed, then ta'liq, and learned Persian and Turkish so well that many Persians and Turks took him for one of their own. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.510}

## Mathematics and astronomy
In 1144 AH he took up mathematics with Shaykh Muhammad al-Najjahi, reading the timekeeping texts until his teacher's knowledge was exhausted, then went on by himself in the quadrant, sundial projections and astronomical tables until "the leadership of the craft passed to him". {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.510} When Shaykh Husam al-Din al-Hindi, versed in the mathematical and philosophical sciences, came to Egypt, he took him into his house and read with him al-Jaghmini with Qadizade's commentary, the Tadhkira, al-Abhari's Hidayat al-hikma, Ashkal al-ta'sis, the Tahrir of Euclid, the Intermediate books, arithmetic, geography and surveying; he declined to learn alchemy ("the divine art") from him. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.510} He learned the science of magic squares from Shaykh Muhammad al-Ghulani al-Kashnawi. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.510}

## Books, instruments and craftsmen
He acquired fine books; Sultan Mustafa sent him copies from his library, and he gathered Persian books such as the Gulistan, the Diwan of Hafiz, the Shahnama and Kalila wa-Dimna; from the estate of Hasan Efendi al-Ruznamaji he bought the brass astronomical globes, and he collected altitude instruments, astrolabes and quadrants and the tools of carpenters, turners, binders and goldsmiths; clockmakers and craftsmen gathered at his house. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.515} His son relates that "students from the Franks came to him and read geometry with him" in 1159 AH, gave him gifts of their own manufacture, and, in his son's view, spread that science in their lands. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.515} He drew many sundials on marble and set them up at al-Azhar, the Ashrafiyya, the mosque of Qawsun and the shrine of al-Shafi'i. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.515}

## Fatwas and writings
Once his students were proficient he gave up teaching these sciences, sending Arab students to his pupil Muhammad ibn Isma'il al-Nafrawi and Persians and Turks to Mahmud Efendi al-Nishi, and devoted himself to law and fatwas, until the judges trusted no fatwa but his. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.516} His son says he wrote only on matters of importance; among his treatises are Nuzhat al-ayn fi zakat al-ma'danayn, Raf' al-ishkal bi-zuhur al-ushr fi al-ushr, al-Aqwal al-mu'riba an ahwal al-ashriba, Bulugh al-amal fi kayfiyyat al-istiqbal, al-Jadawil al-bahiyya on prosody, al-Durr al-thamin fi ilm al-mawazin, two unfinished glosses on Qadizade's commentary on al-Jaghmini and on al-Durr al-mukhtar, a manual of the hajj rites, and instruments he invented, among them "the square instrument" for finding directions and azimuth. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.516} The editor notes that a manuscript of Raf' al-ishkal in the Chester Beatty Library, Dublin, carries a marginal note in his hand on the basin of the Husayni mosque being built in 1175 AH after his design. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.516}

## Death
He fell ill for twelve days with bilious cholera, praying only standing, until he died on Tuesday before noon, 1 Safar 1188 AH; he was prayed over at al-Azhar before a great gathering and buried with his forebears in the desert cemetery beside al-Shams al-Babili and al-Khatib al-Shirbini, aged seventy-seven, and his pupil Shaykh Muhammad al-Sabban lamented him in verses recited at the funeral. {cite:ajaib-al-athar-moreh-2013-v1 p.524}
