## Overview
Fakhr al-Din al-Razi, a well-known imam in theology and Qur'anic exegesis, as Ibn al-Athir describes him. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p.304} The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls him Abu Abd Allah Muhammad ibn Umar ibn al-Husayn and counts him among the most celebrated theologians and exegetes of Islam. It gives his birth as 543/1149, or perhaps 544, at [[place/rayy|Rayy]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 781}

## Family and training
His father, Diya' al-Din Abu al-Qasim, was a preacher, a khatib, in Rayy, and the son's byname Ibn al-Khatib comes from that office. The father also knew kalam and wrote a work called Ghayat al-maram, in which, the article says, he showed himself a warm partisan of [[person/al-ashari|al-Ash'ari]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 781} Al-Subki lists among the son's teachers Abu al-Qasim al-Ansari, a pupil of [[person/imam-al-haramayn-al-juwayni|the Imam al-Haramayn]]. In philosophy his teacher, besides his father, was al-Madjid al-Djili, whom he followed to [[place/maragha|Maragha]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 781}

## Travels and patrons
After finishing literary and religious studies in Rayy, and, according to al-Qifti, after failed researches in alchemy, he went to Khwarazm. There he was drawn into relentless controversies with the [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazilis]], who forced him to leave. He met the same opposition in Transoxania. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 781} Back in Rayy he entered into relations with [[person/shihab-al-din-ghuri|Shihab al-Din al-Ghuri]], Sultan of Ghazna, who heaped money and honours on him. The same happened later with Ala al-Din Khwarazmshah Muhammad ibn Takash, with whom he stayed for some time and who treated him with great consideration. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 782}

In 580/1184, on his way to Transoxania and [[place/bukhara|Bukhara]], he stopped at [[place/sarakhs|Sarakhs]], where Abd al-Rahman ibn Abd al-Karim al-Sarakhsi received him with honour. In gratitude he dedicated to his host his commentary on the Kulliyyat of Avicenna's Canon. Finding no protection in Bukhara, he went on to Herat, where the Ghurid Sultan [[person/ghiyath-al-din-muhammad-ibn-sam|Ghiyath al-Din]] allowed him to open a school for the public inside the royal palace. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 782} Further journeys took him to [[place/samarqand|Samarkand]] and as far as India, where, the article suggests, he may have been sent on a mission. He finally settled in [[place/harat|Herat]], where he spent most of his life and was known by the title shaykh al-Islam. It is said that at the height of his fame more than three hundred disciples or followers went with him when he moved from place to place. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 782}

He was so poor at the start of his career that compatriots in Bukhara had to collect money for him when he fell ill, but he later acquired a vast fortune. He married his two sons to the two daughters of a very rich physician of Rayy and inherited part of the man's wealth on his death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 782}

## Teacher, preacher and pious scholar
The article credits him with a lively, penetrating mind, a prodigious memory, and a methodical, clear way of thinking. It reports that he is said to have learned al-Juwayni's Shamil by heart in his youth. These gifts made him a teacher renowned across Central Asia, consulted from all parts on the most varied questions. He was also, it says, an excellent preacher: of medium height, well built and heavily bearded, with a powerful, warm voice, he moved his listeners to tears and was himself deeply moved when he preached. His preaching converted many [[group/karramiyya|Karramis]] to Sunnism. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 782}

Despite his grounding in philosophy and his many controversies, the article describes him as extremely pious, one of the people of religion and Sufism, and notes that many of his treatises close on a religious note with practical applications. Late in life he often reflected on death. According to Ibn al-Salah, he reproached himself for having given so much of himself to the abstract sciences, philosophy and kalam, which he came to think could not lead to certain truth. The article cites Ibn Abi Usaybia for words attributed to him: that he had tried every method of kalam and every path of philosophy and found in none of them the satisfaction he found in reading the Qur'an. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 782}

## Opponents
The article says his zeal in defending his positions made him many bitter opponents. Besides the Mu'tazilis he had to contend with the Karramis, adherents of an anthropomorphic type of exegesis, who, it says, did not hesitate to use any calumny against him. In 599/1202, while he was at Firuzkuh, a riot was set off against him. The OCR of the passage that follows is partly unreadable. What can be read lists reproaches made against him, among them that he preferred the teaching of Aristotle, al-Farabi and Avicenna to the teaching of Islam, and that he reported the arguments of Islam's adversaries without being able to refute them convincingly. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 782}

## Testament, death and tomb
In 606/1209, gravely ill and sensing his end, he dictated a testament to his disciple Ibrahim ibn Abi Bakr al-Isfahani on Sunday 21 Muharram, 26 July. Its text was preserved by al-Subki and Ibn Abi Usaybia, among others. The article calls it a true profession of Sunni faith and a fine example of total submission to the will of God. In it he commends his children to the Sultan and asks the Sultan and his disciples to bury him according to the ordinances of Muslim law on the mountain of Mazdakhan near Herat. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 782}

The article records that some biographers held that the Karramis poisoned him, and that Ibn al-Ibri and Ibn Abi Usaybia pass on a rumour about a secret burial in his own house, to keep the crowd from ill-treating his remains. The author judges that neither report is likely true, noting that his tomb is still venerated at Herat. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 782} The article therefore dates his death to 606/1209, while the printings of his commentary cited below give 604; the museum records the discrepancy and does not resolve it.

## Theological and philosophical positions
The article presents these points as the views of its author and of the scholars he cites.

It calls al-Razi a convinced follower of al-Ash'ari. In his youthful works, it says, he opposed atomism, as in al-Mabahith al-mashriqiyya. Later, in Mafatih al-ghayb, in Lawami al-bayyinat and in Kitab al-Arbain, he appears to have changed his view or at least to have criticised atomism less severely, and he devoted a Kitab al-Jawhar to the subject, which al-Tusi summarised in his commentary on the Isharat. The article adds that, according to Khwansari, he also criticised al-Ash'ari's doctrine of the divine attributes. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 782}

His deep knowledge of falsafa, including a commentary on the Isharat and on the Uyun al-akhbar of Ibn Sina, let him use large parts of it in his own dogmatic synthesis, the Mabahith being the chief example. Yet he kept his freedom of mind and criticised [[person/ibn-sina|Avicenna]] strongly where he chose not to follow him. The article quotes Kraus as thinking that, for al-Razi, philosophy and theology are reconciled at the level of a Platonistic system that ultimately derives from an interpretation of the Timaeus. It notes his frequent references to the Kitab al-Mutabar of Abu al-Barakat al-Baghdadi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 782} It also reports that Goldziher showed that, though an opponent of the Mu'tazilis, he was influenced by them in certain respects, for example on the infallibility of the Prophet and on the validity of single-narrator traditions in theological argument. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 782}

On his influence on [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]], the article cites Laoust: Ibn Taymiyya used al-Razi's principal works, the Muhassal, the Maalim usul al-din and Kitab al-Arbain, and was led by him towards a deeper personal understanding of philosophy and heresiography. Laoust adds that Ibn Taymiyya's political sociology is hard to understand unless it is seen partly as a reaction to al-Razi's conception of sovereignty and theory of the caliphate. Ibn Taymiyya himself passed a rather severe judgment on al-Razi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 782} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 783}

## Works
The article calls his output huge and encyclopaedic, mostly on kalam, philosophy and exegesis. It reports Brockelmann's division of the surviving manuscripts under thirteen headings, from history, law and Qur'an to philosophy, astrology, rhetoric, medicine, physiognomy, alchemy and mineralogy. It also reports a classification by al-Nashshar, who counts five works of exegesis, forty on kalam, twenty-six on philosophy, seven on language and literature, five on law, seven on medicine, five on talismans and geometry, and two on history. The article warns that this list is not critical and that a deep study of the work remains to be done. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 783} It then describes the main printed works.

Asas al-taqdis, dedicated to the Sultan Abu Bakr ibn Ayyub, applies the way of negation to the knowledge of God. Its four parts treat proofs that God is incorporeal and not in space, the interpretation of ambiguous terms in the Qur'an, the position of the Salaf, and, finally, further treatment of the ambiguous verses. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 783} Lawami al-bayyinat fi al-asma wa al-sifat, on the divine names, is called one of the most substantial treatises of Muslim theology. It has prolegomena in ten chapters, a long systematic study of the ninety-nine names, with more than thirty pages on the name Allah, and a short third part on further names. Al-Razi generally ends with practical spiritual advice. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 783}

His Sharh al-Isharat comments on the physics and metaphysics of Ibn Sina's Isharat. Lubab al-Isharat is a digest of the same work, written afterwards. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 783} The [[work/muhassal-afkar-al-mutaqaddimin|Muhassal afkar al-mutaqaddimin wa al-mutaakhkhirin]] is a précis of the ideas of ancient and modern scholars, philosophers and theologians. Its plan has four cornerstones: preliminaries, being and its modes, rational theology, and the questions known by tradition, which include prophecy, eschatology, faith and the imamate. The article finds that the planned design is almost lost in the course of the book. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 783} The Cairo edition prints at the foot al-Tusi's critical Talkhis, in which, the article says, al-Tusi remarks that the Muhassal was then the only famous work on dogmatics, without justification in his view. Horten's abridged German editions are judged by Kraus to be of doubtful value because of errors. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 784}

Al-Maalim fi usul al-din is described by its author as a compendium of five sciences: dogmatics, legal methodology, law, the principles of differences of opinion, and the rules of dialectic. Only the first part has been printed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 784} Mafatih al-ghayb, also called al-Tafsir al-kabir, is called certainly his most important work, a commentary both philosophical and based on opinion into which he put all his knowledge of philosophy and religion. He often sets out an issue as a question and gives the differing opinions with their arguments, and tries to link verses logically to one another. The commentary opens with a long treatment of the isti'adha and the basmala. The article says assessments differ: detractors of philosophy and kalam such as Ibn Taymiyya speak disdainfully of a commentary in which everything is found except commentary, while admirers reply that everything else is found there as well. It adds that the work has influenced those who wish to modernise aspects of traditional exegesis. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 784} In the museum, see [[work/tafsir-al-kabir-razi|al-Tafsir al-Kabir]].

Al-Munazarat is a kind of autobiography reporting sixteen controversies from his travels, with Shafii, Hanafi, Ashari and Maturidi scholars. Almost half the chapters concern subtle questions of law, and in them he mocks the legal work of [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]]. The rest concerns philosophy and theology, including a refutation of astrology, and in the tenth controversy he gives details on the sources of the Milal wa al-nihal of [[person/al-shahrastani|al-Shahrastani]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 784} Itiqad firaq al-Muslimin wa al-mushrikin treats the Muslim sects concisely and objectively, together with some Zoroastrian, Jewish and Christian groups, and gives the philosophers a chapter. The article notes that he is the only one to treat the Sufis as a sect. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 784}

Al-Mabahith al-mashriqiyya is a work of metaphysics and physics in three books, on being and its properties, on substance and accident, and on pure metaphysics, including the proof of the Necessary Being, its attributes and acts, and prophecy. It borrows heavily from Avicenna's Shifa, Najat and, occasionally, Isharat, while freely disputing some of his principles, notably on necessary emanation and the active intellect. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 784} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 785} Kitab al-Firasa, on physiognomy, has been edited with a French translation by Youssouf Mourad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 785} Kitab al-Arbain fi usul al-din, written for his eldest son Muhammad, treats forty theological questions, from the beginning of the world and the existence of God to the divine attributes, prophecy, the imamate and the method of rational proofs. The article finds striking that here he seems to approve atomism, which he refutes in the Mabahith. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 785}

## After the killing of al-Ghuri
When Shihab al-Din al-Ghuri was killed in 602 AH, some people of Ghazna came to his mamluks and told them that al-Razi had killed their master, because he had brought about the death of the man who killed him and had brought down the Khwarazm Shah. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p.304}

They rose against him to kill him, but he fled and went to the vizier Mu'ayyid al-Mulk, who secretly sent him to a place of safety. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p.304}

This is Ibn al-Athir's report of what the people of Ghazna said, not a judgement of the museum.

## In the printings of his commentary
The cover of the Dar al-Fikr printing of his commentary names him Muhammad al-Razi Fakhr al-Din, son of the learned Diya' al-Din Umar, known as the Preacher of Rayy, with the dates 544 and 604 AH, which the title page of the Dar al-Hadith printing also gives. {cite:mafatih-al-ghayb-dar-al-fikr-1401-j3 p.1} {cite:mafatih-al-ghayb-dar-al-hadith-2012-v15 p.1} His commentary, [[work/tafsir-al-kabir-razi|al-Tafsir al-Kabir]], divides the discussion of each verse into questions that set out the views with their arguments. {cite:mafatih-al-ghayb-dar-al-fikr-1401-j3 p.2}
