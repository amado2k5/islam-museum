## Overview
Muhammad Ali Pasha, known in his own Ottoman milieu as Mehmed Ali Pasha, died in 1265 AH / 1849 CE and was the founder of the Alawite ruling family in Egypt, the [[polity/muhammad-ali-dynasty|Muhammad Ali dynasty]]. Al-Zirikli counts him among the great reformers. {cite:al-aalam} The Encyclopaedia of Islam says he was born in the late 1760s, calls him Ottoman governor-general of Egypt from 1805 to 1848, and records that his heirs ruled Egypt, with varying degrees of effective power, until 1952. It notes that the title of khedive was officially granted only to his grandson Ismail in 1867, and that in 1848 the post passed formally to his son [[person/ibrahim-pasha-misri|Ibrahim Pasha]] because of the old man's mental incapacity. It calls him one of the most important and controversial figures in the modern history of Egypt and divides his career into four periods: the rise to power, 1801 to 1811; the laying of the foundations of a regional empire, 1812 to 1827; the height of Egyptian hegemony, 1828 to 1841; and a phase of realism and retrenchment, 1841 to 1848. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 475}

## Rise to power
Al-Zirikli says that he was of Albanian origin, Arabized, born in Kavala in Rumelia, and took up trade and grew wealthy. He witnessed the battle of Abu Qir in 1214 AH, gathered around him a large number of Albanians whom he relied upon, and courted the Mamluks until they supported him. He became governor of Egypt in 1220 AH. {cite:al-aalam}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam gives a fuller account. He was born in [[place/kavala|Kavala]], a small port in Macedonia, in the late 1760s, the exact date being under debate. His father was an Ottoman soldier of Albanian origin who commanded the local irregulars and also traded in tobacco, and his mother came from the family of the town governor. In 1801 he was made deputy commander of the Kavala contingent sent to join the Ottoman forces against the French in Egypt, and from there began his rise. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 475}

The article stresses method over detail. Between 1803 and 1805 he played the Mamluk factions, the Ottoman governor and his regiments, and the Cairo notables against one another. In April 1803 the Ottoman-Albanian troops mutinied over pay and the governor Khusrew fled; after the commander Tahir Pasha was assassinated, Muhammad Ali took command of the Albanian unit, probably the most effective force in Egypt. He allied with the Mamluks, defeated Khusrew at Damietta and brought him to Cairo as a prisoner. When a new tax provoked the city, he abolished it and drove out the Mamluk leader al-Bardisi, laying the base of an alliance with the urban notables. With their collusion he was proclaimed governor of Egypt, and the Porte, reluctantly, endorsed the appointment. The article reads later Ottoman attempts to move him to other provinces as a sign of how unhappy the government was. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 475}

## Consolidation, 1805 to 1811
Between 1805 and 1811 he removed every challenge to his authority. The main threat came from the Mamluks in Upper Egypt, whom he tried to co-opt where he could and fight where he could not. The deaths of al-Bardisi and al-Alfi in late 1806 and early 1807 left them without leadership. In March 1807 British forces landed in Alexandria, failed to take Rosetta, were contained and later left by agreement. In March 1811 he struck the final blow: the article says he treacherously massacred their leaders, whom he had invited to a ceremony at the Citadel for his son Tusun, who was departing for the Hijaz. Mamluk power in Egypt ended there, though many individual Mamluks later served in high posts. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 476}

He also reduced the independent weight of the ulama, whose leaders drew income from endowments and tax farms and who stood behind many Sufi orders. After he taxed endowment lands in 1809, the ulama led by Umar Makram protested; the article says he split their ranks and exiled Umar Makram. Economically he began cautiously to centralise and to replace tax farmers with his own family and household. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 476}

## Foundations of a regional power, 1812 to 1827
In this period he surveyed and taxed all cultivable land, a process complete by 1821, and set up a system of state monopolies over agricultural trade. Forced labour was used to extend irrigation. By 1816 the state had excluded competition in the traditional cash crops, and it then introduced long-staple cotton, which supplied a sizeable share of revenue from the 1820s. He also began an industrial experiment aimed at import substitution and at supplying the army: arsenals in Cairo, Bulaq, Rosetta and Alexandria from 1815, and textile and other works later. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 477}

His sons led campaigns. In 1811 the Porte asked him to suppress the Wahhabi revolt in Arabia and retake the Holy Cities. Tusun commanded, Muhammad Ali himself went to the Hijaz in late 1813, and after Tusun's death Ibrahim Pasha led an offensive into Najd and defeated the Wahhabis in 1818. Najd was retaken by them in 1824, but the [[place/hijaz|Hijaz]] stayed under Ottoman control. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 477} The occupation of Nubia, Sennar and Kordofan in the [[place/sudan|Sudan]] followed from 1820, motivated, the article says, by the wish to recruit a slave army, to control trade and to remove the last Mamluk enclave. High mortality led him to replace the Sudanese recruits with conscripted Egyptian peasants. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 477}

When Sultan [[person/mahmud-ii-uthmani|Mahmud II]] was bogged down in the Greek revolt, Muhammad Ali was offered Crete and the Morea. Ibrahim took Athens in June 1827, but the Ottoman and Egyptian fleets were destroyed that year in the [[event/battle-navarino-1243|battle of Navarino]], and Ibrahim's forces were evacuated a year later. The article judges that the Greek episode made him look a dangerous challenger to Ottoman authority and European designs rather than a loyal subject. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 477} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 478} Administration was reorganised on a civil model with departments and a cabinet from 1824, and student missions began in 1809. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 478}

## The regional empire, 1828 to 1841
In October 1831 Ibrahim besieged Acre, the pretext being that its governor sheltered Egyptian conscripts who had fled. By mid-1832 the Egyptians held Acre, Palestine, Damascus and other Syrian cities, and in December they defeated the Ottoman army near Konya. Under Russian, British and French pressure, it was agreed in mid-1833 that Muhammad Ali would control Syria, Crete and Adana. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 478} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 479} He then governed territory from the Sudan and the Hijaz to eastern Anatolia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 479}

The period brought schools, presses and public works. Close to 350 students were sent abroad between 1809 and 1848. Between 1825 and 1836 the state founded military and naval schools, schools of medicine, veterinary science, pharmacy, engineering, agriculture, administration, languages and translation. The Bulaq press began printing in 1822 and an official gazette appeared in Turkish and Arabic. The translation school and bureau produced hundreds of translated textbooks, largely under [[person/rifaa-al-tahtawi|Rifa'a al-Tahtawi]]. The article adds that the aim was elite formation rather than mass education, and that the health services served mainly government personnel. It also mentions the Mahmudiyya canal, the Nile barrages and the great mosque rising at the Citadel. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 479}

The strain grew. Syria's administration became unpopular, and the cost of the army and of the empire weighed on the treasury. By the late 1830s little of the factory system still worked, and from 1838 he granted large estates to family members and created the system of 'uhda estates, which the article calls in many ways a return to old practices. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 480} In mid-1838 he told the European powers he meant to declare independence. An Ottoman army was routed at Nezib in June 1839 by Ibrahim, and the Ottoman fleet defected to him. In July 1840 Britain, Austria, Prussia and Russia agreed in London to impose a settlement: hereditary rule in Egypt for him in exchange for leaving Syria. After Beirut was taken and British ships appeared before Alexandria, Ibrahim's army left Syria, and in June 1841 the sultan, [[person/abd-al-majid-i-uthmani|Abd al-Majid I]], confirmed Muhammad Ali as hereditary governor of Egypt in a firman that limited his army and required Ottoman consent to treaties. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 480}

## Retrenchment and death, 1841 to 1849
In the last phase his relations with Istanbul improved, and he travelled to the capital in 1846 and allowed a British overland route from Alexandria to Suez in 1841. Bad harvests, cholera and cattle disease increased tax arrears, and more land passed to estates, which the article says prepared a landed class. The 1838 Balta Limani commercial treaty abrogated the monopolies and he had to apply it in Egypt by the mid-1840s. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 481} In 1847 he became mentally incapacitated, with senility or the effects of silver nitrate given for dysentery named as possible causes. Ibrahim ruled in his place and was invested in July 1848, but died within months, and Abbas followed. Al-Zirikli says he withdrew from affairs in favour of Ibrahim in 1265 AH and died in Alexandria. {cite:al-aalam} The Encyclopaedia of Islam gives his death in [[place/alexandria|Alexandria]] on 2 August 1849, and his burial in the Citadel mosque, which Abbas finished, the [[place/muhammad-ali-mosque|Muhammad Ali Mosque]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 481} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 482}

## Reforms and conquests in Zirikli's account
He built ships on the Nile, brought most of eastern Sudan under Egypt, and built an arsenal in Alexandria. He fought the Saudis and expelled them from the Hijaz, and seized Syria, which was later taken from him. The Ottoman state made Egypt and Sudan a khedivate for his heirs to inherit, in 1256 AH. {cite:al-aalam}

Schools and factories multiplied in his time, and he sent missions to acquire learning in Europe. {cite:al-aalam} His date of birth is confused in the printed text, and the commonly given date is 1769 CE. {cite:al-aalam}

## Assessments
The Encyclopaedia of Islam weighs the cost of his rule. Conscription and forced labour fell heavily on peasants, many were taken in chains, and the benefits went mainly to a small military-administrative elite; it calls the balance sheet rather mixed. It adds that he was often idolised in Egypt and sometimes seen as a precursor of nationalism, and that Europe knew him as a progressive moderniser, "Founder of Modern Egypt". The article's own view is that, myths aside, he was an Ottoman officer whose language was Turkish and who spoke neither Arabic, French nor English, yet made Egypt his seat. It credits him with the conscript army, modern education and a centralised state. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 482}
