## Summary
Nouman Ali Khan is an American Muslim speaker of Pakistani Punjabi descent, born in East Berlin in 1978, and the founder of the Bayyinah Institute for Arabic and Qur'anic Studies in Irving, Texas. He became known for lectures on the Qur'an and classical Arabic that spread widely online, and for speaking about the challenges facing Muslim communities in the United States. The Royal Islamic Strategic Studies Centre in Jordan included him in The Muslim 500, its list of the world's most influential Muslims. {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-en} {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-ar}

He chose the name "Bayyinah" — an Arabic adjective meaning "that which is clear in itself" — for his institute, to reflect his goal of making the Qur'an directly comprehensible to Muslims. {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-en}

## Early life and education
He was born in East Berlin to a Pakistani Punjabi family and spent his preschool years there. His father moved to work at the Pakistan Embassy in Riyadh, and Khan attended the Pakistan Embassy School from second through eighth grade, before the family moved to New York while he was a teenager. {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-en} {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-ar}

The English article recounts that in his youth he drifted from faith and briefly identified as an atheist. His return began when a fellow college student who offered him a ride stopped to pray and Khan joined him, and when he attended nightly Qur'an-translation lectures at Taraweeh prayers given by the visiting Pakistani scholar Dr. Abdus Samee — a turning point that led him to learn classical Arabic in order to study the Qur'an in its original language. {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-en}

## Career and the Bayyinah Institute
Before religious education, Khan worked in information technology. He taught Arabic at Nassau Community College until 2006, when he began working full-time on Bayyinah. {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-en} {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-ar}

He founded the Bayyinah Institute for Arabic and Qur'anic Studies in 2006, based in Irving, Texas. The sources report that he has taught over 10,000 students through the institute's seminars and programs, and that the institute operates without reliance on fundraising, describing itself as financially self-sustaining through program fees. {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-en}

Khan has spoken publicly about the challenges facing American Muslim communities, including the shortage of American-born imams: having visited roughly 150 mosques across the country, he noted that most lacked a full-time imam, and described Bayyinah's role in training future religious leaders to fill that gap. He has also addressed the generational challenge, noting that foreign-born imams may connect with older congregants but struggle to reach younger American-born Muslims. {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-en}

## Books
His books include *Divine Speech: Exploring Qur'an as Literature* (Bayyinah Publishing, 2016), *Revive Your Heart: Putting Life in Perspective* (Kube Publishing, 2017), and the *Arabic With Husna* course books published by his institute. {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-en} {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-ar}

## Perspectives and criticism
This section sets out different positions as reported in the sources. It is not a verdict by the museum.

**The 2017 allegations.** In early 2017, a panel of four Muslim clerics began investigating reports about Khan's conduct with women. Their written summary, obtained by BuzzFeed News, found that Khan had used his religious celebrity to groom female admirers into "secret sham marriages" — relationships with no legal standing that in some cases "culminated in sex" — that he lied to the women about his marital status, sent sexually suggestive messages, and, when confronted, attempted to buy their silence through payments or threatened them. Khan had agreed to cease public speaking and undergo counselling, but the panel found he broke the agreement, resumed speaking and contacting women, and had his attorney threaten lawsuits against the mediators. {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-en}

The matter became public in September 2017, when Omer M. Mozaffar, a Muslim chaplain who had separately been mediating between Khan and his accusers, posted that Khan had "confessed inappropriate interactions with various women." A second panel of six Muslim clerics and academics conducted a separate inquiry and affirmed that Khan had "engaged in conduct unbecoming of any believer, much less someone who teaches about the Holy Qur'an," adding that they "unequivocally recognize and support survivors of abuse who are often silenced in our communities." {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-en}

**Khan's response.** Khan acknowledged contact with women but called the conversations "between consenting adults," saying he was seeking to remarry after his divorce. His attorney called the allegations "unfounded and clearly driven by a damaging motive." {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-en}

**The Irving mosque case (2018).** A 2018 report by Facing Abuse in Community Environments (FACE) found that Khan, while board president of the Islamic Center of Irving, had discouraged a woman from reporting sexual abuse by the mosque's imam, Zia Ul-Haq Sheikh, advising her to seek mental-health services and warning that disclosure would harm Sheikh's reputation. Khan stepped down from the board shortly after Sheikh's resignation. {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-en}

The controversy prompted broader discussion in American Muslim communities about accountability for religious leaders. Ingrid Mattson, an Islamic studies professor and former president of the Islamic Society of North America who participated in one of the inquiries, noted that most Muslim institutions have "so little oversight of the religious leadership" and lack accessible mechanisms for reporting violations. {cite:wikipedia-nouman-ali-khan-en}
