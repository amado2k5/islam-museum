## Summary
Abu Hatim Muhammad ibn Hibban al-Busti, the hadith master, "author of *al-Anwa'*, and of the two books on criticism and vindication, and other works", as [[person/al-dhahabi|al-Dhahabi]] describes him in [[work/mizan-al-itidal|Mizan al-I'tidal]]. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 506} Al-Dhahabi says he was one of the imams of his time, foremost in knowledge of hadith. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 506} He died in the year 354. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 508}

## Study, travel and judgeship
Ibn Hibban sought knowledge at the turn of the year 300 and met Abu Khalifa and [[person/al-nasai|Abu Abd al-Rahman al-Nasa'i]]. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 506} He wrote down hadith in Syria, the Hijaz, Egypt, Iraq, the Jazira and Khurasan, and heard in [[place/bukhara|Bukhara]] from Umar ibn Muhammad ibn Bujayr. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 506} He served for a time as judge of [[place/samarqand|Samarqand]], and was learned in medicine, astronomy, theology and law. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 506} Some years before 340 he settled in [[place/nishapur|Nishapur]], built the khanqah there and taught his works, then went back to his homeland. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 507}

## What was held against him
[[person/ibn-al-salah|Abu Amr ibn al-Salah]] mentioned him in his generations of the Shafi'is and said he erred grossly in his handling of material, and al-Dhahabi agrees: "Abu Amr spoke the truth." {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 507} Al-Dhahabi adds that he made many mistakes, some of which the hafiz Diya' al-Din traced, and that a slip of his became the reason he was attacked. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 507}

**The question of the "limit".** [[person/al-ansari-al-harawi-abd-allah|Shaykh al-Islam Abu Isma'il al-Ansari]] reported Yahya ibn Ammar saying: I saw him, and we expelled him from [[place/sijistan|Sijistan]]; he had much learning but not much religion; he came to us and denied that God has a limit (*hadd*), so we expelled him. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 507} Al-Dhahabi comments that his denying the limit and their affirming it are a kind of superfluous theology, and that silence on both is better, since no text came either denying or affirming it. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 507} He explains that the one who affirms it can be told: you have given God a limit by your own opinion, and what is limited is created; and the one who denies it can be told: you have made your Lord equal to the non-existent, for the non-existent has no limit; whoever declares God transcendent and keeps silent is safe and follows the early generations. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 507}

**His saying "prophethood is knowledge and action".** Abu Isma'il al-Ansari reported, from Abd al-Samad ibn Muhammad from his father, that people condemned Ibn Hibban's saying "prophethood is knowledge and action", judged him a heretic and shunned him, and that the caliph was written to and ordered him killed; he heard from someone else that this is why he was sent out to Samarqand. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 507} Al-Dhahabi holds that the saying has an acceptable reading if he meant that the pillars of prophethood are knowledge and action, since God gave prophethood only to those who had both. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 507} He adds that knowledge by revelation is certain with no conjecture in it, whereas the knowledge of others is partly certain and mostly conjectural, and that prophethood goes with protection from error, which no one else has, however far they reach in knowledge and action. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 508} He compares it to the hadith "the Hajj is Arafa": a statement about a thing can be true of some of its pillars and its chief aim. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 508} He then says that no one may use such a phrase without a context that shows its meaning, and that if Ibn Hibban meant it exclusively — that prophethood is nothing but knowledge and action — this is heresy and philosophy. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 508}

## Lineage and life as Ibn Balban records it
In the introduction to *al-Ihsan*, [[person/ibn-balban-ala-al-din-ali|al-Amir 'Ala' al-Din Ibn Balban]] gives his lineage: Muhammad ibn Hibban ibn Ahmad ibn Hibban ibn Mu'adh ibn Ma'bad ibn Sahid (or Ma'bad ibn Hadiyya), going back to Darim ibn Malik of the Hanzala of Tamim — Abu Hatim al-Tamimi al-Busti, the judge. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 51-52} He quotes [[person/al-hakim-al-nisaburi|al-Hakim]] that he was a vessel of learning in language, law, hadith and preaching and one of the most intelligent of men; he came to Nishapur and heard 'Abd Allah ibn Shiruya there, then went to Iraq and wrote much from [[person/abu-khalifa-al-jumhi|Abu Khalifa the judge]], heard in Ahwaz, Mosul, the Jazira, Syria, Egypt and the Hijaz, wrote in Herat, Merv and Bukhara, travelled to 'Umar ibn Muhammad ibn Bujayr and wrote much from him, and narrated from [[person/al-hasan-ibn-sufyan-al-nasawi|al-Hasan ibn Sufyan]] and [[person/abu-yala-al-mawsili|Abu Ya'la al-Mawsili]]. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 52-53}

He then served as judge in Samarqand and other towns of Khurasan, came to Nishapur in 334 AH and left it for the judgeship of [[place/nasa|Nasa]], returned in 337, settled there and built the khanqah; al-Hakim and others narrated from him. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 53} Ibn Balban also quotes Abu Sa'd al-Idrisi: he was one of the jurists and hadith masters, learned in medicine, astronomy and the arts of knowledge, author of *al-Musnad al-Sahih*, *al-Tarikh* and *al-Du'afa'*, who taught law in Samarqand and then moved to Bust; and al-Khatib: he was trustworthy, firm, excellent and perceptive. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 54}

He died in Sijistan on the night of Friday with eight nights left of Shawwal 354 AH — or, some say, in Bust, in his house, which became a school for his followers and a lodging for visiting hadith students and law students, with stipends and a library. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 54} [[person/ahmad-muhammad-shakir|Ahmad Muhammad Shakir]] notes that he found no statement of his birth date; since the sources say he died in his late seventies, Shakir thinks he was probably born around 280 AH. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 43}

For the preface, condition and invented arrangement of his *Sahih* see [[work/sahih-ibn-hibban|Sahih Ibn Hibban]]; for the debate over his laxity see [[question/tasahul-ibn-hibban|the question card]].
