## Overview
Francis Bacon was an English thinker who lived between the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries; the book Min al-Falsafa al-Yunaniyya ila al-Falsafa al-Haditha presents his core idea: reforming the sciences through the inductive rather than the syllogistic method, and establishing an experimental method for the natural sciences {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 350}.

## Life
He was born in London and entered the University of Cambridge, where he did not complete his studies, leaving dissatisfied with the sciences taught there in the manner of Aristotle and the Scholastics. He then studied law and practiced as a lawyer, was elected a member of the House of Commons, was appointed counselor by the Queen, and under King James I rose to the office of Lord Chancellor, as the book recounts {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 349}.

His political career ended, in the book's account, when the House of Commons accused him of bribery and embezzling state funds; he confessed to what was charged against him, and the House of Lords sentenced him to a fine of forty thousand pounds and barred him from holding public office, sitting in Parliament, and residing near the King {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 349}.

## Works
From the age of twenty-five, Bacon was gripped by an idea: the reform of the sciences through induction. Among his most famous books are Novum Organum, in which he opposed the old logic of Aristotle, The Advancement of Learning, and New Atlantis, which depicts a republic whose citizens cooperate to advance the sciences {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 350}.

## Method
The book divides Novum Organum into two parts. The first is critical, examining the errors into which the mind falls, which Bacon grouped into four types he called the Idols of the Mind: Idols of the Tribe, which are human shortcomings that imagine and generalize without justification; Idols of the Cave, which are an individual's habits and upbringing that obscure the truth; Idols of the Market, which arise from language and poor communication; and Idols of the Theatre, which are deference to the sayings of scholars and philosophers without investigation {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 351}{cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 352}.

The second part sets out the foundations of the experimental method, as Bacon held that Aristotelian logic does not uncover new ideas and that experiment is the best teacher {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 353}. His method has four stages:

- Collecting the facts about every phenomenon through both observation and experiment, as he did when he wrote a history of the winds {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 353}{cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 354}.
- Arranging the facts in three tables: the table of presence, the table of absence, and the table of degrees. In the example of heat, the book mentions that he collected twenty-seven instances for the first table, thirty-two for the second, and forty-one for the third {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 355}.
- True induction, which is comparing the tables and extracting the essential properties that accompany the phenomenon in both presence and absence {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 356}.
- Testing hypotheses, because the conclusion derived by induction remains a hypothesis subject to error until it is verified {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 356}.

## Purpose of the Method
By his method, Bacon aimed to know the forms of things—that is, their essential properties by which things are what they are—and the laws and causes of nature. This knowledge benefits humanity in making use of nature, while at the same time leading it to the knowledge of God, who gave nature its fixed laws {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 357}{cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 358}.

## Impact
The book holds that Bacon laid down the principles of the experimental method, that modern scientific research in Europe followed these principles, and that later thinkers came and completed and expanded the method. It notes that John Stuart Mill derived the five methods of experimental inquiry from the three tables {cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 355}{cite:min-al-falsafa-al-yunaniyya-azhar p. 358}.

## For Young Visitors
Imagine asking: how do we discover the laws of nature? Bacon used to say: do not settle for what adults say—observe phenomena with your own eyes, carry out experiments with your own hands, and then compare the results.
