## Overview
Heraclius, called Hirakl in Arabic and often referred to by the imperial title Kaysar, was emperor of the [[polity/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]] from 610 to 641. He was the victor who took [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]] back from the [[polity/sasanian-empire|Sasanian]] Persians, then lived to see Syria fall to the armies of a faith born during his reign. The Encyclopaedia of Islam has no entry under his name, so this page gathers what its articles on the title Kaysar, the Sasanians, the Yarmuk, Egypt, Damascus and other subjects say about him. In the Arabic sources he appears in two settings: as the foreign ruler who examined a letter from the Prophet, and as the emperor whose armies lost Syria and Egypt. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 877} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 177}

## Life
Heraclius came to the throne of an empire on the edge: the Sasanians had taken Syria, Egypt and, in 614, Jerusalem itself, carrying off the relic of the True Cross. In a six-year counter-invasion he broke the Persian Empire and restored the Cross to Jerusalem in 629 {cite:kaegi-2003}.

In those same years, the hadith tradition preserves, a letter arrived from a prophet in Arabia summoning him to Islam. Heraclius is shown questioning the merchant [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-harb|Abu Sufyan]], then still an opponent of [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]], and concluding that what he described bore the marks of prophecy {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}. Whether the audience happened as told is debated; what is certain is that within a decade his armies met the Muslims and lost Syria {cite:kaegi-2003}.

He died in February 641, having won the greatest war of his century and lost the peace to a force no strategist had planned for.

## Rise and the Persian war
The Encyclopaedia places his accession in a time of upheaval. In Egypt the strife between his predecessor Phocas and Heraclius turned the country into a battleground, and the article on Egypt says his victory was followed by a period of pacification and consolidation, soon broken by the Persian conquest of Egypt, dated 619 to 629 or 630. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 177}

The article on the Sasanians describes the last great Sasanian-Byzantine war in phases. After Heraclius took the throne, Khusraw II, here [[person/khosrow-ii|Khosrow II]], aimed to conquer the whole Byzantine Empire. His general [[person/shahrbaraz|Shahrbaraz]] took Antioch and Damascus, entered the Galilee and the Jordan valley, and besieged Jerusalem in May 614. When it fell, the article reports, tens of thousands of Christians were killed, three hundred monasteries and churches burned, and some 35,000 captives, with the Patriarch and the relic of the True Cross, deported into Sasanian territory. From 616 to 620 Shahrbaraz conquered Egypt. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 88}

In the third phase the Byzantines took the war to the Persians. In 622 and 624 Heraclius invaded Armenia, drove Khusraw out of Ganzak and Shiz, and in 625 defeated three Sasanian armies in Adharbaydjan and Armenia. In 626 the Sasanians, allied with the Avars, failed to take Constantinople, while the Byzantine siege of Tiflis, in alliance with the [[group/khazars|Khazars]], also failed. In 627 Heraclius invaded again with Greek, Laz and Iberian troops and Khazar cavalry, defeated Sasanian forces at Nineveh, and destroyed the royal palace at Dastagerd. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 88} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 89}

In February 628 a group of generals and officials imprisoned Khusraw and proclaimed his son [[person/shiruyeh-ibn-parviz|Kubadh II]], who opened peace talks based on mutual evacuation and the freeing of prisoners. In the summer of 629 Shahrbaraz negotiated with Heraclius on his own, evacuated Egypt and Syria and returned the True Cross to him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 89} The article on Damascus adds that the Persians had occupied the city in 612, evacuated it in 627, and that Heraclius returned the following year. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 293}

## The letter of the Prophet
Arabic writers call Heraclius Hirakl or Kaysar al-Rum. The article on Kaysar notes that a number of texts mention a letter the Prophet is said to have sent through Dihya to the governor of Busra and through him to Heraclius. In many versions of the report the emperor questions Abu Sufyan about the new prophet and, unlike the Persian king, seems inclined towards Islam; only fear of his subjects keeps him from professing it openly. Some versions give the letter without the conversation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 877} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 878}

The article on [[person/dihya-al-kalbi|Dihya al-Kalbi]] says that an account it cites reports he was given the task of carrying the Prophet's message to Heraclius, inviting him to Islam, in the sixth or seventh year of the Hijra, and that on his return his caravan was plundered by the Judham. It adds that several orientalists have noted legendary features in the account and questioned the authenticity of the letter, while Muhammad Hamidullah has argued against them. The same article records that Dihya later commanded a small detachment at the Yarmuk. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 289}

A further tradition, found in al-Bukhari, concerns the emperor's horoscope. Heraclius read in the stars that the king of the circumcised had appeared; an envoy of the Ghassanid king then arrived with news of Muhammad's preaching and, being circumcised himself, told the emperor that circumcision was an Arab custom. The article on circumcision treats this as one of the places where hadith mentions the practice. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 38}

The article on Kaysar also records, as reported in al-Bukhari, that at Hudaybiyya [[person/urwa-ibn-masud|Urwa ibn Masud]] told the Meccans he had been an envoy to the kings, Kaysar, Kisra and [[person/al-najashi|al-Najashi]], and had never seen a king honoured as Muhammad was by his Companions. It also reports a saying ascribed to the Prophet that when Kisra and Kaysar perish, there will be no Kisra or Kaysar after them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 877}

## Syria and the battle of the Yarmuk
The article on the Yarmuk says Heraclius, who was not present in person, gathered a very large Byzantine and Christian Arab force in early 636 to reverse Muslim victories in Syria, Transjordan and Palestine. The battle lasted more than a month if the preliminary manoeuvres are counted and ended on 12 Rajab 15/20 August 636. The Byzantine commanders included the general Vahan and Theodore Trithurios, and the Ghassanid king [[person/jabala-ibn-al-ayham|Jabala ibn al-Ayham]] led the Ghassanid forces, joined by other Christian Arabs whom Heraclius had recruited. Reports differ on whether Heraclius's brother Theodore was present; the article thinks he had probably been recalled to Constantinople in disgrace before the end. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 301} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302}

On the Muslim side, [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah]] withdrew to a strong position, and [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] seized in a night raid the bridge that was the Byzantines' only retreat route. The article judges that the battle destroyed the only viable Byzantine army in Syria and broke the Byzantines' will to give open battle. It adds that the ruling dynasty seems to have tried to shift blame, to an alleged rebellion by Vahan or to failure to heed Heraclius's warnings about ambushes, and that no evidence shows Christian religious dissension affected the outcome. It cautions that all figures for the battle should be regarded with suspicion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302} The article on Damascus also names a brother of Heraclius as commanding the army that moved on the city in the spring of 636. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 294}

## Egypt
The article on Egypt says Heraclius took great pains to restore order there, yet it was still in disarray when the Arabs attacked. In 631 he appointed Cyrus, called [[person/al-muqawqis|al-Muqawqis]] in the Arabic sources, as prefect, army commander and patriarch of the whole country, to secure political and religious uniformity. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 177} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5.1 p. 108} While [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn al-As]] besieged Babylon, Cyrus began talks for a treaty, and Heraclius recalled and reprimanded him for it. The article on Egypt says the death of Heraclius hit the morale of the defenders of the fortress, and that his successors, including Heraclius Junior reigning with his mother Martina, chose pacification and surrender, reinstating Cyrus with full power to conclude a treaty. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 177} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 178} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 180}

## Legacy
In the Muslim sources Heraclius is the honest foreign examiner: the ruler who asked careful questions and saw the answer clearly. In Byzantine memory, the tragic emperor who rebuilt and lost.

The article on Kaysar describes how Arab attitudes to the title changed. In the Meccan period the struggle between Byzantium and Persia is echoed in Sura al-Rum, but in the Medinan period the attitude reversed on religious grounds: Byzantine territory counted as part of the Abode of War and war against Kaysar as jihad, and the Arab victories strengthened that stance. The article adds that the image of Kaysar afterwards rose and fell with the military fortunes of the two states. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 877} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 878}

## For young visitors
The most powerful Christian emperor of his time received a letter from a prophet in Arabia, and asked about him so carefully that he understood before most of Arabia did.
