# Abdurreshid Ibrahim

## Summary
Abdurreshid Ibrahim (1857–1944) was a Tatar scholar, journalist and traveller born at Tara in Siberia, who served as a qadi of the Orenburg Muslim Spiritual Assembly, became a leader of Ittifaq al-Muslimin, and spent decades travelling through Europe, the Middle East and Asia advocating the unity of Muslim peoples. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-en} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr} His two-volume travel account, Alem-i Islam ("The World of Islam"), introduced Japan to Ottoman readers. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-ar} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr} In 1933 he returned to Japan, built the Tokyo Mosque and became its first imam, and he died at Tokyo in 1944. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-ar}

His birth date carries a calendar split that the museum discloses rather than resolves silently: the English and Turkish articles give 23 April 1857, but Wikidata stores that day value under the Julian calendar model, so the date as transmitted is Old Style (New Style: 5 May 1857); the Arabic article independently gives his birth year in Hijri as 1273, matching the tabular conversion of 23 April 1857. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-en} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr} {cite:wikidata-abdurreshid-ibrahim} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-ar}

## Career
He was born at Tara, now in Omsk oblast, the son of Gumer (Omar), descended from the Siberian Bukharans, and identified himself as a Tatar. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-en} He began school at seven, entered the Almenevo village madrasa at ten, was orphaned at seventeen, and continued his studies at Tyumen and at the Qışqar madrasa; in 1878–1879 he taught in the Akmolinsk region. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-en} From 1879 to 1885 he continued his education in Medina, Mecca and Istanbul; the Turkish article specifies five years at Medina ending in an ijaza. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-en} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr} Returning to Russia in 1885, he served as imam-khatib of the cathedral mosque in Tara and taught at its madrasa, and in 1892–1894 he served as qadi of the Orenburg Muslim Spiritual Assembly. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-en}

From 1897 he travelled to build a united anti-Russian Muslim front, visited the Ottoman Empire, Europe, the Middle East and Central Asia, and in 1900 began publishing the Chagatai-language magazine Mir'at ("Mirror") in St Petersburg. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-en} His first Japan visit of 1902–1903, spent on anti-Russian propaganda, ended with his expulsion at the Russian consul's request; in 1904 he was arrested in Istanbul, handed to the Russian consul and sent under guard to Odessa, and was released at the turn of 1905–1906. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-en} In St Petersburg he set up a printing house and published the journals Ülfet, the Arabic Tilmiz and the Kazakh Serke, each closed by the authorities in turn. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr} He became a leader of the Ittifaq al-Muslimin movement and an organiser of the Muslim congresses of 1905–1906, sitting on the party's central committee in 1905–1907. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-en} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr}

His second great journey (1908–1910) took him through Russian and Chinese Turkestan, Mongolia, Japan — where he learned Japanese and founded the Asya Gikai association — Korea, China, Singapore and India, and ended with the Hajj and his return to Istanbul by the Hejaz railway. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-en} In 1909 he spent June to September in China studying its Muslims; he praised their commitment but criticised their ahongs as stubborn when corrected and rejected the traditional narrative that Islam had entered China in the Prophet's own era. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-en} He became an Ottoman citizen in 1912, took part in the Tripolitanian war of 1911 and the Balkan wars, and in the First World War worked among Tatar prisoners of war in Germany. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-ar}

After a third journey in 1918 he settled at Böğrüdelik near Konya, living from 1925 in voluntary exile as a farmer until 1933. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-ar} On 12 October 1933, aged 76, he arrived back in Japan, had the plans for the Tokyo Mosque drawn up and its foundation laid; the mosque opened to worship in 1937 (the Arabic article dates its completion to 1938) and he became its first imam, a post he held until his death. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-ar} He died at Tokyo — Wikidata gives 31 August 1944, the Turkish article 17 August 1944; the record follows Wikidata with the split stated — and was buried in the Muslim cemetery at Tamareien. {cite:wikidata-abdurreshid-ibrahim} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr}

## Works
His best-known work is Alem-i Islam, the account of his travels, first published in Istanbul, which presented Japan's ethics and the secret of its rise to Ottoman readers and built, in the Arabic article's words, a bridge between the Islamic world and Japan. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-ar} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr} The Turkish article names his brochure Çoban Yıldızı ("The Shepherd's Star"), describing Russian oppression of its Muslim peoples, his "Bin Üç Yüz Senelik Nazra" on Muslim unity, and his brochure "Aftonomiya" on autonomy; in St Petersburg he published the journals Ülfet, Tilmiz and Serke, and the English article adds the Chagatai magazine Mir'at of 1900. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-en}

## Positions and criticism
This section sets out positions as reported in the sources; it is not a verdict by the museum.

**Pan-Islamic unity and migration.** In his youth he followed Jadid ideas and aimed, in the English article's words, to liberate all Muslim peoples from colonial oppression; his Istanbul brochure inviting Russia's Muslims to emigrate to Ottoman lands is credited by the Turkish article with prompting the migration of some 70,000 Muslim Turks to Anatolia. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-en} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-tr} The Arabic biography presents the same advocacy in devotional terms, and its larger claims — that he spent twenty years in the Hijaz, where the English article gives a study period of 1879–1885 across three cities, and that he died at nearly a hundred years of age, where his 1857 birth makes him 87 — are flagged in that source's record and not followed here. {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-ar} {cite:wikipedia-abdurreshid-ibrahim-en}

**His faith.** His qadi service of 1892–1894 was an office of the Orenburg assembly; his pan-Islamism is a political ideology; and his Tokyo imamate was a post he held in old age. No consulted source names a school for him, and his faith is therefore recorded as unknown in his data record. {cite:wikidata-abdurreshid-ibrahim}
