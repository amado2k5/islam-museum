## Summary
Aziz Abu Sarah is a Palestinian peace activist from Jerusalem who converted the death of his elder brother in Israeli custody into a life's work of building bridges between Palestinians and Israelis. He is among the most visible young figures in conflict resolution, combining field work in schools and universities, international media, and advisory roles in research and multilateral institutions. {cite:wikipedia-aziz-abu-sarah}

## Upbringing and transformation
He was born in 1980 in Jerusalem. His elder brother Taysir died in 1990 from injuries sustained in Israeli detention after being accused of stone-throwing in the First Intifada, and anger grew in him for years. He learned Hebrew in a language course in West Jerusalem, began working with Israeli-Palestinian dialogue initiatives, and later recounted that the first Jew he spoke to as a human rather than a soldier changed his course. {cite:wikipedia-aziz-abu-sarah}

## Career
He studied journalism and worked in media and civil-society organisations, founded Mejdi Tours for dual-narrative tourism offering Israeli-Palestinian tours guided by two narrators, and led educational projects in Jerusalem schools and colleges. He served as executive director of the Center for World Religions, Diplomacy and Conflict Resolution (CRDC) at George Mason University in Washington, founded the Empower initiative, and co-led dialogue efforts including InterAct and Hands of Peace. He has written for National Geographic, the New York Times and others on the crossing narratives of the conflict. {cite:wikipedia-aziz-abu-sarah} {cite:muslim500-aziz-abu-sarah}

## Recognition and international presence
He has received the UN Intercultural Innovation Award with his organisations, the Einstein Legacy Award, and the Sophia Prize for peacebuilding; he was named an Ashoka fellow, lectured at the TED conference — his talks on brotherhood in pain are widely circulated — and addressed UN Security Council discussions on war and peace. {cite:wikipedia-aziz-abu-sarah}

## Debate and legacy
His dialogue approach draws criticism from some Palestinians who see it as normalisation weakening resistance, and from some Israelis who see it as equating unequal parties; he answers that recognising the other's narrative does not mean conceding rights — the museum reports these positions attributed. His legacy is the "dual narrative" model: turning personal pain into an instrument of peace and making tourism, education and media arenas of mutual recognition. {cite:wikipedia-aziz-abu-sarah}
