## Summary
Abu l-Abbas Ahmad ibn Abi Ya'qub ibn Ja'far ibn Wahb ibn Wadih al-Ya'qubi — usually called simply al-Ya'qubi, and in the medieval sources more often Ibn Wadih — was an Abbasid historian and geographer whose two surviving books stand near the head of both Arabic traditions: the Ta'rikh, a universal history running down to 873, and the Kitab al-Buldan of about 891, one of the earliest works of Arabic administrative geography. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} {cite:wikidata-al-yaqubi} The Arabic biographical tradition gives his name in a different form, Ahmad ibn Ishaq, and both forms are recorded in this entry. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-ar} No source records his birth year: modern historians estimate that he was born between 230 and 240 AH (844–854 CE), and the museum's queue figure of 812 is attested nowhere. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} He died in Egypt shortly after 908, as the last section of this article explains; the familiar date of 897 is a medieval mix-up. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en}

## A secretary's career
Al-Ya'qubi came from a family of Abbasid clients: his great-grandfather Wadih was a freedman of the caliph al-Mansur, his ancestors had settled in Baghdad when al-Mansur founded it, and one forebear served as a court steward under al-Mansur and Harun al-Rashid. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} He was himself a katib, a chancery secretary — "Ahmad the Scribe" — and the official gown he wore marked him as a civil administrator rather than a religious scholar. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} The preface to the Kitab al-Buldan is his own account of a travelling youth: "I had traveled since childhood, and my travels had continued uninterruptedly and had taken me to distant places." {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} A fragment preserved by Ibn al-Faqih puts him for a long period in Armenia, as secretary to its local rulers; in his youth he entered Tahirid service in Khurasan, at Nishapur and Isfahan, until the Saffarids deposed the dynasty in 872–873. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-ur} He then moved to Egypt and joined the administration of the Tulunids, serving in 878 as administrator of the land tax in Barqa during the revolt of Ahmad ibn Tulun's son al-Abbas, and he remained in Egypt through the Tulunid collapse. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} Older accounts sent him to India and China in person; modern research holds that his descriptions of those lands were compiled by interviewing merchants at Persian Gulf ports and during the pilgrimage. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en}

## The works
Classical bibliographers credited al-Ya'qubi with more than eighty titles; three survive. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} The Ta'rikh, composed around 872–873, is one of the earliest narrative digests in classical Arabic: its first volume treats the biblical patriarchs and prophets — drawing on Syriac Christian texts such as the Cave of Treasures — and the histories of Assyria, Babylon, India, Greece, Rome, Sasanian Persia and the pre-Islamic Arabs, and its second covers Islamic history from the Prophet's time through the Umayyads to the caliphate of al-Mu'tamid. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-ur} The Kitab al-Buldan, written in Egypt late in his career, opens with an exhaustive topography of Baghdad and Samarra — quarters, canals, markets and land grants — and then organises the wider world into four quarters, recording postal distances, tax revenues, crops and populations; its unique Munich manuscript has a large gap that later geographers' quotations only partly fill. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-ar} The third survivor, Mushakalat al-nas li-zamanihim, is a short essay on how subjects take on the habits of their rulers, caliph by caliph from Abu Bakr to al-Mu'tadid. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en}

## The question of his allegiance
What al-Ya'qubi believed has been argued since 1876, when M. J. de Goeje called him a "full-blooded Shi'ite", a verdict M. Th. Houtsma reaffirmed in 1883. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} Houtsma, Brockelmann and Yves Marquet went further and hypothesised that he belonged to the Waqifa, the party that held the imamate to have ended with the seventh imam, Musa al-Kazim. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} William Millward and Marquet showed in the 1970s that his outlook, while broadly pro-Shi'ite, drew on a diverse range of non-sectarian sources and served no single faction's interests. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} Elton Daniel challenged the consensus in 2004: the textual rubrics used to prove Shi'i bias, he argued, are scribal artefacts of seventeenth-century Safavid-era manuscripts, and al-Ya'qubi's reverence for the Prophet's family reflects the Hashimi loyalty of an Abbasid client house. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} Sean Anthony's 2016 study answered that his narrative of the early caliphate expresses a distinctively rejectionist — Rafidi — Shi'ite stance, and recent Arabic scholarship describes him as fundamentally Imami. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} The Arabic biographical tradition is itself divided: Muhsin al-Amin's A'yan al-Shia claims him as a Shi'i notable, while other scholars affirm he was a Sunni. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-ar} Because the specific attributions conflict — Waqifi against Imami, and the general Shi'i reading against a reasoned Sunni one — this record states the debate and settles nothing. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-ar}

## Death — when?
Yaqut, citing the lost work of the Egyptian historian al-Kindi, dated al-Ya'qubi's death to 284 AH (897 CE), and the Arabic and Urdu articles still give that year, the Arabic one adding a variant report of 298 AH. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-ar} {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-ur} Wikidata gives 905. {cite:wikidata-al-yaqubi} The Brill biographical sketch of 2018 shows that the 284 date belongs to somebody else: Abu Ja'far Ahmad ibn Ishaq ibn Wadih al-Assal, a contemporary Egyptian hadith scholar who died in Safar 284 AH and with whom al-Ya'qubi was conflated. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en} Al-Ya'qubi's own verses on the fall of the Tulunids in 292 AH (905) and his quatrain welcoming the death of the caliph al-Muktafi in 295 AH (908) prove he outlived both events by years, and he is believed to have died in Egypt shortly after 908 — the date this record carries, marked as debated. {cite:wikipedia-al-yaqubi-en}
