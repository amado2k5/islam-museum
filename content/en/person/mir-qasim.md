## Summary

Mir Qasim (died 8 May 1777) was Nawab of Bengal from 20 October 1760 to 1763. Installed by the East India Company in place of his father-in-law Mir Jafar, he broke with his makers over trade and reform, fought them in alliance with Shuja-ud-Daula and the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II, and was defeated at Buxar on 22 October 1764 {cite:banglapedia-mir-qasim}.

## Reform and war

Mir Muhammad Qasim Ali Khan, son of Mir Razi Khan, bought the masnad with a large payment to the Company and promises to its officers, then set about escaping their control {cite:wikipedia-mir-qasim-en}. He moved his capital from Murshidabad to Monghyr, closer to Bihar, rebuilt his army on European lines, tightened the revenue administration, and, when Company servants abused their duty-free trade passes, abolished all internal duties so that Indian merchants could compete {cite:banglapedia-mir-qasim}. The quarrel became war in 1763: his troops seized Patna, he was deposed on 7 July, and in October 1764 the confederate army he had gathered with the nawab of Awadh and the emperor met Major Munro at Buxar and was decisively beaten {cite:banglapedia-mir-qasim}. His last years were spent a wanderer and in poverty; he died on 8 May 1777 at Kotwal, near Delhi {cite:wikipedia-mir-qasim-en}.

## Faith

No textual source consulted names a branch or school for Mir Qasim. The Arabic article's infobox records his religion as Shia Muslim {cite:wikipedia-mir-qasim-ar}; his family was a sayyid family claiming descent from Ali al-Ridha through a line settled from Najaf, a lineage claim the English article itself flags as needing a citation {cite:wikipedia-mir-qasim-en}; and Banglapedia, the independent encyclopedia entry, says nothing about his religion at all {cite:banglapedia-mir-qasim}. Descent and branch-only infobox attestation do not settle a man's affiliation, so the museum records his faith as unattested and preserves the Shia attestation here.
