# Saladin (Salah al-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub)

## Summary

Salah al-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub, known in Europe as Saladin, was sultan of Egypt and Syria and founder of the Ayyubid dynasty. He was born at Tikrit in 1137 or 1138 into a prominent Kurdish family, grew up in Baalbek and Damascus, and died at Damascus on 4 March 1193. {cite:britannica-saladin}

## From vizier to sultan

He began his career on the staff of his uncle Asad al-Din Shirkuh during the campaigns in Egypt. After Shirkuh's death he became, in 1169, commander of the Syrian troops in Egypt and vizier of the Fatimid caliph. In 1171 he ended the Fatimid caliphate and restored the Abbasid khutba. {cite:britannica-saladin} After Nur al-Din Mahmud died in 1174 he moved into Syria, and between 1174 and 1186 worked to unite Syria, northern Mesopotamia, Palestine and Egypt under his rule, by diplomacy and, when needed, by force. {cite:britannica-saladin}

## Hattin and Jerusalem

He defeated the army of the kingdom of Jerusalem at Hattin and recovered Jerusalem on 2 October 1187, ending nearly nine decades of Frankish rule there. {cite:britannica-saladin} He then faced the Third Crusade at the siege of Acre and the battles of Arsuf and Jaffa. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir}

## From Omar Abdelkafy's series on the Hikam of Ibn Ata Allah
Omar Abdelkafy says that Saladin was shaped by what the Prophet did in the house of al-Arqam, and that throughout his life he would not smile, saying he was ashamed to laugh while Jerusalem was in the hands of the Crusaders. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep10 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep10 00:06:00}

## Salah al-Din and Cairo in Yeomans
Yeomans writes that after the death of al-Adid, the last Fatimid caliph, in 1171, Salah al-Din restored Sunni Islam with the khutba in the name of the Abbasid caliph al-Mustadi, moved the remaining Fatimid family to the Dar al-Muzaffar under house arrest, gave the two palaces to his family and amirs, and himself lived in the Dar al-Wizara. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.101} He cites al-Maqrizi that the sale of the palace contents went on for ten years. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.101} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.102} Yeomans describes him as abstemious and indifferent to luxury, relating Runciman's report from Vincent of Beauvais that the dying sultan had a rag from his shroud paraded on a lance through Damascus, and that he left only forty-seven silver dirhams and one gold ingot, while also seeing in him ambition for his family and a love of power. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.102}

In Yeomans's view war and religion, his chief concerns, left a lasting mark on Cairo: for the first time al-Qahira and Fustat were enclosed in one wall, running from the [[building/citadel-of-cairo|Citadel]] south around Fustat to Bab al-Qantara and north around al-Qahira to the Tower of al-Maqs on the Nile, a work entrusted to the eunuch Baha al-Din Qaraqush. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.104} Yeomans calls this union a political, social and religious revolution that ended the exclusivity of Fatimid al-Qahira. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.104} Ten madrasas were founded in his reign, five by him: al-Nasiriyya and al-Qamhiyya near the Mosque of Amr, al-Nasiriyya at al-Qarafa by the [[building/mausoleum-of-imam-al-shafii|tomb of al-Shafi'i]], one at the shrine of al-Husayn, and al-Qutbiyya in al-Qahira. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.111}
