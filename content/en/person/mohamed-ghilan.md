## Summary
Dr Mohamed Ghilan is a Canadian physician and neuroscientist who has built a public career at the meeting point of brain science and traditional Islamic learning. The Muslim 500 lists him in Science & Technology as a physician, neuroscientist and Islamic educator bridging the two fields — a figure of the Western Muslim intelligentsia whose audience is largely online. {cite:muslim500-mohamed-ghilan}

## Two educations
Ghilan took his doctorate in neuroscience at the University of Victoria in Canada, researching the molecular pathways of memory and neurodegeneration, and alongside his scientific training pursued Islamic studies with teachers in the traditional manner, studying jurisprudence, theology and spirituality. He later trained in medicine. The pairing — laboratory neuroscience by day, classical Islamic texts by study-circle — became the organising fact of his public work. {cite:muslim500-mohamed-ghilan}

## Teaching Islam and science
Through lectures, essays and online courses he argues that the modern sciences and the Islamic intellectual tradition can be brought into honest conversation — neither subordinating revelation to scientism nor dismissing scientific findings. He founded the Andalus Academy, an online institution offering courses in Islamic thought to English-speaking students, and writes and speaks on consciousness, evolution, materialism and the claims of the new atheism — subjects where his scientific training and religious formation intersect. {cite:muslim500-mohamed-ghilan}

## Public presence
His medium is the internet: lectures posted online, long-form essays, social-media commentary reaching an Anglophone Muslim audience seeking intellectually serious engagement between faith and science. The Muslim 500's listing recognises him among the influential Muslims of science and technology — influence measured in an audience of students rather than in laboratory citations. {cite:muslim500-mohamed-ghilan}

## Significance
In the museum's record, Ghilan stands for the diaspora scholar-educator: a Western-trained scientist who took up traditional Islamic disciplines and made their reconciliation his public vocation — the seminar room moved online, reaching students his teachers never could. {cite:muslim500-mohamed-ghilan}

## The podcast as pulpit
Ghilan's Andalus Academy format — long-form conversations about Islamic theology, Western philosophy, evolutionary biology and the ethics of modern technology — represents a new Islamic public sphere: English-language, academically literate, open to contested questions, conducted outside the traditional institutions of fatwa and seminary. His critics and his audience share a description: he asks the questions traditional sermons avoid. Whether that is a virtue or a danger is the argument his programme keeps having — and the argument that made him a figure the Muslim 500 lists among the year's influential Muslims in science and technology. {cite:muslim500-mohamed-ghilan}

## The scholar as broadcaster
Ghilan's record is the museum's example of the scholar who treats the podcast as a seminary: the neuroscientist who argues that the questions Islamic theology asks — about consciousness, about the soul, about the relationship between reason and revelation — are the questions neuroscience is now asking, and who makes the case in English for an audience that has never read the classical texts. The argument is his; the platform is the podcast; and the result — a Muslim intellectual who can speak to both the mosque and the laboratory — is the profile his Muslim 500 entry records. {cite:muslim500-mohamed-ghilan}
