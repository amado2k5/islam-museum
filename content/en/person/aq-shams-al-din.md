## Overview
Akşemseddin — his name was Şemseddin Muhammed b. Hamza — was an Ottoman scholar, Sufi and physician born at Damascus in 792 AH (1389 or 1390 CE). He was the disciple and khalifa of Haci Bayram Veli, founder of the Shamsiyya branch of the Bayramiyya order, and the tutor and adviser of Sultan Mehmed II at the conquest of Constantinople in 1453. He died at Göynük in 1459. {cite:tdv-aksemseddin} {cite:wikipedia-aq-shams-al-din-en}

## Early life and education
He was born at Damascus in 792; the Wikipedia articles pair the year with 1389, the TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi with 1390. His father, Shaykh Hamza, was a descendant of Shihab al-Din al-Suhrawardi, author of the Awarif al-Maarif, and the family's paternal lineage was traced to Abu Bakr. At about seven he came with his father to Anatolia, and the family settled at Kavak near Amasya in 799/1396-97. The sources report that he memorised the Qur'an at seven and received a strong religious education; he became a müderris at the Osmancık madrasa and also acquired a good training in medicine. {cite:tdv-aksemseddin} {cite:wikipedia-aq-shams-al-din-ar} {cite:wikipedia-aq-shams-al-din-tr}

## The Sufi path
Exoteric learning did not satisfy him. At about twenty-five, according to Enisi's Menakibname, he set out in search of a spiritual master, travelling toward Persia and Transoxiana without success. The TDV encyclopedia relates that he considered attaching himself to Haci Bayram Veli, hesitated, and went instead to Aleppo to join Zayn al-Din al-Khafi; but the scholar A. Ihsan Yurd pointed out that Akşemseddin sharply criticised al-Khafi in his Def al-Matain, and judged that he attached himself to Haci Bayram directly. The Turkish biographical tradition also tells of a dream in which he saw a chain around his neck whose other end was in Haci Bayram's hand, drawing him back to Ankara. {cite:tdv-aksemseddin} {cite:wikipedia-aq-shams-al-din-tr}

He entered Haci Bayram's discipleship and underwent a strict regime of riyazet — the cell in which he kept his retreat survives in the basement of the Hacıbayram Mosque in Ankara — and in a short time received the khilafa from his sheikh. The Urdu Wikipedia article, citing the Urdu Encyclopaedia of Islam, dates his bay'a to Haci Bayram to 830/1427 and the conferral of the khirqa of khilafa a few days later. {cite:tdv-aksemseddin} {cite:wikipedia-aq-shams-al-din-ur}

He then left his sheikh: at Beypazarı he built a mosque and a mill, but when crowds gathered around him he withdrew to the village of Evlik near İskilip, and finally settled at Göynük, where he again built a mosque and a mill and occupied himself with teaching his children and his dervishes, and made the hajj. After Haci Bayram's death in 833/1429-30 he succeeded him in the post of irshad. The Shamsiyya branch of the Bayramiyya that he founded was carried on after him by his son Fazlullah at Göynük, by Ibrahim Tennuri at Kayseri and by Hamza al-Shami around Ankara. {cite:tdv-aksemseddin}

## Tutor of the Conqueror and the siege of Constantinople
Akşemseddin had been close to his sheikh in Haci Bayram's dealings with Sultan Murad II, so he knew Murad's son Mehmed from childhood and remained in contact after his accession. Before the conquest he twice went to Mehmed at Edirne, healing Murad's kazasker Çandarlıoğlu Süleyman Çelebi on one visit and one of the sultan's daughters on the other; the princess granted him her rice farms at Beypazarı. {cite:tdv-aksemseddin}

In the spring of 1453 he joined the siege of Constantinople with hundreds of his murids. In the siege's hardest days he wrote letters to Mehmed urging patience and promising that victory was near; researchers judge their effect on the army's morale considerable. After the conquest he read the khutba at the first Friday prayer in Hagia Sophia, and at the sultan's request he located the grave of the Companion Abu Ayyub al-Ansari, who had died in one of the first Muslim sieges of the city; the Arabic narrative reports his words: "I see a light in this place — perhaps his grave is here." He spent a period in Istanbul teaching at the Zeyrek Mosque, as an inscription there shows. When Mehmed then wished to abandon his throne and become the sheikh's disciple outright, Akşemseddin firmly refused, returned to Göynük, sent back the gifts sent after him, and would accept only the building of a fountain in place of the mosque and tekke the sultan wanted to raise for him. {cite:tdv-aksemseddin} {cite:wikipedia-aq-shams-al-din-ar}

## Medicine
He was also famed as a "physician of bodies" (tabib-i ebdan). The TDV encyclopedia credits him as the first physician to raise the question of the microbe and the idea that diseases spread by contagion, at least a century before Fracastoro gave the doctrine definite form. The work attributed to him known as Maddat al-Hayat — its attribution is debated, though most researchers accept it — puts it so: "It is incorrect to assume that diseases appear one by one in humans. Disease infects by spreading from one person to another. This infection occurs through seeds that are so small they cannot be seen but are alive." He also treated disorders of the soul and spirit. {cite:tdv-aksemseddin} {cite:wikipedia-aq-shams-al-din-en}

## Works
Most of his works concern Sufism: the Arabic Risalat al-Nuriyya (written between 838 and 841 AH) in defence of Haci Bayram and his dervishes; the Def al-Matain al-Sufiyya (856/1452), answering those who charged Ibn Arabi and other great Sufis with unbelief; the Turkish Maqamat al-Awliya, on who a murshid is and the degrees of sainthood; and a versified Nasihatname. In medicine he wrote the Kitab al-Tibb and the Maddat al-Hayat. Two of his letters to Mehmed II have been published in modern editions. {cite:tdv-aksemseddin} {cite:wikipedia-aq-shams-al-din-en}

## Death and tomb
He spent his last years at Göynük and died there at the end of Rabi II 863 — February 1459; the Wikipedia articles give the day as 16 February — and his tomb there, built about five years after his death, is still visited; the inscription on its gate carries the same Hijri date as the Menakibname. Two of his sons, Mehmed Sadullah and Nurullah, are buried with him. He had seven sons; the youngest, Hamdullah Hamdi (d. 909/1503), became a noted poet. {cite:tdv-aksemseddin} {cite:wikipedia-aq-shams-al-din-tr}
