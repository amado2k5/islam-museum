## Summary
Abdul Basit Muhammad Abdus-Samad (1345–1409 AH / 1927–1988) was an Egyptian Quran reciter nicknamed the "Golden Throat" and the "Voice of Mecca", known for his melodic mujawwad style of recitation and his vocal control. He joined Egyptian Radio as a reciter in 1951, was appointed reciter at the Imam al-Shafi'i Mosque and then Al-Hussein Mosque in Cairo, and became the first president of Egypt's Syndicate of Quran Reciters in 1984. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-en} {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-ar}

Abdul Basit is commonly grouped with Muhammad Siddiq al-Minshawi, Mustafa Ismail and Mahmud Khalil al-Husary as one of the four prominent Egyptian Quran reciters of the twentieth century who had an outsized impact on the Islamic world. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-en}

## Upbringing and education
He was born in 1927 in the village of al-Mara'za, in Armant, then in Qena Governorate (now part of Luxor Governorate). His grandfather, Sheikh Abdul Samad, was a popular reciter in Upper Egypt, and his father Muhammad Abdus-Samad was also a Quran reciter and a civil servant in the Ministry of Communications. His two elder brothers, Mahmud and Abdul Hamid, studied memorisation at a kuttab, and he joined them at the age of six; his teacher observed his aptitude for memorisation, his attention to tajwid and his vocal ability. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-en} {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-ar}

He completed memorisation of the Quran by the age of ten, then studied the qira'at under Muhammad Salim Hamada and studied al-Shatibiyya, the classical poem of the seven canonical recitations, after Muhammad Salim took up a teaching post at a religious institute in Armant, which spared him the journey to Tanta he had intended. By the age of twelve he was being invited to recite at religious gatherings throughout Qena Governorate, with his teacher recommending him to local communities. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-en} {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-ar}

## Beginnings and the Cairo breakthrough
His wider recognition came in Cairo around 1950, when he attended the celebration of the birth of Sayyida Zainab at her mosque. On the final night, several established reciters were present, including Abdul Fattah al-Sha'sha'i, Mustafa Ismail, Abdul Azim Zahir and Abu al-Aynayn Shu'aysh. After midnight a relative introduced him and he was invited to recite for about ten minutes; the audience repeatedly asked him to continue, extending his recitation to around two hours. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-en}

He then joined Egyptian Radio as a Quran reciter in 1951, his first broadcast recitation being from Surah Fatir, which brought his voice to a wide audience; radio stations in other Arab countries also broadcast his recordings, and he recorded both murattal and mujawwad complete recitations and murattal mushafs for Arab and Islamic countries. He was appointed reciter at the Imam al-Shafi'i Mosque in 1952 and later at Al-Hussein Mosque — the English article gives 1985, the Arabic article 1958 — succeeding Mahmud Ali al-Banna, and in 1984 he became the first president (naqib) of the newly formed Syndicate of Quran Reciters of Egypt. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-en} {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-ar}

## Travels and global reach
Abdul Basit travelled the world reciting at mosques and official events. In 1952 he performed Hajj with his father and recited in the Masjid al-Haram and the Prophet's Mosque, recording recitations for Saudi radio; from his recordings in the two sanctuaries he was nicknamed the "Voice of Mecca". In 1955 he recited in Jakarta, Indonesia, before crowds that reports estimated at more than a quarter of a million people standing through the night until dawn. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-en} {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-ar}

His journeys included Damascus (1955) and Aleppo (1956), Malaysia (1956), accompanying President Gamal Abdel Nasser to the Soviet Union in 1960, when he became the first qari to recite the Quran in the Kremlin in Moscow; King Mohammed V's invitation to Morocco in Ramadan 1961, where he declined offers of Moroccan citizenship and a post as royal court reciter; several visits to Pakistan, including recitation at the Badshahi Mosque in Lahore; Algeria in 1963 after its independence; South Africa in 1966, where a Green Point event in Cape Town drew more than 30,000 people according to Muslim News; Jerusalem and Hebron before 1967; the United States in 1971; Darul Uloom Deoband in India in 1980 for its centenary; and Paris in 1985. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-en}

In 1986 he recorded a complete recitation in the Warsh transmission of Nafi' in Morocco at the request of King Hassan II, completed in approximately thirteen days according to Egyptian media accounts; in 2020 his son Tareq donated the recording to Egyptian Radio and Television, which began broadcasting it that year. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-en}

## Honours and awards
He received many honours, including the Syrian Order of Civil Merit (1956), Lebanon's National Order of the Cedar (1965), an Order of Merit from the President of Senegal (1975), the Order of Intellectual Merit from King Muhammad V of Morocco, the Order of Scholars from Pakistani President Zia-ul-Haq (1984), gold medals from Pakistan and Malaysia, Egypt's Order of Merit from President Hosni Mubarak at the Day of Preachers celebration in 1987, the Egyptian Radio medal on its fiftieth anniversary, and three world Qira'at competition wins in the early 1970s. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-en} {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-ar}

## Illness, death and legacy
In his later years he suffered complications of diabetes and liver disease, contracted hepatitis less than a month before his death, and travelled to London for about a week of treatment with his son Tareq before returning to Egypt. He died on Wednesday 30 November 1988, corresponding to 21 Rabi' al-Thani 1409 AH; his funeral prayer was held the following day, attended by ambassadors, kings and heads of state, and he was buried in the City of the Dead near the mausoleum of Imam al-Shafi'i in Cairo. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-en} {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-ar}

Years after his death, Egypt's Ministry of Endowments named its 27th World Competition of the Holy Quran after him in 2020, and in January 2026 the Holy Quran Academy in Sharjah inaugurated an exhibit dedicated to him at its Museum of Renowned Reciters after his sons donated his personal belongings. A mosque in his hometown of Armant was inaugurated in his name in 2006, and his sons Yasir and Tareq followed their father as reciters. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-basit-abd-us-samad-en}
