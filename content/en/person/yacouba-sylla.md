# Yacouba Sylla

## Summary
Cheikh Yacouba Sylla (c. 1906 – 1988) was a Soninke preacher of the Tijaniyyah in its Hamallist branch and an entrepreneur in Côte d'Ivoire, called "the Patriarch" by his followers. A disciple of Shaykh Hamahullah, who appointed him muqaddam in 1929 to spread the Hamallist teaching, he preached at Kaédi in southern Mauritania until clashes between his followers and the rival Umari Tijanis brought him trial, prison and, in 1930, eight years' exile at Sassandra. {cite:wikipedia-yacouba-sylla-de} Released in 1938, he settled at Gagnoa and built there the religious community known as Yacoubism and a business network of plantations, transport and cinemas, becoming a leading Muslim notable, a friend of President Félix Houphouët-Boigny, and Grand Officer of the Ivorian National Order. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-yacouba-sylla-fr} {cite:wikipedia-yacouba-sylla-de}

The harvest list that led to this record gave his birth as 1880; no source read supports that year, and all of them place his birth about 1906 at Nioro du Sahel. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-yacouba-sylla-fr} {cite:wikipedia-yacouba-sylla-de} {cite:wikidata-yacouba-sylla}

## Discipleship under Hamahullah
Sylla was born about 1906 at Nioro du Sahel, then in French Sudan, a Soninke, son of Sokona Sylla and of Aoua Cissé, a trader and planter. He attached himself early to Cheikh Hamahoullah, who in the 1920s founded within the Tijaniyya an egalitarian reform movement. In 1929 Hamahoullah, then interned by the French at Méderdra, appointed Sylla muqaddam — a deputy authorised to spread the teaching called Hamallism — at the feast of Tabaski; the German article recounts that Hamahoullah several times placed morsels of food in his mouth, an act his present-day followers read as a symbolic investiture. {cite:wikipedia-yacouba-sylla-de} The English article on Hamahullah independently names Yacouba Sylla among his most prominent disciples. {cite:wikipedia-hamahullah-en}

## Preaching at Kaédi
Sylla preached first at Kayes and then at Kaédi, a town of some 4,000 people then wholly dominated by the Tijaniyya in two directions: the Tukolor of the Touldé quarter followed the teaching of Umar Tall, while the Soninke of Gattaga were enthusiastic adherents of Hamahoullah. The ritual marker of the split was the order's prayer Jawharat al-Kamal, recited twelve times by the Umaris — the "twelve grains" — and eleven times by the Hamallists — the "eleven grains" — after the number of beads on the rosary. Sylla gained great influence among the Gattaga Hamallists, in part through a reputation for reading thoughts and knowing strangers' identities, ages and circumstances on sight. He preached the equality of man and woman and the worthlessness of adornment, had women's clothing of semi-transparent tulle burned and gold necklaces sold, organised public confessions for extramarital relations, and spoke publicly for limiting the bride-price, which had inflated sharply. Women were the majority of his following — a French survey of March 1930 counted 350 followers, 225 of them women — and former slaves were strongly represented. {cite:wikipedia-yacouba-sylla-de}

## Trial, massacre and exile
After the first clashes at Kaédi in May 1929 between his Hamallist followers and the Umari Tijanis, Sylla was expelled from Kaédi on 31 August 1929 to his home town of Nioro. There the non-Hamallist Tijanis accused him of holding orgies because he admitted women to dhikr ceremonies, and of spreading seditious songs and preaching the independence of the child within the family and of the individual within society. On 18 October 1929 a court at Nioro sentenced him to two months' prison and two years' exile for disturbing public order; after the prison term he was transferred in mid-December to an internment camp at Koutiala with his brother Fodié. The fighting at Kaédi continued without them: on 15 February 1930 numerous followers gathered to protest the French authorities' partisanship, and the Gardes de Cercle fired on the crowd, killing more than thirty of Sylla's followers. Held responsible, Sylla was exiled on 27 February 1930 by the governor of French Sudan for eight years to Sassandra in Côte d'Ivoire. The unrest outlasted him: in 1933 his brother Fodié proclaimed himself Mahdi, attacked the seat of the colonial authorities, and was imprisoned for several years at Kidal. {cite:wikipedia-yacouba-sylla-de} The French article gives the outline in brief: condemned by the colonial administration in 1930, he was sent to Sassandra, where he remained detained until 1938, stopping at Gagnoa on the journey — the town where he would later settle. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-yacouba-sylla-fr}

## Sassandra and the move to Gagnoa
At Sassandra, serving his eight-year internment, Sylla soon began producing charcoal for sale to Europeans and later worked as a baker. More and more of his followers from Kaédi, Nioro and their own places of internment joined him, until a new community of 250 people had formed, pooling their property and holding public confession ceremonies. With their help he acquired in 1932–33 a first plot of 135 hectares at Kokolopozo (Dioulabougou), 135 kilometres from Gagnoa, and set his followers to growing coffee, cocoa and palm oil; in 1936 he bought a second cocoa and banana plantation twenty kilometres north of Sassandra. When his sentence was lifted on 28 February 1938 he bought, in 1939, a third plot at Gagnoa, planted lemons, coffee and cocoa, built a house and made the town his main residence, opening a fourth plantation at Daloa. By 1939 he was wealthy enough to give his shaykh Hamahoullah a Ford Mercury. {cite:wikipedia-yacouba-sylla-de}

## Entrepreneur and cinema builder
From Gagnoa, Sylla and his followers moved into other sectors: by 1943 he owned seven trucks and ran a haulage business between Gagnoa and Abidjan; in 1947 he bought two building plots, raising a housing complex with a zawiya at Sassandra and a coffee roastery at Gagnoa; in 1949 he opened butcheries, bakeries and cinemas, and founded a branch of his enterprise at Kaédi to draw Mauritanian followers into his economic network. Between 1946 and 1958 he financed the electrification of Gagnoa. {cite:wikipedia-yacouba-sylla-de} The French article details the cinema network: the first hall was built at Gagnoa in the early 1950s in the Dioulabougou quarter near his residence; halls followed at Sinfra, Divo and Oumé by 1954, at Soubré and Daloa by 1957, and at Adzopé in 1965, each seating several hundred, with community members running projection, maintenance, ticketing and security. The programming was not religious, and the halls — at Gagnoa the Cinéma Pax — served the whole population. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-yacouba-sylla-fr}

## Politics
From 1942 Sylla's economic success and standing as a religious leader drew him into the politics of his new home. He formed a close friendship with the young politician Félix Houphouët-Boigny, supported his candidacy in the 1946 elections to the constituent assembly, and worked to implant Houphouët-Boigny's Rassemblement Démocratique Africain at Gagnoa. Muslims held him in high regard for his past as a religious leader who had stood in conflict with the colonial power, while the administration came to value his de-escalating role. After independence in 1960 his followers supported Houphouët-Boigny's regime and received its patronage; to the non-Muslim Bété, who rejected the RDA, he remained a foreign intruder, and in 1955 a group of young Bété men attacked his Dioulabougou plantation complex to destroy it. On 16 December 1977 he was made Grand Officer of the Ivorian National Order. {cite:wikipedia-yacouba-sylla-de} {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-yacouba-sylla-fr}

## Final years and legacy
After 1938 religious teaching played a subordinate role in his activities, yet his followers continued to venerate him as a saint and a Sufi, credited with reading thoughts and seeing into the past. Until 1975 he made regular visitation tours of his community's houses in the Ivorian towns, before paralysis prevented him. Before his death he designated his eldest son Cheikna Sylla as his successor. {cite:wikipedia-yacouba-sylla-de} He died at Gagnoa in 1988; the day is disputed — the French article gives 15 October 1988, while the two Wikidata items give 11 and 15 August 1988 — so the record carries the year on which all agree. {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-yacouba-sylla-fr} {cite:wikidata-yacouba-sylla} {cite:wikidata-cheikh-yacouba-sylla} His grave at Gagnoa is the goal of an annual pilgrimage of his followers. In 1998 Cheikna Sylla founded at Abidjan the Fondation Cheick Yacouba Sylla, whose tasks include preserving the heritage of Ahmad al-Tijani, Shaykh Hamahoullah and Yacouba Sylla, building zawiyas and popularising Sufi gatherings. {cite:wikipedia-yacouba-sylla-de}

In this museum his faith is recorded as Sufi on the documented basis of his Tijani discipleship: the German article calls him a preacher and a Sufi of the Tijaniyya order and narrates his appointment as Hamahullah's muqaddam; the English article on Hamahullah names him among the shaykh's most prominent disciples; the French article ties him to Hamallism and cites Le Monde's profile of him as "the enlightened Sufi". No source read names a legal school (madhhab) for him. {cite:wikipedia-yacouba-sylla-de} {cite:wikipedia-hamahullah-en} {cite:wikipedia-cheikh-yacouba-sylla-fr}
