## Overview
Sayf al-Din Sarghitmish al-Nasiri was a mamluk of [[person/al-nasir-muhammad-ibn-qalawun|al-Nasir Muhammad ibn Qalawun]] who rose to be one of the managers of the Egyptian state after that sultan's death. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/328} The Encyclopaedia of Islam spells his name Sarghatmish or Sarghitmish, and treats him as one of the men who held power behind the Qalawunid sultans. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 445} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 194}

## Origin and rise
Ibn Hajar says a merchant, Ibn al-Sawwaf, brought him in the 730s; al-Nasir Muhammad bought him, and the merchant received an unusual decree of tax relief with the sale. He says Sarghitmish was the most handsome mamluk of his time. {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 2/206} He first came to notice when he went out with Fakhr al-Din Iyas on his appointment to govern Aleppo, and with [[person/shaykhu-al-umari|Shaykhu]] he was among the mamluks drawn into the quarrel with the amir Qawsun, which, Ibn Hajar says, was one cause of Qawsun's fall. {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 2/206} He rose through the ranks to command of a thousand in 749, and in Muharram 752 he was made ra's nawba kabir, the senior amir in command of the guard. {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 2/206}

## Sharing power
After the deposition of [[person/al-nasir-hasan|al-Nasir Hasan]] in 752, a triumvirate of Shaykhu, Sarghitmish and Taz won out; Shaykhu held the sultan's privy treasury while Sarghitmish handled the grants of fiefs and the promotion of mamluks. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 1058} Ibn Taghribirdi describes a fight between Sarghitmish and the household of [[person/taz-al-nasiri|Taz]], in which Taz's brothers were defeated. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/286} Together with Shaykhu he then arranged the deposition of al-Salih and the return of Hasan on 2 Shawwal 755/20 October 1354. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/302} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 259} Hasan seized Taz in early 758 at Sarghitmish's urging. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/307}

## Rule alone and fall
When Shaykhu died in 758, Sarghitmish took over the management of the state alone. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/307} Ibn Taghribirdi says his power grew until he planned to seize the sultan; Hasan learned of it, made arrangements with his own mamluks Taybugha al-Tawil and Yalbugha, and on 20 Ramadan 759 had Sarghitmish arrested as he entered a private audience, together with Tashtamur al-Qasimi, the chief chamberlain, and other amirs. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/308} Sarghitmish's mamluks rode out in arms, fought the sultan's mamluks from morning to afternoon and were beaten; his house near the Bir al-Watawit was plundered. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/308} He was sent to Alexandria and died in prison in Dhu al-Hijja 759/November 1358, according to Ibn Taghribirdi; Ibn Hajar's note says al-Maqrizi gives the same month. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/328} {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 2/207} Ibn Hajar relates that Shaykhu once told Sarghitmish that as long as Taz was in Aleppo no one would dare act against him, and that if he agreed to Taz's arrest he would not last long afterwards; so it turned out. {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 2/207}

## The Sarghitmishiyya madrasa
Sarghitmish founded a madrasa for Hanafi jurists in the Salibah quarter of Cairo, beside the mosque of Ibn Tulun. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/328} The editor of the Nujum quotes al-Maqrizi for its start in Ramadan 756 and completion in Jumada I 757, and says it still stands as the mosque of Sarghitmish with the founder's tomb under the western dome. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/308-309} The Encyclopaedia of Islam dates it 757/1356 and describes an unusual plan of four iwans with a dome over the main iwan, the mausoleum dome rising across the courtyard. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 644} The Hanafi scholar Qiwam al-Din Amir Katib al-Itqani lived in Cairo under his patronage, and Sarghitmish, for his sake, built the madrasa and made him its teacher. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/325-326}

## Character
Ibn Taghribirdi says he was important in the state, learned in several fields, able to discuss jurisprudence and Arabic, a lover of scholars who sat with them often, and a giver of charity, but also marked by injustice, harshness and arrogance. {cite:nujum-zahira 10/328} Ibn Hajar adds that he honoured and respected the Persians, left wealth too great to describe, and was partisan to the Hanafis. {cite:durar-kamina-jil-1993 2/207}
