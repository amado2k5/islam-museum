# Mir Fendereski

Sayyid Amir Abu'l-Qasim ibn Mirza Beg, known as Mir Fendereski (c. 1562/63–1640), was a philosopher, mystic and poet of Safavid Isfahan. His family were sayyids of Astarabad in the Gorgan region; his grandfather Mir Sadr al-Din held estates at Findirisk, the village his name preserves, and his father Mirza Beg was a courtier of Shah Abbas I.

## Life

He was probably born in 970/1562-63 — the biographical tradition places his birth at Findirisk, while Encyclopaedia Iranica argues he may have been born in the east of Persia, perhaps Mashad. After preliminary studies at home he read philosophy at Qazvin and Isfahan; the tradition names his teacher as Allama Chalabi Beg Tabrizi, though Iranica cautions that nothing certain is known of his teachers. He settled in Isfahan, where he taught Avicenna's *Shifāʾ* and *Qānūn*, and moved in the circle of Mir Damad and Shaykh Baha'i — the milieu Henry Corbin called the School of Isfahan.

Independent of mind and impatient with imitation, he travelled repeatedly to Mughal India — in 1606 with the poet Awhadi Balyani, again in 1627, and in 1636, when he was presented to Shah Jahan. He died in Isfahan in 1050/1640, leaving his library to Shah Safi, and was buried in the Takht-e Fulad cemetery in the tomb known as Tikyih-yi Mir; a manuscript reading of 1639 for his death is also preserved.

## Teaching and India

His attested students — Agha Husayn Khwansari, Mirza Rafi'a Na'ini, Rajab Ali Tabrizi and Muhammad Baqir Sabzavari — carried Avicennan teaching into the next generation. Some later lists also name Mulla Sadra among his pupils, but Sadra never mentions him, and Iranica's article on Sadra treats the story as apocryphal; no such relation is recorded here.

India marked his thought. He annotated Panipati's Persian translation of the *Laghu-Yoga-Vāsiṣṭhā*, compiled a glossary of its Sanskrit terms, and made selections from it that set Hindu and Islamic mystical verse side by side. The *Dabistān al-Madhāhib* associates him with the circle of Azar Kaywan and reports that he would harm no animal — the reason given for his never performing the Hajj, since it would have required a sacrifice. Anecdotes remember his felt clothes, his company of ordinary people, and the lion that once wandered into his assembly while he sat unmoved.

## Works and thought

A Peripatetic with mystical leanings, he argued in a treatise on *badāʾ* — the Imami doctrine that God may abrogate an earlier decree — from narrations of the Imams against Nasir al-Din Tusi's rejection of it. His best-known work, the *Ṣenāʿiya*, ranges the arts and professions of an ideal society in a hierarchy that rises from the blacksmith to the philosopher, reading the polity through the analogy of the body. A five-chapter treatise on motion defends the Unmoved Mover against Platonic forms, and an epistle against the Illuminationists denies gradation in essences. His philosophical *qaṣīda*, written in imitation of Nasir Khusraw, drew learned commentaries for centuries.

## Faith

His affiliation is documented as Imami (Twelver) Shi'a: the Golha entry calls him an "Imami mystic, philosopher, and poet", and Iranica records both his *badāʾ* treatise and his place in *Aʿyān al-shiʿa*, the biographical dictionary of Shi'a notables.
