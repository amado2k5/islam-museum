## Summary
Muhammad ibn Abdullah ibn Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim (about 570–632 CE) is the Prophet of Islam; the biographers and genealogists agree on his lineage back to Adnan {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.49}. He was born in the valley of [[group/banu-hashim|Banu Hashim]] in [[place/mecca|Mecca]] on the morning of Monday, 9 Rabi' al-Awwal, in the first year of the event of the Elephant, which al-Mubarakpuri, following Muhammad Sulayman al-Mansurpuri, equates with 20 or 22 April 571 CE {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.54}. His mother [[person/amina-bint-wahb|Amina]] died at al-Abwa' when he was six; his grandfather [[person/abd-al-muttalib|Abd al-Muttalib]], then his uncle [[person/abu-talib|Abu Talib]], took charge of him {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.57} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq pp.57-58}. At twenty-five he went to Syria as a trader with the goods of [[person/khadija|Khadija]], then married her {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq pp.60-61}. His people called him [[concept/al-amin|al-Amin]] {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.62}.

The sira relates that when he had completed forty years the revelation came to him with verses of the [[work/quran|Qur'an]] in the cave of [[place/ghar-hira|Hira]]; al-Mubarakpuri judges that this was the night of Monday, 21 Ramadan, which corresponds to 10 August 610 CE {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.64}. He called to Islam in secret for three years, then openly among the people of Mecca {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.63} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.75}. He then emigrated to [[place/medina|Medina]] ([[event/hijra|the Hijra]]), settling there on Friday, 12 Rabi' al-Awwal 1 AH, which corresponds to 27 September 622 CE, and began with the building of the [[building/prophets-mosque|Prophet's Mosque]] and the [[event/brotherhood-of-muhajirun-and-ansar|brotherhood between the Emigrants and the Helpers]] {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.168} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.178} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.180}. He died in Medina on 12 Rabi' al-Awwal 11 AH, having completed sixty-three years {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.444}. This entry then presents his shama'il, his bodily and moral qualities, as al-Mubarakpuri gives them in *al-Raheeq al-Makhtum* {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.454}.

## The beauty of his form
[[person/umm-maabad|Umm Ma'bad al-Khuza'iyya]] described the Prophet to her husband when he passed her tent as an emigrant. She said he was radiant of face, with a bright forehead and fine character, deep black in his eyes and with a gleam at his neck {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.454}.

Ali ibn Abi Talib described him: he was neither overly tall nor short, but of middle height among the people. His complexion was white with a reddish tinge, his eyes deep black, his eyelashes long, and he was broad at the joints and across the upper back. Between his shoulders was the seal of prophethood, for he is the Seal of the Prophets. He was the most generous of people with his hand, the most courageous in heart, the most truthful in speech and the most faithful to his word {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.454}. Anas ibn Malik said: he was radiant in complexion, neither pale-white nor dark. I never touched silk or brocade softer than the palm of the Prophet {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq pp.455-456}. Al-Bara' said: he was the most handsome of people in face and the finest of them in character {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.455}.

## The perfection of the soul and the nobility of character
He spoke in "comprehensive words", concise and to the point, with neither excess nor shortfall {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.459}. A'isha said: he was never offered a choice between two matters without choosing the easier, so long as it was not a sin; and he never took revenge for himself unless the sanctity of God was violated {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.457}. He was the most generous of people, most generous in Ramadan when Jibril met him, more generous with good than the driving wind, and he was never asked for anything and said no {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.457}. He was the bravest of people. Ali said: when battle grew fierce and eyes reddened, we sheltered behind the Prophet, and none was nearer the enemy than he {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.458}. He was extremely modest; Abu Sa'id al-Khudri said he was more modest than a virgin in her chamber, and when he disliked something it was visible in his face {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.458}. His people called him al-Amin for the good qualities gathered in him {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.62}. A'isha said he would mend his own sandal, sew his own garment and work with his hands in his house {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.458}. [[person/hind-ibn-abi-hala|Hind ibn Abi Hala]] described him as constantly troubled in thought, always reflective, long in silence, and not speaking except when needed {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.459}. Al-Mubarakpuri concluded that these qualities are only brief marks of his perfection, and that the reality of what he was cannot be grasped {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.460}.

## For young visitors
People described the Prophet as beautiful in appearance and good in character: forbearing, generous, brave, humble and trustworthy. He did not take revenge for himself, and he never said "no" to someone who asked.

## Noble lineage
The lineage is divided into three parts. The first, on which the biographers and genealogists agree, runs from the Prophet up to Adnan: Muhammad ibn [[person/abdullah-ibn-abd-al-muttalib|Abdullah]] ibn [[person/abd-al-muttalib|Abd al-Muttalib]] ibn [[person/hashim-ibn-abd-manaf|Hashim]] ibn [[person/abd-manaf-ibn-qusayy|Abd Manaf]] ibn [[person/qusayy-ibn-kilab|Qusayy]] ibn Kilab ibn Murra ibn Ka'b ibn Lu'ayy ibn Ghalib ibn [[person/fihr-ibn-malik|Fihr]] ibn Malik ibn al-Nadr ibn Kinana ibn Khuzayma ibn Mudrika ibn Ilyas ibn Mudar ibn Nizar ibn Ma'add ibn Adnan. The second part, from Adnan up to Ibrahim, is disputed. The third, from Ibrahim up to Adam, relies on reports from the People of the Book, and contains what is not to be doubted as false {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.49}. The details are in [[concept/nasab-al-nabi|the Prophet's lineage and its three parts]], and his family is in [[group/banu-hashim|Banu Hashim]]. It is related that the Prophet said: "I am the son of the two sacrificed ones," meaning Isma'il and his father Abdullah {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.54}.

## Birth and early upbringing
He was born in the valley of Banu Hashim in Mecca, on the morning of Monday, 9 Rabi' al-Awwal, in the first year of the event of the Elephant. The author equates this with 20 or 22 April 571 CE ([[event/year-of-the-elephant-and-birth|the birth]]) {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.54}. His grandfather Abd al-Muttalib named him Muhammad and had him circumcised on his seventh day {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.55}. His first wet nurse was [[person/thuwayba|Thuwayba]], and then [[person/halima-al-sadiyya|Halima bint Abi Dhuayb]] nursed him among [[group/banu-sad-ibn-bakr|Banu Sa'd ibn Bakr]]. He had foster-brothers there, and his cousin [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-al-harith|Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith]] and his uncle [[person/hamza|Hamza]] were also nursed there {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.55}. In his fourth year, according to the verifiers, the [[event/shaqq-al-sadr|incident of the opening of his chest]] took place, and Halima returned him to his mother {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.57}. When he reached six, his mother died at al-Abwa', between Mecca and Medina ([[event/death-of-amina|the death of Amina]]), and his grandfather took him back to Mecca {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.57}. His grandfather died when he was eight years, two months and ten days old, and his uncle [[person/abu-talib|Abu Talib]] took him in ([[event/death-of-abd-al-muttalib|the death of Abd al-Muttalib]]) {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq pp.57-58}.

## Youth before prophethood
At twelve, his uncle Abu Talib took him on a trading journey to Syria. At Busra he met the monk [[person/bahira|Bahira]], who recognized him by the seal of prophethood between his shoulders and feared for him from the Romans and the Jews, so his uncle sent him back to Mecca with some of his servants ([[event/bahira-encounter|the meeting with Bahira]]) {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.58}. In his twentieth year the [[event/harb-al-fijar|War of the Fijar]] took place, and he gathered arrows for his uncles {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.59}. After it he attended the [[event/hilf-al-fudul|Pact of al-Fudul]] in the house of Abdullah ibn Jud'an, and he said after the mission began that if he were called to it in Islam he would answer {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.59}. He had no particular occupation in his early youth, though the accounts agree that he tended sheep, in Banu Sa'd and in Mecca for its people for small wages, and it seems he moved to trade as he grew up {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.59}. At twenty-five he went out as a trader to Syria with the goods of [[person/khadija|Khadija]] ([[event/trade-journey-to-syria-for-khadija|the Syria journey]]), and he married her two months after his return ([[event/marriage-to-khadija|the marriage]]). All his children were from her except Ibrahim {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq pp.60-61}. When he was thirty-five, the Quraysh rebuilt the Ka'ba, and they made him the arbiter in placing the Black Stone ([[event/rebuilding-of-the-kaaba|the rebuilding of the Ka'ba]]) {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq pp.60-61}.

## His character before prophethood in general
He did not drink wine, did not eat what was sacrificed on the idol stones, did not attend the festivals or celebrations of the idols, and could not bear to hear oaths sworn by al-Lat and al-Uzza {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.62}. The author also relates a report that twice he meant to spend a night as young men of Mecca did, and on hearing the sound of a wedding he sat down to listen and God made him fall asleep {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.62}. Among his people he was distinguished by manliness, good character, faithfulness and trust, and his people named him [[concept/al-amin|al-Amin]] {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.62}. As Khadija said of him, he bore the burdens of others, earned for the destitute, hosted guests and helped in the calamities of truth {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.65}.

## Under the shade of prophethood: the beginning of revelation
He used to withdraw to the cave of [[place/ghar-hira|Hira]] in the month of Ramadan each year, from three years before prophethood {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq pp.64-66}. When he completed forty years, true dreams came to him for six months, and then the revelation came. The author judges that this was the night of Monday, 21 Ramadan, corresponding to 10 August 610 CE ([[event/first-revelation|the beginning of revelation]]) {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.64}. He returned to [[person/khadija|Khadija]], who reassured him, and she took him to [[person/waraqa-ibn-nawfal|Waraqa ibn Nawfal]] {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.65}. In the author's view the revelation then paused for ten days {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.66}, and then the first verses of Surat al-Muddaththir came down, with the command to stand up and warn ([[event/second-revelation-al-muddaththir|the descent of al-Muddaththir]]) {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.67}. The author states that the prophetic period was twenty-three years {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.64}.

## The secret call
The author divides the Meccan period into stages. The first was three years of secret call, followed by the open proclamation of the call to the people of Mecca ([[concept/marahil-al-dawa|the stages of the call]]) {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.63}. He and his companions prayed in the mountain passes, concealed from their people ([[concept/al-salat-qabl-al-isra|the prayer before the Night Journey]]) {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.71}. Then he was commanded to call openly, and he proclaimed the call in the gatherings and assemblies of the idolaters {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.75}.

## The Encyclopaedia of Islam account: two ways of asking
The Encyclopaedia of Islam article on Muhammad opens with the Muslim understanding. Belief that he is the Messenger of God stands second only to belief in the oneness of God in the creed of the shahada, and he holds a highly exalted place at the heart of Muslim faith. The article adds that the Qur'an and Islamic orthodoxy insist that he was fully human and had no supernatural powers of his own. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 412}

The article then separates two inquiries. For the believer, God chose Muhammad and was directly responsible for his success. For the historian, the evidence points to a man of remarkable leadership and a charismatic personality who drew strong leaders to him. The article calls the two views not necessarily incompatible, but different inquiries with different methods. The sections below follow its historical inquiry, label its academic judgements as the article's own, and set aside what the believer's inquiry holds. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 412}

## Sources for his life
The article names the [[work/quran|Qur'an]] as the most trustworthy source and also the hardest to use, since it answers Muhammad's changing circumstances but was never meant as biography. Next come the maghazi, sira, tafsir and hadith genres, none of which was written to record his life as such, so each report has to be weighed by its purpose. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 412} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 413}

The most widely used sources date from the third and fourth centuries of the Islamic era. The most respected is the sira of [[person/ibn-ishaq|Ibn Ishaq]], which survives through the recension of [[person/ibn-hisham|Ibn Hisham]] and one by Yunus b. Bukayr, and through quotations in the history of [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]]. The article also lists the maghazi of [[person/al-waqidi|al-Waqidi]], the Tabaqat of Ibn Sa'd, and the hadith collections of [[person/al-bukhari|al-Bukhari]], [[person/muslim-ibn-al-hajjaj|Muslim]] and [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad b. Hanbal]]. It notes that Muslim writers often criticise al-Waqidi as unreliable. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 413}

## Early life in Mecca
The article says no certain chronology exists for the Meccan period. The biographers make him 40 or sometimes 43 at the call, which would put his birth near 570. The article rejects the tradition of birth in the Year of the Elephant, judging that the attack of Abraha must have come well before 570, and prefers to leave the year of birth open. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 413}

On his family, the article accepts that old poems support the tradition that he belonged to [[group/banu-hashim|Banu Hashim]], apparently a respectable but not prosperous family, and that it ranked below the greatest Meccan clans. His father is described as a colourless figure in the sources. His uncles [[person/abu-talib|Abu Talib]], al-Abbas, [[person/hamza|Hamza]] and [[person/abu-lahab|Abu Lahab]] are the first tangible historical figures among his relatives. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 413} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 414}

The article reads Sura 93 as affirming that he grew up an orphan. It treats the colourful stories of his youth, including the opening of his breast, the journeys to Syria, the monk [[person/bahira|Bahira]] and his part in rebuilding the Ka'ba, as valuable for early Muslim perceptions of him but of little worth for the historical Muhammad. These are the article's judgements; the earlier sections of this page give the sira's own account. It says the sources tell almost nothing of the years between his marriage to [[person/khadija|Khadija]] and the first revelations. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 414}

## Emergence as a religious reformer
The article calls the question of how he came to his mission among the hardest to answer. It judges it natural that he first shared some religious conceptions of his milieu, and it points to sympathetic monotheists around him, among them [[person/waraqa-ibn-nawfal|Waraqa ibn Nawfal]], the [[concept/hanif|hanifs]] and the poet Umayya b. Abi al-Salt. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 414} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 415}

Islamic tradition tells of a sudden call, some stories involving Gabriel, while the article reports an argument that his emergence was gradual and involved long reflection, called tahannuth in the sira. It says the verses read as pointing to a first revelation are ambiguous, and it calls the stories identifying Sura 96 or Sura 74 as first revelation highly suspect. The three-year pause of revelation is, in its view, an unexplained puzzle that may come from the biographers' wish to build an exact chronology. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 415}

## His public ministry in Mecca
The article describes the early recitations as an urgent moral and religious appeal: man's responsibility before the God who created him, the coming judgement, and the signs of God in nature. It lists the simple duties of the Meccan years as belief in God, prayer, night vigils, helping those in need, honest dealing, a chaste life and the rejection of the burying of newborn girls. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 415}

At first there was no serious opposition. The article names [[person/khadija|Khadija]] as consistently the first believer, with [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]], [[person/zayd-ibn-haritha|Zayd ibn Haritha]], [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]], [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-awf|Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf]] and [[person/saad-ibn-abi-waqqas|Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas]] among early followers, most of them young or of modest standing. Opposition came when he attacked the gods of Mecca, because the merchants feared for their fairs and trade. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 416}

On the word ummi the article says it means one who had not been given a scripture before, the opposite of the People of the Book, and argues that it does not settle whether he could read and write. It adds that the meaning "illiterate" arose later in theological circles, as support for the doctrine of the miracle of the revelation. This is the article's argument; it also notes that in Muslim piety the reading as illiterate is central, and the [[concept/ummi|ummi]] record gives the Muslim understanding of the term. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 416}

## Abyssinia, the gharaniq story and the boycott
Traditions describe at length the persecution of Muhammad and his followers. The article notes that the Qur'an is silent on many episodes the sira treats as major Meccan events. It reports that Ibn Sa'd tells of two migrations to [[place/abyssinia|Abyssinia]] while Ibn Hisham and al-Tabari speak of one, and that Watt found the episode more complex than the traditional accounts suggest, possibly involving a division in the young community or trading aims. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 417}

The article retells the story, found in al-Tabari and Ibn Sa'd but not in Ibn Hisham, that Satan placed two verses on his tongue accepting intercession of three goddesses. It says most Muslims reject it as a later invention, that European biographers have often accepted it as historical, and that the article itself finds it unacceptable in its present form while allowing a possible kernel behind it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 417}

It judges the story of the boycott of Banu Hashim probably much exaggerated, though the clan's support was what made his position possible. It names [[person/abu-lahab|Abu Lahab]] as the clan's chief opponent. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 417}

## The last years in Mecca
The article judges that his attempt at reform in Mecca failed on the whole, and that his position grew hopeless after the deaths of Khadija and Abu Talib. It reports the unsuccessful journey to [[place/taif|Ta'if]] and the protection he then obtained from Mut'im b. 'Adi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 417} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 418}

On the [[event/isra-and-miraj|Night Journey and Ascension]] the article shows that the sources place them differently: Ibn Sa'd gives two events after Ta'if, Ibn Hisham places them before the two deaths, and al-Tabari places the Ascension before the public ministry. Later tradition joined them, ending at the Temple Mount in Jerusalem, which the article says makes Jerusalem the third holiest city for Muslims. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 418}

At the fairs and pilgrimages he found favourable ground with men from Yathrib. The article says the Medinans wanted a political leader to mend tribal conflicts that had ended in the battle of Bu'ath, and calls the contrast between the inspired preacher and the statesman one of the hardest problems of his biography. After contacts in 621 he concluded at al-Aqaba, in 622, a formal agreement under which the Medinans took him under their protection. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 418}

His followers slipped away to Medina in groups. The article says that some accounts, possibly Shi'i, have Ali remaining until the last moment. It treats the tale of the cave as possibly an exegetical elaboration of a Qur'anic verse, and says that on the usual reckoning he reached Quba on 12 Rabi' I, 24 September 622. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 418} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 419}

## Medina: building a community
The [[event/hijra|Hijra]] became year one of the Islamic calendar. In Medina the Emigrants, [[group/muhajirun|muhajirun]], held a special rank, followed by the Helpers, [[group/ansar|ansar]]. Opposition came from a minority, among them a group around [[person/abdullah-ibn-ubayy|Abd Allah b. Ubayy]], and the old feud of the Aws and Khazraj remained a danger. Three Jewish tribes, [[group/banu-qaynuqa|Qaynuqa]], [[group/banu-nadir|al-Nadir]] and [[group/banu-qurayza|Qurayza]], were prominent. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 419}

The article describes a pairing of each Emigrant with a Medinan, and the agreement known as the Constitution of Medina, preserved by Ibn Ishaq. It judges that the document reflects a later, strained relation with the Jews, includes Jews and polytheists in the community, and was soon made obsolete by changing conditions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 419}

The article says he first sought the favour of Medina's Jews by adopting some of their practices, among them the fast of Ashura, and perhaps facing Jerusalem in prayer. When they did not accept his claim, the [[concept/qibla|direction of prayer]] changed to the Ka'ba in the second year after the Hijra. The article calls this a turning point and links it to the figure of Abraham as the first muslim and founder of the Meccan sanctuary. It adds that the Qur'an also challenged Jewish and Christian scriptural claims, and that Muhammad was not thinking of founding a new religion but of restoring the true one. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 419} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 420} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 421}

## Expeditions and battles
The article recounts small raids, the [[event/expedition-of-nakhla|expedition of Nakhla]] in a sacred month, and the [[event/battle-of-badr|battle of Badr]] in Ramadan 2 AH, where [[person/abu-jahl|Abu Jahl]] led the Meccans and the Muslims won. It says two prisoners were put to death and the others ransomed, and that Badr confirmed his belief in his call. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 421}

It then covers the siege of Qaynuqa, the [[event/battle-of-uhud|battle of Uhud]] in 3 AH, where Muslim archers left their position, the Prophet was wounded and his uncle Hamza was killed, and the expulsion of al-Nadir. It describes the siege of Medina in 5 AH, the trench, and the Meccan withdrawal. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 422}

On [[event/battle-of-the-trench|the Trench]] aftermath, the article reports that the men of Qurayza, numbering between 600 and 900 according to varying accounts, were killed, and the women, children and property divided, citing Ibn Hisham. It states that tradition places responsibility on Sa'd b. Mu'adh, who gave the judgment, but that, in the article's view, various indications point to the Prophet himself as responsible. This is the article's judgement and is not offered here as settled. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 422} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 423}

The article also notes the raid on the Banu Mustaliq, the episode involving [[person/aisha|A'isha]], and the chronological disagreement of the sources about it, as an example of how precise chronology is often unattainable. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 423}

## Hudaybiya, Khaybar and the conquest of Mecca
In March 628 he led followers on an 'umra but was stopped at [[event/treaty-of-hudaybiyya|al-Hudaybiya]], where a ten-year truce was agreed, allowing a pilgrimage the next year. The article calls it a brilliant act of diplomacy, since the Meccans recognised him as an equal. It says some followers were angry at the terms. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 423}

The oasis of [[place/khaybar|Khaybar]] was taken early in 7 AH. The article says the inhabitants stayed as tenants paying a yearly tribute, later called [[concept/jizya|jizya]], and that this set the precedent for later terms. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 423}

The article doubts the traditional letters to the Byzantine and Persian rulers and others summoning them to Islam, finding them unlikely before the conquest of Mecca, though letters to Arab tribes and to the Negus are accepted. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 424}

After the reverse at [[event/battle-of-mutah|Mu'ta]], where Zayd ibn Haritha was killed, a Meccan decision to support a client clan against Muhammad's allies, the Khuza'a, freed him from the truce, and he marched in Ramadan 8 AH. The article reports that [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-harb|Abu Sufyan]] obtained an amnesty for the Quraysh and that he entered the city almost without a fight, demanding the destruction of idols. [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] and [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn al-As]] became Muslims in this period. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 424} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 425}

## From Hunayn to the death
The article follows with [[event/battle-of-hunayn|Hunayn]], the long delay at Ta'if, the gifts to former opponents, and the many delegations of 9 AH. It describes the expedition to [[event/expedition-to-tabuk|Tabuk]] as indecisive in battle but followed by submissions from small Christian and Jewish states in the north. It cautions that historians exaggerate when they present all of Arabia as converted. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 425}

The article reports that at the end of 10 AH, in March 632, he led the [[event/farewell-pilgrimage|Farewell Pilgrimage]], which it says set the standard for all time. A month before his death he prepared an expedition to Transjordan under the young [[person/usama-ibn-zayd|Usama ibn Zayd]]. He fell ill, and died reportedly on a Monday, 8 June 632, in the arms of his wife A'isha. The article says he left no legal successor and that the inner circle had to choose a leader in the tribal manner, and relates a report that he was buried under the floor of A'isha's apartment. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 426}

## The article's assessment
The article states that the modern biographer is hindered because the early sources saw his successes as proofs of divine service and not as the acts of a man. In its own historical judgement, the essential clue to his success was his unshakeable belief that God had called him. It adds that the Qur'an presents him as a man like any other who would die, and that later devotion wove a network of superhuman features around him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 426} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 427}

## The Prophet in popular Muslim piety
A separate part of the article, by Annemarie Schimmel, treats the veneration seen in qawwali and mawlid gatherings, in poetry across many regions. It reports popular stories such as the splitting of the moon, the palm trunk that sighed, and stories of the gazelle, and says the folk understanding of ummi as illiterate became central to piety. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 428} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 429}

It describes belief in his [[concept/shafaa|intercession]], the recitation of blessings on him as a major formula after the shahada, celebration of his birth, and the custom of naming boys after him. It concludes that devotion to him is the strongest binding power among Muslims, whether they see him as intercessor, primordial light, friend of humans and animals, or political leader. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 429}

## The image of the Prophet in Europe
The third part of the article studies how medieval and later Europeans pictured him. It says medieval Latin scholars knew a surprising amount of accurate detail about his life, but that this passed through non-Muslim transmitters and was used by Christian authors for polemic, so an objective life remained impossible. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 429} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 430}

The article describes the dominant medieval aim as proving he could not have been a prophet, with charges of imposture, sickness, ambition, warfare and sexuality, and describes popular epics that showed him as an idol, an image it reads as polemic against Islam as supposed polytheism. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 431} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 432} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 433}

From the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries it traces a move toward fairer views, with Boulainvilliers, Voltaire and Carlyle, while hostile portraits continued in some English and German writing. It also describes dramas and poems of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries that used his figure to explore genius, faith and fanaticism. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 434} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 435} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 436}

## For Sprouts
The Prophet of Islam. Muslims believe the Qur'an was revealed to him, and he founded the first Muslim community in Medina.

## For Explorers
Muhammad, the Prophet of Islam, was born in Mecca around the year 570. Muslims believe the Qur'an was revealed to him. He lived in Mecca, then moved to Medina, where the first Muslim community took shape. He died in the eleventh year of the Hijra, aged sixty-three. People in Mecca knew him as truthful and trustworthy before revelation began.

## For Scholars
Muhammad ibn Abdullah was born in Mecca around 570 CE and died in 632 CE. Muslims believe he is the Prophet to whom the Qur'an was revealed. The people of Mecca knew him for honesty and trust before his mission, and he traded and worked among his people. Revelation came when he was forty, and he called his people to the oneness of God. He then migrated to Medina, where he founded the first Muslim community. His life is told in the sira and the hadith, and the museum gives each report with its source and its degree of reliability.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 9)

The Sheikh relates that the Prophet told his Companions he sees them from behind his back as he sees them in front, and the Sheikh holds this is not far from his standing: if ordinary people, with their sins, see beautiful visions in dreams, how much more the Prophet {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:31:00}.

He relates that after the age of sixty the Prophet did not rise from the second prostration to the next rak'a in one movement but sat briefly to rest, and that Aisha asked him to rest a little for fear his feet would swell from so much standing (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:44:00}.

The Sheikh points out that the Prophet, though protected from sin and forgiven, answered Aisha when she asked him to sleep a little, and then he gives his answer {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:44:30}.

He says the Prophet told her how could he rest when the angel of the horn has put the horn to his mouth, bent his forehead, lent his ear and fixed his gaze on the sky waiting for the command to blow, and the Sheikh describes this as fear that his Lord might order him to blow {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:45:00}.

The Sheikh uses this to explain another saying, the Prophet's words that if they knew what he knows they would laugh little and weep much and would not take pleasure in women on their beds, and he says the Prophet lived these realities, which is why the Companions and the mothers of the believers watched his movements {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:45:30}.

He tells of Umm Salama seeing the Prophet pray alone at night and wondering at his state: he stepped forward in his prayer and stretched out his hand, then drew back and stepped backward, then finished his prayer and gave the salam, and she asked him about the strange thing she had seen that night {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:46:00}.

He told her, as the Sheikh relates, that while he was praying a door of Paradise was opened and a bunch of grapes from one of its trees appeared, so he reached out his hand to pick it for her {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:46:30}.

He was told that he was still in the world and that a worldly stomach is not fit for those grapes, so he drew his hand back; then a door of Hell was opened, he feared it and stepped backward, and he finished his prayer asking forgiveness {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:47:00}.

The Sheikh then says the Prophet asked Bilal what he did, because he had heard the sound of his steps in Paradise, and that he saw a woman walking ahead of him who, when asked who she was, told him she was a woman whose husband had left her orphans and she had stayed with them {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:47:30}.

The woman says she raised them, the Sheikh notes that she will enter Paradise, and he then recalls a man's saying that he finds the scent of Paradise, and asks whether someone who smells that scent stops or goes toward it {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:48:00}.

He relates that the Prophet would pass by night among his nine wives, leaving one and going to the other eight, waking them and saying that many women who are comfortable in this world will be in hardship in the next, and reminding them of the terrors of the Day of Resurrection and of death (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:50:30}.

The Sheikh asks whether a mother of the believers would sleep when she heard the Prophet say this every day, and then says he would come to the last house and wake Fatima and Ali for prayer, and that Ali used to take a short nap after the night prayer {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep09 00:51:00}.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 10)

The Sheikh says that a group of people enters Paradise by God's mercy and not by their deeds, and that the Prophet's Companions asked him «not even you, Messenger of God?» and he said «not even I, unless God wraps me in His mercy»; he then asks who is speaking, and answers that it is the one God promised forgiveness and the praised station, and who is the first intercessor allowed to speak before God {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:04:00}.

He cites the hadith of intercession: the people at the gathering go from prophet to prophet, from Adam to Isa, each saying «it is not for me», and then the Prophet says «it is for me»; the Sheikh says the Prophet nevertheless used to say that he does not enter Paradise by his deeds {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:04:30}.

He describes how the Prophet spent his night and day seeking God's good pleasure, so that his speech was remembrance, his looking was reflection and his silence was thought on the oneness of his Lord, and all his senses were occupied with his Lord {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:05:00}.

He says the truths were laid before the Prophet, and that it is a mercy of God to people that He veiled this from them so that their lives could be pleasant, since life is not pleasant amid disquiet, and he tells the believer that the fine prayer rug he is sitting on glorifies God {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:17:00}.

He mentions that the Prophet's soul was transparent and expressed light, so pebbles glorified God in his hand, that at the conquest of Makkah he said there was a stone there that used to greet him whenever he passed, and that the palm trunk yearned for him as a nursing infant yearns {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:17:30}.

He says the Companions heard all this, not the Prophet alone, because their souls were luminous and the darkness of bodily matter did not veil them {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:18:00}.

He relates that a bedouin in the desert asked the Prophet who he was, and he said he was Muhammad ibn Abdullah, the Messenger of God; the bedouin asked who testifies to it, and the Prophet said this tree testifies {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:19:00}.

He continues that the Prophet called the tree to come by God's leave, and it came moving along the ground; he asked it who he was, and it said: you are Muhammad, the Messenger of God {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:19:30}.

The Sheikh holds that what God taught His prophet, abundant as it is, is like a sip from the sea compared with God's knowledge; whoever takes a bucket or a hundred thousand buckets from the sea leaves it undiminished {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:29:30}.

The Sheikh urges learning from the Prophet's tenderness, says a kind word opens hearts, and asks whether any of us can run his household this way {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:48:30}.

He says researchers discovered some years ago that hitting a child on the face kills 300 brain cells and stroking his hair grows 300, then ties this to what is reported about the merit of stroking an orphan's head, a reward for every hair, and says only his Lord knew the Prophet's true worth (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:49:00}.

The Sheikh criticises Muslims for being sentimental in their love of the Prophet, and asks whether any of them follows him as spouse, neighbour, teacher or learner, and whether Muslims have his character in forbearance, loyalty, taking and giving; he says the Companions knew his worth (a sharp criticism by the Sheikh) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:49:30}.

He then recites verses describing the Prophet's character: the best teacher, merciful, faithful to his covenants (a poetic citation) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:50:00}.

He recites further verses describing the Prophet as the dawn of the world, through whom God made the spring of eloquence burst forth, and says the Messenger of God is the morning of the whole world (a poetic citation) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:50:30}.
