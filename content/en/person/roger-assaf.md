## Summary
Roger Assaf is a Lebanese playwright, director and actor who has been a central figure of Arab theatre for more than five decades — and whose life runs through the whole modern history of Lebanon: Christian bourgeois childhood, medical studies abandoned for the stage, Marxist militancy, the Palestinian cause, conversion to Islam, civil war and cultural reconstruction. The Muslim 500 lists him in Arts & Culture as a pioneer of modern Arab theatre and co-founder of the Al-Hakawati Theatre Company; Wikipedia records the outlines of this extraordinary biography. {cite:muslim500-roger-assaf} {cite:wikipedia-roger-assaf}

## Formation
Born in Lebanon in 1941 to a Lebanese father and a French mother, Assaf was raised a Maronite Christian, studied medicine at the Université Saint-Joseph for four years, then abandoned it for the theatre that had already claimed him since childhood. He co-founded Beirut's experimental theatre institutions of the 1960s — the Théâtre de Beyrouth, the College of Arts at the Lebanese University — and worked with the Rahbani brothers' generation that built modern Lebanese theatre. {cite:wikipedia-roger-assaf}

## Al-Hakawati
In 1979 he founded Al-Hakawati — "the Storyteller" — the theatre company that became the flagship of an Arab theatrical tradition: collectively created plays drawing on village life in the south of Lebanon, on Palestinian memory and on the epic form of the hakawati, the traditional coffeehouse storyteller. His wife and collaborator Hanane Hajj Ali, the celebrated actress, has been his partner on stage and in life since. {cite:muslim500-roger-assaf} {cite:wikipedia-roger-assaf}

## A life of engagement
Assaf's biography is also a political archive: leftist militancy, support for the Palestinian cause, a play — Majdaloun — censored and banned by the authorities, work through the civil war, and conversion to Islam in 1986 — a documented fact the sources report, though none names a school for him and none is recorded. He received the Golden Lion at the Venice Biennale for Fatima's Gate on the 2006 war, and lives between Lebanon and France. {cite:wikipedia-roger-assaf} {cite:muslim500-roger-assaf}

## Significance
In the museum's record, Assaf stands for the theatre as the memory of a nation: the Lebanese storyteller who put a country's wars and faiths on the stage, and whose own journey — Christian birth, Muslim conversion, fifty years of shared stage — embodies the entangled worlds the museum documents. {cite:muslim500-roger-assaf} {cite:wikipedia-roger-assaf}

## The story and the storyteller
Assaf's theatre is also a theory of memory: he has described Al-Hakawati as a way of making the stage carry the voices that Lebanese history keeps forgetting — the villager, the refugee, the storyteller whose name was the company's name. The practice is documented in the record: collectively written plays, rural tours, performances in refugee camps, the work of keeping a national memory on a stage the state kept trying to censor. The Muslim 500 lists him for that record; the museum records the actor who made a theatre out of the people the theatre usually forgets. {cite:muslim500-roger-assaf} {cite:wikipedia-roger-assaf}

## The storyteller as witness
Assaf's record is the museum's example of the artist who treats his art as witness: the Lebanese playwright who understood that the stage was the only place where his country's history could be told without censorship, and who built the company that told it. The stage is the story; the company is the instrument; and the result — a Muslim convert whose theatre is the record of a nation that kept trying to forget itself — is the profile his Muslim 500 entry records. The museum notes the career and leaves the censorship to those who imposed it. {cite:muslim500-roger-assaf}
