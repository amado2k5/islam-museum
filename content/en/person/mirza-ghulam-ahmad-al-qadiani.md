## Overview
Ahmad ibn Murtada ibn Muhammad al-Qadiani, called Mirza Ghulam Ahmad ibn Ghulam Murtada ibn 'Ata Muhammad and styled "the second Messiah", is described by al-Zirikli as the leader of the Qadianis and founder of their sect. He was an Indian who wrote some Arabic; his nisba is from Qadian, a village of the Punjab, where he was born and buried. Al-Zirikli gives his birth as 1255 AH, 1839 CE, with a question mark, and his death as 1326 AH, 1908 CE {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 256}. The data card of this museum carries 1835 CE for his birth; this text alone does not explain the difference.

## Life and claims
Al-Zirikli says he read some Arabic literature and busied himself with theology (kalam), and that he served the English government during its occupation of India, working as a clerk in the lower court at Sialkot. He reports that when the thirteenth Hijri century ended he described himself as the renewer of the century, then declared himself the Mahdi, then went further and claimed that God had revealed to him that he was the Messiah, son of Mary {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 256}. He adds that a large number of Indians believed in him as a prophet who followed the Islamic law and as the Ahmad meant in the verse that announces a messenger to come whose name is Ahmad [[passage/quran-61-6|Qur'an 61:6]] {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 256}.

## Writings
He wrote books in Arabic and Urdu; among those in which Arabic predominates, al-Zirikli names Hamamat al-bushra ila ahl Makka wa-sulaha' Umm al-Qura, Tiryaq al-qulub, Haqiqat al-wahy and Mawahib al-Rahman, the last printed in Qadian in 1903. From it he quotes passages in which the author says that his Lord speaks to and teaches him, that he is the promised Messiah and the awaited Imam, and that resisting the government with the intention of jihad is forbidden to every believer {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 256}. Al-Zirikli notes that his son Mahmud Ahmad wrote two books defending his father, which he believes were printed, and that he still has followers in India and Pakistan {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 256}.

## Replies to him
Al-Zirikli records that many of his contemporaries set out to refute him and to declare him an unbeliever, among them Husayn ibn Muhsin al-Saba'i al-Yamani in al-Fath al-Rabbani, Anwar Allah al-Hyderabadi in Ifadat al-afham wa-izalat al-awham, and Muhammad Ali al-Rahmani al-Kanpuri in al-Sahifa al-Rahmaniyya in nine parts {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 256}. Muhammad Iqbal wrote a work on Qadianism whose title, as al-Zirikli gives it, calls it a revolt against the prophethood of Muhammad, a conspiracy against Islam and a separate religion {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 256}. Al-Zirikli also reports that one of the scholars of India told him that the English were the Qadiani's greatest helpers in spreading his call, in order to split the unity of the Muslims in India and divert them from resisting the occupation of their country {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 256}.

## A note on perspective
This entry is al-Zirikli's account, written from the Sunni frame that governs this museum; the claims and the replies are attributed to him and to those he cites. It does not contain the Ahmadiyya community's own account of Mirza Ghulam Ahmad or the academic studies of him. Both are still to be added and need sources of their own.
