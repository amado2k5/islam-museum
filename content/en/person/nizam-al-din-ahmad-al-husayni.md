## Summary

Nizam al-Din Ahmad al-Husayni (7 July 1618 – May 1674) was a sayyid of an Iranian-descended family who became prime minister of the Sultanate of Golconda in the Deccan (1656–1672).{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-en}{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-ar} Neither article names a confession for the man himself; his Safavid-linked sayyid descent and the Twelver Qutb Shahi court he served are context, not attestation, and this museum records his faith as unknown.

## Background and early life

He belonged to a sayyid family long settled in Iran, descended from the religious scholar and landowner Sadr al-Din Dashtaki (died 1498), who had financially supported the Mansuriyya Madrasa in Shiraz; his great-grandfather Ibrahim ibn Salam Allah (died 1582/83) had married the Safavid princess Gawhar Shad Begum, daughter of Ibrahim Mirza.{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-en} His father Muhammad Ma'sum (died 1622/23) and his uncle Nasir al-Din (died 1614/15) left Iran for Taif and Mecca, while another branch of the family stayed in Fars, and surviving letters and legal papers show that Nizam al-Din Ahmad knew and used Persian, though he appears to have written poetry only in Arabic.{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-en}

He was born on 7 July 1618 in Taif and was taught jurisprudence, hadith, Arabic and logic, reportedly possessing a great memory that enhanced his capacity to convey knowledge.{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-en}{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-ar} He married the daughter of the distinguished Meccan jurist and merchant Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Manufi (died 1634/35).{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-en}

## Career in Golconda

In the seventeenth century the western Indian Ocean became a zone of exchange fed by migration, and Golconda was home to immigrant Arab scholars from Iran and the Arabian Peninsula.{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-en} On reaching the Deccan in 1645/6 he married a second wife, a daughter of Abdullah Qutb Shah (r. 1626–1672), the sultan of Golconda; the marriage paved his way to prominence and to his patronage of Arabic literary culture.{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-en}{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-ar}

When the prime minister Muhammad Sa'id Ardistani shifted his allegiance to the Mughal Empire in 1656, Nizam al-Din Ahmad was appointed prime minister the same year, became a leading figure at court alongside Hayat Bakhshi Baygum, the sultan's mother and regent, and was elevated to the role of Ayn al-Mulk, "Eye of the Kingdom", his authority allegedly surpassing that of the other ministers.{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-en} He acted as a gatekeeper introducing foreign merchants to the sultan, resided for a time in the Golconda citadel, and had a palace built for him in Hyderabad in 1658/59.{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-en} At the zenith of his power he corresponded with Khalifeh Soltan and Mirza Mohammad Karaki, two leading religious statesmen of Safavid Iran, and submitted petitions claiming landed property in Iran.{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-en}

After Abdullah Qutb Shah's death in 1672 and the succession struggle, his authority diminished; he was imprisoned by Sayyid Muzaffar, a military commander who had immigrated from Iran, and was killed in May 1674, likely due to poisoning.{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-en}{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-ar} He was the father of the writer Ali Khan, known as Ibn Ma'sum al-Madani, author of Sulafat al-Asr.{cite:wikipedia-nizam-al-din-ahmad-al-husayni-ar}
