## Summary
Ahmad ibn Jaʿfar ibn Hamdan ibn Malik ibn Shabib, Abu Bakr al-Qatiʿi (274–368 AH), a hadith scholar of Baghdad. The Musnad, the Zuhd and the History were transmitted from him, on the authority of Abd Allah ibn Ahmad ibn Hanbal. {cite:muntazam-ibn-al-jawzi j.7 p.96-97}

## Lineage and transmission
He was called al-Qatiʿi after the flour quarter (Qati'at al-Daqiq) where he lived. He heard from Abu Bakr ibn Ibrahim ibn Ishaq, Ishaq ibn al-Hasan al-Harbiyyan, Bishr ibn Dusi, al-Kudaymi, al-Kajji, Abd Allah ibn Ahmad and others. He narrated a great deal and was trustworthy. Abd Allah ibn Ahmad would come to them, read to them whatever they wanted, and seat him on his lap (his mother was the daughter of the brother of Abd Allah ibn al-Hassas). People would say to him, "Does it not hurt you?" and he would say, "I love him." {cite:muntazam-ibn-al-jawzi j.7 p.96}

## Criticism that went unheeded
When al-Qatiʿa was flooded by the Black Water, some of his books were lost, and he began compiling replacements. Some people criticised him and said he had written from a book in which he had no record of hearing it. Such a thing, however, is no reproach to him: leading scholars such as al-Daraqutni, Ibn Shahin, al-Barqani, Abu Nuʿaym and al-Hakim related from him, and no one refrained from narrating from him or stopped relying on him. It is related that he changed in the last part of his life, so that he no longer recognised anything that was read to him. He died in the year 368 AH and was buried in the cemetery of Bab Harb, near the grave of Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal. {cite:muntazam-ibn-al-jawzi j.7 p.97}

## Transmitter of Fada'il al-Sahaba, and what its editor says of him
Al-Qati'i is the link through which Ahmad ibn Hanbal's [[work/fadail-al-sahaba-ahmad|Fada'il al-Sahaba]] reached later generations: he heard it from [[person/abdullah-ibn-ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Abd Allah ibn Ahmad]], and [[person/ibn-al-allaf-abu-tahir|Ibn al-Allaf]] read it to him on Saturday 16 Jumada al-Akhira 368 AH {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.55}. The book's editor records that he was born in 274, travelled to Basra, Kufa, Mosul and Wasit, and transmitted abundantly from Abd Allah, from whom he heard the *Musnad*, *al-Zuhd* and *al-Fada'il* {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.40}. He is named "Ibn Malik" after his ancestor, and that is how he appears in the book's chains {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.40} {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.54}.

### What the imams said of him
Al-Daraqutni said: trustworthy, an ascetic, of early standing, and I heard that his prayers were answered; al-Hakim said: trustworthy and reliable {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.40-41}. Al-Barqani said that he spoke of him as weak before al-Hakim, who objected, saying "he is my shaykh", and spoke well of him {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.41}. Ibn al-Salah accused him of confusion and deterioration of memory; al-Dhahabi answered that this was excessive and that Abu Bakr had the highest chains of anyone in his time {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.41}.

### His additions to the book
Al-Qati'i added to *Fada'il al-Sahaba* narrations from his own teachers other than Abd Allah; these are the third kind of material in the book, most of which the editor judges weak, with many fabricated reports from abandoned transmitters {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.46}. He supports this with [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]]'s statement in *Minhaj al-Sunna* that al-Qati'i's additions contain many fabricated hadiths that some mistook for Ahmad's own {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.46-47}. Some chapters of the book are devoted to his additions, such as "From the merits of Ali, from the hadith of Abu Bakr ibn Malik from his teachers other than Abd Allah" {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1382}.
