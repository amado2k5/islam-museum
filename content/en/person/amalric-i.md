## Overview
Amalric I, whom Ibn al-Athir calls Mari and the Encyclopaedia of Islam calls Amalric or Amaury, was king of the Franks at [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]] in the years when Fatimid Egypt was passing into the hands of [[person/nur-al-din-mahmud-ibn-zangi|Nur al-Din]]'s generals. The Encyclopaedia has no article of its own on him. This record is put together from the passages in its articles on Dirgham, the Fatimids, Damietta, Nur al-Din, the Isma'ili leader Sinan and others, and from Ibn al-Athir's report of his death.

## Accession
The article on Nur al-Din dates Amalric's accession to Rabi' I 558, February 1163, and says that from then on the Franks turned their attention towards Egypt. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 141} By its account the Fatimid caliphate was already coming apart, and the king of Jerusalem took an interest in Egypt, where the emirs were in revolt against the vizier [[person/talai-ibn-ruzzik|Talai ibn Ruzzik]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 141}

## The first expeditions
The articles do not agree on every date. The article on the Fatimids says Amalric made a first incursion into Egypt in 1161, when he exacted a payment of tribute from Talai, and came back in 1162, but had to retreat before the deliberate flooding of the Delta. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 894} The article on [[person/dirgham-al-lakhmi|Dirgham]] puts the invasion in September 1162, with the aim of claiming the tribute Talai had already promised; Dirgham, then a commander, was sent against him and was defeated, but the Egyptians breached the Nile dikes to flood the plain, and Amalric had to withdraw into Palestine. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 343} The museum keeps both statements and does not choose between them.

At the end of 1163 or the start of 1164 his advance guard was again on Egyptian soil. Dirgham tried first to win Nur al-Din to his side and then offered Amalric a treaty, hostages and an annual tribute on condition that he withdraw. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 343} The article on Nur al-Din adds that Dirgham's offer was a treaty of allegiance that would have made Egypt a vassal of the kingdom of Jerusalem. Amalric accepted, but he was harassed by Nur al-Din's attacks in Syria, did not have enough troops to fight on two fronts, and could not send an army in time to prevent [[person/asad-al-din-shirkuh|Shirkuh]] from reaching the Delta in 559/1164. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 141}

## Rivalry for Egypt
The article on the Zangid ruler describes a competition between Amalric and Nur al-Din over the political and military domination of Egypt, the key to hegemony in the region, and says that between 559/1164 and 564/1168 their armies entered Egypt three times. In Rabi' II 564, January 1169, Shirkuh became master of Egypt as vizier of the Fatimid caliph. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 470}

The vizier [[person/shawar|Shawar]], restored in 559/1164, is described in the article on the caliph al-Adid as having asked for Amalric's intervention to drive Shirkuh's forces out of Egypt. Shirkuh capitulated at [[place/bilbays|Bilbays]] and went back to Syria. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 216} The article on Bilbays says the town was besieged by Amalric. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 1282}

## Fustat and Cairo, 564
The article on [[place/al-fustat|Fustat]] calls the year 564 catastrophic there. The Frankish armies of Amalric were encamped just south of the city, at Birkat al-Habash. Shawar, their former ally, had summoned them four years earlier, and he now had Fustat evacuated and set on fire, fearing that the Franks would use it as a base against [[place/cairo|Cairo]]. The fire burned for fifty-four days. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1001} The museum's card is [[event/fustat-burning-cairo-564|The Burning of al-Fustat]]. The article on al-Adid says Shawar, alarmed, tried to negotiate the withdrawal of the Frankish troops, and that an appeal to Nur al-Din followed which it describes as signing the warrant for Shawar's imminent fall. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 216}

## Damietta and the Byzantine pact
The article on the Fatimids records that in 1168 the Byzantine emperor Manuel Comnenus concluded a pact with Amalric for an attack on Egypt, which took place the following year. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 893} After Shirkuh died on 22 Jumada II 564, 23 March 1169, his nephew [[person/salah-al-din-yusuf-ibn-ayyub|Salah al-Din]] became vizier, and Amalric, alarmed at his seizure of Egypt, appealed to the whole of Christendom. The Franks chose Damietta as their objective, but a lack of co-ordination between Byzantines and Franks led to the abandonment of the siege. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 142} The article on [[place/dimyat|Damietta]] puts the end of the siege in Rabi' I 565, December 1169. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 317} See [[event/siege-of-damietta-1169|Siege of Damietta (1169)]]. Not long afterwards, the article on the Fatimids says, Salah al-Din ended the Fatimid caliphate in 567/1171. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 895}

## Other contacts
An article on the Armenian princes of Cilicia says that Amalric intervened to re-establish peace in the affair of Thoros, the Armenian prince who in 1162 once more raised the standard of revolt. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 51} The article on Sinan, the Isma'ili chief in Syria, says that in 569/1173 he sent an embassy to Amalric seeking a formal rapprochement with the Latin state of Jerusalem. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 482}

## Death and succession
Ibn al-Athir writes that Mari, king of the Franks, died at the beginning of 569, and that he was the greatest of their kings in courage, judgement and cunning. He left a leper son, who the Franks made king in name, while [[person/raymond-iii-tripoli|Raymond]], count of Tripoli, took charge of the government. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p. 68} The museum's card is [[event/death-of-amalric-569|Death of Amalric]], and the son is [[person/baldwin-iv|Baldwin IV]]. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, in the article on Nur al-Din, gives Nur al-Din's own death as 569/1174, but it does not date Amalric's. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 482}

## What is not known
The sources read give nothing about Amalric's birth, his parents or his family, and the Encyclopaedia of Islam gives no date for his death. The Latin chronicles of the kingdom of Jerusalem, which the museum has not read for this record, would be the next place to check dates and the relationship between events in Egypt and events in Syria.
