## Overview
Isma'il ibn Ja'far al-Sadiq ibn Muhammad al-Baqir was a Hashimi of Quraysh. Al-Zirikli calls him the forefather of the Fatimid caliphs and says the Isma'iliyya are named after him; they were originally a Shi'i sect and are set apart from the Twelvers by holding his imamate after his father, where the Twelvers hold the imamate of his brother Musa al-Kazim {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 311}. Al-Zirikli gives his death as 143 AH, 760 CE, and no year of birth {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 311}. Al-Safadi enters him as the eldest son of Ja'far al-Sadiq, the one to whom the Isma'ili sect is attributed {cite:wafi-bi-l-wafayat-safadi-dar-ihya-2000 vol. 9 p. 62}.

## What the history books record
Al-Zirikli says nothing in the history books to hand shows that he was a person of note in his lifetime, and that he died in his father's lifetime {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 311}. He quotes Ibn Khaldun that Isma'il died before his father, that Abu Ja'far al-Mansur asked for him, and that the governor of Medina testified that he was dead {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 311}. He also quotes the author of Tadhhib al-Kamal: an imam who died young, from whom no hadith is reported {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 311}.

## Reports of his death
Al-Zirikli reports that the editor of Firaq al-Shi'a transmits that Isma'il died at al-Urayd and was buried at al-Baqi in 133 AH {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 311}, and that Huart, in the Encyclopaedia of Islam, says he died in Medina in 143 AH, five years before his father {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 311}. In the Imami account, as al-Zirikli quotes the author of Daw' al-Mishkat, an Imami, Isma'il kept company with his father, narrated from him and died in his lifetime without claiming the imamate; some people claimed it for him by mistake, out of the father's love for him, and when he died most of those who had thought so among his father's companions turned away, while some distant people and ignorant ones remained {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 311}.

## The Isma'ili account
Al-Zirikli quotes al-Nawbakhti in Firaq al-Shi'a: the Isma'ili sect denied that Isma'il died in his father's lifetime; they said it was a ruse by his father, who feared for him and hid him from the people, and they claimed he would not die until he ruled the earth and took charge of the people {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 311}. Some Isma'ilis hold that his father made his death public out of dissimulation so that the Abbasids would not seek to kill him {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 311}.

Al-Safadi gives the Isma'ili position in their own voice. They say Isma'il was the one designated at the outset, that al-Sadiq took no other wife and no slave girl during his mother's life, and that they differ on his death: some say he died in his father's lifetime and that the point of designating him was still the passing of the imamate to his children, as Musa designated Harun, who died before Musa; others say he did not die and his death was announced out of dissimulation {cite:wafi-bi-l-wafayat-safadi-dar-ihya-2000 vol. 9 p. 62}. They cite the witnessing and the written record of his death as unusual, and the report that al-Mansur was told Isma'il had been seen in Basra, whereupon he wrote to al-Sadiq, who sent him the record bearing the testimony of his governor in Medina {cite:wafi-bi-l-wafayat-safadi-dar-ihya-2000 vol. 9 pp. 62-63}.

## The Isma'ilis after him
Al-Zirikli cites the book Itti'az al-Hunafa' that his son Muhammad, known as al-Maktum, rose after his death, and that they concealed his name, as they later concealed the names of others, out of caution towards the Abbasid caliphs {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 311}. He quotes Ibn Khaldun that the Isma'ilis call Muhammad the complete seventh of the manifest imams and the first of the hidden imams: Muhammad ibn Isma'il, then his son Ja'far al-Musaddaq, then his son Muhammad al-Habib, then his son Ubaydallah al-Mahdi, founder of the state in Ifriqiya and the Maghrib that Abu Abdallah al-Shi'i established among the Kutama {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 311}. He notes that the Qarmatians, whose state was in Bahrayn, were among the Isma'ilis, and that the Isma'ili creed among the Kutama went back to da'is whom Ja'far al-Sadiq had sent to the Maghrib; when Abu Abdallah al-Shi'i came from Yemen he found it there and revived it {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 311}.

From Huart, al-Zirikli reports that Isma'il's sons left Medina because of the political persecution of the Alids: Muhammad, the eldest, went to the region of Damawand near Rayy and hid there, his sons hid in Khurasan and then went to Qandahar and India, and his brother Ali went to Syria and then the Maghrib; Isma'il's sons sent da'is to the Islamic world from their hiding places {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 311}. Among the best-known da'is was Maymun al-Qaddah, whose son became head of the Qarmatian sect {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 312}. Of the Isma'ilis of his own day al-Zirikli names the Nizaris in India, led by the Aga Khan, and the Sulaymaniyya in Yemen, also called the Makarima and the Da'udiyya, of the Banu Murra of Yemen, living in Aden, Hudayda, Bayt al-Faqih and the mountains of Haraz and Hamdan, and also called the Bohras {cite:alam-zirikli-dar-al-ilm-2002 vol. 1 p. 312}.

## Al-Safadi on their doctrine
Al-Safadi sets out the Isma'ili doctrine as they relate it. After Isma'il comes Muhammad ibn Isma'il, the complete seventh, with whom the cycle of seven is complete; from him begin the hidden imams, who live concealed in the lands while their da'is work openly. The earth is never without a living imam, manifest or hidden; when the imam is manifest his proof is concealed, and when he is hidden his proof and his da'is are manifest. The rulings of the imams revolve around sevens, like the days of the week, the heavens and the planets, and those of the naqibs around twelve {cite:wafi-bi-l-wafayat-safadi-dar-ihya-2000 vol. 9 p. 63}. He says those who hold the imamate of Muhammad ibn Isma'il are called the Mubarakiyya, that some of them are reported to say he is a prophet and that prophecy continues in his line, and he adds that if this is true of them they are, in his own judgement, unbelievers in truth; the judgement is al-Safadi's and is not adopted here {cite:wafi-bi-l-wafayat-safadi-dar-ihya-2000 vol. 9 p. 63}.

Al-Safadi divides the Isma'ilis into early, middle and late. The middle group, he says, attached themselves to the sect to hide behind the Shi'a and for protection from the sword, and are nicknamed the Batiniyya, Qarmatians, Babakiyya, Sab'iyya, Khurramiyya and Muhammira, each to be treated under the man they trace themselves to; the late group are those who hold the imamate of this Isma'il and that the imamate does not leave his line {cite:wafi-bi-l-wafayat-safadi-dar-ihya-2000 vol. 9 p. 63}. He quotes Ibn Abi al-Dam, judge of Hama, in his book on the Islamic sects, that the basis of their doctrine is the necessity of an infallible imam, and that nothing else contrary to the foundations of religion is reported of them, unlike what is reported of the Batiniyya and others; he adds that al-Hasan ibn Muhammad al-Sabbah al-Nizari went up to the fortress of Alamut in Sha'ban 483 AH after travelling to the land of his imam and learning from him how to conduct the summons {cite:wafi-bi-l-wafayat-safadi-dar-ihya-2000 vol. 9 p. 64}.
