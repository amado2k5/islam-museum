## Summary
Laila Lalami is a Moroccan-American novelist and essayist and a professor of creative writing at the University of California, Riverside. The Muslim 500 lists her in Arts & Culture as a novelist and UC Riverside professor whose novel The Moor's Account was a Pulitzer Prize finalist; Wikipedia records her formation in Morocco, Britain and the United States. {cite:muslim500-laila-lalami} {cite:wikipedia-laila-lalami}

## Between languages
Lalami was born in 1968 in Rabat, Morocco, and raised in a French-speaking post-colonial education while Arabic and Amazigh sounded at home — the layered linguistic inheritance she has written about in essays. She earned a British Council fellowship to study in the United Kingdom, took a master's in linguistics, then moved to the United States where she completed a PhD in linguistics before turning to fiction full time. {cite:wikipedia-laila-lalami}

## The novels
Her fiction works the border between Morocco and its others: Secret Son ⁦(2009)⁩, a story of a boy from the Casablanca slums who discovers a different inheritance; The Moor's Account ⁦(2014)⁩, which retells the 1527 Narváez expedition through the eyes of the enslaved Moroccan Mustafa al-Zamori — Estebanico — the first African explorer of America, and was a finalist for both the Pulitzer Prize and the Man Booker Prize; The Other Americans ⁦(2019)⁩, a novel of immigration and belonging in California; and Conditional Citizens ⁦(2020)⁩, an essay collection on citizenship and exclusion. {cite:wikipedia-laila-lalami} {cite:muslim500-laila-lalami}

## The voice on citizenship
Her essays and criticism — in The Nation and elsewhere — return to the question the novels embody: what a citizen is, and who is denied the full meaning of the word. Writing from the position of a naturalised Moroccan-American, she has become a regular public commentator on immigration, identity and the literature of the border. {cite:wikipedia-laila-lalami}

## Significance
In the museum's record, Lalami stands for the Maghribi writer in English: the novelist who pulled a Moroccan slave's voice out of the colonial archive and put it on a Pulitzer shortlist, and whose essays ask the questions of belonging that her characters live — the diaspora's literature of citizenship at its most accomplished. {cite:muslim500-laila-lalami} {cite:wikipedia-laila-lalami}

## The archive's voice
Lalami's most ambitious project is also her simplest: to take a historical footnote — the Moroccan slave Estebanico, the first African to walk across North America — and give him the interiority the archive denied him. The Moor's Account is the record of that act; and her essays on citizenship are the record of its argument: that the archive's silences are not accidents but decisions, and that literature is the instrument for repairing them. The museum keeps both: the novelist who restored a voice, and the critic who explains why the voice was missing. {cite:wikipedia-laila-lalami} {cite:muslim500-laila-lalami}

## The novelist as archivist
Lalami's record is the museum's example of the novelist who treats fiction as an act of recovery: the Moroccan-American who understood that the archive had silenced the voices it was supposed to keep, and who made the case in English for an audience that had never been asked to hear them. The fiction is the story; the archive is the subject; and the result — a Muslim novelist whose books are a record of what the archive forgot — is the profile her Muslim 500 entry records. The museum notes the career and leaves the recovery to those who read it. {cite:muslim500-laila-lalami}
