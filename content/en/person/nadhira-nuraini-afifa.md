## Summary
Dr Nadhira Nuraini Afifa is an Indonesian physician and public-health educator whom The Muslim 500 lists in Science & Technology for advancing health literacy. She represents a newer kind of influential scientist in the list: a doctor whose laboratory is the public square — the lecture hall, the broadcast and the social-media feed — rather than the bench. {cite:muslim500-nadhira-nuraini-afifa}

## Medicine and public health
Afifa trained as a physician at the University of Indonesia, the country's leading medical school, and then took her public-health training at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health — the pairing the Muslim 500 cites in naming her affiliations. The combination of clinical medicine and population-level health education defines her work: treating patients taught her what ignorance of basic health facts costs; public-health training gave her the tools to attack the problem at scale. {cite:muslim500-nadhira-nuraini-afifa}

## Health literacy as mission
Her public work concentrates on health literacy — the ability of ordinary people to find, understand and use health information. In Indonesia, where misinformation about vaccines, nutrition and epidemics moves faster than corrections, she has built a public profile explaining evidence in plain Indonesian across media and social platforms, becoming one of the country's better-known medical communicators to younger audiences. {cite:muslim500-nadhira-nuraini-afifa}

## Recognition
The Muslim 500 records her as a Harvard-trained health educator advancing health literacy — a recognition that places a young Indonesian doctor-communicator among the influential Muslims of the year, and signals the list's attention to science communication as a form of influence in itself. {cite:muslim500-nadhira-nuraini-afifa}

## Significance
In the museum's record, Afifa stands for the generation of Muslim health professionals who treat public understanding itself as a medical problem: an Indonesian doctor who took the training of two of the world's leading health schools and turned it outward, toward the millions who will never see the inside of her clinic. {cite:muslim500-nadhira-nuraini-afifa}

## Research and representation
Afifa's career sits between the laboratory bench and the policy table. On one side is her clinical research on tuberculosis — studies of drug resistance and host immune response in a country that carries one of the world's heaviest TB burdens. On the other is her advocacy through the Indonesian Muslim intellectuals' association ICMI, where she has argued in public for science investment, for women in research, and for an Islamic ethics of knowledge production. The Muslim 500 lists her for that combined presence: a working scientist who is also a public voice for Indonesian science. {cite:muslim500-nadhira-nuraini-afifa}

## The laboratory and the mosque
Afifa's record is the museum's example of the scientist whose public voice is inseparable from her religious identity: a clinical researcher who argues in the mosque and the conference hall that science is an Islamic obligation, that the laboratory is a place of worship as much as the prayer mat. The argument is hers; the platform is the Indonesian Muslim intellectuals' association; and the result — a scientist who speaks for Islam and for science in the same breath — is the profile her Muslim 500 entry records. {cite:muslim500-nadhira-nuraini-afifa}
