## Summary
Abu al-Walid Muhammad ibn Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn Rushd of Cordoba, known as Ibn Rushd al-Hafid ("the grandson"), was a Maliki jurist and chief judge of [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]], a physician and a philosopher, whom the Latins call Averroes. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 12} {cite:al-aalam p. 849} The editor of Bidayat al-Mujtahid distinguishes him from his grandfather [[person/ibn-rushd-al-jadd|Ibn Rushd al-Jadd]] (d. 520 AH), author of al-Muqaddimat on the opening of Malik's Mudawwana. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 12}

## Lineage and family
He was Muhammad, son of the judge and jurist Ahmad, son of the imam and chief judge Abu al-Walid Muhammad ibn Ahmad ibn Rushd al-Jadd, and grew up in a family known for learning and judgeship in al-Andalus. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 9} His father, [[person/abu-al-abbas-ahmad-ibn-rushd|Ahmad ibn Abi al-Walid ibn Rushd]], was a jurist and judge (487-563 AH) who studied under his own father and taught his son Abu al-Walid. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 12} Hallaq reports from Ibn Abi Usaybi'a that Ibn Rushd left a son who was a physician, Abu Muhammad Abd Allah, and other sons who worked in law and judgeship, and that his son the judge Ahmad (d. 622 AH) heard from him. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 12} {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 13}

## Birth and education
He was born in Cordoba in 520 AH, a few months - or, some say, one month - before his grandfather the judge died. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 9} He studied law and excelled in it, heard hadith, recited the Muwatta' to his father from memory, mastered medicine, and turned to kalam, philosophy and logic until he became proverbial in them. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 10} It is related that from the age of reason he never left study and reading except on the night his father died and his wedding night, that he drafted some ten thousand leaves in what he wrote, annotated and abridged, that people turned to his opinion in medicine as they did in law, and that he knew the poetry of al-Mutanabbi and Habib (Abu Tammam) by heart. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 10}

## Teachers and students
He studied law under [[person/ibn-bashkuwal-khalaf|Abu al-Qasim ibn Bashkuwal]], Abu Marwan Abd al-Malik ibn Masarra, Abu Bakr ibn Samhun and Abu Ja'far ibn Abd al-Aziz; Abu Abd Allah al-Mazari gave him a licence; he worked with the hadith master Abu Muhammad ibn Rizq; and he learned medicine from Abu Marwan ibn Jazbul of Valencia and Abu Ja'far ibn Harun, with whom he stayed for some time and from whom he took much of the philosophical sciences. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 14} {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 15} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 10} Those who heard from him include [[person/ibn-hawtallah|Abu Muhammad ibn Hawt Allah]], Abu Bakr ibn Jahwar, Abu al-Hasan Sahl ibn Malik and his son the judge Ahmad; Abu al-Rabi' ibn Salim al-Kala'i and Ibn al-Taylasan transmitted from him. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 15} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 10}

## Judgeship and the Almohads
He was appointed judge of Cordoba after Abu Muhammad ibn Mughith and his conduct was praised; before Cordoba he had spent some time in [[place/seville|Seville]]. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 13} He stood high with al-Mansur and was honoured in his state, and al-Mansur's son al-Nasir also respected him greatly; when al-Mansur was in Cordoba in 591 AH on his way to campaign against Alfonso, he summoned him and drew him close, as Hallaq reports from Ibn Abi Usaybi'a. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 13} Al-Bakri adds that he did not use his standing with rulers to raise his rank or gather wealth, but for the good of his city and of al-Andalus. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 10}

## Ordeal and death
Al-Bakri says he was tried at the end of his life: Sultan Ya'qub [[person/yaqub-al-mansur-al-muwahhidi|al-Mansur]] tried him over what was alleged against him of an inclination to "the sciences of the ancients"; people stopped studying with him, and he was confined to his house, no one entering, until he died confined in his house in [[place/marrakesh|Marrakesh]]. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 13} Hallaq quotes Makhluf that he was tried by exile and the burning of his books at the end of Ya'qub al-Mansur's days, when he was denounced and religious and political matters were ascribed to him. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 15} {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 16} Al-Zirikli, for his part, says al-Mansur exiled him to Marrakesh and had some of his books burned, then was reconciled with him and allowed him to return, and that he died in Marrakesh and his body was carried to Cordoba. {cite:al-aalam p. 849} He died in 595 AH. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 13}

## Works
Al-Bakri says his works exceed sixty, in philosophy and its commentaries, kalam, astronomy, legal theory, grammar and medicine, and that most were lost when his books were burned in the ordeal. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 11} In law and legal theory they include [[work/bidayat-al-mujtahid|Bidayat al-Mujtahid wa-Nihayat al-Muqtasid]], a commentary on his grandfather's Muqaddimat, an abridgement of al-Ghazali's [[work/al-mustasfa|al-Mustasfa]], Minhaj al-Adilla on legal theory and al-Tahsil on the disagreements of scholars; in philosophy [[work/fasl-al-maqal|Fasl al-Maqal]], Manahij al-Adilla fi al-Kashf an Aqa'id al-Milla, [[work/tahafut-al-tahafut|Tahafut al-Tahafut]] answering [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]], and epitomes and commentaries on Aristotle; in medicine al-Kulliyyat and a commentary on Ibn Sina's medical poem; in Arabic grammar al-Daruri. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 11} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 12} {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 16} Al-Zirikli notes that he devoted himself to Aristotle, translated him and added much, and that his books were translated into Latin and Hebrew. {cite:al-aalam p. 849}

## What scholars said of him
His contemporary al-Dabbi called him a well-known jurist and memoriser who took part in many sciences; Ibn Abi Usaybi'a, unrivalled in law and juristic disagreement; Ibn Farhun, that al-Andalus produced no one like him in accomplishment, learning and merit; al-Maqqari, that he brought Malik's school closer than anyone before him. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 14} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 13} Al-Bakri describes him as, for all his eminence, the most humble of men, and as more given to understanding (diraya) than to transmission (riwaya). {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 10}
