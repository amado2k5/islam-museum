## Overview
Abu al-Walid Muhammad ibn Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn Rushd of Cordoba, known as Ibn Rushd al-Hafid ("the grandson"), was a Maliki jurist and chief judge of [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]], a physician and a philosopher, whom the Franks call Averroes {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 12} {cite:al-aalam p. 850}. The Encyclopaedia of Islam presents him as the "Commentator of Aristotle", famous in the medieval West as Averroes, a scholar of the religious sciences and the natural sciences, theologian and philosopher, born in Cordoba in 520 AH/1126 CE and dead in Marrakesh in 595 AH/1198 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 941} The editor of Bidayat al-Mujtahid distinguishes him from his grandfather [[person/ibn-rushd-al-jadd|Ibn Rushd al-Jadd]] (died 520 AH), author of al-Muqaddimat on the opening of Malik's Mudawwana {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 12}. He was born in Cordoba in 520 AH (1126 CE) into a family known for learning and judgeship in al-Andalus {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 9} {cite:al-aalam p. 850}. Al-Bakri's biographical note says he studied law, recited the Muwatta' to his father from memory, mastered medicine, and turned to kalam, philosophy and logic {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 10}. He became judge of Cordoba after a period in [[place/seville|Seville]], and stood high with al-Mansur and was honoured in his state {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 13}.

Al-Bakri says his works exceed sixty, among them [[work/bidayat-al-mujtahid|Bidayat al-Mujtahid]] in law, [[work/fasl-al-maqal|Fasl al-Maqal]], [[work/tahafut-al-tahafut|Tahafut al-Tahafut]], in which he answered [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]], al-Kulliyyat in medicine, and epitomes and commentaries on Aristotle's books {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 11} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 12}. Al-Bakri says Sultan Ya'qub [[person/yaqub-al-mansur-al-muwahhidi|al-Mansur]] put him to trial at the end of his life over an alleged leaning to the sciences of the ancients, and he was confined to his house until he died in [[place/marrakesh|Marrakesh]] in 595 AH {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 13}. Al-Zirikli, for his part, says al-Mansur exiled him to Marrakesh and burned some of his books, then was reconciled with him and allowed him to return, and that he died in Marrakesh and his body was carried to Cordoba {cite:al-aalam p. 850}.

## Lineage and family
He was Muhammad, son of the judge and jurist Ahmad, son of the imam and chief judge Abu al-Walid Muhammad ibn Ahmad ibn Rushd al-Jadd, and grew up in a family known for learning and judgeship in al-Andalus. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 9} The Encyclopaedia of Islam likewise says that his grandfather, a Maliki jurisconsult, had been judge and imam of the Great Mosque of Cordoba, and that his father was also a judge. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 941} His father, [[person/abu-al-abbas-ahmad-ibn-rushd|Ahmad ibn Abi al-Walid ibn Rushd]], was a jurist and judge who lived from 487 to 563 AH, studied under his own father and taught his son Abu al-Walid. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 12} Hallaq reports from Ibn Abi Usaybi'a that Ibn Rushd left a son who was a physician, Abu Muhammad Abd Allah, and other sons who worked in law and judgeship, and that his son the judge Ahmad, who died in 622 AH, heard from him. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 12} {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 13}

## Birth and education
He was born in Cordoba in 520 AH, a few months, or, some say, one month, before his grandfather the judge died. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 9} He studied law and excelled in it, heard hadith, recited the Muwatta' to his father from memory, mastered medicine, and turned to kalam, philosophy and logic until he became proverbial in them. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 10} It is related that from the age of reason he never left study and reading except on the night his father died and his wedding night, that he drafted some ten thousand leaves in what he wrote, annotated and abridged, that people turned to his opinion in medicine as they did in law, and that he knew by heart the poetry of al-Mutanabbi and Habib, that is Abu Tammam. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 10}

## Teachers and students
He studied law under [[person/ibn-bashkuwal-khalaf|Abu al-Qasim ibn Bashkuwal]], Abu Marwan Abd al-Malik ibn Masarra, Abu Bakr ibn Samhun and Abu Ja'far ibn Abd al-Aziz; Abu Abd Allah al-Mazari gave him a licence; he worked with the hadith master Abu Muhammad ibn Rizq; and he learned medicine from Abu Marwan ibn Jazbul of Valencia and Abu Ja'far ibn Harun, with whom he stayed for some time and from whom he took much of the philosophical sciences. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 14} {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 15} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 10} Those who heard from him include [[person/ibn-hawtallah|Abu Muhammad ibn Hawt Allah]], Abu Bakr ibn Jahwar, Abu al-Hasan Sahl ibn Malik and his son the judge Ahmad; Abu al-Rabi' ibn Salim al-Kala'i and Ibn al-Taylasan transmitted from him. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 15} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 10}

## His training in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The article stresses the excellent legal education of the future Commentator. It names Abu Muhammad ibn Rizq as his teacher, says he became very competent in khilaf, the science of legal disagreement, and says he learned the Muwatta' by heart. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 941} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942} It reports from Ibn al-Abbar that he studied only "a little" with Ibn Bashkuwal, and that law and its principles, diraya, interested him more than the transmission of hadith, riwaya. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942} He also worked on Ash'ari kalam, which he was later to criticise. In medicine he was the pupil of Abu Ja'far Harun of Trujillo, who also taught hadith, and Ibn al-Abbar names Abu Marwan ibn Jurrayul as another teacher and a foremost practitioner. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942} The article notes that the biographers do not mention philosophical studies. Ibn Abi Usaybi'a says, following al-Badji, that he studied the "philosophical sciences" with the physician Abu Ja'far, and Ibn al-Abbar remarks in passing that he "inclined towards the sciences of the Ancients", which the article takes as probably an allusion to his knowledge of Greek thought. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942}

## Judgeship and the Almohads
He was appointed judge of Cordoba after Abu Muhammad ibn Mughith and his conduct was praised; before Cordoba he had spent some time in [[place/seville|Seville]]. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 13} He stood high with al-Mansur and was honoured in his state, and al-Mansur's son al-Nasir also respected him greatly; when al-Mansur was in Cordoba in 591 AH on his way to campaign against Alfonso, he summoned him and drew him close, as Hallaq reports from Ibn Abi Usaybi'a. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 13} Al-Bakri adds that he did not use his standing with rulers to raise his rank or gather wealth, but for the good of his city and of al-Andalus. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 10}

## Career at the Almohad court according to the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The article places him in Marrakesh in 548 AH/1153 CE, where Renan supposes he was occupied with the intentions of the Almohad [[person/abd-al-mumin-ibn-ali|Abd al-Mu'min]] for the colleges then being founded, and says that his commentary on De Caelo shows him engaged in astronomical observation there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942} It suggests that he may have met [[person/ibn-tufayl|Ibn Tufayl]] at this time, who was to present him to Abu Ya'qub Yusuf, the successor of Abd al-Mu'min. In the account of al-Marrakushi, taken from a pupil of Ibn Rushd, the prince asked whether the heavens had existed from all eternity or had a beginning; Ibn Rushd was uneasy at so dangerous a question, but Yusuf began a discussion with Ibn Tufayl that displayed wide knowledge of the ancient philosophers and the theologians, and Ibn Rushd, put at ease, spoke in turn and showed the extent of his learning. He received gifts and from then on the prince's favour; the article dates the event to 1169 or slightly earlier. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942} Al-Marrakushi also says the caliph complained of the obscurity of Aristotle's texts and their translations, and that Ibn Tufayl, thinking himself too old and too busy, asked Ibn Rushd to undertake the explanation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942}

He remained in favour throughout the reign of Abu Ya'qub Yusuf, 558 to 580 AH/1163 to 1184 CE. In 565 AH/1169 CE he was judge of Seville, and in a passage of his paraphrase of De partibus animalium, finished that year, he notes the duties of his post and his separation from his books, which had stayed in Cordoba. In 567 AH/1171 CE he was back in Cordoba as judge. He kept up his output of commentaries despite his duties and travelled to several towns of the Almohad empire, above all Seville. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942} In 578 AH/1182 CE, in Marrakesh, he succeeded Ibn Tufayl as chief physician to Abu Ya'qub Yusuf, and then received the office of chief judge of Cordoba. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942}

## The disgrace and the end, according to the Encyclopaedia of Islam
Under Ya'qub al-Mansur, who reigned from 580 to 595 AH, Ibn Rushd still enjoyed the prince's favour, and only in the last years, from 1195, did he fall into disgrace. The article says that several stories exist and that the caliph, then at war with the Christians in Spain, seems to have thought it advisable to gain the support of the jurists, who had long imposed a rigorous orthodoxy on the people. Ibn Rushd was [[event/ibn-rushd-exiled-to-lucena-591|banished]] to [[place/lucena|Lucena]] near Cordoba after appearing before a tribunal of the chief men of Cordoba, his doctrine was pronounced anathema, and edicts ordered that philosophical works be burned and that these studies, thought dangerous to religion, be forbidden. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942} The article adds that rivals and doctrinal opponents seized the occasion to mock him in vulgar epigrams, and that once the caliph was back in Marrakesh, in a Berber milieu less sensitive in matters of doctrine, he repealed the edicts and summoned the philosopher to his court again. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942} Ibn Rushd died in Marrakesh on 9 Safar 595 AH/11 December 1198 CE, was buried outside the gate of Taghzut, and was later carried to Cordoba, where the young mystic [[person/ibn-arabi-muhyi-al-din|Ibn al-Arabi]] attended his funeral. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942}

## The ordeal and death in the other sources
Al-Bakri says he was tried at the end of his life: Sultan Ya'qub [[person/yaqub-al-mansur-al-muwahhidi|al-Mansur]] tried him over what was alleged against him of an inclination to "the sciences of the ancients"; people stopped studying with him, and he was confined to his house, no one entering, until he died confined in his house in [[place/marrakesh|Marrakesh]]. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 13} Hallaq quotes Makhluf that he was tried by exile and the burning of his books at the end of Ya'qub al-Mansur's days, when he was denounced and religious and political matters were ascribed to him. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 15} {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 16} Al-Zirikli, for his part, says al-Mansur exiled him to Marrakesh and had some of his books burned, then was reconciled with him and allowed him to return, and that he died in Marrakesh and his body was carried to Cordoba. {cite:al-aalam p. 849} He died in 595 AH. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 13} These accounts differ on the place of exile and the manner of the ordeal, and the museum records them side by side without choosing between them.

## Works
Al-Bakri says his works exceed sixty, in philosophy and its commentaries, kalam, astronomy, legal theory, grammar and medicine, and that most were lost when his books were burned in the ordeal. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 11} In law and legal theory they include [[work/bidayat-al-mujtahid|Bidayat al-Mujtahid wa-Nihayat al-Muqtasid]], a commentary on his grandfather's Muqaddimat, an abridgement of al-Ghazali's [[work/al-mustasfa|al-Mustasfa]], Minhaj al-Adilla on legal theory and al-Tahsil on the disagreements of scholars; in philosophy [[work/fasl-al-maqal|Fasl al-Maqal]], Manahij al-Adilla fi al-Kashf an Aqa'id al-Milla, [[work/tahafut-al-tahafut|Tahafut al-Tahafut]] answering [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]], and epitomes and commentaries on Aristotle; in medicine al-Kulliyyat and a commentary on Ibn Sina's medical poem; in Arabic grammar al-Daruri. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 11} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 12} {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 16} Al-Zirikli notes that he devoted himself to Aristotle, translated him and added much, and that his books were translated into Latin and Hebrew. {cite:al-aalam p. 849}

## The chronology of the works
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, relying on the chronology established by M. Alonso, says that when Ibn Rushd was presented to the caliph he had already written paraphrases or short commentaries on the Organon, the Physics and the Metaphysics, and the first version of his great medical work, the Kulliyyat, for which he asked his friend Abu Marwan Ibn Zubr to write a companion on particular matters, so that together they would form a complete treatise on medicine. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942} He continued with short or middle commentaries between 1169 and 1178; the years 1174 to 1180 were the period of his original works, among them treatises on the intellect, On the Substance of the Sphere, the Fasl al-Maqal, the Kashf and the Tahafut; the great commentaries came later. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942} Only a small number of works survive in Arabic, and the majority are preserved only in Latin or Hebrew translations, some of the Arabic texts being written in Hebrew letters. The surviving Arabic works named include middle commentaries on the Physics, On the Heavens, On Generation and Corruption, the Meteorology and On the Soul, the great commentary on the Metaphysics, the Fasl al-Maqal with its appendix, and the Kashf. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 943}

## How scholars have read the works
The article reports a long dispute over where Ibn Rushd's own ideas are to be found. For the Latin schoolmen he was above all the Commentator, as in Dante's line. Renan, it says, drew attention to differences between the commentaries and the philosopher's personal views, suspected that the distinction might be a precaution allowing him to speak more freely, and judged the doctrine of the Tahafut to contradict Ibn Rushd on several points; the article records that this judgement rests on the Latin version, which has interpolations, and that Renan took the theological writings for artifices meant to deceive the Maliki jurists, an assessment the article calls entirely without foundation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 942} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 943} Munk tried to extract his personal ideas from the commentaries; Asin Palacios looked for them in the Tahafut, the Fasl and the Kashf; Gauthier took a middle line and attributed to him a doctrine close to al-Farabi's on the philosopher and the prophet, in which one truth has two expressions, abstract and symbolic, so that philosophy and religion never clash. Cruz Hernandez, the article says, concludes that it is absurd to choose in advance between the philosopher and the theologian, and that the sincerity of the whole work and the unity of its thought must be admitted. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 943}

## Ways into philosophy and his masters
The article holds it certain that he approached philosophy through the theoretical sciences. As a jurist he was interested in legal theory, and it quotes Ibn al-Abbar on Bidayat al-Mujtahid, that in it he gives the reasons for disagreement and their motives. What drew him in law was a strictness of method and logic that falls short of the philosophical syllogism. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 943} His first schooling in philosophy came from a physician, and at the end of the Kulliyyat he stresses the method of gathering particular facts and general questions. While writing it he was studying the Organon and the Physics, which led him to the metaphysical problem, and he saw in Aristotle chiefly the logician of strict demonstration; his commentary on the Posterior Analytics, of 1170, deepened his grasp of the theory of knowledge. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 943} This, the article says, let him tell the true Aristotle from the picture given by Greek commentators such as Alexander of Aphrodisias and by philosophers of Islam such as [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]], whose philosophy he criticised vigorously while respecting his medical work. He was interested in the logic of [[person/al-farabi|al-Farabi]] and drew on his moral and political doctrines for his commentary on Plato's Republic, but stood chiefly in the tradition of [[person/ibn-bajja|Ibn Bajja]], on whose treatise on union with the intellect and book on the regime of the solitary he commented. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 943} He also wrote a commentary on [[work/hayy-ibn-yaqzan|Hayy ibn Yaqzan]]; the article sees real similarities with Ibn Tufayl, but says that the mystic vision of knowledge to which Ibn Tufayl's story leads is not found in Ibn Rushd. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 943}

## The Fasl al-Maqal
The article dates the three theological and philosophical treatises in this order: the Fasl al-Maqal and its appendix, the Kashf, of 575 AH/1179 CE, which mentions the Fasl, and the Tahafut al-Tahafut, which Bouyges holds was not written before 1180. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 943} In the Fasl, on its account, Ibn Rushd defines philosophy in line with the Qur'anic recommendations, reads the relevant verses as commending rational inference, alone or together with legal analogy, and so holds that the Law itself establishes the legitimacy of rational speculation, whose perfect method is demonstration. Entering a quarrel among theologians about faith and knowledge, he argues that the believer is obliged to speculate rationally about beings, within the capacity of each person, and that this obliges him to examine the works of the ancients, so that forbidding the examination is contrary to the Law, provided the person has a keen sense of the truth and religious and moral qualification. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 943} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 944}

Because people give assent through demonstrative, dialectical or rhetorical discourse, the article says, God addresses them through all three. Where rational research reaches a truth not mentioned in the revealed text, he likens it to the jurists' inference of rulings the text does not state; where the manifest sense of the text disagrees with a demonstrated conclusion, it is to be interpreted figuratively, as the jurists do, though the philosopher's interpretation, which agrees with a certain syllogism, has the stronger title. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 944} He reads the verse on clear and ambiguous verses as justifying interpretation for those firmly grounded in knowledge, and he does not rely on [[concept/ijma|consensus]] to decide what to interpret, criticising it with arguments the article finds reminiscent of [[person/ibn-hazm|Ibn Hazm]]. On [[concept/takfir|accusations of unbelief]] he holds that the condemnations launched against the philosophers are not a decisive ruling, attacks the one al-Ghazali launched against them, and argues in turn that the theologians often misuse [[concept/tawil-quran|interpretation]] themselves. He adds that a philosopher who errs on hard questions may be excused and rewarded, like a judge who errs in his effort. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 944}

The article further sets out his view that some texts are to be taken literally by everyone, some must be interpreted by scholars but not by others, and some are doubtful, such as those on the next life, where all accept the affirmation and scholars may differ over the description. Scholars should write only demonstrative books, accessible to those able to follow them, and the leaders of the community should keep such books from the ordinary man; al-Ghazali, with good intentions, did not follow this and so erred. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 944} The article insists that, for Ibn Rushd, there is one truth, not two expressions of it in the languages of reason and imagination, and that this differs from the later "double truth" of Western Averroists. It says the majority should be taught only the general methods the Qur'an gives, that to reveal interpretations to the common man gives rise to sects, which it calls the error of the [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazila]] and the [[concept/ashariyya|Ash'aris]], and that the agreement of law and reason is a practical agreement of two methods toward one conclusion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 945}

## The Kashf
The Kashf an Manahij al-Adilla, the article says, foreshadows the Tahafut still more clearly, and aims to show that the theories of the sects satisfy neither scholars nor the common man. In five chapters it treats the existence of God, His unity, His attributes, His corporeality and His actions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 945} It reports his critique of the Ash'ari argument from the world's coming into existence, which in his view leads to an eternal action and effects, and of their atomism, and his view that the question of the world's creation or eternity echoes debates from Plato and Aristotle to John Philoponus. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 945} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 946} He criticises [[person/imam-al-haramayn-al-juwayni|al-Juwayni]] for overlooking the necessity of what exists. Against the Sufis he admits that mystic training may help toward rational knowledge but cannot replace it, and he says he found no Mu'tazila books in Spain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 946} He draws on the Qur'anic proofs from providence and creation, and notes the Qur'anic idea of the heavens as commanded. On the attributes he separates the Qur'anic doctrine from the theologians' questions on which the Qur'an is silent, and he shows a certain agnosticism about whether the attributes are added to the essence. On divine corporeality he faults the Mu'tazila for denying it altogether and the Ash'aris for seeking a compromise, because the ordinary man has no idea of an incorporeal being, and he proposes teaching that God is light. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 946} On the fifth chapter, he calls predestination one of the most difficult questions, keeps the texts for and against, and affirms both dependence on conditions created by God and human authorship of acts. He holds that causes operate not only as God's instruments but because God created them as causes, and he agrees with Ash'arism that God is both just and the creator of good and evil. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 946} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 947} The article says he condemns theology, finds the literal sense of scripture on the whole wiser, and holds that philosophers, where they do not follow demonstration, must adhere to the literal sense like the ordinary man. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 947}

## The Tahafut al-Tahafut
According to the article, the Tahafut gathers the results of his paraphrases and middle commentaries together with his basic personal ideas on religious questions. It answers al-Ghazali's [[work/tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahafut al-Falasifa]], yet al-Ghazali is not its only target: many of the criticisms of Ibn Sina in that book are accepted for their conclusions. The article therefore calls it a reconstruction of the true philosophy, that of Aristotle himself, against both the Neoplatonic philosophers of Islam and the theological systems, and says that his original philosophical doctrine is to be found there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 947} The two thinkers, it says, are separated on whether philosophical reasoning has the strictness of mathematics. On the creation of the world he proposes an eternal creation: no empty time can have preceded the world, since time is the measure of movement, and each revolution of the sphere depends on the first agent rather than on a chain of all past revolutions. He holds that God's creative will is not to be conceived on the model of ours, and rejects the emanationist principle that the One can give rise only to one. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 947}

In ontology he criticises with al-Ghazali Ibn Sina's idea of the Being necessary in itself, holds that essence and existence are distinguished only in thought, and accepts a true possibility understood as potentiality, with the cause as the agent that leads it into actuality. The world as a whole is an organised whole, necessary through the interplay of causes and a command of God, and God is a real agent, rightly called Creator. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 948} The article quotes Gilson contrasting this God, who forms part of the universe, with Ibn Sina's transcendent God, yet maintains that Ibn Rushd's God remains transcendent and that his thinking conforms to Muslim orthodoxy. It says his divine attributes are essential and express the richness of the essence, and that God's knowledge resembles neither our knowledge of universals nor of particulars, though it is closer to the latter. On the last things, he holds that demonstration establishes only the intellect's spirituality and immortality, and that this has led some to say he denied personal immortality, a doctrine the article assigns to his commentaries on Aristotle; in the Tahafut, it says, nothing proves that faculties using organs weaken with them, and resurrection of the body, though not demonstrable, is not conceived as a return of life to the earthly body. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 948}

## The commentary on the Metaphysics
The article calls the great commentary on the Metaphysics a major work, in which he explains the Aristotelian doctrine while expressing his own point of view, choosing among interpretations the one that suits his ideas, consulting two or three Arabic translations and discussing Alexander, Themistius, Nicholas of Damascus and John Philoponus. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 948} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 949} In it metaphysics studies being as being, and meanings of words that are analogical; it follows physics, which supplies concrete experience of beings, and does not become abstract generality. It reaches its culmination if there exist natures separate from matter, so that theology, whose object is unmoved and eternal, stands above the science of the heavenly bodies. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 949} He gives importance to the accidental, holding that the world as a whole is necessary but contains events that happen only more or less often, and he rejects a wholly determinist view. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 949} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 950} On substance, he holds against Ibn Sina that eternal perceptible substance falls within physics and that the heavenly bodies are neither divisible nor corruptible; he explains generation through matter, form and privation, and criticises Alexander and Themistius on the origin of forms, rejecting a giver of forms in favour of an agent that only moves matter from potentiality to actuality. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 950} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 951} Against theologians who admit one efficient cause only, he defends real secondary causes, co-ordinated by the movement of the sphere, which is moved by desire for the first unmoved mover. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 951}

## The intellect and the Republic
On the intellect he opposes Alexander, who held the material intellect to be generated and corruptible, and takes up a thesis he attributes to Theophrastus, Themistius and most Peripatetics, that the material intellect exists and the separate agent intellect is as its form. The article calls the question difficult and says he seems to have taken the habitual intellect as the way the agent intellect is present in us, with an action that begins and ends, while in perfected intellection we understand through the agent intellect itself, which has led to the statement that individuality disappears. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 951} The article ends its survey of the thought by noting that a Latin Averroes could differ on details, and mentions the commentary on Plato's Republic preserved in Hebrew, in which, not knowing Aristotle's Politics, he took Plato's work as complementary to the Ethics, and in which he drew on al-Farabi, transposed Greek institutions into Muslim terms and alluded to Muslim public law and to the Almohad empire compared with the Almoravids. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 951}

## Reception and judgement
The article says that Ibn Rushd had few disciples in Islam, but that his fame among the Western schoolmen is well known. Renan and many after him claimed that his thought held nothing original, because, it argues, they belittled his religious and legal works and saw the philosophers of Islam as mere heirs of Greece. Looking at the whole body of works and the unity of his thought, the author concludes that the "Commentator" was a true philosopher. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 951} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 952}

## What scholars said of him
His contemporary al-Dabbi called him a well-known jurist and memoriser who took part in many sciences; Ibn Abi Usaybi'a, unrivalled in law and juristic disagreement; Ibn Farhun, that al-Andalus produced no one like him in accomplishment, learning and merit; al-Maqqari, that he brought Malik's school closer than anyone before him. {cite:bidaya-hallaq-v1 p. 14} {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 13} Al-Bakri describes him as, for all his eminence, the most humble of men, and as more given to understanding, diraya, than to transmission, riwaya. {cite:bidaya-bakri-bayt-al-afkar p. 10}
