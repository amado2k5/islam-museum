## Overview
Urwa ibn al-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam, son of [[person/zubayr-ibn-al-awwam|al-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam]] {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}. The Encyclopaedia of Islam describes him as an eminent traditionist, one of the Seven Jurists of [[place/medina|Medina]], and the founder of historical study in Islam. It says he was born in Medina, very probably in 23/643-4, and died there, probably in 93/711-2 or 94/712-13. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 966}

## Family and early years
His mother was [[person/asma-bint-abi-bakr|Asma]], daughter of the first caliph [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] and sister of the Prophet's wife [[person/aisha|Aisha]]. His full brother, the counter-caliph [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr]], was twenty years older than he was. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 966} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 967}

The article stresses that no contemporary reports about Urwa survive, only traditions kept by later generations, so a biographer must weigh individual reports and also judge the tradition as a whole. It proceeds on the view that the sources, despite contradictions and some distorted transmissions, give a broadly correct picture of his life and scholarship. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 967}

Urwa grew up in Medina amid the disputes of the caliphate of [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]]. After Uthman was killed and [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] was chosen as caliph in 35/656, his father fled to Mecca, where the article presumes he took his two sons. Father and son later went to [[place/basra|Basra]], where al-Zubayr, helped by [[person/talha-ibn-ubaydullah|Talha ibn Ubayd Allah]] and Aisha, organised the revolt against Ali that became known as the [[event/battle-of-the-camel|Battle of the Camel]] in 36/656. Urwa was too young to fight in it. After his father's death he seems to have returned to Medina with Aisha. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 967}

## Scholarly circle and the Egypt report
His inherited wealth gave him time to collect and study historical reports. The article does not know when he began. It notes a report that, towards 60/680, he met friends every night in the mosque of Medina in a study circle. Another report places his last visit to Aisha, his most important informant, in 55/675, which suggests he was no longer living in Medina then and was perhaps already in Mecca. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 967}

The article calls al-Baladhuri's report of a seven-year stay in Egypt highly doubtful, because the other transmitted data do not fit it, and it concludes that he cannot have spent seven continuous years there at any time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 967}

## His testimony about Egypt
al-Baladhuri, citing al-Waqidi through a chain, reports that Urwa said he had stayed in Egypt seven years and married there. He saw its people exhausted, with burdens laid on them beyond their strength. He said that Amr had conquered it only by agreement, by a covenant and by a fixed tribute imposed on them {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407}.

## The Zubayrid counter-caliphate
Urwa sided with his brother Abd Allah. On the day of the battle of [[event/waqat-al-harra|al-Harra]] in 63/683 he is said to have burned his books of law, an act he later deeply regretted. In 69/688 Abd Allah sent him on an unsuccessful mission to the Alid [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-hanafiyya|Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 967}

According to al-Masudi, Urwa also acted as an envoy during the [[event/siege-of-mecca-692|siege of Mecca]] in 73/692 by the general [[person/al-hajjaj-ibn-yusuf|al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf]], but Abd Allah, urged on by his mother, refused to surrender and accept the offer of safety. After Abd Allah's death Urwa went first to Medina to leave his belongings, then to [[place/damascus|Damascus]], bringing [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik]] news of the victory before al-Hajjaj's messengers arrived. The caliph treated him with respect, pardoned him and guaranteed the safety of his possessions. He is said to have gone back to Mecca for his brother's funeral. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 967}

## Later life in Medina
As head of the Zubayrid family Urwa settled again in Medina. The article calls this the start of a long period of scholarly work, and of a correspondence with Abd al-Malik, who asked him about important events of the early days of Islam. His son and successor [[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|al-Walid]] continued it. In the year of al-Walid's pledge of allegiance Urwa went to Damascus with his sons Muhammad and Hisham. There one of his feet, afflicted by gangrene or cancer, was amputated, and Muhammad died in an accident during the journey. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 967}

al-Salabi reports that his leg was amputated from the middle of the shin on his journey to al-Walid, after an ulcer had appeared on it. He reports that his son Muhammad was killed by the beasts on that journey, and that he died in the year 93 AH {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 p.135-136}.

In 87/706 [[person/umar-ibn-abd-al-aziz|Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz]] arrived in Medina as governor. He is said to have named ten jurists, Urwa and five more of the Seven Jurists among them, to a council advising him, presumably above all on legal questions. Urwa died on his estate near the village of al-Fur in the settlement of [[place/al-rabadha|al-Rabadha]], about 200 km east of Medina, and was buried there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 967}

## Traditionist and historian
The article says the whole body of Hadith criticism treats Urwa as a trustworthy transmitter, praises his piety and calls him an inexhaustible sea of knowledge. European scholars early called him the father of the historical school of Medina. It adds that the traditions under his name cover all the major events of early Islam, and that the transmissions most central to the Prophet's life, the Sira and Maghazi, owe much to his collecting. It stresses, however, that he was not only a historian. Another group of traditions in his name is legal, dealing with property, marriage and divorce, the position of women and slaves, and ritual rules such as ablution, prayer and pilgrimage. Many of these explain Qur'anic passages or apply the sunna of Medina. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 967}

As a late Successor he belongs to the first generation of scholars who gathered traditions systematically. His chains of transmission do not always meet the later standards, and at times he names no authority. Two thirds of the traditions in his corpus go back to Aisha as his direct informant, though the article says the chain may in some cases have been extended back to her by later transmitters. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 967}

## Al-Umari's assessment
[[person/akram-diya-al-umari|Akram Diya' al-Umari]] counts him among the Successors who pioneered sira-writing, describing him (d. 94 AH) as "a trustworthy hadith scholar of the Successors, counted among the famous seven jurists of Medina". {cite:sahih-sira-umari-ulum-hikam-1994 p.53} {cite:sahih-sira-umari-ulum-hikam-1994 p.54}
