## Summary
Ghazi Mohammad Ayub Khan Barakzai (1857 – 7 April 1914) was a Barakzai prince who was briefly Emir of Afghanistan, from 12 October 1879 to 31 May 1880. He is remembered in Afghanistan as a national hero, the Victor of Maiwand, for his defeat of a British-Indian army at the Battle of Maiwand during the Second Anglo-Afghan War. {cite:wikipedia-mohammad-ayub-khan-en}

He is a different person from Ayub Khan, the later president of Pakistan, and he was the son of the Emir Sher Ali Khan — and therefore the grandson, not the son, of Dost Mohammad Khan, as both articles' accounts of his parentage show. {cite:wikipedia-mohammad-ayub-khan-en} {cite:wikipedia-mohammad-ayub-khan-ar}

## Early life
He was born at Kabul in 1857, a son of Sher Ali Khan and of Qamar Jan, daughter of the Mohmand chief Sa'adat Khan of Lalpura; his full brother was Mohammad Yaqub Khan, who preceded him on the throne. He served as governor of Herat before his brief emirate. {cite:wikipedia-mohammad-ayub-khan-en}

## Maiwand and the loss of the throne
When his brother Yaqub Khan's concessions to Britain brought on his deposition, Ayub Khan took the throne in October 1879 and led the Afghan forces in the war. On 27 July 1880 his army overwhelmed a British-Indian brigade at Maiwand, one of the worst defeats of a British force in the century; the Afghan folk heroine Malalai is said to have rallied the fighters there, a connection of shared battle presence which this museum records in prose only. On 1 September 1880 General Roberts defeated him at Kandahar, and his emirate was over. {cite:wikipedia-mohammad-ayub-khan-en}

He briefly held Herat and tried again for Kandahar in 1881, was defeated by the new emir Abdur Rahman Khan, and fled to Persia. {cite:wikipedia-mohammad-ayub-khan-en} {cite:wikipedia-mohammad-ayub-khan-ar}

## Last years
After negotiations in 1888 he became a pensioner of the British Raj and settled at Lahore, where he lived until his death on 7 April 1914. He was buried at Peshawar, in the Durrani graveyard near the shrine of Sheikh Habib; the Arabic article gives Peshawar as his place of death, and the difference is recorded in the data file. He had eleven wives, fifteen sons and ten daughters. {cite:wikipedia-mohammad-ayub-khan-en} {cite:wikipedia-mohammad-ayub-khan-ar}

## In Encyclopaedia of Islam, 2nd edition (Leiden: Brill, 1954–2008), 13 volumes
Ayyub Khan was the fourth son of Shir 'Ali, Amir of Afghanistan, and brother of Ya'qub Khan. When in 1873 Shir 'Ali named his favourite son 'Abd Allah Jan as heir-apparent, Ayyub fled to Persia. In 1879, when Ya'qub Khan succeeded Shir 'Ali as amir, Ayyub returned to Afghanistan and was appointed governor of Herat. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 845}

## In Encyclopaedia of Islam, 2nd edition (Leiden: Brill, 1954–2008), 13 volumes: The Second Afghan War
Towards the end of the Second Afghan War (1878-80), Lord Lytton's government chose a Sadozai prince, also named Shir 'Ali, as wali of Kandahar. Ayyub Khan ousted him from that post, and on 27 July 1880 decisively defeated a British army under General Burrows at Maiwand. Sir Frederick (afterwards Lord) Roberts then marched rapidly from Kabul to Kandahar, routed Ayyub's troops and forced him to retire on Herat. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 845}

## In Encyclopaedia of Islam, 2nd edition (Leiden: Brill, 1954–2008), 13 volumes: Against 'Abd al-Rahman
When 'Abd al-Rahman Khan became amir at Kabul, his first task was to extend his control over the country. In July 1881 Ayyub Khan, who held Herat, declared a jihad against him, as a British nominee, and occupied Kandahar. Towards the end of 1881 'Abd al-Rahman crushed him, expelled him from Herat and forced him to take refuge at Mashhad in Persia. In 1887, during the Ghalzai rebellion, he tried once more to regain his position in Afghanistan, but was defeated and compelled to flee to India, where he remained for a time.
