# Ibrahim al-Yaziji

## Summary
Ibrahim al-Yaziji (2 March 1847 – 1906) was a Lebanese philologist, poet and journalist, the son of Nasif al-Yaziji, and one of the leading figures of the Nahda, the Arab cultural revival. He edited newspapers in Beirut, translated the Bible into Arabic on a Jesuit commission, moved to Cairo in 1894 and founded the magazine Ad-Diya there in 1898, simplified the forms of the Arabic letters in a way that fed into the Arabic typewriter, and argued that the Arabic language, not religion, should be the bond of Arab identity. {cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-al-yaziji-en} {cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-al-yaziji-ar}

## Life and work
He was born in Beirut into a house of learning: his father Nasif was the celebrated poet and grammarian, and Ibrahim's education was thorough enough that, at twenty-three, he engaged the formidable Ahmad Faris al-Shidyaq in print over verses of his father's diwan — a debate the newspapers reported and that pushed him deeper into language and literature. He edited the newspaper al-Najah in 1872 and later worked on al-Tabib. The Jesuit fathers invited him to render the Bible into Arabic; he studied Syriac and Hebrew for the task and produced between 1876 and 1880 a translation praised for the richness of its language, the second Arabic Bible after the Protestant version of 1866 on which his own father had worked. In 1894 he emigrated to Egypt, and in Cairo in 1898 he founded Ad-Diya, which he published until his death there in 1906. His works include Naj'at al-ra'id, a dictionary of synonyms, the poetry collection al-Iqd, and Lughat al-jara'id on the language of the press. {cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-al-yaziji-en} {cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-al-yaziji-ar}

## Language above religion
Among his best-known commitments, the English article reports, was "championing Arabic as the bond of identity, over religion, among those for whom the language is a common tongue" — a sociopolitical project of the Nahda that he advocated "explicitly and famously" in his lifetime, in Yasir Suleiman's words. His other lasting innovation was typographic: he reduced the forms of the Arabic letters from some 300 to 60, making them more like Latin characters and contributing to the creation of the Arabic typewriter. {cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-al-yaziji-en}

## Contemporaries on him
Al-Zirikli called him a scholar of literature and language, and Kahhala a man of letters and a linguist. Muhammad Rashid Rida, quoted in the Arabic article, placed him "in the first rank of the Christian literati of the Levant", praising the regard his people had for his learning while complaining of his conceit and pride. The Arabic article also repeats, without a source, the claim that he belonged to the Freemasons. {cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-al-yaziji-ar}
