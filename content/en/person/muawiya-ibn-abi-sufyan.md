## Overview
[[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan]], Sakhr ibn Harb ibn Umayya al-Qurashi al-Umawi, "Commander of the Faithful", as Ibn Hajar calls him. {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1431} He was born five years before the mission, it is also said seven years before, and it is also said thirteen; "the first is more famous". {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1431} He was the son of [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-harb|Abu Sufyan]] and [[person/hind-bint-utba|Hind bint Utba]], and his sister was [[person/umm-habiba-bint-abi-sufyan|Umm Habiba]], mother of the believers. {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1432}

## His appearance and companionship
Abu Nu'aym said that he was among the eloquent scribes, forbearing and dignified. {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1431} Khalid ibn Ma'dan said that he was tall, fair and bald-headed. {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1431} "He accompanied the Prophet and wrote for him." {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1431} Al-Mada'ini said that Zayd ibn Thabit wrote the revelation, and that Mu'awiya wrote for the Prophet in matters between him and the Arabs. {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1432} In the Musnad of Ahmad, and in its origin in Muslim, Ibn Abbas relates that the Prophet said: "Call Mu'awiya to me", and he was his scribe. {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1432}

## From command to caliphate
Ibn Hajar summarises: [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] appointed him over Syria after his brother Yazid ibn Abi Sufyan, and Uthman confirmed him. "Then he persisted and did not give the oath to Ali; then he fought him and became independent in Syria; then he added Egypt to it; then he called himself caliph after the two arbitrators." Then he became sole ruler when [[person/hasan-ibn-ali|al-Hasan]] made peace with him and the people united around him, "so that year was called the year of unity." {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1431} {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1432} For the details see [[event/battle-of-siffin|Siffin]] and [[event/arbitration-of-siffin|the arbitration]]. Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan said: "Ibn Hind lived twenty years as an amir and twenty years as a caliph"; Ibn Hajar comments that this is a loose statement, since he did not complete twenty years as caliph. {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1432}

## Family and early years in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, presents Mu'awiya as the founder of the Umayyad dynasty of caliphs based in Syria. It adds that he was not, as is often asserted, the first Umayyad caliph, since that was [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]], his second cousin. On the article's reckoning he ruled as generally acknowledged caliph from 41/661 to 60/680. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 291} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 292} His father was [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-harb|Abu Sufyan]], Sakhr ibn Harb, and his mother was [[person/hind-bint-utba|Hind bint Utba]]; because of her he is sometimes called Ibn Hind, and the article ties the other nickname, "son of the liver-eater", to an act of vengeance attributed to her at Uhud. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 292}

The sources disagree on his birth and age. He is said to have been born five, seven or thirteen years before the call to prophethood, conventionally set in 610, and to have been 73, 75, 78, 80 or 85 years old at his death in Rajab 60/April-May 680. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 292}

His father was a leading figure of pre-Islamic Mecca and chief of the clan of Abd Shams there. The article says he led the Meccans at [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]] in 3/625 and organised the confederacy that unsuccessfully besieged Medina in 5/627, but afterwards ceased to be a leading opponent of Muhammad. In 7/629 Mu'awiya's widowed sister [[person/umm-habiba-bint-abi-sufyan|Umm Habiba]] married the Prophet, and in 8/630 Abu Sufyan went to Medina to negotiate and then submitted and became a Muslim when Muhammad entered Mecca. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 292}

On Mu'awiya's own conversion the article cites Ibn Hajar, who ascribes the report to al-Waqidi and prefers it to a variant: Mu'awiya made his Islam manifest at the time of the conquest of Mecca, but had in fact been a secret Muslim since the treaty of [[event/treaty-of-hudaybiyya|al-Hudaybiyya]] in late 6/628. The article adds that he and his father were nonetheless counted among the freed prisoners who had fallen into the Prophet's hands, either at al-Hudaybiyya or through the conquest of Mecca, and among those whose hearts were to be reconciled, as was his brother [[person/yazid-ibn-abi-sufyan|Yazid ibn Abi Sufyan]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 292} The date of his conversion is therefore reported in different ways. The differing reports are set out in the sections above and in the record's perspectives, with the Sunni sources first.

The article says he went on to serve as one of the Prophet's scribes, and that he was, so it is said, one of seventeen literate men of Quraysh at the time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 292}

## Governor of Syria
In 13/634 the caliph Abu Bakr sent Mu'awiya to Syria, where he commanded the vanguard of the army led by his brother Yazid in operations against the Byzantines. The article reports, on the authority of Sayf ibn Umar, that he was present at the occupation of Jerusalem in 16/637, a report it says is wrongly dated. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 292} When Yazid died in the plague of [[event/plague-of-amwas|Amwas]] in 18/639, [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] put Mu'awiya in his brother's place, and the next year Mu'awiya commanded the conquest of [[event/conquest-of-caesarea|Caesarea]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 292}

The article stresses that the extent of his responsibilities is not entirely clear. One report gives him the district of Damascus and its taxation, another Damascus and the Jordan district, a third a wider Syria excluding Palestine. Under Uthman, according to the article's reading of the sources, Homs, Qinnasrin and the Jazira were distinct areas that came under Mu'awiya only when their governor, Umayr ibn Sa'd, stood down or was dismissed, and Uthman then also made Palestine over to him. Sayf places the joining of Syria and the Jazira under his sole control two years after Uthman's accession, and al-Waqidi knew that it had happened by 31/651-2. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 292}

While discontent with Uthman's representatives grew in Iraq, Egypt and Medina in the early 650s, the article says Mu'awiya's Syria stayed free of it, with the single exception of [[person/abu-dharr-al-ghifari|Abu Dharr]], who was quickly silenced. Mu'awiya held the Byzantine front energetically, set up strong coastal garrisons and began Arab maritime warfare in the Mediterranean, an activity Umar had earlier forbidden. The article gives several reasons for Syria's stability: its fighting men came largely from cohesive Arab clan groups, the existing military districts were kept, and no single garrison city was founded, so that tribal leaders kept their established positions, unlike the mixed population of [[place/kufa|Kufa]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 292} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 293}

## The break with Ali and Siffin
The article notes that no Syrians were among the provincials who besieged Uthman in Medina in 35/656. Mu'awiya was one of the governors Uthman asked for help, and he sent a relief force that turned back at Wadi al-Qura on learning that Uthman had been killed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 293} After that he bided his time in Syria while [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] sought to establish himself, and while Ali dealt with the opposition at the [[event/battle-of-the-camel|Battle of the Camel]]. The article says Ali made no secret of his intention to dismiss Mu'awiya, and that Mu'awiya avoided paying him any allegiance. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 293}

By the time Ali sent a representative after the Camel, the article reports, Syrian resistance was mounting, joined to a wish to hold Ali responsible for the murder of the dead caliph. At this point [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn al-As]] agreed to support Mu'awiya in return for a promise of Egypt, and advised him to rally the notables of Syria by pinning responsibility for Uthman's death on Ali. They were called on to fight for consultation in choosing the caliph, as in Uthman's case, and for vengeance for Uthman, and they swore allegiance to Mu'awiya in those terms in his capacity as amir, not as caliph. Ali's representative returned without Mu'awiya's oath, bringing word that the Syrians had resolved to fight Ali on the grounds that he had killed Uthman and sheltered his other killers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 293} For the confrontation see [[event/battle-of-siffin|Siffin]].

On the battle itself the article contrasts two accounts. The widely reported view is that the Syrians were losing and Mu'awiya ordered the raising of copies of the Qur'an and a call for a halt to the fighting. Against it the article sets a contention by non-Muslim sources that the Syrians won, which it says is supported by Umayyad court poetry. Of the [[event/arbitration-of-siffin|arbitration]] it quotes Khalifa ibn Khayyat: the arbiters agreed on nothing. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 293} The article adds that the Syrians then acknowledged Mu'awiya as caliph, but four more years passed before he enjoyed general recognition; the date of that first acknowledgement is cut off in the scanned text and is not given here. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 293}

## Egypt, the death of Ali and the Year of the Community
Egypt was taken in Safar 38/July 658, and Amr formally became its governor two months later. The article calls this a blow to Ali's prestige, since Ali was preoccupied with the Kharijites in Iraq; it also says Mu'awiya's attempt to subvert Basra that year failed, while Ali's position in Iraq grew weaker. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 293} Mu'awiya waited, limiting himself to small roving expeditions on the fringes of Iraq and into the Hijaz and the Yemen, and may have made a truce with Ali in 40/660-1. In July 660 a formal ceremony of allegiance to him as caliph took place at Jerusalem. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 293}

In Ramadan 40/January 661 Ali was struck with a poisoned sword in Kufa by the Kharijite [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-muljam|Ibn Muljam]] and died some days later. The succession of [[person/hasan-ibn-ali|al-Hasan ibn Ali]] was short-lived: Mu'awiya moved on Iraq with an army, al-Hasan settled for compensation in return for abdication, and Mu'awiya entered Kufa in Rabi I or Jumada I 41, July or September 661. The article says this year is the so-called year of the community, conventionally regarded as marking the beginning of his caliphate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 293}

## Wars with Byzantium
The article describes a long Byzantine contest. In the Mediterranean he had already challenged Byzantine sea power, dealt with Cyprus and raided Rhodes and Sicily; in 655 he commanded an Arab fleet of 200 vessels that defeated a Byzantine fleet of 700 to 1,000, in what the article calls the Battle of the Masts. On the northern land frontier he had advanced into Anatolia as far as Amorium by 646, but the frontier lay around Adana when the war with Ali forced him to come to terms with the Byzantines on the basis of tribute. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 293}

After 661 he settled and fortified Syrian coastal towns, and Alexandria was fully fortified, the work being finished by 670, so that Egypt again became the springboard for expansion into North Africa. After the death of Constans II in 668 an enlarged Arab navy raided as far as Sicily in 669, while the army in North Africa made [[place/qayrawan|al-Qayrawan]] its base and raided into what is now Algeria. Rhodes was overrun in 672 and Crete in 674, and the naval expeditions that followed amounted to a seven-year blockade of the Marmara and Constantinople, which ended shortly before his death when the Arab fleet was beaten off with Greek fire in 679. Annual land incursions brought booty and kept the army in training. The article suggests that late in his reign he may again have made arrangements involving tribute to the Byzantines; the reason is cut off in the scanned text. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 293} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 294}

## Governing the provinces
The article says Mu'awiya had to define and run a Syria-based caliphate able to draw together the divided parts of the Arab domain and keep authority over an unruly tribal soldiery. In the Jazira, nomadic Qaysi tribesmen had enjoyed a free run, and Kufans and Basrans hostile to Ali moved there during the civil war; according to Sayf, Mu'awiya responded by separating Qinnasrin and the Jazira from Homs as a military district of its own, though elsewhere the same step is attributed to [[person/yazid-ibn-muawiya|Yazid]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 294}

In Iraq the problem, as the article describes it, was too many Arabs competing for resources in the garrison cities. [[person/ziyad-ibn-abi-sufyan|Ziyad ibn Abihi]], whom the article calls Mu'awiya's virtually autonomous viceroy in the East and whom Mu'awiya had adopted as a brother, dealt with it by revising the stipend registers of Kufa and Basra, reorganising the tribal groupings and moving an estimated 50,000 tribesmen and their families to [[place/khurasan|Khurasan]] in 51/671, settling them in villages of the [[place/marw|Marw]] oasis and extending the conquests as far as the Oxus. The reorganisation in Iraq included the confiscation of former Sasanian crown lands and other lands that had been locally regarded as the collective property of the original conquerors. Opposition was stifled by the exemplary execution of [[person/hujr-ibn-adi|Hujr ibn Adi]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 294}

The article, following Patricia Crone, describes a power structure resting on tribal leaders, the ashraf, whose standing largely continued from before Islam and who backed the Sufyanid line. Tribal groupings at Kufa were reshaped, first in tenths, then in sevenths, and under Ziyad in quarters, and at Basra into fifths, sometimes following existing confederacies and sometimes breaking them up. The balance was delicate, with rivalry within and between the divisions and refusals to submit to particular leaders, as by the early Kharijites or Hujr. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 294}

Governors were drawn from the caliph's kinsmen and those close to his circle: Umayyads in Medina, Umayyads and then a Makhzumi in Mecca, Amr and then Maslama ibn Mukhallad in Egypt, and Ziyad in Iraq. The article calls the principle indirect rule with a good deal of provincial autonomy. A governor had full civil and military authority in his large province: he organised the army, led the worship, administered justice, appointed sub-governors, supervised taxation and, in principle at least, sent the surplus to Damascus. Existing bureaucratic machinery, run by local people, was used. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 294} The article says there was no central bureaucracy distinct from the provincial bureaucracy of Syria. Mu'awiya is said to have set up a chancery for letters and for the seal, and the postal service, and to have required the provinces to contribute to the central treasury, but the article judges that the evidence points to a rudimentary central administration. It gives Egyptian figures of an annual revenue of three or five million dinars of which he received only 600,000, and an Iraqi revenue of 100 million dirhams of which he received no more than 6.66 million. He therefore depended on Syria's revenues, the Iraqi crown lands he had taken over, a fifth of booty and other irregular income. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 295}

## Style of rule
Like Uthman, Mu'awiya used the title "deputy of God". The article observes that the caliphate was still a fluid institution, since the rights and duties of the caliph and the mode of succession had been neither spelt out nor agreed upon, and that in practice he does not seem to have pressed the claim to divine sanction very far. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 295} He ruled through the clan and tribe system, through the ashraf, and through personal contact with delegations, and the article, quoting a modern scholar, describes his hilm as patient and tireless skill in handling people through knowledge of their interests and passions. It cites a semi-apocryphal story in which he says that if a single hair joined him to his people he would never let it break. It judges that he held his realm together without ever having to use his Syrian troops, and quotes the contemporary Nestorian monk John of Phenek, who wrote of justice and great peace in his time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 295} For related records see [[concept/hilm|hilm]] and [[concept/bayah|the oath of allegiance]].

## His Syrian base and the succession of Yazid
In Syria itself, the article says, his position rested on troops drawn above all from the confederacy of [[group/qudaa|Quda'a]], whose leading tribe was [[group/banu-kalb|Kalb]]. Kalb had earlier served the Byzantines against the Sasanians and Lakhmids, and Mu'awiya brought them into the Arab power structure by marrying [[person/maysun-bint-bahdal|Maysun bint Bahdal]] of Kalb, who became the mother of his son Yazid. Joined with settled tribesmen in central and southern Syria, mainly Kindis, this formed a strong confederacy led successively by Malik ibn Bahdal and his son Hassan ibn Malik, with stipends for 2,000 men and privileges of consultation and precedence. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 295}

Towards the end of his life Mu'awiya had the oath of allegiance taken to Yazid as his successor. The article says it was not only that Yazid was of noble birth, mature years and acknowledged judgment; most important, in its analysis, was that he continued the link with Kalb and so the Kalb-led confederacy on which Sufyanid power rested. It reports that the main elements of the confederacy showed no protest that the oath broke with the earlier Arab practice of choice by acclaim, and that Mu'awiya tried to win over others who might have had qualms, notably members of Banu Hashim. The initiative nonetheless met resistance, especially from [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Abdallah ibn al-Zubayr]] and [[person/husayn-ibn-ali|al-Husayn ibn Ali]] in the Hijaz. The resistance grew when Yazid succeeded in 60/680, and civil war broke out again after Yazid's early death in 64/683. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 295}

## What was said about him
In the Sahih of al-Bukhari, from Ikrima: I said to Ibn Abbas that Mu'awiya prayed a single rak'a of witr, and he said: "He is a jurist"; in another version: "He accompanied the Messenger of God." {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1432} From Ibn Abbas: "I have never seen anyone sweeter to kingship than Mu'awiya", and when Umar looked at him he would say: "This is the Kisra of the Arabs." {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1432} In the version of Abu Ya'la, the Prophet said to him: "O Mu'awiya, if you are given command, fear God and be just"; its chain includes Suwayd, of whom there is criticism, and al-Bayhaqi relates it through another channel. {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1432}

## Those who transmitted from him
He transmitted from the Prophet, from Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and his sister Umm Habiba. From the Companions, Ibn Abbas, Jarir al-Bajali, Mu'awiya ibn Hudayj, al-Sa'ib ibn Yazid, Abdallah ibn al-Zubayr, al-Nu'man ibn Bashir and others transmitted from him; and from the great Successors, Marwan ibn al-Hakam, Abdallah ibn al-Harith ibn Nawfal, Qays ibn Abi Hazim, Sa'id ibn al-Musayyib and Abu Idris al-Khawlani. {cite:ibn-hajar-isaba p.1432}

## How later writers have judged him
The article reports a range of assessments and does not itself pronounce between them. It says that to posterity his accession marked the end of the rightly-guided caliphate, and it cites the scholar Maxime Rodinson's remark that the Quraysh who had rejected Muhammad gained an empire through his work. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 295} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 296} It adds that the move of the caliphate from Medina to Syria, the introduction of hereditary succession and the growth of the state apparatus have led some scholars, Lammens and Crone and Hinds among them, to say that he turned the caliphate into a monarchic institution of the Persian or Byzantine type. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 296}

Even so, the article says, his image is ambivalent: he was seen not only as the man who perverted the caliphate into kingship but also as a clever and successful ruler, and the sources show considerable admiration for him. It notes that dispassionate analysis was rare: Ibn Rushd saw his caliphate as a shift from the ideal constitution to a timocratic one, and Ibn Khaldun as a stage in the transformation of group solidarity into kingship. More commonly, it says, he was either cursed or venerated, the legitimacy of his caliphate mattering more than its historical character, and modern Muslim writing on him and the Umayyads is also highly partisan. The article does not say which school or sect held which of these judgements, and it does not set out a Shi'i position as such; this section therefore gives the academic account only, and the Sunni statements recorded in the sections above remain the museum's starting frame. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 7 p. 296}

## In al-Tabari
His name appears in the history of the Muslims in Syria: he was governor of Caesarea in the events of the nineteenth year, according to the accounts of Abu Ma'shar and al-Waqidi. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.4 p.102} In the index of the fourth volume, there is mention of Ali sending Jarir ibn Abdallah to him calling him to obedience, and of Siffin and the fighting over the water. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.4 p.584} {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl}

## In Ahmad ibn Hanbal's Fada'il al-Sahaba
[[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]] gives Mu'awiya a chapter with the hadith of al-Irbad ibn Sariya, who heard the Prophet call people to the pre-dawn meal and then say, "O God, teach Mu'awiya the Book and reckoning and protect him from punishment" (no. 1748, graded good by corroboration by the editor [[person/wasi-allah-abbas|Wasi Allah Abbas]]; Ibn Abd al-Barr faulted it for the unknown al-Harith ibn Ziyad); the same through Shurayh ibn Ubayd as *mursal* (1749, weak for *irsal* though its narrators are trustworthy, strengthened by the previous); and from Maslama ibn Mukhallad with the addition "and establish him in the lands" (1750, weak for a break). {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1155} {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1156} {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1157} {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1158}


## Seal bureau: checking,copy and seal,not postal transport
[[invention/muawiya-seal-copy-registry|The seal-and-copy registry]] concerns Muawiya's institutional commissioning of correspondence controls. Duri describes reading/commenting on letters,a scribe's document,checking/sealing and retained copies; separately he attributes an Iranian-influenced control through Baladhuri to Ziyad {cite:tdv-divan-duri early-Umayyad}. This is not invention of writing,seals or every archive. The barid already in the biography concerns transport,not another name for this function. A letter can require both a route and controls. Muawiya's recorded role is commissioner,not writer of every copy or craftsman of every seal.

## Alteration narrative and dating limits
