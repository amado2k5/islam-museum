## Summary
Tri Mumpuni — affectionately known in Indonesia as Bu Puni — is an engineer and social entrepreneur who has spent around three decades electrifying remote Indonesian villages with small community-owned hydropower plants. The Muslim 500 lists her in Science & Technology; Wikipedia records that the projects she helped develop brought hydroelectric power to more than half a million Indonesians. {cite:muslim500-tri-mumpuni} {cite:wikipedia-tri-mumpuni}

## The problem of the archipelago
Indonesia stretches across more than seventeen thousand islands, and its national grid never reached thousands of villages in the hills and forests of the interior — places with running streams but no wires. Where diesel generators were too expensive to fuel, darkness settled at sunset. Mumpuni's answer was micro-hydro: small plants, built and owned by the village itself, turning the power of a nearby river into light, pumps, workshops and refrigeration. {cite:wikipedia-tri-mumpuni} {cite:muslim500-tri-mumpuni}

## IBEKA and the community model
With her husband, the engineer Iskandar Kuntoadji, she led IBEKA — Yayasan Institut Bisnis dan Ekonomi Kerakyatan, the People's Business and Economics Institute — an NGO that designed village power schemes in which the community contributes labour, sets its own tariffs, elects its managers and keeps the revenue. The model turned electricity into a village institution: the plant funds schools, health posts and small enterprise long after the engineers leave. Projects multiplied from Java to Sumatra, Sulawesi, Kalimantan and Papua. {cite:wikipedia-tri-mumpuni}

## Recognition
Her work has drawn international honours: she was elected to the board of a United Nations-supported rural energy body and received major environmental and development awards, including the Ramon Magsaysay Award, often described as Asia's Nobel, for her decades bringing power — and with it schools, clinics and enterprise — to remote communities. {cite:wikipedia-tri-mumpuni} {cite:muslim500-tri-mumpuni}

## Significance
In the museum's record, Mumpuni represents applied engineering as social service: an Indonesian Muslim woman who read the map of her country — rivers without wires — and spent a working life closing the gap, village by village. Her half-million electrified lives are the count of a technology put at the service of the poorest. {cite:muslim500-tri-mumpuni} {cite:wikipedia-tri-mumpuni}

## The long patience of rural work
What distinguishes Mumpuni's model from a government electrification project is ownership: the community contributes labour and money to build its own micro-hydro plant, governs it through a village institution, and keeps the revenue for schools and clinics. Where state programmes often built assets that decayed without local maintenance, IBEKA's villages built institutions that lasted. The lesson — that a turbine is easy and a village cooperative is hard — is why her decades of work are studied by development practitioners far beyond Indonesia, and why the Ramon Magsaysay committee gave its award in 2011 to a woman whose engineering ended where the grid never reached. {cite:wikipedia-tri-mumpuni} {cite:muslim500-tri-mumpuni}

## The engineer as institution-builder
Mumpuni's record is the museum's example of the engineer who understands that the hardest part of a technical problem is the human one: the village that must agree to build, the cooperative that must agree to govern, the institution that must outlast the turbine. Her career is the profile of that understanding: the engineer who spent decades building not just power plants but the social machinery that keeps them running. {cite:muslim500-tri-mumpuni} {cite:wikipedia-tri-mumpuni}
