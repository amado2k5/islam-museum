## Summary
Edward Said (1 November 1935 – 25 September 2003) was a Palestinian-American professor of literature at Columbia University, a founder of postcolonial studies, and the author of Orientalism (1978), probably the best-known of his works. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

Britannica calls him "an Arab Christian" and summarises his achievement in one sentence: in Orientalism he "showed how the Western world had historically constructed distorted images of 'the Orient' to justify colonialism and other forms of political subjugation". {cite:britannica-edward-said}

He was also a pianist and music critic, a public advocate of Palestinian statehood, and, with Daniel Barenboim, a co-founder of the West-Eastern Divan Orchestra. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

## Early life and education
Said was born on 1 November 1935 in Jerusalem, then in Mandatory Palestine, into a family of Palestinian Christians; he held American citizenship through his father, Wadie "William" Said, a Palestinian businessman who had served in the United States Expeditionary Forces in the First World War and founded a Cairo stationery company in 1919. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en} {cite:britannica-edward-said}

His mother, Hilda Said, née Musa, was of part-Palestinian, part-Lebanese descent and was raised in Nazareth; both of his parents were Arab Christians adhering to Protestantism, and Said was raised Protestant, first attending the Anglican diocese's St George's School in Jerusalem. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

The family left Jerusalem for Cairo at the onset of the 1947–49 Palestine war, and in Cairo Said attended Victoria College, from which, the English article records, he was expelled in 1951 for troublemaking; he was sent on to Northfield Mount Hermon School in Massachusetts, where he ranked first or second in a class of 160, and he was fluent in English, French and Arabic. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

At Princeton he took his AB in 1957, writing his senior thesis on André Gide and Graham Greene, and at Harvard his MA in 1960 and his PhD in 1964. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en} {cite:britannica-edward-said}

## The Columbia scholar
Said joined Columbia University's departments of English and comparative literature in 1963, became a full professor in 1969, the Parr Professor in 1977, and in 1992 a University Professor, the university's highest academic rank; he remained at Columbia until his death, in between holding visiting posts at Harvard, Stanford and Johns Hopkins, lecturing at more than two hundred universities, and serving as president of the Modern Language Association. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en} {cite:britannica-edward-said} {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-ar}

His first book, Joseph Conrad and the Fiction of Autobiography (1966), grew out of his Harvard dissertation, and Beginnings: Intention and Method (1974) won the inaugural Lionel Trilling Book Award in 1976, the first of his two Trilling Awards. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

In 1993 he delivered the BBC Reith Lectures, published as Representations of the Intellectual, arguing that the intellectual's role is to speak truth to power. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

## Orientalism and the public intellectual
Orientalism, published in 1978, argued that Western study of the Arab and Islamic world proceeded from a fabricated image of the Orient — a body of Eurocentric prejudice against Arab-Islamic peoples and their culture — and that this scholarship sprang from a political agenda serving colonial ambition; the book founded the field of postcolonial studies, and Britannica counts it among the most influential academic texts published after the Second World War. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en} {cite:britannica-edward-said}

The book drew a famous polemic with the historian Bernard Lewis, whom Said criticised for Orientalist bias; Lewis replied by questioning Said's own scholarship, and Terry Eagleton's more sympathetic verdict — quoted by the English article — calls Said's work "one of the most fertile and most charitable" elucidations of a partial picture. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

His later books carried the argument outward: The Question of Palestine (1979), Covering Islam (1981), The World, the Text, and the Critic (1983), Culture and Imperialism (1993) — which extended the analysis to the resistance cultures of the colonised — and the memoir Out of Place (1999), which recounts his formation "between two unacceptable identities". {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en} {cite:britannica-edward-said}

The June 1967 war turned him toward public politics: his essay "The Arab Portrayed" (1968) opened a lifelong campaign against Western media representation of Arabs, and from 1977 to 1991 he sat as an independent member of the Palestine National Council, resigning over the Oslo Accords, which he denounced as an instrument of Palestinian surrender. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

His 1979 essay "Zionism from the Standpoint of Its Victims" affirmed both the legitimacy of a Jewish homeland and the Palestinian right to self-determination; for years a supporter of a two-state solution, from 1999 he argued that only a binational one-state solution could bring a true peace. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

His criticism of Yasser Arafat led the Palestinian Authority to ban his books in 1995, yet after Arafat praised him at Camp David in 2000 the ban was lifted; while terminally ill he condemned the 2003 invasion of Iraq in "Resources of Hope" (Al-Ahram Weekly, 2 April 2003), and he co-founded the Palestinian National Initiative in his last year. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

## The stone-throwing controversy
On 3 July 2000, visiting the Blue Line in southern Lebanon, Said was photographed throwing a stone toward an Israeli guardhouse, an act that drew wide coverage and sharp criticism — Commentary dubbed him a "Professor of Terror", a Lebanese witness quoted in the Arabic press gave an account at odds with his, and the Freud Society in Vienna cancelled a lecture he was to give in 2001. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

Said's own explanation, recorded in the same article, was that the throw was a father-and-son contest of skill and "a symbol of joy at the end of the occupation" of southern Lebanon, that it was "a pebble; there was nobody there", and that no one was endangered; Columbia's provost Jonathan Cole answered his critics with a five-page letter that treated the act as falling short of sanctionable conduct and defended his academic freedom, and no disciplinary action followed. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

The museum records the episode with both the criticism and his reply attributed, as the sources give them. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

## Music and the Divan Orchestra
Music ran beside literature through Said's whole life: an accomplished pianist, he wrote music criticism for The Nation and the books Musical Elaborations (1991), Parallels and Paradoxes (2002, with Daniel Barenboim), and the posthumous On Late Style (2006). {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

In 1999 he and Barenboim co-founded the West-Eastern Divan Orchestra, which brings young Arab and Israeli musicians together to perform across the world, followed by the Barenboim–Said Foundation in Seville; in 2002 the two men shared the Prince of Asturias Award for Concord. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en} {cite:britannica-edward-said}

Among his many honours were the Sultan Owais Prize for 1996–97 — he was its first American recipient — the Lannan Literary Award for Lifetime Achievement in 2001, and some twenty honorary degrees. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

## Belief and affiliation
This section reports what the sources record of Said's belief, without a verdict from the museum.

Edward Said was not a Muslim, and this record does not present him as one. Two independent sources attest his Christian background and upbringing: the English Wikipedia article records that he was born into a family of Palestinian Christians, that both of his parents were Arab Christians adhering to Protestantism, that he was raised Protestant and educated first at an Anglican school in Jerusalem, and that he was buried in a Protestant cemetery in Lebanon; Encyclopaedia Britannica calls him, in terms, "an Arab Christian". {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en} {cite:britannica-edward-said}

The same English article records his adult position: "although raised Protestant, Said became an agnostic in his later years", and scholars quoted in its references describe him as "of Christian background, a confirmed agnostic, perhaps even an atheist". {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}

He stands in this museum, on the founder's list of influential figures of the modern Muslim world, as a major Palestinian intellectual whose work has reshaped how the Muslim world is studied and represented — recorded here under his own documented identity, not under a faith he did not hold. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en} {cite:britannica-edward-said}

## Death and legacy
Said died in New York on 25 September 2003, aged 67, after a twelve-year struggle with chronic lymphocytic leukaemia; he was survived by his wife Mariam, his son Wadie and his daughter Najla, and was buried in the Protestant Cemetery at Brummana in Lebanon. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en} {cite:britannica-edward-said}

His name now marks the Edward Said Chair of Modern Arab Studies at Columbia, the Edward Said National Conservatory of Music in Palestine (renamed from Birzeit University's music school in 2004), and the Barenboim–Said Academy in Berlin, opened in 2012. {cite:wikipedia-edward-said-en}
