## Brief
Husayn, the younger grandson of the Prophet, son of [[person/fatima|Fatima]] and [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]]. In 680 CE he refused the pledge of allegiance to the caliph Yazid I, and he was killed with his family and companions at Karbala. The religious meaning of that event, especially in Shi'i sentiment, far exceeds its military scale.

## Life
He was born in Medina around January 626 CE {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}. He grew up in the household of prophethood, and traditions mention his resemblance to the Prophet.

When Mu'awiya died in 680 CE and his son Yazid was installed, the first hereditary succession to the caliphate, Husayn refused the pledge of allegiance {cite:madelung-1997}. The people of Kufa invited him and promised him support, so he set out for Iraq with a small group of his family and companions. Umayyad forces intercepted them at Karbala. On the tenth of Muharram 61 AH (10 October 680 CE) Husayn and most of his men were killed, and the women and children were taken captive, among them his sister [[person/zaynab-bint-ali|Zaynab]] {cite:watt-1956-medina}.

## The account in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The sections below follow the article on al-Husayn in the second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam, written by L. Veccia Vaglieri, who draws on the early Arabic historians. Where the article passes a judgement, it is the article's and is attributed as such.

### Childhood and youth
According to most sources, Husayn was born at [[place/medina|Medina]] at the beginning of Sha'ban 4 AH, January 626 CE. He was still a child when the Prophet died and could have kept few memories of his grandfather {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 639}. The article notes hadiths that give the Prophet's affectionate words for his two grandsons, among them one that whoever loves them loves him, and one that [[person/hasan-ibn-ali|al-Hasan]] and al-Husayn are the masters of the youth of Paradise. It says the last saying carries great weight for the Shi'is, who treat it as one of the basic grounds for the right of the Prophet's descendants to the imamate and who give "master of the youth of Paradise" as an epithet to each brother. Other traditions show the Prophet holding his grandsons on his knees or shoulders, or letting them climb on his back during prayer, and the article points to [[person/ibn-kathir|Ibn Kathir]] as having collected many of these from Ibn Hanbal and al-Tirmidhi {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 639}. In his youth Husayn lived in the shadow of his father, obeyed his orders and took part in his campaigns {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 639}.

### Under Mu'awiya
The article says that even after Ali's death Husayn did not yet stand out as a personality. It reports that he reproached his brother al-Hasan for giving up power but then accepted the situation, receiving an allowance of one or two million dirhams and visiting Damascus often, where he was given further gifts. Several times, even before al-Hasan's death, Shi'is among them [[person/hujr-ibn-adi|Hujr ibn Adi]] urged him to rise, and the reply reported from al-Baladhuri was that nothing could be done while [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya]] lived. Mu'awiya was told by his governor at Medina, [[person/marwan-ibn-al-hakam|Marwan ibn al-Hakam]], of the Shi'is who visited Husayn and was not alarmed; he advised Marwan to avoid a clash and wrote to Husayn mixing generous promises with advice not to provoke him, and Husayn answered proudly in writing {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 639}.

The article names only two occasions on which Husayn acted boldly. One was when he defended his claim to certain possessions against powerful Umayyads. The other was when Mu'awiya asked the leading men of the state to recognise his son [[person/yazid-ibn-muawiya|Yazid]] as successor: Husayn was one of five who refused a claim that, in the article's words, introduced a new principle into succession to the caliphate {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 639}.

### Refusal of the pledge to Yazid
Immediately after Mu'awiya's death in Rajab 60 AH, March or April 680 CE, the governor of Medina, [[person/al-walid-ibn-utba-ibn-abi-sufyan|al-Walid ibn Utba]], acting on Yazid's orders, summoned Husayn and [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr]] to the palace at an unusual hour to oblige them to pay homage. Both guessed that Mu'awiya was dead, were resolved to keep to their refusal and feared for their lives. Ibn al-Zubayr fled to [[place/mecca|Mecca]] the next night. Husayn went to the palace with his supporters, offered condolences and asked that the pledge be put off, on the ground that it must be made in public to be valid; he won two days' delay and then left at night with his family for Mecca. Al-Walid, although Marwan urged him to use force, was unwilling to take serious measures against the Prophet's grandson and was dismissed for his inaction {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 639} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 640}. At Mecca the townspeople liked to attend on Husayn, and Ibn al-Zubayr, who the article says already had secret ambitions, was suspected of jealousy towards him {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 640}.

### The sources
The article names [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]] and [[person/al-baladhuri|al-Baladhuri]] as the most important texts for the rising and for Karbala. Al-Tabari gives many traditions on the authority of [[person/abu-mikhnaf|Abu Mikhnaf]], who died about 157 AH, 774 CE, with chains going back to contemporary witnesses, further traditions of Hisham ibn Muhammad al-Kalbi mostly received from Abu Mikhnaf, and a few by other transmitters. Al-Baladhuri used mostly the same sources in summary form. Other historians such as al-Dinawari, al-Ya'qubi and Ibn Abd Rabbihi add almost nothing, since they built on Abu Mikhnaf. The article says his work was so respected that even the earliest Shi'i writers, such as [[person/al-shaykh-al-mufid|al-Mufid]], took their narrative chiefly from it. It adds that from about the seventh century AH, thirteenth CE, the story was partly altered by romantic additions, such as single combats in which Husayn's opponents fall by the dozen, and that Ibn Kathir criticised these exaggerations by Shi'is {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 640}.

### The Kufan invitation and Muslim ibn Aqil
The news of Mu'awiya's death was welcomed at [[place/kufa|Kufa]], most of whose people were Shi'is, and letters and messengers invited Husayn there. The article says the Kufans could no longer bear the Umayyad regime, which they held guilty of seizing the fay', of letting God's property pass to the powerful and rich, and of killing the best men while sparing the worst, an allusion to Hujr ibn Adi. Husayn replied that he understood their hope of being united through him, and that an imam must act according to the Book of God, take money honestly, judge with truth and devote himself to God's service. Before deciding, he sent his cousin [[person/muslim-ibn-aqil|Muslim ibn Aqil]] to test the ground. Muslim gathered thousands of pledges and even presided over an assembly from the pulpit of the mosque {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 640}.

His activity was reported to Yazid, who no longer trusted the governor, al-Nu'man ibn Bashir al-Ansari, and gave Kufa to [[person/ubayd-allah-ibn-ziyad|Ubayd Allah ibn Ziyad]], already governor of Basra. Ibn Ziyad arrived in disguise and took energetic measures that frightened Husayn's sympathisers. Muslim tried in vain to organise an immediate revolt, fled and hid, was found and was put to death on 9 Dhu al-Hijja 60 AH, 11 September 680 CE. The article adds that he had written Husayn an optimistic letter and seems to have sent him the thousands of pledges {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 640}.

### The journey to Iraq
The article lists those who warned Husayn against a rising: his brother [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-hanafiyya|Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya]] at Medina, [[person/abdullah-ibn-umar|Abd Allah ibn Umar]] and [[person/abdullah-ibn-abbas|Abd Allah ibn Abbas]] on the road to Mecca, and others, with Ibn Abbas repeating his advice insistently at Mecca. It says Ibn al-Zubayr also tried to dissuade him, but in its judgement insincerely, since he was glad to be left alone in Mecca. Husayn did not give up. He performed the umra instead of the hajj and slipped away, while the governor Amr ibn Said al-Ashdaq was completing the pilgrimage rites outside the town, with about fifty armed relatives and friends together with women and children, on 8 Dhu al-Hijja 60 AH, 10 September 680 CE {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 640}. Amr sent a party under his brother Yahya, but it came to nothing more than a clash with whips and sticks. At al-Tan'im Husayn met a caravan from Yemen carrying cloaks and dye plants meant for the caliph and judged that he had a right to take the load {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 640}.

On the way he met the poet [[person/farazdaq|al-Farazdaq]], who told him frankly that the hearts of the Iraqis were with him and their swords with the Umayyads, and his cousin [[person/abdullah-ibn-jafar|Abd Allah ibn Ja'far]], who brought a letter of safe-conduct obtained from Amr. The article says Husayn's answers to all who tried to turn him back ran in the same vein: that God does as He wishes and that he left the choice of what is best to Him. Zuhayr ibn al-Qayn, a supporter of Uthman who had been avoiding camping near Husayn, was invited to meet him, changed his mind and became one of his keenest supporters {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 640} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 641}.

Ibn Ziyad had posted men on the roads from the Hijaz to Kufa. At al-Tha'labiyya Husayn heard of the execution of Muslim and of [[person/hani-ibn-urwa|Hani ibn Urwa]] and would have turned back, but the sons of Aqil, set on avenging their brother or sharing his fate, made him change his mind. At Zubala he learned that his messenger to Kufa had been discovered and killed; he read a proclamation to his followers, told them of the Kufans' treachery and invited them to leave him. Those who had joined on the road went away, and only those who had come with him from the Hijaz remained {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 641}.

Horsemen under [[person/al-hurr-ibn-yazid|al-Hurr ibn Yazid al-Tamimi]] then came upon him. Husayn had them given water, and al-Hurr and his squadron took part in two prayers led by him. The article reports that Husayn explained to them why he had set out: that they had no imam, that he wished to be a means of union, and that if they had changed their minds he would go away. Al-Hurr had orders to take him to Ibn Ziyad without fighting; he did not dare to stop Husayn when he went on, but suggested a route leading neither to Kufa nor to Medina and offered to write to Ibn Ziyad. Husayn would not agree. When Zuhayr proposed attacking al-Hurr's small force, Husayn refused to begin hostilities {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 641}.

### Karbala
Husayn camped at [[place/karbala|Karbala]] on 2 Muharram 61 AH. On the third day an army of 4,000 arrived from Kufa under [[person/umar-ibn-sad|Umar ibn Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas]], who the article says tried in vain to escape the task and obeyed under threat of losing his post. Ibn Ziyad demanded that all the rebels pay homage to Yazid and had them cut off from the river, and for three days Husayn's party suffered from thirst; a sortie led by his brother [[person/al-abbas-ibn-ali|al-Abbas]] filled only a few water-skins. Ibn Sa'd held talks with Husayn at night. The article says no one was present, but that it was rumoured that Husayn offered three alternatives: to go and fight at a frontier as an ordinary soldier, to go to Yazid and pledge to him in person, or to return whence he came. It reports that Ibn Ziyad, advised by Shamir ibn Dhi al-Jawshan, then ordered Ibn Sa'd to attack or to hand the command to Shamir {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 641}.

On the evening of 9 Muharram Ibn Sa'd advanced. Husayn asked for a night's respite and it was granted. He addressed his relatives and supporters, whom the article says his son Ali later recalled, told them he expected the end next day and asked them to go; with few exceptions they showed complete devotion to his cause. They spent the night preparing the camp's defences and in prayer {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 641} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 642}.

On the morning of 10 Muharram Husayn drew up his men, 32 horsemen and 40 on foot, and gave the standard to al-Abbas. The article reports that he addressed his opponents, asking them to consider whether it was lawful for them to kill him, reminding them of the saying about the masters of the youth of Paradise and reproaching the Kufans for having summoned him; told that he must first submit to his cousins, he answered that he would not humiliate himself like a slave {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 642}. The article argues that the fighting ran from dawn into the afternoon in duels, attacks, pauses and skirmishes around the tents, and rejects the view of Lammens that it was over within an hour, calling the selection of one tradition against the rest a method of disputable value. At noon Husayn and his followers prayed in the manner of the prayer of fear. In the afternoon his party was closely encircled, his companions fell before him, and the way lay open to the Talibis, who had not yet entered the fight. The first of them killed was his son [[person/ali-al-akbar-ibn-al-husayn|Ali al-Akbar]], then a son of Muslim ibn Aqil, sons of Abd Allah ibn Ja'far and of Aqil, and [[person/al-qasim-ibn-al-hasan|al-Qasim]], son of al-Hasan {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 642}. The article says al-Abbas also fell, and that al-Mufid places his death near the river, at the spot where his tomb was later built {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 643}.

Husayn, whom the article describes as nearly fifty-five years old and recently ill, was at first slow to fight but in the end fought on. His sister Zaynab spoke to Ibn Sa'd as he stood by. Wounded, Husayn fell. Sinan ibn Anas al-Nakha'i cut off his head, which Khawali ibn Yazid al-Asbahi carried to Ibn Ziyad. The soldiers then plundered the camp. A sick boy, Ali, later known as [[person/ali-zayn-al-abidin|Zayn al-Abidin]], was the only son of Husayn to survive, and the article records that the many Husaynids descend from him. It gives the dead of Karbala as 72, of whom 17 were Talibis, and 88 men of Ibn Ziyad's army, a figure it finds hard to reconcile with other counts {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 643}.

### After the battle
Husayn was buried with the others by the Asadis of the village of al-Ghadiriyya where they fell {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 643}. His head and those of other Talibis were taken to Kufa and then to [[place/damascus|Damascus]]. According to the article, Ibn Ziyad behaved insultingly, whereas Yazid, in most traditions, was respectful, seemed to regret his governor's haste, cursed Ibn Ziyad and is said to have declared that he would have pardoned Husayn had he come to him. The women and children were taken to Kufa and Damascus; Yazid at first spoke harshly but in the end treated them kindly, compensated them for stolen property and sent them back to Medina with an escort, and Ali was spared though at risk of execution as an adult {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 643} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 644}. The article lists seven accounts of where the head lies, among them beside Ali at Najaf, at Karbala with the body, at Medina, at Damascus, at al-Raqqa, and in a place to which the Fatimids are said to have moved it and where a mosque bears his name. It refers readers to the entries on the repentance of the Kufans and the movement of [[person/sulayman-ibn-surad|Sulayman ibn Surad]] in 64 to 65 AH {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 644}. See also the [[event/battle-of-karbala|Battle of Karbala]].

## Views of Husayn, as the Encyclopaedia of Islam reports them
### Sunni and general Muslim regard
The article says that across the Muslim world there was sympathy and regard for Husayn. Only adherents of the Umayyad movement called him a rebel against established authority, which excused Yazid for his death. Their view was opposed by those who despised the Umayyad regime and by Muslims who would not accept that the killers had acted in good conscience, yet looked for reasons not to blame either Husayn or the Companions and their successors who had stayed neutral to avoid civil war; the article cites Ibn Khaldun's Muqaddima for this attitude. It adds that in this near-universal esteem, grounded in his descent from the Prophet and in the conviction that he sacrificed himself for a cause, it is hard to separate Sunni opinion from Shi'i, except for certain privileges and attributes that only the Shi'is grant him. The article suggests that the favourable Sunni attitude was shaped by the moving accounts Abu Mikhnaf collected, largely from Kufans who repented, and describes that collection as markedly pro-Alid {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 646}.

### Shi'i tradition
The article says that, as imam, Husayn shares the privileges the Twelver Shi'is, Ismailis and Zaydis give to their imams; like them he is a mediator with God for those who call on him, and through his intercession his followers find guidance and salvation. As one of the holy Five he received the same divine grace as his brother, and as the Prophet's grandson he is owed reverence. Shi'i sources credit him with piety, shown by twenty-five pilgrimages on foot from Medina to Mecca and a thousand rak'as a day, a number the article says is considered exaggerated, and with generosity, forbearance, humility and eloquence. From the belief that the imams know what was, is and will be, the article explains, Shi'is hold that Husayn knew his fate in advance and set out knowingly, and one tradition has God offering him the choice between sacrifice and victory, which makes his act voluntary. On his aim, it reports that Shi'i texts say he offered himself and his possessions to God to revive and redeem his grandfather's religion and save it from the ruin caused by Yazid's conduct, to show the shame of the hypocrites, to teach the necessity of rising against unjust and impious rulers, and to serve as an example to the community. The article states that the idea of a redemptive sacrifice for the sins of the world is, in those terms, foreign to Shi'i belief, as far as the texts it consulted show, and suggests it may have entered later laments and recent poems, perhaps helped by Christian ideas {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 646}.

### The legend of Husayn
The article devotes a section to the legend that grew around Husayn in devotional literature. It describes three kinds of belief: cosmological ones, in which Husayn and the other members of the [[group/ahl-al-bayt|ahl al-bayt]] are figures of light created before the world, attributed by the article to the extremist Shi'is; end-time ones, in which Husayn awaits the Mahdi; and, most numerous, accounts in which he remains the historical figure but is surrounded by marvels {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 644}. These include marvels of his birth and childhood, signs reported at his death such as a darkened sky and red skies, dreams in which the Prophet is seen with Husayn's blood, the punishment said to have overtaken those who wronged him, wonders of the severed head, and powers attributed to him in life; the article notes that many reports of this kind are cast as hadiths with varied chains of transmission {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 644} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 645}. These are the beliefs of the sources it cites, reported here as such.

### Academic judgements
The article records two Western judgements. Wellhausen, it says, denied that Husayn had any religious motive and saw only an ambitious man's bid for supreme power; Lammens regarded him as frivolous and improvident. Both, it says, discounted the speeches attributed to him as later forgeries. The author of the article replies that, although the transmitters probably reworked this material, there emerges from it and from the facts a man driven by an ideology, the establishment of a regime that would meet the demands of true Islam, convinced he was right and stubborn in pursuing his ends, as the author says religious zealots generally are, and admired by supporters equally convinced. The author adds that this may not be a true portrait of Husayn the individual, but it was the portrait the next generation drew, from sentiment or from politics, and that it led to his exaltation and legendary standing among the Shi'is {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 646} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 647}.

## Legacy
Karbala became the supreme model of martyrdom in Islam: a story that every generation retells in every language, to argue about justice, power, and what a single person can refuse.

## For young visitors
Husayn was offered safety if he accepted a ruler he saw as unjust. He refused, and he died for that refusal. Millions of people still remember his choice every year.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 10)
The Sheikh mentions that al-Husayn ibn Ali had a lisp in his pronunciation of the letter ra, close to ghayn, and compares it to the way the French pronounce r (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:45:00}.

He relates that the Prophet used to tease him about it when he was about four years old, saying it was a lisp he had inherited from his uncle Musa, and he ties the account to Musa, whom his Lord called in the sacred valley (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:45:30}.

## For Sprouts
Husayn was the Prophet's younger grandson, son of Fatima and Ali, born in Medina about 626 CE. He was offered safety if he accepted a ruler he saw as unjust; he refused, and died at Karbala in 680 CE — and millions still retell his choice every year.

## For Explorers
Husayn ibn Ali was the Prophet's younger grandson, son of Fatima, born in Medina in 4 AH (about January 626 CE) and raised in the house of prophecy; the accounts recall his likeness to the Prophet. When Mu'awiya died in 680 CE and Yazid was installed — the first hereditary succession — Husayn refused the pledge; the Kufans invited him promising support, so he set out for Iraq with a small band of family and companions. Umayyad troops blocked him at Karbala, and on the tenth of Muharram 61 AH he and most of his party died, and the women and children — his sister Zaynab among them — were taken captive. Karbala became the highest example of martyrdom in Islam: a story each generation retells to argue about justice and power.

## For Scholars
Husayn ibn Ali ibn Abi Talib was the Prophet's younger grandson and Fatima's son, born in Medina in 4 AH (about January 626 CE; other datings at al-Sallabi), raised in the house of prophecy with the accounts recalling his likeness to the Prophet and the sayings 'Husayn is of me and I am of Husayn' and 'Hasan and Husayn are the masters of the young of Paradise'. When Mu'awiya died in 680 CE and Yazid was installed in the first hereditary succession he refused the pledge; the Kufans called him promising support, and he marched in a small band of family and companions. Umayyad troops blocked him at Karbala; on Ashura, 10 Muharram 61 AH (10 October 680 CE) he was martyred with most of his men, and the women and children — Zaynab among them — taken captive. The event's religious weight, above all in Shia feeling, exceeds its military size by far. Karbala became the highest example of martyrdom in Islam: a story each generation retells in every tongue to argue about justice, power and what one person can refuse.

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "History Taught Me"

[[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] devotes episodes 18 to 23 of his programme [[work/allamani-al-tarikh-program|"History Taught Me"]] to what he calls "the revolt of al-Husayn" against Yazid ibn Mu'awiya {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:02:30}. What follows is his account and his reading, attributed to him.

### Why he counts it among the gravest revolts
Al-Suwaidan holds that this revolt was among the most dangerous the Umayyads faced, not because al-Husayn led a large army that could break the state, but because in his view al-Husayn stood for the conscience of a community holding on to its right to be governed by the one it chooses {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:04:00}. He refers to a hadith that the first thing of Islam to change is governance and the last is prayer, and reads the shift from consultation (shura) to appointment as that first change {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:04:30}. He says the revolt was one of the great tragedies of the community's history, that it stirred anger among the Umayyad state's opponents and that Abbasid propaganda used it to win supporters {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:05:00}, and that the Umayyad state fell more than sixty years later {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:05:30}.

### Lineage and standing
He introduces him as Abu Abd Allah al-Husayn ibn Ali ibn Abi Talib ibn Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim, the Prophet's grandson and the son of his daughter [[person/fatima|Fatima]] {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:06:00}. He says al-Husayn was born in 4 AH and was killed on the day of Ashura in Muharram 61 AH at Karbala in Iraq {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:06:30}.

From Imam Ahmad's Musnad he relates the report of Ya'la al-Amiri: he went out with the Prophet to a meal they had been invited to, and found al-Husayn, a small child, playing with the boys; the Prophet laughed with him as he darted this way and that, until he caught him and kissed him, said of him that al-Husayn was of him and he of al-Husayn, and prayed for love upon whoever loves him {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:08:00}. Al-Suwaidan says this story will be seen reflected at the end of al-Husayn's story {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:08:00}.

From Sahih al-Bukhari he relates that a man from Iraq asked Abd Allah ibn Umar about a pilgrim in ihram who kills flies, and Ibn Umar marvelled that the people of Iraq asked about flies when the son of the Prophet's daughter had been killed, the one whom the Prophet, with his brother al-Hasan, counted among the sweetest things he had in this world {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:09:00}. He cites from the Musnad the hadith of Abu Sa'id al-Khudri on al-Hasan and al-Husayn as the masters of the youth of Paradise, says it is so well known that even al-Husayn's killers knew it, and asks how they dared {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:09:30}.

He says al-Husayn grew up in the house of Fatima and Ali, learned the law, jurisprudence and the love of worship, became a devout scholar, and remained honoured and respected across the whole community {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:10:00}. When strife broke out among the Companions he was in the army of his father [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]] at the Camel and at Siffin {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:10:30}.

### From al-Hasan's peace to the pledge for Yazid
Al-Suwaidan relates that [[person/hasan-ibn-ali|al-Hasan ibn Ali]] took charge after his father was killed, inclined to peace and pledged allegiance to Mu'awiya in 41 AH, so that the community came together and the year was called the Year of Unity {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:11:30}. He says the peace carried a condition that after Mu'awiya the matter would be decided by consultation among the people, with al-Hasan as its leading candidate, but al-Hasan died during Mu'awiya's caliphate {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:11:30}. When Mu'awiya sensed his death approaching he decided to appoint his son Yazid; most people pledged to him, and some leading figures opposed the appointment, al-Husayn among them {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:12:00}.

Al-Suwaidan stresses that Mu'awiya's caliphate was legitimate by a complete pledge in which al-Hasan, al-Husayn and everyone took part; there were Companions then more excellent than he, but the community agreed on him by the principle of accepting the less excellent while the more excellent is present, so in his view it is not permissible to impugn Mu'awiya's caliphate, since that would impugn the pledges of al-Hasan and al-Husayn {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:14:00}. He praises al-Hasan's abdication and draws from it a lesson in putting the community's unity before personal interest {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:14:30}. He says al-Husayn did not at first favour the peace but went along with his elder brother out of concern for the community's good, and pledged to Mu'awiya {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:15:30}.

He names four leading men who refused the pledge for Yazid before Mu'awiya's death: al-Husayn, [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr]], [[person/abdullah-ibn-abbas|Abd Allah ibn Abbas]] and [[person/abdullah-ibn-umar|Abd Allah ibn Umar]] {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:16:30}. He repeats that in his view this was not a Sunni-Shia question but a refusal to see consultation abolished {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:03:00}, and cites the verses [[passage/quran-42-38|al-Shura 38]] and [[passage/quran-3-159|Al Imran 159]] {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:03:30}. He says Mu'awiya pressed the four through his governor in Medina, they refused, and he died without their pledge {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:18:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:04:00}.

### His stand after Mu'awiya's death
He relates that Abd Allah ibn Umar and Abd Allah ibn Abbas, although they thought the step wrong, pledged after Mu'awiya's death for fear of strife and bloodshed, leaving al-Husayn and Ibn al-Zubayr {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:05:30}. He says Ibn al-Zubayr held al-Husayn more entitled to the caliphate than himself and did not put himself forward, but judged politically that the people of Iraq could not be trusted, having failed Ali before and produced the Kharijites, so he preferred that al-Husayn stay in the Hijaz or go to Yemen {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:07:00}.

Al-Suwaidan lists the causes of al-Husayn's rising: God's decree first, then Mu'awiya's failure to keep al-Hasan's condition in the peace, then al-Husayn's view that handing rule down to Yazid went against Islam's way of government {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:08:30}. He stresses that al-Husayn did not rise against Mu'awiya in his lifetime, keeping his pledge {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:09:00}, and that the governor of Medina, [[person/al-walid-ibn-utba-ibn-abi-sufyan|al-Walid ibn Utba]], tried to take the pledge from him and Ibn al-Zubayr but could not force them {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:09:30}, and al-Husayn left Medina for Mecca {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:10:00}.

He divides al-Husayn's stand into two stages: first the refusal to pledge, then working to resist Umayyad rule and putting himself forward as an alternative {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:10:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:14:30}. He grounds the first on two reasons. One is personal: in his view Yazid was not fit for the caliphate, lacking the conditions of probity and competence, and the community had men more learned, more upright and more capable, such as al-Husayn, Ibn Umar, Ibn al-Zubayr and Ibn Abbas {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:11:30}. The other, which he calls the main reason, is political: the absence of consultation and the seizure of rule by a single candidate {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:11:30}. He sees the refusal as withholding legitimacy from Umayyad rule, which the Umayyads wanted above all {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:12:00}.

Al-Suwaidan takes up the jurists' description of the act as rising against the imam, and argues that it was not, since such rising is against an imam whose pledge has been concluded and settled, while al-Husayn and the senior scholars had not pledged to Yazid; for him al-Husayn was a rival candidate {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:14:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:15:00}.

He says al-Husayn reached Mecca on 3 Sha'ban 60 AH and left for Iraq on 8 Dhu al-Hijja of the same year, about four months in which he declared no revolt, corresponding with the people of Iraq and gathering supporters, which al-Suwaidan regards as his right {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:16:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:16:30}. He explains that the word "Shi'a" then meant supporters and partisans, not a sect in today's sense, and that the difference was political, not one of creed or law, citing the verse [[passage/quran-37-83|al-Saffat 83]] {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:18:00}.

Why did he not declare the revolt in Mecca? Al-Suwaidan answers that he wanted Mecca's sanctity not to be violated by making it a field of battle, and relates Ibn Abbas's counsel and warning about the people of Iraq who had broken faith with his father, and al-Husayn's answer that being killed somewhere else was dearer to him than being killed in Mecca and having its sanctity violated through him {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:19:00}. He cites a hadith on Mecca's inviolability {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:19:30}. He adds that Medina was under an Umayyad governor, so Iraq was the likely place because of his many supporters there {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:20:00}.

### Those who counselled him
He relates that al-Husayn resolved to go to Kufa with his family, his women and his relatives {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:10:00}; al-Suwaidan reads the women's going as a sign that he did not set out for battle or armed revolt but for the political support he had in Kufa {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:10:30}. His brother [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-hanafiyya|Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya]], whom al-Suwaidan introduces as Ali's son by a woman of Banu Hanifa and one of the Muslims' learned and excellent men, objected and told him not to trust the people of Kufa, but al-Husayn persisted {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:11:30}. Ibn Abbas came and told him he feared he would perish, that the people of Iraq had betrayed his father, and advised him to stay until the Iraqis expelled their governor, or else to go to Yemen with its fortresses, its valleys and his father's partisans, and write to people from there {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:11:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:12:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:12:30}. Al-Husayn said he knew him to be a caring adviser but had resolved to go; Ibn Abbas then asked him not to take his women and children, fearing he would be killed as Uthman was killed while his women and children looked on {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:13:30}. Al-Suwaidan concludes that Ibn Abbas did not consider al-Husayn's rising illegitimate, only politically unwise {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:13:30}.

He says Ibn al-Zubayr also advised him against going to Iraq while agreeing with his stand, Abd Allah ibn Umar objected, and [[person/abdullah-ibn-jafar|Abd Allah ibn Ja'far]] came to him {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:14:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:15:00}. Al-Suwaidan points to the advisers' agreement that the promises of the people of Kufa could not be trusted, and their expectation that he would be killed if he went {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:16:00}, and to their awareness that kinship with the Prophet would not prevent his killing, since Uthman and Ali had been killed before {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:16:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:16:30}. Ibn Abbas wrote to him once more and al-Husayn persisted; al-Suwaidan explains the persistence as a matter of principle: not to acknowledge the abolition of the community's right to choose its ruler, even at the cost of his life {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:17:30}.

### On the road to Iraq
He says al-Husayn left Mecca on the Day of Tarwiya, 8 Dhu al-Hijja 60, heading for Iraq, with his household, his women and sixty elders of Kufa who had been with him in Mecca {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:08:30}. On the way he met the poet [[person/farazdaq|al-Farazdaq]] at Dhat Irq and asked him about the people of Kufa; when al-Husayn showed him their letters, al-Farazdaq told him their hearts were with him and their hands against him {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:11:00}. He relates that al-Husayn sent a messenger to Kufa announcing his coming; Ibn Ziyad's men seized him and Ibn Ziyad had him killed, and a second messenger sent to Muslim ibn Aqil met the same fate {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:16:00}. When news stopped coming and Bedouin told him that no one entered or left Kufa, al-Husayn sensed danger, yet kept pointing to the mass of letters he held {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:17:30}, until Ibn al-Ash'ath's messenger reached him with word of Muslim's capture, and then the killing of Muslim and of Hani ibn Urwa and the Kufans' desertion were confirmed, which struck him hard {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:18:00}. His account continues under the [[event/battle-of-karbala|Battle of Karbala]].

### The lesson he draws
Al-Suwaidan says al-Husayn did not win the battle, but his killers did not keep their hold: the Umayyad state fell about sixty years later, and his sacrifice fixed the principle that choosing the ruler is the community's right {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:14:00}. He holds that the point of remembering al-Husayn is not weeping but following his example {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:02:30}. He closes the series by saying that whoever believes in a principle sacrifices for it, and speaks of his own lasting grief over al-Husayn's killing {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep23 00:22:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep23 00:22:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep23 00:23:00}.

## In Abd al-Rahman Mansour's programme 'Ahalina'
[[person/abd-al-rahman-mansour|Abd al-Rahman Mansour]] mentions al-Husayn with his brother al-Hasan in his programme [[work/ahaleena|'Ahalina']]: the two used to ride on the Prophet's back while he prayed, and the Prophet came down from the pulpit during a Friday sermon to carry them when they stumbled in the mosque. {cite:assabile-abd-al-rahman-mansour ahaleena/ep07 00:08:00} He mentions that the Prophet kissed them, and that they used to sit in his gatherings while still young, from which he argues that a child's opinion should be heard and not despised. {cite:assabile-abd-al-rahman-mansour ahaleena/ep12 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-abd-al-rahman-mansour ahaleena/ep11 00:06:00}
