## Brief
Husayn, the younger grandson of the Prophet, son of [[person/fatima|Fatima]] and [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]]. In 680 CE he refused the pledge of allegiance to the caliph Yazid I, and he was killed with his family and companions at Karbala. The religious meaning of that event, especially in Shi'i sentiment, far exceeds its military scale.

## Life
He was born in Medina around January 626 CE {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}. He grew up in the household of prophethood, and traditions mention his resemblance to the Prophet.

When Mu'awiya died in 680 CE and his son Yazid was installed, the first hereditary succession to the caliphate, Husayn refused the pledge of allegiance {cite:madelung-1997}. The people of Kufa invited him and promised him support, so he set out for Iraq with a small group of his family and companions. Umayyad forces intercepted them at Karbala. On the tenth of Muharram 61 AH (10 October 680 CE) Husayn and most of his men were killed, and the women and children were taken captive, among them his sister [[person/zaynab-bint-ali|Zaynab]] {cite:watt-1956-medina}.

## Legacy
Karbala became the supreme model of martyrdom in Islam: a story that every generation retells in every language, to argue about justice, power, and what a single person can refuse.

## For young visitors
Husayn was offered safety if he accepted a ruler he saw as unjust. He refused, and he died for that refusal. Millions of people still remember his choice every year.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 10)
The Sheikh mentions that al-Husayn ibn Ali had a lisp in his pronunciation of the letter ra, close to ghayn, and compares it to the way the French pronounce r (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:45:00}.

He relates that the Prophet used to tease him about it when he was about four years old, saying it was a lisp he had inherited from his uncle Musa, and he ties the account to Musa, whom his Lord called in the sacred valley (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep10 00:45:30}.

## For Scholars
Husayn ibn Ali ibn Abi Talib was the Prophet's younger grandson and Fatima's son, born in Medina in 4 AH (about January 626 CE; other datings at al-Sallabi), raised in the house of prophecy with the accounts recalling his likeness to the Prophet and the sayings 'Husayn is of me and I am of Husayn' and 'Hasan and Husayn are the masters of the young of Paradise'. When Mu'awiya died in 680 CE and Yazid was installed in the first hereditary succession he refused the pledge; the Kufans called him promising support, and he marched in a small band of family and companions. Umayyad troops blocked him at Karbala; on Ashura, 10 Muharram 61 AH (10 October 680 CE) he was martyred with most of his men, and the women and children — Zaynab among them — taken captive. The event's religious weight, above all in Shia feeling, exceeds its military size by far. Karbala became the highest example of martyrdom in Islam: a story each generation retells in every tongue to argue about justice, power and what one person can refuse.

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "History Taught Me"

[[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] devotes episodes 18 to 23 of his programme [[work/allamani-al-tarikh-program|"History Taught Me"]] to what he calls "the revolt of al-Husayn" against Yazid ibn Mu'awiya {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:02:30}. What follows is his account and his reading, attributed to him.

### Why he counts it among the gravest revolts
Al-Suwaidan holds that this revolt was among the most dangerous the Umayyads faced, not because al-Husayn led a large army that could break the state, but because in his view al-Husayn stood for the conscience of a community holding on to its right to be governed by the one it chooses {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:04:00}. He refers to a hadith that the first thing of Islam to change is governance and the last is prayer, and reads the shift from consultation (shura) to appointment as that first change {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:04:30}. He says the revolt was one of the great tragedies of the community's history, that it stirred anger among the Umayyad state's opponents and that Abbasid propaganda used it to win supporters {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:05:00}, and that the Umayyad state fell more than sixty years later {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:05:30}.

### Lineage and standing
He introduces him as Abu Abd Allah al-Husayn ibn Ali ibn Abi Talib ibn Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim, the Prophet's grandson and the son of his daughter [[person/fatima|Fatima]] {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:06:00}. He says al-Husayn was born in 4 AH and was killed on the day of Ashura in Muharram 61 AH at Karbala in Iraq {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:06:30}.

From Imam Ahmad's Musnad he relates the report of Ya'la al-Amiri: he went out with the Prophet to a meal they had been invited to, and found al-Husayn, a small child, playing with the boys; the Prophet laughed with him as he darted this way and that, until he caught him and kissed him, said of him that al-Husayn was of him and he of al-Husayn, and prayed for love upon whoever loves him {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:08:00}. Al-Suwaidan says this story will be seen reflected at the end of al-Husayn's story {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:08:00}.

From Sahih al-Bukhari he relates that a man from Iraq asked Abd Allah ibn Umar about a pilgrim in ihram who kills flies, and Ibn Umar marvelled that the people of Iraq asked about flies when the son of the Prophet's daughter had been killed, the one whom the Prophet, with his brother al-Hasan, counted among the sweetest things he had in this world {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:09:00}. He cites from the Musnad the hadith of Abu Sa'id al-Khudri on al-Hasan and al-Husayn as the masters of the youth of Paradise, says it is so well known that even al-Husayn's killers knew it, and asks how they dared {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:09:30}.

He says al-Husayn grew up in the house of Fatima and Ali, learned the law, jurisprudence and the love of worship, became a devout scholar, and remained honoured and respected across the whole community {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:10:00}. When strife broke out among the Companions he was in the army of his father [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]] at the Camel and at Siffin {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:10:30}.

### From al-Hasan's peace to the pledge for Yazid
Al-Suwaidan relates that [[person/hasan-ibn-ali|al-Hasan ibn Ali]] took charge after his father was killed, inclined to peace and pledged allegiance to Mu'awiya in 41 AH, so that the community came together and the year was called the Year of Unity {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:11:30}. He says the peace carried a condition that after Mu'awiya the matter would be decided by consultation among the people, with al-Hasan as its leading candidate, but al-Hasan died during Mu'awiya's caliphate {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:11:30}. When Mu'awiya sensed his death approaching he decided to appoint his son Yazid; most people pledged to him, and some leading figures opposed the appointment, al-Husayn among them {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:12:00}.

Al-Suwaidan stresses that Mu'awiya's caliphate was legitimate by a complete pledge in which al-Hasan, al-Husayn and everyone took part; there were Companions then more excellent than he, but the community agreed on him by the principle of accepting the less excellent while the more excellent is present, so in his view it is not permissible to impugn Mu'awiya's caliphate, since that would impugn the pledges of al-Hasan and al-Husayn {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:14:00}. He praises al-Hasan's abdication and draws from it a lesson in putting the community's unity before personal interest {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:14:30}. He says al-Husayn did not at first favour the peace but went along with his elder brother out of concern for the community's good, and pledged to Mu'awiya {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:15:30}.

He names four leading men who refused the pledge for Yazid before Mu'awiya's death: al-Husayn, [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr]], [[person/abdullah-ibn-abbas|Abd Allah ibn Abbas]] and [[person/abdullah-ibn-umar|Abd Allah ibn Umar]] {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:16:30}. He repeats that in his view this was not a Sunni-Shia question but a refusal to see consultation abolished {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:03:00}, and cites the verses [[passage/quran-42-38|al-Shura 38]] and [[passage/quran-3-159|Al Imran 159]] {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:03:30}. He says Mu'awiya pressed the four through his governor in Medina, they refused, and he died without their pledge {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep18 00:18:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:04:00}.

### His stand after Mu'awiya's death
He relates that Abd Allah ibn Umar and Abd Allah ibn Abbas, although they thought the step wrong, pledged after Mu'awiya's death for fear of strife and bloodshed, leaving al-Husayn and Ibn al-Zubayr {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:05:30}. He says Ibn al-Zubayr held al-Husayn more entitled to the caliphate than himself and did not put himself forward, but judged politically that the people of Iraq could not be trusted, having failed Ali before and produced the Kharijites, so he preferred that al-Husayn stay in the Hijaz or go to Yemen {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:07:00}.

Al-Suwaidan lists the causes of al-Husayn's rising: God's decree first, then Mu'awiya's failure to keep al-Hasan's condition in the peace, then al-Husayn's view that handing rule down to Yazid went against Islam's way of government {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:08:30}. He stresses that al-Husayn did not rise against Mu'awiya in his lifetime, keeping his pledge {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:09:00}, and that the governor of Medina, [[person/al-walid-ibn-utba-ibn-abi-sufyan|al-Walid ibn Utba]], tried to take the pledge from him and Ibn al-Zubayr but could not force them {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:09:30}, and al-Husayn left Medina for Mecca {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:10:00}.

He divides al-Husayn's stand into two stages: first the refusal to pledge, then working to resist Umayyad rule and putting himself forward as an alternative {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:10:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:14:30}. He grounds the first on two reasons. One is personal: in his view Yazid was not fit for the caliphate, lacking the conditions of probity and competence, and the community had men more learned, more upright and more capable, such as al-Husayn, Ibn Umar, Ibn al-Zubayr and Ibn Abbas {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:11:30}. The other, which he calls the main reason, is political: the absence of consultation and the seizure of rule by a single candidate {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:11:30}. He sees the refusal as withholding legitimacy from Umayyad rule, which the Umayyads wanted above all {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:12:00}.

Al-Suwaidan takes up the jurists' description of the act as rising against the imam, and argues that it was not, since such rising is against an imam whose pledge has been concluded and settled, while al-Husayn and the senior scholars had not pledged to Yazid; for him al-Husayn was a rival candidate {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:14:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:15:00}.

He says al-Husayn reached Mecca on 3 Sha'ban 60 AH and left for Iraq on 8 Dhu al-Hijja of the same year, about four months in which he declared no revolt, corresponding with the people of Iraq and gathering supporters, which al-Suwaidan regards as his right {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:16:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:16:30}. He explains that the word "Shi'a" then meant supporters and partisans, not a sect in today's sense, and that the difference was political, not one of creed or law, citing the verse [[passage/quran-37-83|al-Saffat 83]] {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:18:00}.

Why did he not declare the revolt in Mecca? Al-Suwaidan answers that he wanted Mecca's sanctity not to be violated by making it a field of battle, and relates Ibn Abbas's counsel and warning about the people of Iraq who had broken faith with his father, and al-Husayn's answer that being killed somewhere else was dearer to him than being killed in Mecca and having its sanctity violated through him {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:19:00}. He cites a hadith on Mecca's inviolability {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:19:30}. He adds that Medina was under an Umayyad governor, so Iraq was the likely place because of his many supporters there {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:20:00}.

### Those who counselled him
He relates that al-Husayn resolved to go to Kufa with his family, his women and his relatives {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:10:00}; al-Suwaidan reads the women's going as a sign that he did not set out for battle or armed revolt but for the political support he had in Kufa {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:10:30}. His brother [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-hanafiyya|Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya]], whom al-Suwaidan introduces as Ali's son by a woman of Banu Hanifa and one of the Muslims' learned and excellent men, objected and told him not to trust the people of Kufa, but al-Husayn persisted {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:11:30}. Ibn Abbas came and told him he feared he would perish, that the people of Iraq had betrayed his father, and advised him to stay until the Iraqis expelled their governor, or else to go to Yemen with its fortresses, its valleys and his father's partisans, and write to people from there {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:11:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:12:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:12:30}. Al-Husayn said he knew him to be a caring adviser but had resolved to go; Ibn Abbas then asked him not to take his women and children, fearing he would be killed as Uthman was killed while his women and children looked on {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:13:30}. Al-Suwaidan concludes that Ibn Abbas did not consider al-Husayn's rising illegitimate, only politically unwise {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:13:30}.

He says Ibn al-Zubayr also advised him against going to Iraq while agreeing with his stand, Abd Allah ibn Umar objected, and [[person/abdullah-ibn-jafar|Abd Allah ibn Ja'far]] came to him {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:14:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:15:00}. Al-Suwaidan points to the advisers' agreement that the promises of the people of Kufa could not be trusted, and their expectation that he would be killed if he went {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:16:00}, and to their awareness that kinship with the Prophet would not prevent his killing, since Uthman and Ali had been killed before {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:16:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:16:30}. Ibn Abbas wrote to him once more and al-Husayn persisted; al-Suwaidan explains the persistence as a matter of principle: not to acknowledge the abolition of the community's right to choose its ruler, even at the cost of his life {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:17:30}.

### On the road to Iraq
He says al-Husayn left Mecca on the Day of Tarwiya, 8 Dhu al-Hijja 60, heading for Iraq, with his household, his women and sixty elders of Kufa who had been with him in Mecca {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:08:30}. On the way he met the poet [[person/farazdaq|al-Farazdaq]] at Dhat Irq and asked him about the people of Kufa; when al-Husayn showed him their letters, al-Farazdaq told him their hearts were with him and their hands against him {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:11:00}. He relates that al-Husayn sent a messenger to Kufa announcing his coming; Ibn Ziyad's men seized him and Ibn Ziyad had him killed, and a second messenger sent to Muslim ibn Aqil met the same fate {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:16:00}. When news stopped coming and Bedouin told him that no one entered or left Kufa, al-Husayn sensed danger, yet kept pointing to the mass of letters he held {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:17:30}, until Ibn al-Ash'ath's messenger reached him with word of Muslim's capture, and then the killing of Muslim and of Hani ibn Urwa and the Kufans' desertion were confirmed, which struck him hard {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep22 00:18:00}. His account continues under the [[event/battle-of-karbala|Battle of Karbala]].

### The lesson he draws
Al-Suwaidan says al-Husayn did not win the battle, but his killers did not keep their hold: the Umayyad state fell about sixty years later, and his sacrifice fixed the principle that choosing the ruler is the community's right {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep19 00:14:00}. He holds that the point of remembering al-Husayn is not weeping but following his example {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep20 00:02:30}. He closes the series by saying that whoever believes in a principle sacrifices for it, and speaks of his own lasting grief over al-Husayn's killing {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep23 00:22:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep23 00:22:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan alamani-al-tarikh/ep23 00:23:00}.
