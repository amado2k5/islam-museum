## Overview
Muhammad Rashid Rida, whose full name ran Muhammad Rashid ibn Ali Rida ibn Muhammad Shams al-Din ibn Muhammad Baha al-Din ibn Mulla Ali Khalifa, lived from 1865 to 1935. The Encyclopaedia of Islam describes him as one of the most productive and influential authors of Islamic reform, of Pan-Islamism and, to a certain extent, of Arab nationalism. His name is bound above all to the journal al-Manar, which he edited from its foundation in 1898. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 486}

A scholar and journalist who studied under [[person/muhammad-abduh|Muhammad Abduh]] and wrote a two-volume life of him, he was also a political actor, a founder of an institution of learning, and a writer on the caliphate. The sections below follow the Encyclopaedia of Islam article, and then the praise given to him by a contemporary, which the museum had recorded earlier. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923}

## Birth and education
He was born on 27 Jumada I 1282/23 September 1865 in Qalamun, a village near [[place/tripoli|Tripoli]] on the Mediterranean coast of northern Lebanon. The article says that its inhabitants were all Sunni Muslims and that most of them claimed descent from the Prophet; it refers readers to other works for the family's alleged Husaynid pedigree. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 486} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 487}

His first schooling was in the village kuttab. He went on to an Ottoman state school in Tripoli and above all to a madrasa founded there in 1879, whose director was Shaykh [[person/husayn-al-jisr|Husayn al-Jisr]], who lived from 1845 to 1909. The article treats al-Jisr as an important influence on his development, and adds that the pupil later had heated differences of opinion with him. The OCR of the passage describing exactly what he took from this teacher is damaged, so the details are not given here. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 486}

## Cairo and al-Manar
In the winter of 1897-8 he travelled to Egypt. The day after reaching [[place/cairo|Cairo]] he went to see Muhammad Abduh to explain his plan for a journal on Islamic reform. The first issue of al-Manar appeared on 22 Shawwal 1315/mid-March 1898. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 486}

The house he later acquired in Cairo served at once as his home, printing press, bookshop and store. Despite the success of many of his publications and occasional gifts from friends and patrons, the article says he was seldom free of financial worry. It also notes that he was married three times, the first two marriages ending after a short time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 486}

## Journeys and political hopes
After the Ottoman constitution was restored in 1908 he visited Syria. Later journeys took him to Istanbul in 1909-10, to India in 1912 and home by way of Muscat and Kuwait, to the Hijaz in 1916 and again in 1926, to Syria in 1919-20, to Europe in 1921-2 and to the Pan-Islamic Congress at Jerusalem in 1931. He reported on each in al-Manar. The article says that for him these journeys were tied to specific political aims, and that he was often disappointed in his immediate hopes. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 486}

On the Syrian journey of 1908, an incident in the Umayyad Mosque at Damascus and the riot that followed showed him that he faced considerable opposition there and could not count unreservedly on the Young Turks. In Istanbul he sought to remove misunderstandings between Arabs and Turks, and to found a modern Islamic institution of higher learning whose graduates, better scholars than those trained in the traditional way, could defend Islam along modernist lines. After some early successes he concluded that neither aim could be reached, at least not as he conceived them, because of opposition from influential members of the ruling Committee of Union and Progress. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 486}

## Dar al-Da'wa wa'l-Irshad
Back in Egypt, he at once began preparing an association to support the planned institution of higher education. It was founded in 1911 as Dar al-Da'wa wa'l-Irshad. Regular teaching began in March 1912 but was stopped soon after the First World War broke out, for lack of donations. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 486} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 487}

## Arab politics
Disappointed with the Young Turks, he began to shape political plans along Pan-Arab lines. Already in 1902-3 al-Manar had printed in instalments Umm al-Qura by [[person/abd-al-rahman-al-kawakibi|Abd al-Rahman al-Kawakibi]]. From 1911-2 he took part in the public campaign against the Young Turks and founded a secret association with Pan-Arab aims, the Jam'iyyat al-Jami'a al-Arabiyya. According to the article it sought to reconcile the rulers of the Arabian Peninsula with one another and to link the Arab secret societies. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 487}

These activities brought him into contact with most of the peninsula's rulers, among them [[person/ibn-saud|Ibn Saud]] and the Sharif of Mecca. They also strained for years, especially during the war, his relations with friends such as [[person/shakib-arslan|Shakib Arslan]], who despite reservations sided with the [[polity/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 487}

During the war he supported the [[event/arab-revolt-1334|Arab Revolt]] in the Hijaz and for a time afterwards remained among its propagandists. On his journey to Syria in 1919-20 he was elected president of the Syrian Congress, but he went back to Egypt when French mandate troops entered Syria. In the following years his relations with [[person/husayn-ibn-ali-sharif-makkah|Husayn ibn Ali]], King of the Hijaz, and with the Hashimite dynasty in general worsened. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 487}

## The Wahhabiyya and Ibn Taymiyya
After the house of Al Saud took power in the [[place/hijaz|Hijaz]] and the Hashimites were expelled in 1924-6, the article places him among the authors who tried to justify the change on historical and political grounds. Its chief evidence is his book al-Wahhabiyyun wa'l-Hijaz, published in Cairo in 1925-6 and made up of articles from al-Manar and the daily al-Ahram. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 487}

The article reports his own account of how his view changed. In youth, under Ottoman propaganda, he had taken the Wahhabis for fanatical sectarians. After reaching Egypt, through reading the chronicle of [[person/abd-al-rahman-al-jabarti|al-Jabarti]], other works and direct information, he came to hold that it was the Wahhabis, not their opponents, who defended true Islam, even if they tended to certain exaggerations. In the same period he worked for the rehabilitation of [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] and his school. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 487}

## Relations with the Shia and with Sufism
The article says this shift worsened his relations with the Shia and his judgement of the Shia's role in Islamic history. Earlier articles in al-Manar had already drawn protests from Shii scholars, among them Sayyid Muhsin al-Amin al-Amili. The article also notes that he and other al-Manar authors made many statements in favour of Pan-Islamic unity and interconfessional understanding. After his endorsement of the Wahhabiyya he became a chosen target of Shii polemics, and his replies to them were gathered in a two-part work, the first part printed in Cairo in 1929-30. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 487}

In his younger years he had been for a time a disciple of the [[group/naqshbandiyya|Naqshbandi]] order. He early criticised ideas he thought false and harmful in Sufism, and his increasingly critical remarks about the Sufi orders, especially those of his own day, later set him against leading scholars of [[building/al-azhar|al-Azhar]]. The article, citing Albert Hourani, says these remarks should not be read as a wholesale rejection of Sufism. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 487}

## The caliphate
In the Islamic debates on the past and future of the caliphate after the First World War, he leaned increasingly towards a renewed, Arab caliphate. His book al-Khilafa aw al-Imama al-Uzma, published in Cairo in 1923, answers the abolition of the sultanate by the Great Turkish National Assembly on 1 November 1922, which left the caliphate without secular authority. He took a lively part in later discussions of the question, in the preparations of several Pan-Islamic congresses and in their sessions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 487}

## Influence and open questions
The article says he exercised extraordinary influence in many parts of the Muslim world as a spokesman of the [[group/salafiyya|Salafiyya]], and that his views on theology and the Sharia, developed across many writings, cannot be treated in its space. It also says that many aspects of his political activity, such as his attitude to the British occupation forces in Egypt, still need study. Many of his publications are series of articles first printed in al-Manar and later collected in books. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 487}

## Contemporary praise
Earlier, the museum recorded that Shakib Arslan, in his commentary on Hadir al-Alam al-Islami, calls him the great master and praises his command of Arabic, his skill in connecting Islamic law to contemporary questions and his reply to critics of Islamic law. This is the judgement of a contemporary and friend and is not neutral. {cite:stoddard-arslan-hadir-1923}
