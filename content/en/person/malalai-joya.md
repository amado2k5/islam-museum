## Summary
Malalai Joya is an Afghan activist who became in her mid-twenties the youngest elected member of Afghanistan's parliament (the constitutional Loya Jirga and the 2005 National Assembly), internationally known for outspoken speeches against the "warlords" inside parliament itself — a stand that led to her suspension in 2007 and a continued feminist and political struggle between Afghanistan and exile. {cite:wikipedia-malalai-joya}

## Early life and education
She was born in 1978 in Farah province, western Afghanistan, spent her childhood as a refugee in Pakistan and Iran with her family fleeing the war, then returned to study literature and worked as a relief organiser in remote areas during the first period of hardline rule, when girls' schools were closed. {cite:wikipedia-malalai-joya}

## Parliament and expulsion
Elected in 2005 as the youngest member for her province, she used the first sessions to attack the presence of civil-war faction figures inside the assembly, declaring that the hall gathered "the worst violators of human rights". Parliament suspended her membership in 2007 on accusations of insulting colleagues; since then she has lived under personal protection, surviving documented assassination attempts — her sources record four attempts on her life. {cite:wikipedia-malalai-joya} {cite:muslim500-malalai-joya}

## Writing and exile
She published her memoir A Woman Among Warlords with journalist Derrick O'Keefe, which became a reference account of the recent Afghan period, and continued — from exile and clandestinely inside the country — activism for women's and widows' rights and charitable clinics, opposing openly both militia rule and foreign military presence. {cite:wikipedia-malalai-joya}

## Positions and debate
She describes herself as a secular democrat rejecting political Islam, warlords and foreign occupation alike — a position debated by many: critics accuse her of exaggeration and turning against elected institutions, supporters see her as the single honest voice in a compromised parliament, and some Afghan-government and Western backers consider her discourse unrealistic — the museum reports all these positions attributed, without verdict. {cite:wikipedia-malalai-joya}

## Legacy
Her career is read in the study of Afghan women's movements as the most famous parliamentary attempt to confront the armed elite from inside: one voice in a hostile chamber, it cost her seat and her safety and made her a global symbol of opposition to weapons and corruption together. {cite:wikipedia-malalai-joya}
