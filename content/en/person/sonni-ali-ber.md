## Summary
Sonni Ali Ber — also written Sunni Ali, and known as Si Ali — reigned from about 1464 to 1492 as the fifteenth ruler of the Sonni dynasty of Songhai, and transformed a relatively small Niger-bend state into the Songhai Empire. {cite:wikipedia-sonni-ali-en} No source consulted gives a birth year: 1464, the year his Wikidata item files as his birth, is his accession, and the queue figure of 1440 is attested nowhere. {cite:wikidata-sonni-ali} He was the son of Sonni Muhammad Da'o, the tenth Sonni ruler in the kinglists of the Tarikh al-Sudan and the Tarikh al-Fattash, and his mother was from Fara, an area still heavily pagan, in whose milieu he was raised. {cite:wikipedia-sonni-ali-en}

## Conquests
On his accession the Songhay already controlled the Niger basin from Dendi to Mema. His first major conquest was Timbuktu: held by the Tuareg since the Malian retreat, its chief 'Umar asked for Songhai protection in 1469, and Sonni Ali took the city and conducted a repressive policy against its scholars, whom he saw as associated with the Tuareg. {cite:wikipedia-sonni-ali-en} He organised a powerful fleet on the Niger and in 1473 used it to besiege Djenne, which surrendered only after being reduced to starvation; he attempted a canal hundreds of kilometres long from Ras el Ma toward Walata before abandoning the project in 1483 to defeat a Mossi invasion, and he conquered Massina, the Inner Niger Delta, the Sanhaja lands called Nunu and the lands of Kunta, though Borgu eluded him. {cite:wikipedia-sonni-ali-en} His main capital was Gao, though he based himself at Kukiya, Kabara and Tindirma as his campaigns required. {cite:wikipedia-sonni-ali-en}

## Religion, as the sources contest it
Sonni Ali ruled over both urban Muslims and rural non-Muslims at a time when the traditional coexistence of beliefs was being challenged, and his own faith is the most contested thing about him. As a Sonni he received an Islamic education, but he practised a syncretic, unorthodox faith: he professed Islam and yet worshipped idols, believed the pronouncements of soothsayers, sought help from magicians and venerated certain trees and stones by slaughtering at them, in al-Maghili's indictment. {cite:wikipedia-sonni-ali-en} He did observe the Islamic prayers, fasted and gave alms; but, as Toby Green notes, he did not permit the acceptance of the sharia in Songhai and saw no barrier to enslaving Muslims, which led some writers to describe him as outwardly or nominally Muslim. {cite:wikipedia-sonni-ali-en} The picture is not one-sided even in the hostile sources: al-Sa'di recognised that despite his bad treatment of the scholars Sonni Ali acknowledged their worth and showed kindness and respect to some of them, and the funeral stelae from Kukiya cast doubt on the chroniclers' criticism, since they wrote on behalf of the Askias who had overthrown the Sonni dynasty. {cite:wikipedia-sonni-ali-en} Because the sources contest his Islam itself and none names a school or order for him, his faith is recorded in this museum as unknown, with these readings preserved.

## Death and succession
His death in 1492 is a matter of conjecture: according to the Tarikh al-Sudan he drowned in a boating accident while crossing the Niger, on 6 November 1492, while oral tradition believes he was killed by his sister's son, Askia Muhammad Ture. {cite:wikipedia-sonni-ali-en} {cite:wikidata-sonni-ali} His son Sunni Baru was immediately proclaimed king by the army commanders, but Askia Muhammad challenged him on the ground that Baru was not a faithful Muslim, defeated him, and took power in 1493, founding the Askia dynasty. {cite:wikipedia-sonni-ali-en}
