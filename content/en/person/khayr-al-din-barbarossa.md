## Overview
Khayr al-Din, known to Europeans as Barbarossa, was a Turkish corsair who became Grand Admiral of the Ottoman fleet. The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls him a famous corsair and a national hero in Turkish memory, and says that his name struck terror in the Mediterranean while he also had a reputation for generosity and prudence. It credits him with giving the Ottoman empire a well-organised North African province and with strengthening the Ottoman maritime presence. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1207} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1210}

The Arabic account by al-Salabi, which this page also keeps, gives his name as Khayr al-Din ibn Ya'qub ibn Yusuf, a descendant of the Muslim Turkish conquerors settled on the island of Lesbos, whose mother was a Muslim woman of al-Andalus. It places his death in Istanbul around 953 AH, about 1546 CE, and treats the date as derived. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p. 207-218}

## Birth and early career
The article says he was born at Mytilene, called Metellin, where his father Ya'qub, a cavalryman holding a military fief, had settled after the Turkish conquest of 866/1462. His birth is placed around 1466 on the evidence of Hajji Khalifa, who makes him more than eighty at his death. The article gives less credence to other figures, one of them an age of sixty-three at death and another a Venetian report of 1534 that calls him over fifty. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1207}

He began by carrying merchandise in a ship of his own to Saros, Salonica and Negroponte. When Selim I forbade sailing on the Aegean without his authorisation, in order to stop his brother and rival Korkud from fleeing in 1513, Khayr al-Din fitted out a second boat at Mytilene and carried grain from the north to Africa. He bought ninety-five enslaved Black Africans and sold them in Rumelia, and from there he rejoined his elder brother [[person/uruj-barbarossa|Uruj]] at [[place/djerba|Djerba]] with his two ships. He achieved many exploits by land and sea but, in general, stayed in his brother's shadow. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1207}

## Naval jihad
In al-Salabi's telling, he went with Uruj into naval jihad in the Aegean archipelago from his youth, around 1510 CE. They then moved their activity to North Africa, and the Hafsid sultan granted them the right to settle on Djerba. They tried to liberate Bejaia in 1512 CE, entered [[place/jijel|Jijel]] in 1514 CE and killed its defenders. After Uruj was killed in 1518 CE, Khayr al-Din continued to rule [[place/algiers|Algiers]]. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p. 207-218}

## Algiers after Uruj
After Uruj died in 1518, Khayr al-Din, now controlling only Algiers, faced an expedition led by Hugo of Moncada, viceroy of Sicily. The article notes that the sources disagree on its size, giving figures from forty ships and five thousand men to a hundred and seventy ships and twenty thousand men. Around the middle of August the invaders landed at the mouth of the Harrash and took a height. Khayr al-Din, with six hundred Turks and twenty thousand local troops, attacked the Spanish from behind and forced them to re-embark. A storm sank many of the ships and he took numerous prisoners, among them thirty-six high-ranking officers. He then put to flight the sultan of Tlemcen, who had allied with the Spanish and marched on Miliana. In spring 1520 he retook Tenes, which had rebelled, while his squadron of eighteen ships put fifteen Spanish ships to flight. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1207}

From then on, according to the Turkish chronicle the article relies on, the Christians called him Barbarossa because of the colour of his beard. Like his brother, he divided the Algerian lands into two provinces under local governors, Ahmad ibn al-Qadi in the east and Muhammad ibn Ali in the west. He took the honorific Khayr al-Din and the title of sultan, according to an inscription of the Algiers mosque he built, dated April 1520, which also calls him a warrior for the faith. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1207}

## Ottoman overlordship
Around the turn of October and November 1519, Khayr al-Din sent an embassy to the Ottoman sultan with four ships, forty slaves for Selim I and as many for the pashas of the Diwan, and a petition from the Algerian people. The envoys were well received. Selim accepted the overlordship of the kingdom of Algiers, sent an investiture decree and a flag, and supplied two thousand Janissaries and some artillery. He also authorised Khayr al-Din to enrol volunteers, promising them the rights of the Janissaries. From that moment the sultan's name was struck on the coins of Algiers and recited in the Friday sermon, which the article reads as an affirmation of Ottoman suzerainty. See also [[event/algiers-joins-ottoman-1519|the entry of Algiers under Ottoman sovereignty]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1208}

The article adds that Khayr al-Din helped Abd Allah unseat Mas'ud at [[place/tlemcen|Tlemcen]], receiving a tribute of ten thousand gold pieces. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1208}

## Loss and recovery of Algiers
His rule in Algiers was interrupted when the sultan of Tunis, Muhammad, allied with Ibn al-Qadi and with Kara Hasan, head of the Turkish garrison at Cherchell, and attacked him. Defeated in the field and besieged in the town with little food and ammunition, Khayr al-Din had to flee to Jijel with nine ships after the townspeople rose against him. With the help of the head of the Banu Abbas of Little Kabylia, he took Collo in 1521, and then Bone and [[place/constantine|Constantine]] in 1522. He also took up privateering, and the article reports that he captured nine grain ships with seven of his own and shared the grain among a population afflicted by famine. In spring 1523 he seized ships in the Gulf of Genoa and five grain vessels off the coast of Rome, then took control of Djerba, where the corsairs Sinan Reis and Aydin Reis joined him. With a fleet of forty-one ships they raided the coasts of the western Mediterranean and took large amounts of booty. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1208}

In 1525, invited by townspeople hard hit by the loss of corsair revenue, he marched from Jijel on Algiers. Ibn al-Qadi came out with twenty thousand men, was beaten, and was beheaded by his own men. From 1526 to 1528 Khayr al-Din consolidated his position. He retook Cherchell, obliged the sultan of Tlemcen to pay six years of back tribute and twenty thousand gold pieces a year after that, took Tenes and Constantine again after rebellions, and made Ibn al-Qadi's brother Husayn, in Great Kabylia, pay a tribute of silver. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1208}

## The Penon and the western Mediterranean
The Spanish still held the island fortress of the Penon, about three hundred metres off Algiers, poorly armed and provisioned. At the start of May 1529 Khayr al-Din attacked it and took it on the twenty-seventh. With the rubble he built a causeway joining the islet to the land, giving his ships a roadstead sheltered from the winds. A few days later an Algerian squadron seized nine Spanish ships that had come to relieve it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1208}

That summer he sent fifteen ships under Aydin Reis to the area of Oliva in Spain to help the Moriscos in revolt. The article reports that Aydin Reis carried as many Moriscos as he could and put a Spanish squadron to flight off Formentera, and that two galleys were sent to Istanbul to announce the victory to Suleyman. A messenger there told Khayr al-Din of the negotiations with the king of France. In 1531, news came that [[person/andrea-doria|Andrea Doria]] was sailing toward Cherchell with about forty ships, some of them French. Khayr al-Din planned to meet him with forty-eight ships but was delayed by shortage of supplies, and when he reached the Balearics, Doria had already attacked the fortress, been repelled, and fled to Cadiz, leaving, the Turkish chronicle says, 1,400 dead and 646 prisoners. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1208}

In 1532 he attacked the sultan of Tlemcen, who had the support of fourteen Spanish ships based at Oran, and raised his tribute to thirty thousand gold pieces. He then sent five ships to raid the Spanish coasts, taking fourteen of the fifteen ships that guarded them. Soon afterwards he put down a revolt of seven thousand captive Christians led by twenty Spanish nobles, for whom he had earlier refused a ransom of twenty thousand sequins. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1208}

## The summons of the sultan
The article says that in 1533 Khayr al-Din acted as an intermediary between [[person/francis-i-france|Francis I]] and Suleyman, receiving a French ambassador on his way to Aleppo. Invited by an investiture decree that Sinan Cavush brought him, he left Hasan Agha in charge of Algiers and set out for Istanbul with seventeen of his ablest captains and forty-four ships. On the way he set fire to a squadron of eighteen ships before [[place/messina|Messina]], and he reached the Ottoman capital at the end of the year. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1208} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1209}

In 940 AH, 1533 CE, according to al-Salabi, Sultan [[person/sulayman-al-qanuni|Suleyman the Magnificent]] summoned him with a letter carried by Sinan Cavush. He placed the letter over his head, gathered the scholars, the shaykhs and the notables, and read them the sultan's command. He then sailed with forty-four ships, and on the way he defeated a squadron of the Habsburg fleet near the Morea, until he reached [[place/istanbul|Istanbul]]. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p. 207-218}

## Kapudan Pasha
Shortly afterwards he went to Aleppo at the invitation of the grand vizier [[person/ibrahim-pasha-franji|Ibrahim Pasha]], who officially gave him the office of Kapudan Pasha with the title Djezair. The article dates this to the last third of Rajab 940, February 1534, and argues that the title means beglerbeg of the islands of the Mediterranean, not of Algiers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1209}

According to al-Salabi, the sultan clothed him in honour, entrusted him with the arsenal, and gave him the title of Kapudan Pasha, minister of the navy. He then sailed out of the Dardanelles with the new Ottoman fleet toward the southern coasts of Italy and Sicily, and recovered Coron and Lepanto. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p. 207-218}

## Tunis, 1534 and 1535
Some months later he set sail with eighty-four ships, eighteen of them his own, five belonging to corsairs and sixty-one newly built. His task was to take Tunis, whose throne Rashid, brother of Mawlay al-Hasan, had claimed. The article reports raids on Reggio, San Lucido, Cetraro, the Gulf of Naples and its islands, Sperlonga and Fondi, then the capture of Bizerte and of La Goletta on 16 August, and of Tunis on the eighteenth, which Mawlay al-Hasan had abandoned. Mawlay al-Hasan was afterwards beaten at Kairouan by an army of ten thousand. Khayr al-Din then calmed an Arab revolt in favour of Rashid, who had stayed in Istanbul. See [[event/conquest-of-tunis-1534|Khayr al-Din takes Tunis]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1209}

In April 1535 a new French ambassador, La Forest, visited Khayr al-Din at Tunis to prepare a joint Franco-Ottoman expedition against Corsica, Sardinia and Sicily. Charles V assembled an armada of three hundred ships and some thirty thousand men, retook La Goletta on 14 June and attacked Tunis on the twentieth with the support of Arabs and of over four thousand Christian captives who broke their chains and threw the Turkish ranks, nine thousand seven hundred strong, into disarray. Khayr al-Din was driven out of Tunis and made for Bone and then Algiers. See [[event/conquest-of-tunis-1535|Charles V's conquest of Tunis]]. He then sailed with thirty-two ships to the Balearics, where the article says he burnt and sacked Port Mahon and Palma, and he returned to Istanbul on 15 October. The next spring he went with thirty ships to the coasts of Calabria and sacked La Castella. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1209}

## The Aegean and Preveza
Until spring 1537 he was preparing the fleet that Suleyman had promised Francis I in support of his Italian expedition. The fleet of 280 ships gathered along the Albanian coast. While most of it under the third vizier Lutfi Pasha headed for Apulia, Khayr al-Din took sixty galleys to escort twenty ships of provisions from Egypt, then took part in the unsuccessful siege of [[place/corfu|Corfu]]. After the main fleet went home he seized Nio, Zea and Naxos, and in 1538 he attacked and occupied Skiathos, Skyros, Tinos, Andros, Karpathos and other Venetian islands in the Aegean. Crete was raided for a week, with more than fifteen thousand captives taken. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1209}

On 27 September the Ottoman fleet of 120 ships under Khayr al-Din met off Preveza a Christian fleet of 138 galleys and 70 ships assembled by the emperor, the pope and Venice under Andrea Doria. The engagement ended with the retreat of Doria's imperial forces after skirmishing between the vanguards. The article notes published evidence of secret negotiations between Doria and Khayr al-Din, which it takes to explain why the two admirals never provoked each other. Khayr al-Din, without hurrying, pursued the Christian fleet and forced it to retreat. The next year he went with 150 ships and retook Castelnuovo. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1209}

## His last years in France
His last appearance at sea was in 1543, when, after the agreements between Suleyman and Francis I, he was to co-operate with the French fleet in the western Mediterranean. After raiding the Italian coasts, staying three days at Reggio and halting at the Tiber, he joined the Duke of Enghien, commander of the French fleet, at [[place/marseille|Marseille]]. The article says the two fleets sacked Villefranche and besieged [[place/nice|Nice]] without success, and that Khayr al-Din blamed the shortcomings of the French fleet. The arrival of Doria's fleet and the army of the Marquis del Vasto made him retreat and spend the winter at Toulon, which its inhabitants evacuated. From there he sent twenty-five galleys to raid the Gulf of Lyons and the Spanish coasts. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1209}

Al-Salabi tells it differently in one point: with the Franco-Ottoman alliance he made Marseille his fleet's base, led the allied forces to drive the Duke of Savoy from Nice for the French king, then lay at Toulon, most of whose people had left at the king's order, until 1544. Europe's outcry rose; among the propaganda al-Salabi cites is the claim that Khayr al-Din tore out the church bells, so that in Toulon only the muezzin's call was heard. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p. 231-232}

## Return and death
The Peace of Crepy in 1544 ended the war between Charles V and Francis I, and the article says Khayr al-Din sailed back to Istanbul after pillaging the coasts of Tuscany and the kingdom of Naples. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1209}

In al-Salabi's account, after the [[event/treaty-crepy-1544|Treaty of Crepy]] of 18 September 1544 he evacuated Toulon, paused off Genoa, whose senate bought safety with costly gifts, took Elba and several coastal towns, and reached Istanbul with ships heavy with booty. He did not live long after. The author preserves his retort when Charles threatened him with his brothers' fate: that his soldiers' limbs would be scattered, his ships sunk, and his captains would return crowned with the shame of defeat. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p. 232-233}

The article says he spent the rest of his life in pious works, among them a madrasa and a mosque at Besiktas, of which no trace remains. He died on 4 July 1546 and was buried at Besiktas. The mausoleum, built by the architect Sinan, became the place where new kapudan pashas were invested, and the fleet would fire a salvo in greeting before leaving on an expedition. See [[building/tomb-of-hayreddin-barbarossa|the tomb of Hayreddin Barbarossa]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1209} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1210}

## Family and character
Little is known of his private life. He married twice: first at Algiers, to a local woman who bore him a son, [[person/hasan-ibn-khayr-al-din|Hasan]], who was several times beglerbeg of Algiers; and second, at the age of seventy-seven, to the eighteen-year-old daughter of the governor of Reggio, Flavia or Maria Gaetani. A daughter of his is buried near his tomb. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1210}

The article also records a letter to him from Pietro Aretino praising his generosity, height, valour and prudence, and Khayr al-Din's reply that Aretino had more the look of a captain than of a writer. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1210}

## Sources for his life
The article calls the most important contemporary source the Turkish Ghazawat of Khayr al-Din Pasha by Sayyid Murad, based on reports from Khayr al-Din and his companions and on what the author saw himself. It exists in prose and verse, is preserved in several manuscripts and is still unedited. Scholars have mostly used it through a French translation of an Arabic summary and through an Italian translation made from a Spanish version in 1578. See also [[work/gazavat-i-hayreddin-pasa|Gazavat-i Hayreddin Pasa]]. The article names further sources, among them Yetim Ali Celebi, Hajji Khalifa, Gomara, Sandoval and Haedo, and judges a 1968 English biography to be of little worth. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1210}
