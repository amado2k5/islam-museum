## Summary

Abu al-Qasim Khalaf ibn Abbas al-Zahrawi was an Andalusian physician and surgeon associated with [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]], author of *al-Tasrif*, completed around 390 AH/1000; its final book presents surgery with instrument drawings and explanations {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191} {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133}. His described devices include the [[invention/zahrawi-mishab|urethral-stone drill]], distinguished in the medical-history study from bladder-stone forceps {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1286–1289}.

## A life with sparse testimony

Esin Kahya's TDV biography cautions that al-Zahrawi's fame is not matched by abundant early biographical information; his name refers to Madinat al-Zahra near Cordoba, regarded by most researchers as his birthplace, but this record selects no exact birth year from divergent estimates {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191}. It discusses Ibn Hazm's praise of the book he had seen, and al-Humaydi's account of a learned and accomplished physician who died after 400 AH/1010 {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191}. These are early witnesses reported by the biography, not manuscripts independently examined for this record {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191}.

The biography attributes the 404 AH/1013 death date, advanced age and service to al-Mansur to a much later account by Leo Africanus; the earliest accounts give no court-service information {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191}. The common death date is therefore recorded cautiously, and the later report is not turned into a verified appointment under a particular caliph; this differs from the 2003 study's confident statement of service to Abd al-Rahman III {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191} {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 p. 1284}. TDV also discusses Ibn Sumayk and Ibn Wafid among witnesses to medical teaching, and Ibn Abi Usaybia's description of expertise in medicines and treatment {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191}.

## An encyclopaedia, not only surgery

The 30-book *al-Tasrif* begins with medical principles, diseases and symptoms; its middle sections concern medicines, foods, preparation, names and measurements, not operations alone {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133}. TDV describes it as the product of approximately fifty years of learning and experience, drawing on earlier medicine rather than isolated knowledge {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133} {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191}. It distinguishes translation of the pharmaceutical section later called *Liber servitoris* from the surgical translation, preserving separate transmission histories {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133}.

The surgical book covers cautery, cutting/puncture/wounds and fracture/dislocation care; TDV identifies more than 200 instrument illustrations, including cauteries, scissors, probes and saws {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133}. The biography discusses eye and dental work, fractures, urinary disease and other areas; these are historical practices, not museum recommendations for treatment {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191}.

## Instruments, experience and evidence limits

Abdel-Halim and colleagues translate surgical chapters 60–61 from a modern Arabic edition, distinguishing the author's text from historians' commentary {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1283–1284}. The drill passage reports personal experience and gives a material and functional description, not patient records or results for modern statistical evaluation {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1288–1289}. The browser therefore records the describer role, without turning the abstract's “founder of lithotripsy” language into priority for all surgery across time {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1283, 1287–1289}.

The paper explicitly calls him a Muslim surgeon: that is the identity basis, not the Ansari ancestry reported in some biographies {cite:alzahrawi-stones-abdelhalim-2003 pp. 1283–1284}. Sunni is the museum's explicit default frame for this context, not a quotation asserting personal legal-school allegiance; the biography read names no contrary tradition {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191}.

## Suture material and the experimental qualification

Surgical chapter87 describes [[invention/zahrawi-intestinal-suture|animal-intestinal filaments for internal suturing]]. Leclerc's translated page211 explicitly says that this approach and the neighbouring ant-closure account remain experimental {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861 ch.87 p.211}. Earlier discussion draws on Galen's suturing knowledge, so the contribution does not make al-Zahrawi the inventor of suturing from nothing {cite:zahrawi-leclerc-chirurgie-1861 ch.87 pp.207–208}. Majeed credits catgut among his surgical developments, but the editorial is not a worldwide-priority investigation or pedigree of every later medical thread {cite:majeed-islam-medicine-2005 pp.1486–1487}. The witness used here is an actual French translation with its page image, not independent Arabic collation or modern clinical validation.

## The book after its author

TDV discusses Gerard of Cremona's Latin surgical translation, later European editions and Guy de Chauliac's use; it also records Channing's Arabic-Latin edition in 1778, Leclerc's French translation in 1861 and Spink/Lewis's Arabic-English edition in 1973 {cite:tdv-zehravi pp. 189–191} {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133}. It separates Hebrew translations of portions and pharmaceutical transmission from this surgical route, and describes Sabuncuoglu's Turkish work presented in 1465 as an adaptation with observations and additions, not a literal copy {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133}. Translators and revisers thus remain in the story; circulation of the book does not establish unchanged circulation of every instrument {cite:tdv-tasrif-zehravi pp. 132–133}.
