## Overview
Ahmad 'Urabi (1841–1911) was an Egyptian officer of fellah (peasant) origin who led the nationalist movement known as the 'Urabi revolt. He became Minister of War and deputy head of the council of ministers in Mahmud Sami al-Barudi's nationalist ministry in February 1882, led the resistance to the British occupation until his decisive defeat at Tell el-Kebir on 13 September 1882, was tried and sentenced to death — the sentence commuted to lifelong exile — and lived in Ceylon from 1882 to 1901. He died in Cairo on 21 September 1911. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-ar} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-en} {cite:britannica-ahmad-urabi}

The English article's infobox styles him "Prime Minister of Egypt" from July to September 1882, but the body of that article itself hedges the description, and the Arabic article and Britannica give the sounder framing used above — Minister of War in a nationalist ministry headed by al-Barudi — which the museum follows. Britannica dates his birth to 1839, against the articles' and Wikidata's agreed 31 March 1841. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-en} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-ar} {cite:britannica-ahmad-urabi}

## Early life and education
Ahmad Muhammad 'Urabi — the Arabic article attaches him to a Husayni lineage — was born on 31 March 1841 at Hurriyat Razna near Zagazig in the Sharqia province, the son of the village 'umda (headman) and shaykh, of a comparatively well-off fellah family. He memorised the Qur'an as a child, was schooled by the village scribe Mikhayil Ghattas, and entered al-Azhar at the age of eight in 1849, remaining there for some years. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-ar} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-en} {cite:britannica-ahmad-urabi}

He was conscripted into the Egyptian army and rose with rare speed, reaching the rank of lieutenant colonel by about the age of twenty. He served in a support role in the Egyptian–Ethiopian War of 1874–76 and returned from it — in Wilfrid Scawen Blunt's account — incensed at its mismanagement and at what Egyptian soldiers had suffered in it. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-en}

## The nationalist movement and the War Ministry
'Urabi led, with his fellow officers, the protest against the privileges of Turkish and Circassian officers and against Khedive Tewfik's law barring peasants from holding officers' rank; the law was repealed. In 1879 he co-founded the Egyptian Nationalist Party, and his followers knew him as "El Wahid" ("the Only One"). His movement demanded a constitution, parliamentary rights over the budget, and an end to the Anglo-French financial control (the Caisse de la Dette) over Egypt. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-en} {cite:britannica-ahmad-urabi}

On 4 February 1882 he entered government as Minister of War and deputy head of the council in Mahmud Sami al-Barudi's nationalist ministry, and the 'Urabi movement found itself in power, facing the Khedive and the foreign control together. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-ar} {cite:britannica-ahmad-urabi}

## The revolt of 1882 and the occupation
On 11–12 June 1882 bloody riots broke out in Alexandria, and in July the British fleet under Admiral Seymour bombarded the city after an ultimatum to dismantle its fortifications. 'Urabi organised the resistance, and the movement proclaimed Khedive Tewfik a traitor — as Britannica puts it — after he took refuge with the British. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-en} {cite:britannica-ahmad-urabi}

The Egyptian army checked the British at Kafr el-Dawwar; the engagement at Kassassin followed on 28 August 1882 (the "Moonlight Charge"); then General Wolseley won his decisive victory at Tell el-Kebir on 13 September 1882, and Cairo surrendered to the occupation. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-en}

## Trial and exile in Ceylon
'Urabi was tried on 3 December 1882, defended by Broadley and Richard Eve; the defence — as summarised in Elizabeth Thompson's account carried in the article — maintained that his action had been lawful and loyal to Egypt. He pleaded guilty under the Dufferin understanding; the death sentence was commuted to lifelong exile, and he left Egypt on 28 December 1882 for Ceylon. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-en}

In exile he became a patron of Muslim education in Ceylon: Hameed Al Husseinie College was founded through his efforts on 15 November 1884, and Zahira College in Colombo on 22 August 1892 under his patronage; his house in Kandy (Halloluwa Road) is today the Orabi Pasha Cultural Centre. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-en}

Pardoned by Khedive Abbas II, he returned to Egypt on 1 October 1901 and lived in Cairo until his death on 21 September 1911. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-en} {cite:britannica-ahmad-urabi}

## Positions and criticism
This section reports the assessments as the sources attribute them; the museum issues no verdict of its own. Britannica records that he died "an unpopular figure in relative obscurity", and that his image was revitalised in the 1950s under Gamal Abdel Nasser, who presented the revolt of 1882 as a nationalist precursor of 1952; the English article notes that Muhammad Naguib in 1952 consciously echoed 'Urabi's words to Tewfik — that Egyptians were "no longer inheritable". {cite:britannica-ahmad-urabi} {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-en}

In contemporary British writing sympathetic to him — Blunt's account among it, as carried in the English article — 'Urabi appears as a popular leader wronged by Turco-Circassian privilege and by the mismanagement of the Ethiopian war; in hostile imperial writing he figures as a military mutineer whose demagogy paved the way for occupation. The sources used here agree on the centrality of his movement's constitutional demands: a constitution, parliamentary rights over the budget, and an end to foreign financial control. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-en} {cite:britannica-ahmad-urabi}

As to his religion, no source names a school for him: the Arabic article's infobox gives his religion as Islam, generically; the English article and Britannica name none; Wikidata records Islam without specification. He is therefore left unclassified in the data record. {cite:wikipedia-ahmad-urabi-ar} {cite:britannica-ahmad-urabi} {cite:wikidata-ahmad-urabi}
