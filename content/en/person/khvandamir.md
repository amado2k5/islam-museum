## Summary
Ghiyath al-Din Muhammad, known as Khvandamir (1475–1535), was a Persian historian of Herat whose universal history, the Habib al-siyar, was regarded by both the Safavids and the Mughals as their first official court account. {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-en} He was the maternal grandson of Mirkhvand, author of the Rawzat as-safa, was tutored by him, and completed the seventh volume and epilogue that his grandfather left unfinished at his death in 1498. {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-en} His own first works were written under the patronage of Ali-Shir Nava'i, to whom he dedicated the Ma'athir al-muluk and the Khulasat al-akhbar, and after Nava'i's death a laudatory biography, the Makarim al-akhlaq. {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-en}

## Under three empires
Khvandamir served Badi' al-Zaman Mirza as secretary and diplomat, composing the Dastur al-vuzara, a biographical list of viziers, and after 1506 was installed as one of the two sadrs of the Timurid kingdom. {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-en} He kept his post under the Uzbek conqueror Muhammad Shaybani, but the Safavid conquest of Herat in 1510 left him unemployed — most likely, the English article explains on the authority of Bockholt's Encyclopaedia of Islam THREE entry and Newman's Safavid Iran, for religio-political reasons, since Khvandamir was a Sunni Muslim and the Safavids were zealous Shia Muslims. {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-en} In 1521 he began the Habib al-siyar in Herat under new patrons, finishing a first version in 1524 and an extended second in 1525. {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-en} Accepting Babur's invitation, he reached India in 1528 — the Persian article dates the journey to 935 AH — wrote a third version of the history at the Mughal court, and composed the Qanun-i Humayuni on the reign of Babur's son Humayun. {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-en} {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-fa}

## Death and the attestation of his branch
Khvandamir died at Delhi in 1535 or 1536 by the English article's reckoning; the Persian article gives 942 or 943 AH and the Arabic 942 AH with 1534 in its infobox, so the year is recorded as debated. {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-en} {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-fa} {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-ar} He was buried, by his own will, near the shrine of Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi. {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-en} {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-fa} His two sons divided between the two worlds he had served: Amir Mahmud stayed in Iran and wrote a history of the first Safavid half-century, while Abdallah Khan became an official under Akbar. {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-en} On his faith, exactly one statement exists in the sources read: the Sunni identification quoted above, a single scholarly family's attestation of the branch that names no legal school. Neither the Persian nor the Arabic article names a religion for him, and Wikidata carries none. {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-fa} {cite:wikipedia-khvandamir-ar} {cite:wikidata-khvandamir} The museum therefore preserves the attestation in his record, flagged, without adopting a school for him.
