## Overview
Abd Allah al-Ma'mun ibn [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]] was the seventh Abbasid caliph. The Encyclopaedia of Islam places his birth on 15 Rabi' I 170 AH, 14 September 786, on what it calls the night of the three caliphs, and his death near [[place/tarsus|Tarsus]] in Rajab 218 AH, August 833. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 351} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 358} The article presents him as a ruler who first governed the east as heir, won a civil war against his brother, and then tried to hold the empire together through reconciliation with the Alids, a close relationship with the [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazila]], support for translation from Greek and a campaign of examination of the scholars known as the [[event/mihna-of-the-quran|mihna]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 356} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 358}

## Birth, family and education
He was the eldest of the eleven sons of al-Rashid. His mother, Marajil, a concubine from Badhghis, died soon after his birth, and he was brought up by [[person/zubayda-bint-jafar|Zubayda]], a granddaughter of al-Mansur, wife of al-Rashid and mother of Muhammad, the future [[person/al-amin|al-Amin]], who was born in Shawwal 170 AH. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 351} He received a classical education in Arabic with [[person/al-kisai|al-Kisa'i]] as tutor, in adab, music and poetry, and in the religious sciences: hadith, which he also transmitted, and fiqh, where he is said to have excelled in Hanafi jurisprudence. The article describes his love of knowledge as making him the most intellectual caliph of the Abbasid family and judges that this accounts for the way his caliphate developed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 351}

## Heir in Khurasan
In 177 AH, 794 CE, al-Rashid named al-Amin as first successor. Al-Ma'mun was declared second heir in 183 AH, 799 CE, once he reached puberty, under the guardianship of [[person/jafar-ibn-yahya-al-barmaki|Ja'far ibn Yahya al-Barmaki]], and a third heir, al-Qasim, was added. The arrangement was proclaimed at [[place/mecca|Mecca]] during the pilgrimage of 186 AH, 802 CE, and it entrusted greater [[place/khurasan|Khurasan]] to al-Ma'mun. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 351} On the night of 1 Safar 187 AH, 29 January 803 CE, al-Rashid had Ja'far executed and arrested [[person/al-fadl-ibn-yahya-al-barmaki|al-Fadl ibn Yahya]] and his brothers, ending the patronage of the [[group/barmakids|Barmakids]]. Al-Ma'mun's guardian then became [[person/al-fadl-ibn-sahl|al-Fadl ibn Sahl]], the son of a Zoroastrian from a village near Kufa, who entered Barmakid service and converted to Islam in 190 AH. At eighteen al-Ma'mun married his cousin Umm Isa, a daughter of al-Hadi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 351}

In 192 AH al-Rashid went east to deal with the revolt of Rafi' ibn Layth and took al-Ma'mun and al-Fadl ibn Sahl with him. He died at [[place/tus|Tus]] on 3 Jumada II 193 AH, 24 March 809 CE, after al-Ma'mun had gone ahead to Marw with part of the army. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 351} The new caliph al-Amin ordered the army and the treasury back to Baghdad. According to the article, al-Ma'mun answered by devolving responsibility on the Sahlids and by pacifying the eastern provinces: local chieftains were recognised, the army's pay was raised, the land tax was reduced by a quarter, and he regularly presided over the court of grievances. The article adds that he consulted the jurists and theologians, among them Mu'tazila, who were being persecuted in Iraq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 351}

## Rupture with al-Amin
The article describes a rivalry between two circles of supporters. Around al-Amin stood the caliph's mawali and the sons of the Khurasani army settled in Iraq, who feared the loss of their privileges, while the eastern provinces backed the heir who defended their autonomy. The article rejects the common explanation by the origin of the two mothers, one Arab and one Iranian, and calls the identification of the east with Iran and the west with the Arab lands a historical misunderstanding. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 352}

The conflict began in 194 AH, 810 CE, when al-Amin added his young son Musa to the list of heirs. Al-Ma'mun refused to return to Baghdad, and al-Amin then demanded surplus revenue, fiscal agents and a postal officer at Marw. In 195 AH the caliph's name was removed from the coinage and the tiraz of Khurasan. Al-Amin proclaimed Musa first heir, which the article calls a flagrant violation of the Meccan documents, and al-Ma'mun replied by taking the title of Imam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 352} The article remarks that this title carries a more religious authority than that of Commander of the Faithful. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 352}

## The civil war
In Jumada II 195 AH, March 811 CE, Ali ibn Isa ibn Mahan was sent as governor of Jibal to restore the caliph's authority over Khurasan. Facing his army near [[place/rayy|Rayy]], with forty thousand men against four to five thousand according to the article, [[person/tahir-ibn-al-husayn|Tahir ibn al-Husayn]] killed him on 7 Shawwal 195 AH, 3 July 811 CE, and earned the title Dhu al-Yaminayn. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 353} Tahir then took Jibal. In Rajab 196 AH, March 812 CE, a commander in Baghdad arrested al-Amin and proclaimed al-Ma'mun, but a counter-coup restored al-Amin. On the same date al-Ma'mun was officially proclaimed caliph at Marw, and al-Fadl ibn Sahl received the title Dhu al-Riyasatayn. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 353}

Tahir advanced through Khuzistan and Iraq, and a second army under [[person/harthama-ibn-ayan|Harthama ibn A'yan]] invested [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] from the east in Dhu al-Hijja 196 AH, August 812 CE. The article says the [[event/siege-of-baghdad-198|siege]] lasted thirteen months and was prolonged by the resistance of the 'ayyarun, men of humble origin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 353} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 354} The western provinces recognised al-Ma'mun in 197 AH. Tahir captured al-Amin and had him executed on the night of 24 to 25 Muharram 198 AH, 24 to 25 September 813 CE. The article notes that this was the first time an Abbasid caliph had been killed in this way by rebel soldiers, in contrast to the more respectful attitude of Harthama. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 354}

## Alid revolts and the designation of Ali al-Rida
Al-Hasan ibn Sahl was given the government of Iraq, but his treatment of the defeated abna' revived resistance. The article records Zaydi Alid risings in 199 and 200 AH, at Kufa under Ibn Tabataba and Abu al-Saraya, at Basra, at Mecca and in Yemen. Harthama put down the revolt at Kufa, and Abu al-Saraya was executed in Rabi' I 200 AH, October 815 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 354}

The article reads these outbursts as the reason for a spectacular reconciliation between Alids and Abbasids, a return to the principles of the first Hashimite mission. Al-Ma'mun spared the Alid rebels and, going further than his grandfather al-Mahdi, chose as his successor [[person/ali-al-rida|Ali ibn Musa al-Kazim]], with the title al-Rida min Al Muhammad. He proclaimed him on 2 Ramadan 201 AH, 24 March 817 CE, and replaced the Abbasid black with green. The article states that this ran counter to the policy of the previous century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 354} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 355} Harthama travelled to Marw to warn the caliph about Iraq, and al-Fadl ibn Sahl, according to the article, turned al-Ma'mun against him and had him executed in Dhu al-Qa'da 200 AH, June 816 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 354} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 355}

## Ibrahim ibn al-Mahdi and the return to Baghdad
When the news reached Iraq, the Abbasids reacted against the loss of what they saw as their rights, and the sons of al-Mahdi led the opposition. [[person/ibrahim-ibn-al-mahdi|Ibrahim ibn al-Mahdi]] accepted the caliphate on 28 Dhu al-Hijja 201 AH, 17 July 817 CE, after his half-brother al-Mansur refused it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 355} According to the article, al-Fadl ibn Sahl hid the gravity of the situation until Ali al-Rida told al-Ma'mun that a caliph had been proclaimed at Baghdad. Al-Ma'mun judged that only agreement among the social groups of the empire could save the regime. He left Marw on 10 Rajab 202 AH, 22 January 818 CE. At Sarakhs, on 2 Sha'ban 202 AH, 13 February 818 CE, al-Fadl ibn Sahl was killed, the article says at al-Ma'mun's instigation, while the other Sahlids were spared and al-Hasan ibn Sahl became vizier. Al-Ma'mun was betrothed to Buran, al-Hasan's daughter, and the marriage was celebrated in 210 AH. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 355}

Ali al-Rida died at Sanabad near Tus on 29 Safar 203 AH, 5 September 818 CE. The article notes that the Shi'is were convinced al-Ma'mun had had him poisoned, and that the place came to be called [[place/mashhad|Mashhad]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 355} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 356} Ibrahim ibn al-Mahdi withdrew on 16 Muharram 204 AH, 13 July 819 CE, and al-Ma'mun made a triumphal entry into Baghdad on 17 Safar 204 AH. A month later he took up black again, while keeping the title of Imam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 356}

## Government after his return
The article says that from then on the effort to control the empire and guide the community was al-Ma'mun's own responsibility, with the advice of the Mu'tazila and of the judge [[person/ahmad-ibn-abi-duad|Ahmad ibn Abi Duad]]. The secretaries of the earlier administration were disbanded and then recalled, [[person/ahmad-ibn-abi-khalid|Ahmad ibn Abi Khalid]] served as his personal secretary until his death in 211 AH, and former adversaries were pardoned: [[person/fadl-ibn-al-rabi|al-Fadl ibn al-Rabi']], and in 210 AH Ibrahim ibn al-Mahdi. Only [[person/ibn-aisha-al-abbasi|Ibn A'isha]] is named as executed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 356}

The article describes the [[polity/tahirid-dynasty|Tahirid]] governorship of Khurasan, established in 205 AH, as a lasting compromise in line with the Meccan documents: Tahir ruled greater Khurasan, a cousin commanded the police in Baghdad, and Tahir's son [[person/abd-allah-ibn-tahir|Abd Allah]] extended the caliph's authority over autonomous provinces with the central army. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 356}

## Translation, learning and the Mu'tazila
The article credits al-Ma'mun with encouraging translation into Arabic from Greek and Syriac of philosophy, astronomy and mathematics, and dates the founding of the [[building/bayt-al-hikma|Bayt al-Hikma]] at Baghdad to 217 AH, 832 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 356} Scholars of every persuasion, Muslims, Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians and Sabians, from many regions contributed to the advance of Arab science. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 357} He promoted debates between representatives of different religions and, above all, between scholars of different tendencies about the interpretation of Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 357}

The article places al-Ma'mun's adoption of Mu'tazili positions in a political setting. In its reading, Mu'tazili thought, developed by [[person/abu-al-hudhayl-al-allaf|Abu al-Hudhayl al-Allaf]] and his nephew [[person/al-nazzam|al-Nazzam]], among others, offered a compromise that could conciliate the Alids, and the Imam took from it the doctrine of the created Qur'an, which he could use to correct the established order through his own knowledge. The same section notes that Ali was proclaimed the best of the Companions after the Prophet in 211 AH, which the article presents as a bid for Shi'i support, and that it places the adoption of Mu'tazilism as the official doctrine in 212 AH. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 356} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 357} These are the article's interpretations, and the traditionists and most jurists, who held to the Qur'an and the Sunna as sources of law, are described as distrusting the borrowing from foreign cultures. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 357}

## Campaigns against Byzantium
The first campaign began in 215 AH, 830 CE, under al-Ma'mun's personal command, and took fortresses in Cappadocia. On the way he encountered the Sabaeans of [[place/harran|Harran]], and a fatwa is reported to have recognised an accord between Islam and this community. The Byzantine emperor Theophilus then attacked the fortresses of al-Massisa and Tarsus, and a second campaign followed in 216 AH, 831 CE, in which al-Ma'mun rejected an offer of a prisoner exchange and truce. He then went to Egypt, put down the revolt and introduced a fiscal reform, in which the kabala system replaced the earlier method of collection. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 358} In 217 AH, 832 CE, he returned to the Byzantine front, and the fortress of Lu'lu'a capitulated after a siege. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 358}

## The mihna and his death
In Rabi' I 218 AH, April 833 CE, four months before his death, al-Ma'mun instituted the [[event/mihna-of-the-quran|mihna]], while preparing a campaign against [[place/amorium|Amorium]]. The article says the aim was that Mu'tazilism be adopted by all the ulama. It describes a contest for influence over the common people between the Imam, who claimed the right to interpret the law and to change established rules in the name of social justice, and the ulama and fukaha, traditionalists who refused this claim and believed the changes would serve the elite. It adds that most of the ulama conceded, from loyalty or fear, while a minority refused and formed the core of a resistance supported by the urban poor of Baghdad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 358} This is the article's account, and the museum records the positions of the two sides without adjudicating between them.

Shortly before his death he chose his brother al-Mu'tasim as successor rather than his son al-Abbas, and his testament, in the article's words, recommended pursuing his politico-religious work, which was incomplete when he died near Tarsus in Rajab 218 AH. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 358} The article compares him with the Umayyad Abd al-Malik, as rulers who restored unity after long civil wars, and notes that al-Mu'tasim continued the mihna, kept the Mu'tazili advisers, and moved the capital to [[place/samarra|Samarra]] from 221 AH, while [[person/al-kindi|al-Kindi]], tutor of al-Mu'tasim's sons, worked on bringing Neo-Platonism into Mu'tazili theology. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 358}

## The Arabic chronicles in brief
Abd Allah al-Ma'mun ibn Harun al-Rashid, an Abbasid caliph. He was given allegiance in Baghdad after the deposition of his brother al-Amin in 196 AH, and died in 218 AH. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.8 p.428} {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.8 p.646} Al-Tabari mentions the Mihna of the Qur'an in his reign. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.8 p.631}

## His upbringing and affair
Al-Suyuti says that he was born on the night of the death of his uncle al-Hadi and the allegiance of his father, in 170 AH. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p. 456} Al-Mas'udi says that al-Rashid said of him that he recognised in him the firmness of al-Mansur and the asceticism of al-Mahdi, and that his mother was a Badhghisi woman called Maraji. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 337-340}

Al-Mas'udi says that at the start of his affair, when al-Fadl ibn Sahl had the upper hand over him, he leaned toward considering astrological judgements. When he came to Iraq he gave that up, sat with the theologians, favoured the disputants, and kept the jurists and men of letters in his session, paying them their stipends. He describes him as the most forgiving of people and the most generous with money. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 672}

## His caliphate
Al-Suyuti says that he marched in 204 AH and entered Baghdad in Safar, and that in 218 AH he tested the people with the doctrine that the Qur'an was created. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p. 487-489} He says that among the caliphs only [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman ibn Affan]] and al-Ma'mun had memorised the Qur'an. {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p. 496-497}

Al-Mas'udi says that he died at al-Badidun, at the spring of al-Qushayra, in 218 AH at the age of forty-nine, and was buried in Tarsus. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 399-402}

## Measurement of the earth in his days
Al-Farghani states that one degree of the earth's circle was estimated at fifty-six and two-thirds miles, the mile being four thousand cubits of the black cubit, and that this measurement was tested in the days of al-Ma'mun, with a group of scholars agreeing on it. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 100} The book gives neither the date nor the place of the measurement, so none is added here.

## The obliquity of the ecliptic in his days
Al-Farghani states that the measurement al-Ma'mun ordered to be tested, on which many scholars agreed, set the greatest obliquity of the ecliptic at twenty-three degrees and thirty-five minutes, against the value of Ptolemy. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 112} {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 150} In the eighth chapter he again states that one degree by al-Ma'mun's measurement is fifty-six and two-thirds miles. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 163}
Golius states that al-Ma'mun returned from a campaign in Byzantine lands and died near Tarsus on 19 Rajab 216 AH, and was buried there, and that a poet lamented him in verses that mention his interest in the science of the stars. {cite:farghani-elements-astronomy p. 538} This date differs from the 218 AH given elsewhere on this page.

## For Sprouts
Al-Ma'mun was an Abbasid caliph of the ninth century, son of Harun al-Rashid. He loved learning and books, and translation flourished at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad in his reign — which also saw the Mihna that tested the scholars. He ruled about twenty years and died in 833 CE.

## For Explorers
Abd Allah al-Ma'mun ibn Harun al-Rashid, seventh Abbasid caliph (198–218 AH / 813–833 CE), took power after his brother al-Amin was deposed and died in the brothers' civil war; he ruled from Merv before returning to Baghdad. His reign is famous for the translation movement at the House of Wisdom and for patronage of philosophy, astronomy and geography, including the celebrated measurement of the earth. On its other side was the Mihna: he imposed the Mu'tazili doctrine of the created Qur'an as a test on judges and scholars, and Ahmad ibn Hanbal was jailed for refusing — an episode Sunni memory reads as state intrusion into belief. Al-Ma'mun died at Tarsus in 218 AH; his brother al-Mu'tasim succeeded him.

## For Scholars
Abd Allah al-Ma'mun ibn Harun al-Rashid (198–218 AH / 813–833 CE), seventh Abbasid caliph, was acclaimed after the deposition of his brother al-Amin in the civil war that besieged Baghdad; he stayed in Khurasan for years before returning to Baghdad in 204 AH. His reign is bound to the height of the translation movement: the House of Wisdom became the centre of rendering Greek, Persian and Sanskrit works, and the caliph patronized scientific projects like the measurement of the earth's circumference at Sinjar and the verification of astronomical tables, and is remembered loving debate and the sessions of the mutakallimun. On the same coin of state power was the Mihna: in 218 AH, shortly before his death, al-Ma'mun imposed on the judges the Mu'tazili declaration of the created Qur'an and tested the scholars on it; Ahmad ibn Hanbal resisted and was punished under al-Mu'tasim, who continued the policy — in Sunni memory the emblem of the state's quarrel with the fuqaha. Al-Ma'mun died on campaign at Tarsus in 218 AH and his brother Abu Ishaq al-Mu'tasim succeeded; his era stands in history as both the height of early Abbasid civilization and its doctrinal crisis.
