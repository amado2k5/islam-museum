## Overview
Mansa Musa, one of the kings of the [[polity/mali-empire|Mali empire]], the most famous of them according to Ibn Khaldun. He ruled for about a quarter of a century and was succeeded by [[person/mari-jata|Mari Jata]]. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose article on him this page now follows, gives his reign as 712 to 738 AH, that is 1312 to 1337, and calls him king, mansa, of Mali. It describes him as apparently the grandson of Abu Bakr, called Manding Bori, who was the brother of Mari Jata, the legendary hero credited with founding Mali as a powerful empire. The article says he reigned at the peak of Mali's prosperity and that the Arabic sources remember him as a pious and virtuous ruler. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 443}

## How he came to power
The article reports a story told by Mansa Musa himself to an informant of the Mamluk writer [[person/ibn-fadl-allah-al-umari|al-Umari]], a man named Ibn Amir Hajib who often kept his company in Egypt. According to it, Musa came to power because his predecessor, Muhammad, from another branch of the same family, appointed him deputy before setting out on a sea expedition to find the limits of the Atlantic Ocean. That predecessor never came back. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 443}

## The pilgrimage
He made the pilgrimage in 724 AH with a procession whose attendants numbered more than twelve thousand, clothed in brocade and Yemeni silk. The news of his generosity and the breadth of his realm in the Maghrib, Egypt and the East spread after that. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar}

He exchanged gifts with the Marinid sultan Abu al-Hasan, and among what he sent was a giraffe, which the people of the Maghrib talked about for a long time. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam dates the pilgrimage to Mecca to 1324 and says he left a deputy to rule in his absence. It says the journey made a profound impression on the Egyptians and was recorded by Arab writers. According to the seventeenth-century Timbuktu chronicles, the Tarikh al-Sudan and the Tarikh al-Fattash, whose authors relied heavily on oral sources, he was accompanied by an entourage of thousands, including his favourite wife, Inari Konte. He travelled by way of [[place/timbuktu|Timbuktu]], where he had one of several mosques built along his route. In [[place/cairo|Cairo]] he is said to have flooded the market with so much gold that its value fell throughout Egypt. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 443}

Other articles of the same work give slightly different years. The article on Gao places his return from the pilgrimage in 724 AH, 1324, while the article on the Hausa dates the journey to 725 AH, 1325. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1019} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 295}

The Mali article presents royal pilgrimage as an older habit of West African rulers. It names earlier kings, Uli, who passed through Cairo, and Sakura, whose pilgrimage Ibn Khaldun recorded, and calls Musa the most famous of all royal pilgrims. It adds that the visits of Mali's kings to North Africa, Egypt and [[place/mecca|Mecca]] made the kingdom known, often under the name Takrur in Egyptian chronicles, as a Muslim realm rich in gold, and that religious, cultural and commercial ties between Egypt and Mali grew closer. At home, the baraka ascribed to pilgrims was respected by Muslims and non-Muslims alike and added to the king's authority. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 278}

## Builders, scholars and the poet al-Sahili
On the pilgrimage Musa met the Andalusian poet and architect Abu Ishaq Ibrahim al-Sahili. According to a friend of Ibn Khaldun whom the article cites, al-Sahili went back with him to Mali and built for the mansa an elaborately domed building. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 443} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 444} The Mali article adds that al-Sahili died in Timbuktu in 1346, and takes his settling there as a sign that the town was already an intellectual centre of some importance. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 278}

Four sharifs of Quraysh also travelled with Musa and settled in Mali with their families. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 444}

The article on [[place/gao|Gao]] mentions a general of the emperor, Sagamandia, who at the time of the return from the pilgrimage had a mosque with a mihrab built there, perhaps on the plans of al-Sahili. The same article records that a vassal, Dia Assibai, had to give up his two sons as hostages, and that one of them, Ali Kolen, escaped and later restored the Songhai state. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1019}

## Policy after his return
The Mali article says Musa followed a more vigorous Islamic policy after coming back from the hajj. He built new mosques and sent local scholars to study in [[place/fes|Fes]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 278}

The biography adds that he kept up a diplomacy aimed at advancing Mali's interests in the wider Islamic world, writing to the sultan at Cairo and exchanging embassies and gifts with the Marinid ruler of the Maghrib, [[person/abu-al-hasan-al-marini|Abu al-Hasan]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 444} The Mali article dates the start of that exchange of ambassadors and gifts to 737 AH, 1337, and says it continued under their successors until 762 AH, 1360 to 1361. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 278}

The Mali article adds that the traveller [[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]] visited Mali in 753 to 754 AH, 1352 to 1353, in the reign of Sulayman, Musa's brother, and that his account shows how much older custom survived beneath a veneer of Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 278}

## The Hausa lands
An article on the Hausa notes that Mandingo missionaries reached Kano in the fourteenth century, in the reign of Yaji, and had a mosque built. The author suggests, with some caution, that this may be an aspect of the Islamic expansion that took place under Mansa Musa, and that the Mandingoes may have been merchants as well as missionaries, seeking Sudanese gold. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 295}

## The Fajigi legend
The article gives space to the way people in the Sudan remembered the pilgrimage. It describes an oral tradition whose hero is Makanta Jigi, or Fajigi, called the Father of hope who went to Mecca. In the basic story, the mansa makes the journey because of a regrettable incident involving his mother, Gongo, also called Kankan, which may have caused her death. In Mecca he obtains the most important of the spiritually powerful altars used in traditional Manding ritual, including those of the Komo society. On the way home he gives some of them out, with potions and amulets, to people who helped him. Crossing the rivers of Mali in a magic canoe, some of the cargo falls into the water and turns into creatures such as fish and scorpions. The canoe itself sinks in a lake or river and is still treated as a powerful altar that receives offerings. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 444}

The author remarks on the paradox of this account, in which the mansa most famous for devotion to Islam is the one who supplied his people with the equipment of their ancestral religion. From the traditional non-Muslim point of view, the article says, Musa becomes the source of a narrative that let later generations claim their own religious features were rooted in the same soil as the new faith. The author reads the legend as evidence of an accommodation between indigenous practice and Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 444}

## His death
Ibn Khaldun says that he died twenty-five years after he became king. The year of death is probably between 737 and 739 AH, and needs an additional source.

The Encyclopaedia of Islam, giving the end of the reign as 738 AH, 1337, mentions a claim by al-Umari. According to it, Musa came home from the pilgrimage intending to hand his sovereignty to his son and go back to live near the sanctuary at Mecca, but died before he could do so. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 6 p. 444}
