## Overview
Ahmad III was the twenty-third Ottoman sultan, a son of [[person/mehmed-iv-uthmani|Mehmed IV]]. He was born in 1084/1673 and reigned for twenty-seven years, from 1115/1703 to 1143/1730. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, whose account this page follows, shows a reign in two phases: a first half of unstable government, court intrigue and wars with Russia, Venice and Austria, and a second half, after 1130/1718, dominated by the grand vizier [[person/nevshehirli-damat-ibrahim-pasha|Ibrahim Pasha]] and marked by pleasure, the arts and European models, the period called the Age of Tulips. It ended in a rising in Istanbul and the sultan's abdication, and he died in retirement in 1149/1736. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 290} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 292}

## Accession and the first years
Ahmad came to the throne on 10 Rabi' II 1115, which is 21 August 1703, when his brother [[person/mustafa-ii-uthmani|Mustafa II]] abdicated after a rising of the [[group/janissaries|Janissaries]]. The new sultan soon removed its leaders and made [[place/istanbul|Istanbul]] the court's usual residence again. For several years many people known or suspected to have taken part were dismissed, banished or executed, which the article says harmed the efficiency of government. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 290}

His wish to curb the soldiery also showed when he dismissed seven hundred bostandjis from palace service and replaced them with devshirme recruits, the last use of that levy, and later when he sharply reduced the Janissary establishment. The article adds that in the first half of his reign he lived in dread of revolutionaries. For three years he could not find a capable grand vizier, and government steadied only when [[person/corlulu-ali-pasha|Corlulu Ali Pasha]] was appointed in Muharram 1118, May 1706. For the next eight or nine years a palace circle strongly influenced him: the Valide Sultan, the chief of the black eunuchs, and his favourite, later known as [[person/silahdar-damat-ali-pasha|Silahdar Damad Ali Pasha]]. It distrusted grand viziers from outside the palace service. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 290}

## Charles XII and the war with Russia
Little of note happened until July 1709, when Charles XII of Sweden, beaten by Tsar Peter the Great at Poltava, took refuge at [[place/bender|Bender]] on the Dniester, in Ottoman territory. Until then the Porte had not used the preoccupation of Austria and the western powers with the War of the Spanish Succession to recover lands lost at Carlowitz in 1699, nor Russia's preoccupation with the Great Northern War to undo the tsar's Black Sea concessions of 1700. Charles pressed the sultan to fight Peter, and the ambassadors of Louis XIV and of Venice urged the same. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 290} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 291}

In 1710 Corlulu Ali Pasha, who had just renewed the Russian treaty, was dismissed. His successor Koprulu Numan Pasha proved too independent for the palace circle and fell two months later. The appointment in September of [[person/baltaci-mehmed-pasha|Baltaci Mehmed Pasha]] was followed on 20 November by a declaration of war. The main Ottoman complaints were Peter's warships at Azov, his fortresses along the frontier and his interference with the Tatars. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 291}

The article says Peter overran most of Moldavia through the treachery of its prince Demetrius Cantemir, but ran short of food, was surprised by the main Ottoman army as he marched south along the Pruth, and was surrounded. By the resulting [[event/treaty-pruth-1123|treaty of the Pruth]] he agreed to return Azov, raze the other fortresses, leave the Tatars and Poland alone, keep no ambassador at Istanbul and stop intriguing with the sultan's Orthodox subjects. Since the grand vizier could have forced almost any concession, he came under suspicion of having been bribed into leniency and was dismissed about three months later, partly through intrigues by Charles, who was disappointed by the terms. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 291}

Charles spent most of the next three years urging a renewal of war, and war on Russia was declared three more times, in December 1711, November 1712 and April 1713, each time averted by Russian concessions. A final agreement signed at Adrianople in June 1713, to last twenty-five years, confirmed the treaty of the Pruth. Charles still refused to leave without money and troops, and in spring 1714 he was removed by force from Bender to Demotika and then to a palace near Adrianople. In the autumn he went home with his Swedish troops through Wallachia, Transylvania and Hungary. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 291}

## The Morea and the war with Austria
On 27 April 1713 Silahdar Ali Pasha, the sultan's favourite and son-in-law, became grand vizier. With peace on the Russian side, the Porte could try to regain what Venice had taken at Carlowitz. The article says Venetian rule in the Morea was very unpopular and its Orthodox inhabitants had repeatedly appealed to the Porte. A pretext came in 1714, when [[polity/republic-of-venice|Venice]] refused to hand over the Vladika and other Montenegrins who had fled to its territory after a Russian-instigated rebellion. War was declared on 9 December 1714. In June and July 1715 an army under Silahdar Ali himself, working with the fleet, retook the whole province with little serious fighting, and the fleet took Tenos, Aegina, Cerigo and Santa Maura and reduced Suda and Spinalonga on Crete. This is the [[event/ottoman-venetian-war-1714|Ottoman-Venetian war]] of 1714. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 291}

The successes alarmed Austria. In April 1716 the Emperor Charles VI made a treaty of mutual assistance with Venice, and in June he forced a declaration of war by ultimatum. The Ottoman attack on Corfu failed, and in August Eugene of Savoy routed the main Ottoman army near Peterwardein, where Silahdar Ali was mortally wounded. Eugene took Temesvar and occupied the Banat and Little Wallachia, and in summer 1717 he besieged [[place/belgrade|Belgrade]] and on 16 August completely defeated a larger relieving force, as described under the [[event/battle-of-belgrade-1717|battle of Belgrade]]. The garrison surrendered three days later. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 291}

Peace was signed on 21 July 1718 at Passarowitz. Belgrade and its region, the Banat and Little Wallachia passed from the Porte to Austria, while Venice ceded the Morea, the Cretan ports, Tenos and the south-eastern districts of Herzegovina to the Porte, and received Cerigo and the strongholds it had captured in Albania and Dalmatia. A commercial treaty also gave Austrian and Venetian traders new advantages. See the [[event/treaty-passarowitz-1130|treaty of Passarowitz]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 291}

## The Age of Tulips
The grand vizier who made that peace was another favourite, Nevshehirli Ibrahim Pasha, who by marrying the sultan's thirteen-year-old daughter Fatima Sultan, the nominal wife of Silahdar Ali, also became a damad, a son-in-law of the dynasty. For the last twelve years of the reign he dominated the court. The article describes Ahmad as pleasure-loving and fond of the arts, able with Ibrahim, who shared his tastes, to indulge them and set new fashions. It links this to a growing interest of the powerful in the arts and learning after the devshirme was gradually abandoned in the seventeenth century, alongside declining military and administrative efficiency, and to the stronger place of the Greek community of the Phanar in metropolitan society. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 291}

The period is called lale devri, the Age of Tulips, after the flower whose cultivation became a craze. The article cites the poet Nadim's verse inviting people to laugh, play and enjoy the world as showing its secular spirit. Pavilions and gardens were built more often than mosques and mausoleums, to western designs. An ambassador sent to Louis XV was told to study French institutions and report which could suit Ottoman use, and in 1724 his son helped [[person/ibrahim-muteferrika|Ibrahim Muteferrika]] set up the first printing press in Istanbul. A French engineer officer was invited to plan army reform on western lines, and a French convert to Islam organised a fire service. The army reform came to nothing, but the Admiralty was reorganised and three-decker warships were built for the first time. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 291} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 292}

A society was founded to translate books, and five libraries were established in the capital, among them the sultan's own, with Nadim as curator. Porcelain works at Kutahya and Izmit were revived and a new one founded in Istanbul, the Byzantine walls were repaired from 1722 to 1724, and a dam brought water to the capital from springs near Belgrade. The article names as the best surviving monuments the mosque Ahmad built for his mother at Uskudar and his fountain outside the Imperial Gate of Topkapi Palace, for which he wrote the chronogram himself. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 292}

## Persia
Ibrahim Pasha's policy was to avoid war, yet the Tulip Age saw a temporary extension of Ottoman rule over much of western Persia. The article ties this to the decline of the Safavids and the Afghan invasion, which ended in the capture of [[place/isfahan|Isfahan]] in 1135/1722 and left the country in anarchy, a temptation to both Russia and the Porte. In 1135/1723 the Ottomans occupied Tiflis, as the [[event/capture-of-tbilisi-1723|capture of Tbilisi]] records, and Russia seized Darband and Baku. After tension that nearly brought a new war, a Russo-Ottoman treaty of 1724 divided the region: Peter was to keep Darband, Baku and Gilan, and the sultan to have Georgia, Erivan, Shirvan, Azerbaijan and all Persian territory west of the line from Ardabil to Hamadan. The Ottomans took over this region and organised it into some ten new provinces. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 292}

When the Afghan [[person/ashraf-hotaki|Ashraf]] proclaimed himself shah in April 1725, he demanded that these conquests be given up. The Porte refused, and in November 1726 he defeated the Ottoman commander Ahmad Pasha. A year later he made peace and recognised the sultan's sovereignty over the provinces, which formed part of the empire until 1730. In 1729 Ashraf was overthrown by the future [[person/nadir-shah-afshar|Nadir Shah]], who the next year defeated the Ottomans and made them give up all their gains. See also the [[event/ottoman-persian-war-1722|Ottoman-Persian war of 1722-1726]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 292}

## The revolt of 1730 and abdication
The loss of the Persian lands brought a revolt in Istanbul, which Ibrahim and the sultan were slow to meet. The article says the capital's Muslims, who had first disapproved of the conquests, were angered by their loss. It adds that Ibrahim was already unpopular for nepotism and fiscal policy, that conservatives disliked and the poor resented the luxurious, so-called Frankish manners of the court, and that the army reform project had alarmed the Janissaries. The leader was Patrona Khalil, an Albanian Janissary affiliate and former levend, acting under the influence of two discontented scholars and with the approval of many Janissary officers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 292}

The revolt began on 28 September 1730, and within hours thousands had gathered in the At Meydani. Ahmad and Ibrahim, camped at Uskudar, returned to the palace that night. For two days talks failed, as the rebels demanded the grand vizier, the Shaykh al-Islam, the chief admiral, the steward and others. In the night of 30 September, finding no support among his troops, the sultan decided to give up his favourite, whose body and those of the admiral and the steward were brought out next morning. Ahmad agreed to abdicate on condition that his own life and his sons' lives be spared, and on 1 October 1730, 18 Rabi' I 1143, his nephew [[person/mahmud-i-uthmani|Mahmud I]] succeeded him. He died in retirement in 1149/1736. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 292}

## Character and family
The article describes Ahmad as handsome and as an accomplished calligrapher, letter writer and poet. It says he was normally mild but ruthless toward those he feared or who displeased him, that he disliked war partly for its cost, and that he was very fond of money and gathered treasure, which clashed with his love of amusement and display. Ibrahim Pasha served both by raising revenue and cutting other spending. Ahmad was attached to his harem but, unlike some predecessors, did not let its members influence public affairs. He had thirty-one children, so the reign saw frequent festivities for the circumcision of his sons and the marriage of his daughters. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 292}

## Other events of the reign
The article lists a revolt of the Muntafiq Arabs near Basra in 1117/1705, another Arab revolt there put down in 1727-8, Ottoman sovereignty affirmed over certain Black Sea areas of the Caucasus early in the reign, the conquest of Oran from Spain by Algerian forces in 1708, recurring trouble in the Armenian community over Jesuit propaganda in 1706-7 and 1727-8, and two insurrections in Egypt in 1712-3 and 1727-8. The khans of the [[polity/crimean-khanate|Crimea]] played a considerable part, above all Devlet Giray, who strongly backed Charles XII's anti-Russian schemes. During the Austrian war the Porte accepted an offer of help from Francis Rakoczy, Prince of Transylvania, but he reached Istanbul too late. The treachery of Cantemir and of the prince of [[polity/principality-of-wallachia|Wallachia]] in the Pruth campaign led, from 1716, to Phanariote Greeks being appointed governors of the principalities. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 292} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 293}

## Al-Salabi's account
Ali Muhammad al-Salabi writes that the banner of holy war stayed raised in Ahmad's time and that the state regained the Morea and Azak. He says the Ottomans besieged the Russian tsar Peter and his army, and that the grand vizier Baltaci Mehmed Pasha then lifted the siege and signed the treaty of the Pruth in Jumada al-Akhira 1123, for which the sultan dismissed him. He adds that the treaty of Edirne followed in 1125, through the mediation of the Netherlands and England, with Russia giving up the Black Sea shores, and that the Ottomans defeated the Venetians. He records that Belgrade fell to Austria in 1129, which is 1717, and that the treaty of Passarowitz was concluded in 1130, which is 1718. {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.310-311}
