## Overview
Mansur ibn Sarjun was a Christian notable of [[place/damascus|Damascus]] during the last years of Byzantine rule in Syria. The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls him the controller-general and the father of St John of Damascus, the theologian recorded in the museum as [[person/yuhanna-al-dimashqi|John of Damascus]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 293} The single passage the article devotes to him concerns the Muslim siege of the city, so this record is short.

## Damascus before the surrender
The article sets the scene. The Persians of Khusraw II had occupied Damascus in 612, a city whose population was largely Jacobite Monophysite and unfriendly to the Melkite Byzantines. In 627 the city was evacuated, and the following year Heraclius returned. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 293} In Muharram 14/March 635 the Byzantine troops fell back to Damascus, and a few days later the Muslims under [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] were at the gates and began a blockade. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 293}

## Negotiator
According to the article, the population's dislike of Byzantine rule led a group of notables, among them the bishop and the controller-general Mansur ibn Sarjun, to negotiate with the Muslims in order to avoid useless suffering for the people of the city. In Rajab 14/September 635 the Eastern Gate was opened to the Muslims and the Byzantine troops withdrew northwards. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 293} The article adds that there are several traditions about how the city was taken, among them those of Ibn Asakir and al-Baladhuri, and that Caetani has argued against some of them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 293} It does not say what Mansur personally agreed or what became of him afterwards. See also the museum's card for the [[event/conquest-of-damascus-13|conquest of Damascus]].

## Not to be confused
The museum also has a record of [[person/sarjun-ibn-mansur|Sarjun ibn Mansur]], a scribe of the Umayyad caliph Abd al-Malik, taken from al-Baladhuri. The Encyclopaedia's Damascus article does not link the two men, and the museum does not assert that they are father and son. In a separate article on Yazid ibn Mu'awiya, the Encyclopaedia mentions a Christian named Sardjun who had been prominent in Mu'awiya's administration and stayed influential under Yazid, and notes that Robert Hoyland has questioned whether this Sardjun was the father of John of Damascus, as Lammens and others had assumed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 321} That article says nothing about Mansur.

## What is not known
No dates of birth or death are given. The sources read here do not say whether Mansur kept his office after the surrender, nor give his wife, other children or burial. Further detail would have to come from Byzantine and Arabic chronicles on the capitulation of Damascus, which have not yet been read for this record.
