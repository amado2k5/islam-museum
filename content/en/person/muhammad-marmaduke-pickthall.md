Muhammad Marmaduke Pickthall (7 April 1875 – 19 May 1936), born Marmaduke William Pickthall in London, was a novelist esteemed by D. H. Lawrence, H. G. Wells and E. M. Forster, and a scholar of the Middle East who had travelled widely in Ottoman lands and defended the Ottoman cause in print.{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-marmaduke-pickthall-en}

After a talk on 'Islam and Progress' to the Muslim Literary Society in Notting Hill, he publicly declared his Islam on 29 November 1917, taking his shahada at the Woking Muslim Mission.{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-marmaduke-pickthall-en} He defined himself in his own words: 'I call myself a Sunni Muslim of the Hanafi school', and had no patience with the Qadiani sect; he preached Friday sermons at Woking and in London, ran the Islamic Information Bureau and its paper The Muslim Outlook, and was a trustee of the Shah Jahan Mosque and the East London Mosque.{cite:murad-pickthall-biography}{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-marmaduke-pickthall-en} From 1920 he worked in India, editing the Bombay Chronicle and serving the Nizam of Hyderabad as a school principal, and there he published 'The Meaning of the Glorious Koran' (1930), the translation for which he is best known. He returned to England in 1935 and died at St Ives the following year; he is buried in the Muslim section of Brookwood Cemetery.{cite:wikipedia-muhammad-marmaduke-pickthall-en}
