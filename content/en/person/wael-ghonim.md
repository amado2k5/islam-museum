## Summary
Wael Ghonim is an Egyptian Internet activist and computer engineer who became an international figure in 2011 as one of the organisers of the social-media campaign that helped spur the mass demonstrations that forced President Hosni Mubarak from power. {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en} {cite:britannica-wael-ghonim} No source read for this record names a religion or school for him, and none is recorded in his data. {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en} {cite:britannica-wael-ghonim}

## Background and education
He was born to a middle-class family on 23 December 1980 in Cairo, and grew up in Abha, Saudi Arabia, moving back to Cairo at thirteen. {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en} {cite:britannica-wael-ghonim} He earned a degree in computer engineering from Cairo University in 2004 and an MBA in marketing and finance from the American University in Cairo. {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en}

## Career before the revolution
He worked as marketing and sales manager of Gawab from 2002 to 2005, then founded the financial portal Mubasher.info. {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en} He joined Google in 2008 and in January 2010 became its head of marketing for the Middle East and North Africa, based in Dubai. {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en} {cite:britannica-wael-ghonim}

## "We are all Khaled Said" and the revolution
In June 2010 he anonymously created the Facebook page "We are all Khaled Said", in support of a young Egyptian tortured to death by police in Alexandria; the page became a forum on police brutality with hundreds of thousands of members. {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en} {cite:britannica-wael-ghonim} After the Tunisian uprising, he used the page to call for action on 25 January 2011, publishing the event "January 25: Revolution against Torture, Corruption, Unemployment and Injustice". {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en} He disappeared on 27 January and was held in secret detention for 11 days, interrogated about the page, which he administered with AbdelRahman Mansour. {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en} {cite:britannica-wael-ghonim}

## Release and symbol
Released on 7 February, he appeared the same day on Dream TV, praised the protesters, mourned the dead whose names and pictures the host read out, and was overcome on camera. {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en} The interview is often credited with re-energising the protest movement. {cite:britannica-wael-ghonim} On 9 February he addressed the crowds in Tahrir Square: "This is not the time for individuals, or parties, or movements. It's a time for all of us to say just one thing: Egypt above all." {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en} He consistently rejected the role of spokesman or hero, comparing the revolution to Wikipedia: everyone contributing, no one the hero. {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en} Time included him in its Time 100 list of 2011, and he received the JFK Profile in Courage Award on behalf of the people of Egypt. {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en}

## After the revolution
His memoir Revolution 2.0 was published in 2012, and he donated the proceeds of his book deal, some 2.5 million dollars by the article's account, to charity work in Egypt. {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en} He founded Tahrir Academy, a non-profit online learning platform for young Egyptians, which halted its activities in 2015 when it could no longer secure funding. {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en} {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-ar} In 2015 he co-founded Parlio, a social-media platform acquired by Quora in March 2016, and he is a non-resident senior fellow at Harvard's Ash Center for Democratic Governance and Innovation. {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-en} {cite:wikipedia-wael-ghonim-ar}
