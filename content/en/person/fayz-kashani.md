# Fayz Kashani

Muhammad ibn Murtada ibn Mahmud al-Kashani (1598–1680), known as Muhsin and by his pen-name Fayz, was a jurist, traditionist, exegete, philosopher and mystic of Safavid Persia, and one of the most prolific authors of the seventeenth-century Islamic world.

## Life

He was born at Kashan on 14 Safar 1007 / 15 September 1598, the son of the scholar Shah Murtada, and was raised between Kashan and Qom. At twenty he went to Isfahan, then travelled to Shiraz, where he studied under Sayyid Majid Bahrani, and returned to Isfahan to join the circles of Shaykh Baha'i and to study philosophy under Mir Damad. After the hajj he settled at Qom, where Mulla Sadra taught him for eight years and gave him his daughter in marriage — Fayz became, with Abd al-Razzaq Lahiji, one of the two great transmitters of Sadra's school. In later life he taught at the Molla Abd-Allah madrasa in Isfahan and led the Friday prayer there. He died at Kashan on 22 Rabi' II 1091 / 22 May 1680 and was buried in a cemetery he had himself bought and endowed. Among his students was Qadi Sa'id al-Qummi, the philosopher of the Qom school.

## Works and thought

A documented bibliography counts 122 works in more than 550,000 lines, about forty of them published; the Arabic article puts the total close to 200, and both counts are kept here. His best-known book is al-Wafi, a vast compendium of the Imami hadith corpus. Beside it stand Tafsir al-Safi, a Qur'an commentary drawn from the Imams' reports; al-Mahajja al-bayza, a reworking of al-Ghazali's Ihya' in an Imami frame; 'Ayn al-yaqin on theology; Mafatih al-shara'i' on law; and the mystical Kalimat maknuna. He also left some 20,000 verses of poetry and an autobiography, Sharh al-Sadr.

His method was Akhbari: in law and doctrine he rested on the transmitted reports of the Imams rather than on ijma' or independent reasoning, and several of his positions drew criticism in his lifetime — his rulings on the impurity of non-Muslims, his permissive view of music, and his opinion that prayer need not be performed in Arabic. The Arabic article also records the debates around his sympathy for wahdat al-wujud and his view that hell is not eternal.

## Faith

His affiliation is documented in the sources' own words as Twelver Shi'i (Imami): the English article introduces him as an "Akhbari Twelver Shi'i", the Arabic article calls him a Shi'i Imami scholar, and Encyclopaedia Iranica's companion articles place him among the leading moderate Akhbaris within Imamite Shi'ism. The card records shia-twelver on that basis, with his Akhbari orientation stated above.
