# Mirza Fatali Akhundov

## Summary
Mirza Fatali Akhundov (12 July 1812 – 1878) was an Iranian Azerbaijani playwright, philosopher and literary critic who founded Azerbaijani dramaturgy and modern literary criticism. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en} {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-az} The Encyclopaedia Iranica, quoted in the English article, calls him "one of the earliest and most outspoken atheists to appear in the Islamic world", and his Letters of Kamal al-Dowleh (1865) is a published attack on religion. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en} He also campaigned to replace the Arabic-based alphabet used for Azerbaijani and Persian with a Latin one. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en}

## Career
He was born at Nukha (present-day Shaki), in the Shaki Khanate then under Qajar Iran, on 12 July 1812; the Encyclopaedia Iranica entry notes that his own autobiographical account gives 1812 while other documents give 1811 and 1814. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en} {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-fa} In 1834 he moved to Tiflis (Tbilisi) and spent the rest of his life there as a translator of Oriental languages in the service of the Russian Empire's Viceroyalty of the Caucasus, teaching at a Tiflis district school from 1837. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en} His first published work was an "Oriental poem" in Persian lamenting the death of Pushkin (1837). {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en} In the early 1850s he wrote six comedies — the first comedies in Azerbaijani literature and the first works of a national dramaturgy — whose critical picture of Azerbaijani life drew responses in the Russian and foreign press and, in Persian translation, helped give birth to modern Iranian theatre. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en} The German Magazine of Foreign Literature called him a "dramatic genius" and "the Azerbaijani Molière". {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en}

He died at Tiflis in 1878, and the sources split on the day: the Russian article and Wikidata give 10 March 1878 (26 February Old Style), while the English and Persian articles give 9 March; his data record follows 10 March at debated status and discloses the split. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-ru} {cite:wikidata-mirza-fatali-akhundov} {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en} {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-fa} The Azerbaijani article records that, in accordance with his will, he was buried in Tiflis's old Muslim cemetery beside his teacher Mirza Shafi Vazeh. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-az}

## Works
His six comedies of 1850–1855, among them The Story of Monsieur Jourdan and The Adventures of the Lankaran Khanate Vizier, established realism as the leading trend in Azerbaijani literature. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en} His short novel The Deceived Stars (1859) laid the foundation of Azerbaijani realistic historical prose. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en} The Letters of Kamal al-Dowleh (1865), cast as letters from an Indian prince travelling in Iran, set out his materialist philosophy and his attack on religious dogma. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en} {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-ru} He wrote under the pen name Sabuhi. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-ru}

## Positions and criticism
This section sets out positions as reported in the sources; it is not a verdict by the museum.

**Atheism.** Two independent scholarly traditions attest unbelief for Akhundov himself. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en} {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-ru} The Encyclopaedia Iranica entry by Hamid Algar, quoted in full in the English article, describes him as one of the earliest and most outspoken atheists in the Islamic world, and the Persian article repeats that judgement in Persian — the same source family, counted once. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en} {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-fa} Independently, the Russian article's account of his philosophical views states that, being an atheist, he criticised all religions, above all the dogmas of Islam, citing Azerbaijani philosophical scholarship (Mamedov, 1962). {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-ru} His faith is therefore recorded as atheist-or-sceptic, documented; the Soviet-era characterisations do not stand alone, because the Iranica attestation is independent of them and rests on his own published writings. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en} {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-ru}

**The youthful profession.** The Russian article also records, from a single testimony dated 1832, that the young Akhundov — then being groomed for the clergy by his uncle — professed Shia Islam. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-ru} One testimony about his youth cannot carry a faith segment under the two-source rule, so it is preserved here and in his data record's faith note, not recorded as a segment. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-ru} His recorded sympathy for Zoroastrianism and his correspondence with the Parsi scholar Maneckji Limji Hataria, noted in scholarship cited by the English article, are sympathy, not a conversion attested by any source. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en}

**Alphabet reform and empire.** He devoted years to a project for replacing the Perso-Arabic script with a Latin-based alphabet, presenting versions of it in 1857, 1863 and 1873, without success in his lifetime. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en} The English article reports that his sharp pen was directed against whatever he believed hindered the advance of the Russian Empire, which for him was a force for modernisation, and that Walter Kolarz described him as so devoted to the Russian cause that he urged his compatriots to fight Turkey during the Crimean War. {cite:wikipedia-mirza-fatali-akhundov-en}

## In Encyclopaedia of Islam, 2nd edition (Leiden: Brill, 1954–2008), 13 volumes
Mirza Fath 'Ali Akhund-zada (1812–1878) was the first writer of original plays in a Turkish idiom, the Azerbaijani (Adhari) Turkish of the Caucasus. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 353}

## In Encyclopaedia of Islam, 2nd edition (Leiden: Brill, 1954–2008), 13 volumes: Encyclopaedia of Islam account
He was the son of a trader from Persian Azerbaijan and was born at Sheki, now Nukha. The Encyclopaedia of Islam gives 1812; Caferoglu gives 1811. A relative gave him a good literary and philosophical education, which drew him closer to liberal ideas than the Islamic theology he had meant to study. After lessons from a religious teacher at Ganja (Karabakh) he trained at the newly opened Russian intermediate school for Muslims at Sheki. In his youth he wrote in the style of Persian poetry, including an elegy on Pushkin's death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 353}

The advance of the theatre at Tiflis under the military governor, Prince Vorontsov (1844–48), gave him his push towards drama; he worked in Vorontsov's chancellery as an oriental interpreter. Between 1850 and 1857 he wrote six comedies and a historical-satirical narrative in Adhari Turkish. The plays include the Story of Molla Ibrahim Khalil the alchemist (1850); the Story of Monsieur Jourdan, a herbalist, and of the famous magician Mostali Shah (1850); the Adventures of the vezir of a khan (1850); the Story of the bear that caught the robber (1852); the Adventures of the miser (1852–53); and the Story of the attorneys in a lawsuit (1855). The narrative, of 1857, is given in the article as "The betrayed stars". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 353}

In the plays and the narrative he put progressive ideas against feudalism, the corruption of justice and the superstition then widespread in the Caucasus. He also urged loyalty to the Russian authorities, to ease the passage of the Transcaucasian Muslims into modern life. Several plays were published in Russian in the official journal Kavkaz and performed in Russian at Tiflis and St Petersburg; pupils of the Azerbaijani state schools gave the first performances in the original language at the end of the 1870s. A complete edition appeared at Tiflis in 1859, and a second in 1938 for the 125th anniversary of his birth. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 353-354}

His plays were translated into Persian and into several European languages. He was called the "Caucasian Gogol" or the "oriental Molière". He also wrote treatises on political science against absolutism and theocracy, and two memoranda on an alphabetical system of his own design, meant to make the Islamic languages, especially the Turkish ones, easier to write and better suited to progress. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 354}
