## Summary
Ayaan Hirsi Ali is a Somali-born Dutch-American writer, activist and former politician, included in this museum as an influential figure in the story of the modern Muslim world. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} She renounced Islam and identified as an atheist from 2002, became one of the most prominent critics of Islam of her generation, and announced her conversion to Christianity in November 2023; both positions, and the responses to them, are recorded in this article attributed from the sources, with no verdict from the museum. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} {cite:ewtn-hirsi-ali-christian}

## Early life
She was born Ayaan Hirsi Magan in Mogadishu, Somali Democratic Republic, on 13 November 1969. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} Her father, Hirsi Magan Isse, was a Somali intellectual and opposition figure who was imprisoned for opposing the government of Siad Barre in the year she was born; at the age of five, Hirsi Ali underwent female genital mutilation arranged by her grandmother while her father was imprisoned, a practice he opposed. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} After her father escaped from prison the family left Somalia and lived in Saudi Arabia, Ethiopia and finally Kenya, where they lived for ten years and she attended the Muslim Girls' Secondary School in Nairobi. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en}

## Asylum and education in the Netherlands
In 1992, at 23, she travelled via Germany to the Netherlands to escape an alleged forced marriage, requested political asylum and obtained a residence permit, gaining Dutch citizenship five years later. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} She worked in short-term jobs and as a Somali-Dutch interpreter and translator, studied social work, and then took a master's degree in political science at Leiden University. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} She became a fellow at the Wiardi Beckman Stichting, the think tank of the Labour Party, before switching to the centre-right People's Party for Freedom and Democracy (VVD). {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en}

## Leaving Islam and atheism
She renounced Islam and acknowledged her disbelief in God in 2002, after reading Herman Philipse's Atheist Manifesto and, following the September 11 attacks, checking the justifications cited by Osama bin Laden against the Qur'an and hadith herself. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} The Catholic News Agency report likewise records that in 2002 she renounced her Muslim faith and declared herself an atheist. {cite:ewtn-hirsi-ali-christian} She was counted a central figure in New Atheism from its beginnings, alongside Richard Dawkins, Christopher Hitchens, Daniel Dennett and Sam Harris. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en}

## Parliament and Submission
In 2003 she was elected to the lower house of the Dutch parliament for the VVD, where she campaigned on the integration of non-Western immigrants and the rights of Muslim women, and her statements provoked controversy, including a religious-discrimination complaint over remarks about Muhammad which the prosecutor's office declined to pursue. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} With the filmmaker Theo van Gogh she wrote and narrated the short film Submission (2004), which criticised the treatment of women in Islamic society. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} On 2 November 2004 Van Gogh was murdered in Amsterdam by Mohammed Bouyeri, who left a letter threatening Hirsi Ali; she went into hiding under state protection. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} In 2005 Time named her one of the 100 most influential people in the world. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en}

## The citizenship controversy and move to America
In 2006 the television programme Zembla reported that she had given false information in her asylum account, including about her name and the circumstances of the marriage she fled; she resigned from parliament, in a political crisis that also brought down the governing cabinet indirectly. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} She has acknowledged using her paternal grandfather's surname on her application; the differing accounts of her asylum story are part of the public record of her career and are recorded here as the sources give them. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} Moving to the United States, she joined the American Enterprise Institute, later also working with Harvard University and the Hoover Institution, founded the AHA Foundation for women's rights in 2007, and became a United States citizen in 2013. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} She is married to the historian Niall Ferguson. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en}

## Books and positions — attributed
Her books The Caged Virgin, Infidel: My Life (2007), Nomad: From Islam to America (2010) and Heretic: Why Islam Needs a Reformation Now (2015) became bestsellers. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} In her own stated positions, she opposes female genital mutilation, forced marriage, honour killing and child marriage, argues that Islam as a political doctrine is incompatible with Western constitutional orders, and in Heretic called for a reformation of Islam; after the Arab Spring she withdrew the claim in Nomad that Islam is beyond reform. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} These are her positions as she states them; the museum records them without adopting them.

## Criticism and responses — attributed
This section sets out claims and responses as reported in the sources; it is not a verdict by the museum.

The English article records that non-Western critics have accused Hirsi Ali of adhering to alleged ideologies of "Islamophobia" or "neo-orientalism" and have questioned her scholarly credentials "to speak authoritatively about Islam and the Arab world", saying she promotes the notion of a supposed Western "civilizing mission". {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} Other critics, as the article reports them, have accused her of strengthening racism rather than weakening sexism, while supporters cited in the same coverage present her as a dissident who has lived under permanent protection for speaking about the treatment of women. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} Her account of her own past has also been contested, as the Zembla investigation and the 2006 citizenship crisis show, and she has responded publicly to those challenges over the years, including by acknowledging the name she used on arrival. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en}

## Conversion to Christianity
In November 2023 Hirsi Ali announced that she had become a Christian, in an essay for UnHerd titled "Why I am now a Christian". {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} {cite:ewtn-hirsi-ali-christian} In the essay, as the CNA report quotes her, she wrote that she had turned to Christianity in part "because I ultimately found life without any spiritual solace unendurable — indeed very nearly self-destructive", that "Atheism failed to answer a simple question: What is the meaning and purpose of life?", and that Western civilisation faced threats from Russia and China, global Islamism and "woke ideology" against which secular approaches were inadequate. {cite:ewtn-hirsi-ali-christian} The English article records the responses the conversion drew: some Christians interpreted it as merely a cultural response and not a spiritual one, and some atheists said they were baffled that she had not addressed materialist rebuttals of Christianity; in a later discussion with Richard Dawkins she said she believed in the resurrection, and Dawkins acknowledged she was not just a political Christian. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} Her data record therefore records her current faith as christian, documented by her own essay as reported by CNA and by the English article, with her 2002–2023 atheist period stated in the same segment's note. {cite:wikipedia-ayaan-hirsi-ali-en} {cite:ewtn-hirsi-ali-christian}
