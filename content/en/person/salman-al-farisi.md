## Brief
Salman the Persian was a seeker who crossed religions and empires — Zoroastrian, then Christian, then Muslim — before finding his way to Muhammad in Medina. The tradition remembers him for one idea that saved a city: the trench.

## Life
Born in Persia about 568 CE, Salman left home on a long search that the sources narrate almost as a novel: through churches of Syria and Iraq, sold into slavery, until he reached Medina {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}. There he embraced Islam about 622, and the community helped ransom his freedom {cite:ibn-ishaq-guillaume}.

At the [[event/battle-of-the-trench|siege of 627]], with Medina ringed by the largest army Arabia had seen, tradition credits Salman with the tactic unknown to Arabian warfare: dig a trench the cavalry cannot cross. It held {cite:ibn-ishaq-guillaume}. He lived on into the caliphate, remembered as governor of al-Mada'in, dying there about 656 {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

## Legacy
In the Prophet's reported words, "Salman is one of us, the People of the House" — a Persian, once enslaved, counted inside Islam's most honored family. He remains the tradition's great argument that Islam is not an ethnicity.

## In the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, supplement volume, has an article on Salman al-Farisi, also called Salman Pak, by G. Levi Della Vida. It opens by calling him a semi-legendary figure of early Islam, a Companion of the Prophet and the person whom later tradition regards as the first convert to Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 774} The sections below follow that article and label whose view each statement is.

### The traditional account, as the article summarises it
According to one tradition, whose fullest form goes back to [[person/ibn-ishaq|Ibn Ishaq]], Salman was the son of a village headman of [[place/jayy|Jayy]] near [[place/isfahan|Isfahan]]. Other stories place him near [[place/ramhurmuz|Ramhurmuz]] and give his Persian name as Mahbeh or Ruzbeh. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 774} Drawn to Christianity as a boy, he left his father's house to follow a Christian monk, changed teachers several times and reached Syria. From there he travelled down to the [[place/wadi-al-qura|Wadi al-Qura]] in western Arabia, looking for the prophet who was to restore the religion of Abraham, whose coming his last teacher had foretold on his deathbed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 774}

Bedouin of [[group/banu-kalb|Kalb]], acting as his guides, betrayed him and sold him as a slave to a Jew. He was taken to Yathrib, and soon after he arrived the emigration of [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] took place. Recognising in Muhammad the marks that the monk had described, Salman became a Muslim. He bought his freedom from his Jewish master, after being helped, in the account, by Muhammad himself to raise the sum. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 774}

### The ditch, and the academic assessment
The name of Salman is linked with the siege of Medina by the Meccans: tradition says he advised digging the ditch, the khandaq, with which the Muslims defended themselves. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 774} The article's author sets against this the study of J. Horovitz, which, in his words, shows that the earliest accounts of the day of the ditch do not mention Salman's part. He judges that the story was probably invented to give a Persian the introduction of a defence whose name is of Persian origin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 774} This is an academic position and not the view of the traditional sources summarised in the sections above, which credit Salman with the idea of the trench at the [[event/battle-of-the-trench|Battle of the Trench]].

The author adds that the other reports about Salman's career, his part in the conquest of Iraq and of Fars and his governorship of [[place/al-madain|al-Mada'in]], are in his judgement equally without authority and almost all come from the historian Sayf ibn Umar, whose bias he calls well known. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 774} In his view the historical Salman is of the vaguest, and it is hard even to accept that the legend rests on the real conversion of a Medinan slave of Persian origin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 776}

### Why the figure grew
According to the article, Salman's fame is almost entirely due to his Persian nationality. He is the prototype of the converts who played so large a part in the growth of Islam, and he became the national hero of Muslim Persia and a favourite figure of the [[concept/shuubiyya|Shu'ubiyya]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 776} The author explains most of the traditions about Salman by one theme: the Prophet is reported to foretell that the Persians will form the better part of the Muslim community and to declare Salman a member of his own family, the [[group/ahl-al-bayt|ahl al-bayt]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 776}

### Later veneration and the afterlife of the figure
The article reports that Salman appears as one of the founders of Sufism alongside the people of the Suffa, as named in al-Luma, and that the supposed site of his tomb became a place of worship very early, at the latest in the fourth Islamic century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 776} The tomb is still pointed out near ancient al-Mada'in, at a place called Salman Pak, meaning Salman the Pure, near the former Asbandur suburb. Its mosque was seen by Pietro della Valle in 1617, renovated by Sultan Murad IV and restored again in 1322/1904-5, and it is the object of many pilgrimages, especially by Shi'is returning from [[place/karbala|Karbala]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 776} Other traditions put his tomb near Isfahan, where his cult is attested in the seventh Islamic century, and elsewhere, for example Lydda. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 776}

Salman has a part in the development of the futuwa and the craftsmen's corporations. He is venerated as patron of barbers, which gave rise to a tradition, unknown in the old collections, that he was the Prophet's barber, and he is one of the main links in the mystic chain in several dervish orders. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 776}

### Views in Shi'i and other sects
The article states that among the extremist Shi'i sects Salman is placed immediately after [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] in the series of divine emanations. The [[group/nusayriyya|Nusayriyya]] make him the third member of a trinity formed by three mystic letters, standing for Ali, Muhammad and Salman, of which he is the gate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 776} These are the doctrines of those sects as reported by the article, and not the position of the Sunni sources or of the academic account above.

### Date of death and reported long life
The article says Salman's death is placed in 35/655-6 or 36/656-7. In the author's view this date serves only to show that historical tradition had no record of any activity of his after the accession of Ali at the end of 35/656. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 776} Like other people said to have become Muslim after long experience of other religions, he is credited with an extraordinary lifespan, reported as 200, 300, 350 and even 553 years. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 776}

## For young visitors
Salman crossed countries and religions looking for the truth, was enslaved, freed — and then dug the ditch that saved Medina.

## In Ahmad Farid's lessons 'The Boat'
In [[work/the-boat-ahmad-farid|'The Boat']] [[person/ahmad-farid|Ahmad Farid]] sets Salman the Persian against [[person/abu-lahab|Abu Lahab]] to show that the noblest people are the most pious, not the richest or best-born. {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep17 00:04:00} Abu Lahab was of pure lineage and the Prophet's uncle but was not honoured by Islam, he says, while Salman was not an Arab, let alone of Quraysh or of the Prophet's family, and yet was honoured by piety; he quotes the line of poetry that Islam raised Salman of Persia while disbelief brought down the noble Abu Lahab. {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep17 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep17 00:04:30} Salman, he says, took pride only in his Islam. {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep17 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-ahmad-farid the-boat/ep17 00:05:00}

## In Mosaad Anwar's series 'Those Given Glad Tidings of Paradise'
In episode 23 of [[work/the-ten-promised-paradise-mosaad-anwar|'Those Given Glad Tidings of Paradise']], [[person/mosaad-anwar|Mosaad Anwar]] presents Salman al-Farisi as «the true seeker of the truth», and says he searched for the truth for twenty years, was the first of the Persians to Islam, was the man behind the idea of the Trench, and is one of those for whom Paradise yearns. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:01:30} He gives his kunya as Abu Abd Allah and tells the story in Salman's own voice: he was a Persian of [[place/isfahan|Isfahan]], from a village called [[place/jayy|Jayy]], where his father was the dihqan, that is, the headman. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:01:30} His father loved him so much that he kept him shut in the house as a girl is kept, and he devoted himself to the Magian religion until he became the keeper of the fire, tending it and never letting it die down. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:02:00}

His father then sent him to his estate; on the way he passed a church, heard the Christians at prayer and was taken with it, judging it better than his own religion, and he stayed until his father sent people to find him. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:02:30} His father argued with him, then put him in fetters and confined him; Salman sent word to the Christians to tell him when merchants arrived from [[place/al-sham|Syria]], broke his fetters and went off with them. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:03:00} In Syria he served a bishop whom the speaker calls a bad man: he urged people to give alms and then hoarded them for himself until he had gathered seven jars of gold and silver; when he died Salman showed the people his hoard, and they crucified and stoned him, and a devout ascetic came after him. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:04:00} Then came a chain of deathbed referrals: from Syria to a man in [[place/mosul|Mosul]], then to a man in Nusaybin, then to a man in [[place/amorium|Amuriya]], each of them, he says, a devout ascetic. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:05:30} The man of Amuriya told him as he was dying that the time of the prophet of the last age had drawn near: he would appear in the land of the Arabs and emigrate to a land between two lava fields with palms between them, and among his signs were that he accepted gifts but did not eat charity and bore the seal of prophethood between his shoulders. The speaker glosses harra as the ground of a dormant volcano. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:06:30}

He narrates that Salman earned a cow and a few sheep at Amuriya and gave them to some men of [[group/banu-kalb|Kalb]] to take him to the land of the Arabs; they betrayed him and sold him as a slave, and a Jew bought him and then sold him to a cousin of his from [[group/banu-qurayza|Banu Qurayza]], who took him to [[place/medina|Medina]]. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:07:30} While at work he heard the news of the Prophet's emigration to Medina and asked about it, and his master struck him and told him to get back to his work. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:08:30} Then he tested the signs: he brought dates as charity, and the Prophet gave them to his Companions without eating; he brought dates as a gift, and the Prophet ate; then he followed him at a funeral in [[place/baqi-al-gharqad|Baqi' al-Gharqad]] and moved round behind him, and the Prophet let his cloak fall from his back, and Salman saw the seal. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:09:00} Now certain, he bent over him kissing him and weeping and told him his story, which the Prophet wanted his Companions to hear; the speaker sums it up as a journey of twenty years, from fire-worship to Christianity, from priest to priest, then betrayal and slavery. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:10:30} This general course of the journey, through the churches of Syria into slavery and on to Medina, agrees with what this record gives from [[person/muhammad-ibn-sad|Ibn Sa'd]]. {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}

He explains [[concept/mukataba|mukataba]]: a slave buys himself from his master for a sum he pays off, and once he has paid it he is free; he says Islam confirmed it and that the Prophet told Salman to make such a contract. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:12:00} His master set the price at three hundred palm trees and forty uqiyas of gold. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:12:00} The Prophet told the Companions to help their brother, and each brought what palm shoots he could until there were three hundred, and the Prophet planted them with his own hand; not one of them died. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:12:30} Then the Prophet was brought, from some booty, a piece of gold the size of a hen's egg, and gave it to Salman; Salman said it would not be enough, and the Prophet told him God would pay his debt with it; he paid forty uqiyas from it and was freed. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:13:00} That the community helped him to his freedom is also stated in this record from [[person/ibn-ishaq|Ibn Ishaq]]. {cite:ibn-ishaq-guillaume}

He says Salman was present at the [[event/battle-of-the-trench|Trench]] and missed no campaign after it. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:14:00} He relates that the polytheists came with ten thousand fighters intending to wipe out the Muslims; the Prophet consulted his Companions, and Salman said that his people, when besieged, used to dig a trench, and the Prophet took up his idea. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:14:00} Every ten men dug forty cubits, which he estimates at about twenty-five metres, and the trench ran along the north of Medina, because the city was otherwise shielded by mountains and palm groves and could be reached only from the north. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:14:30} He describes the Prophet digging and carrying earth with them, the Companions chanting that they had pledged themselves to Muhammad to strive, and his answer, a prayer for the [[group/ansar|Ansar]] and the [[group/muhajirun|Muhajirun]] that there is no true life but the life of the hereafter. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:15:30} The besiegers were taken by surprise by the trench; then a storm wind tore out their tent pegs and overturned their cooking pots, and they went back empty-handed. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:16:00}

He relates a hadith he attributes to [[work/sahih-al-bukhari|al-Bukhari]], in the Book of Fasting, narrated by Abu Juhayfa: the Prophet made Salman and [[person/abu-al-darda|Abu al-Darda']] brothers; Salman visited his house and found Umm al-Darda' in neglected clothes, because her husband had no interest in this world; Salman refused to eat until Abu al-Darda' broke his voluntary fast, kept him from standing in prayer early in the night, and the two prayed at its end; and he told him that his Lord, his own self and his family each had a right over him, so he should give each its due; and the Prophet said Salman was right. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:16:00} He relates from [[person/al-dhahabi|al-Dhahabi]]'s Siyar and Ibn Sa'd's Tabaqat, through Jarir ibn Hazim from a shaykh of Banu Abs from his father, that a man who had bought meat and fodder made Salman carry it for him without knowing who he was; when people told him that this was the Companion of the Messenger of God, Salman refused to set the load down until he had brought it to the man's house. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:18:00} He relates from [[person/al-tabarani|al-Tabarani]], with a chain whose men he says are those of the Sahih, from Abu Wa'il: he and a companion visited Salman, who set out bread and salt and said that but for the Prophet's forbidding affectation he would have gone to more trouble for them; the companion wished for dates, so Salman pawned his water vessel and bought him some; and when the companion praised God for making them content, Salman said that had he been content, the vessel would not be in pawn. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:19:30}

He relates from [[person/ibn-majah|Ibn Majah]], narrated by [[person/anas-ibn-malik|Anas]] and graded sound by [[person/al-albani-muhammad-nasir-al-din|al-Albani]], that Sa'd and [[person/abdullah-ibn-masud|Ibn Mas'ud]] visited Salman as he was dying and he wept, saying the Prophet had charged them that each should take from this world no more than a rider's provision, and they had not kept it; yet he left only some twenty-odd dirhams. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:21:30} The speaker sets Salman weeping over what he reckons at about twenty-five pounds against those who own millions today. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:22:00} He says death came to Salman when he had reached eighty. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:22:30} He relates from al-Dhahabi's Siyar, from Buqayra, Salman's wife, that he lay in an upper room with four doors and told her to open them all, since visitors would come to him and he did not know by which door, and to sprinkle musk around his bed; she came back to find that he had died as though asleep. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:22:30} He closes by repeating the hadith that Paradise yearns for [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]], [[person/ammar-ibn-yasir|Ammar]] and Salman. {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep23 00:24:00}

On [[passage/quran-2-62|verse 62]], al-Tabari gives al-Suddi's account of Salman from Jundishapur, his meeting with a monk reading the Gospel, his travels to Mosul and Bayt al-Maqdis, and his sale at Medina, and says the verse came down about his companions; the editor notes the report is broken and that the story of his conversion is told in differing ways hard to reconcile. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 150} {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v02 p. 155}

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "A Story and an Idea"
[[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] opens his programme [[work/kissa-wa-fikra-program|"A Story and an Idea"]] with Salman, whom he presents as the model of "sacrifice for the true idea": a man ready to give up wealth, freedom and homeland in search of the truth. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:01:30} What follows is his account.

He says Salman came from Isfahan, where his father was the head (dihqan) of their village and one of its richest men, combining, as a Magian leader, political, financial and religious standing. Salman rose in Magianism until he was the keeper of the fire, tending it so that it never went out by day or night. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:02:00} Sent by his father to oversee a great estate, he passed a church, was struck by the Christians' prayer and judged their religion better than his own; he stayed until sunset and learned that the religion had its origin in al-Sham. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:05:30} His father confined him at home and put iron fetters on his feet; Salman secretly asked the Christians to tell him of a caravan bound for al-Sham, freed himself and left with it. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:07:30} Al-Suwaidan draws a lesson from the father: a question of belief needs calm dialogue, and force and prison only harden conviction. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:08:00}

In al-Sham Salman served a bishop who urged alms and hoarded them for himself; when he died, Salman showed the people his treasure, two large jars of silver and gold. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:10:30} From this al-Suwaidan draws the rule that one follows the idea and not the person: the fault lay in the carrier, not in the idea. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:11:30} The successor was a true ascetic, who at his death sent him to a man in Mosul; from there he went on to Nusaybin and then to Amorium, where he earned some cows and sheep. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:12:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:14:30} The last of these teachers told him that no one remained on the true way, but that a prophet's time was near: he would come with the religion of Abraham, emigrate to a land of palms between two lava fields, accept a gift but not alms, and bear the seal of prophethood between his shoulders. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:15:30}

Merchants of the tribe of Kalb agreed to carry him in return for his animals, then sold him as a slave at Wadi al-Qura; a Jew of Banu Qurayza bought him and took him to Yathrib, where he recognised the palms between the two lava fields. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:18:00} While working in a palm tree he heard of a man from Mecca who had come to Quba claiming prophethood. He offered dates first as alms and then as a gift and saw both signs fulfilled, and at al-Baqi' he saw the seal and kissed it, weeping. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:20:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:21:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:22:00} The Prophet asked him to tell his story to the Companions; al-Suwaidan says it has come down "in Sahih Muslim and elsewhere". {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:23:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:23:30}

The Prophet told him to make a contract of manumission (mukataba), and the Companions helped him pay it. Slavery kept him from Badr and Uhud; freed after the third year of the Hijra, he took part in every later campaign. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:24:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:24:30} Al-Suwaidan says he died in the caliphate of Uthman, reportedly at more than one hundred and twenty. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:25:00} His closing lesson: whoever seeks a true idea needs criteria, testing it against experience, asking the wise, reading the scholars, and continuing to search. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep01 00:27:00}
