Mohamed Ennakhli (1869–1924) was a Tunisian Zaytuna scholar and reformer born at Kairouan.{cite:wikipedia-mohamed-ennakhli-fr}{cite:wikidata-mohamed-ennakhli} He joined the Zaytuna in 1304 AH and studied under Salem Bouhageb, Mahmoud Ben Mahmoud and Mohamed Ennajar, rising to the first rank of its teachers, and over some thirty-five years he formed a whole pleiad of scholars who went on to the highest civil and religious offices in Tunisia.{cite:wikipedia-mohamed-ennakhli-ar}{cite:wikipedia-mohamed-ennakhli-fr} His liberal ideas were inspired by the great nineteenth-century Muslim reformers, above all Muhammad Abduh and Jamal al-Din al-Afghani; he also taught at the Khaldounia, the school founded in 1896 to teach the subjects the Zaytuna did not, such as mathematics and the physical sciences, and he was an active member of the Sadiki College improvement council set up in 1906.{cite:wikipedia-mohamed-ennakhli-fr}

Exegete, jurisconsult, historian, writer and poet, Ennakhli argued in his lessons, lectures and writings for the necessity of reconciling science and religion.{cite:wikipedia-mohamed-ennakhli-fr} Among his students were Mohamed Tahar Ben Achour, the grand cadi Mohamed Sadok Ennaifer and the Algerian reformer Abdelhamid Ben Badis, head of the Association of Algerian Muslim Ulama.{cite:wikipedia-mohamed-ennakhli-ar}{cite:wikipedia-mohamed-ennakhli-fr} Muhammad Makhlof, in Shajarat al-Nur al-Zakiyya, remembered him as a penetrating critic of rare intelligence and plain speech; the only work printed in his lifetime was a twelve-page treatise on the life of the Arabic language, while his studies on the Muslim woman, on geography, on prosody and on Qur'an commentary remained in manuscript.{cite:wikipedia-mohamed-ennakhli-ar} He died at Tunis in 1924, and his funeral was a public one, his body being carried to Kairouan for burial as he had wished.{cite:wikipedia-mohamed-ennakhli-ar}
