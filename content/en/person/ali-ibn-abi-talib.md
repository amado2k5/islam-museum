## Overview
[[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]] ibn Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim was the cousin of the [[person/muhammad|Prophet]] and the husband of his daughter [[person/fatima|Fatima]] {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.871}. He was raised in the Prophet's house before Islam and was one of the first to embrace it, and the reports in Ibn al-Athir differ on who was first: Ali, [[person/khadija|Khadija]] or [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.872-873}. When the Prophet emigrated, Ali stayed in Mecca to return the trusts and slept in his bed on the night of departure, then joined him in [[place/medina|Medina]] {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.872-874}. He was present at [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]], Uhud, the Trench and the other campaigns except Tabuk, when the Prophet left him in charge of his family, and [[event/battle-of-khaybar|Khaybar]] was taken at his hands {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.871} {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.875}. The Prophet sent him to Yemen as a judge, and Ali related much from the Prophet {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.875-876}.

He was the fourth of the Rashidun caliphs, given allegiance in the Prophet's mosque in Medina in Dhu al-Hijja 35 AH, after the killing of [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]] {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.881}. Ibn al-Athir reports that some Companions held back from the pledge without his compelling them, and that the people of Syria, with [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya]], did not pledge and fought him {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.882} {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.883}. Three Kharijites then swore to kill him, Mu'awiya and [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn al-As]], and [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-muljam|Abd al-Rahman ibn Muljam]] struck him in [[place/kufa|Kufa]] as he went out to the dawn prayer in Ramadan 40 AH {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.884-885}. He died on the night of Sunday, with eleven nights of Ramadan remaining; his age is disputed, and al-Waqidi held sixty-three the soundest report; his son al-Hasan prayed over him {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.886} {cite:marifat-sahaba-abu-nuaym p.2002}.

## Islam and emigration
He kept his Islam hidden from Abu Talib, and he had been raised in the Prophet's house before Islam. Ibn al-Athir says that Ali embraced Islam at the age of ten, according to Mujahid. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.872}

On the question of who was first to embrace Islam, the reports differ: Ibn Abbas related that Ali was first, others that Khadija was first and then Ali, and some that Abu Bakr was first, while Ibrahim al-Nakha'i denied that Ali was the first. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.872-873}

When the Prophet emigrated to Medina, Ali stayed in Mecca to return the trusts that were with the Prophet, and slept in his bed on the night of departure. He then followed him after three days, his feet swollen from the journey; the Prophet spat on his hands, rubbed his feet with them, and prayed for his well-being. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.872-874}

## Battles
He witnessed Badr and other battles, but did not witness Tabuk, because the Prophet left him with his family. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.874} Ibn al-Athir relates from al-Bara' that Ali "fought and supported" on the day of Badr, and from Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas that he saw him striking the heads of the idolaters with the sword. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.874}

At Uhud he was struck with many blows, said to be sixteen, and Ibn al-Musayyab related that Gabriel was lifting him from the ground after each blow. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.874} Ali related that when the people abandoned the Prophet on the day of Uhud, he looked among the dead and did not find him, then charged the enemy until he found him among them. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.874-875}

At Khaybar the banner was carried, it is related, by Abu Bakr and then Umar, and then it was given to Ali, who had an eye inflammation; the Prophet spat in his eyes and he was cured, and God gave victory through his hands. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.875} {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.879}

At al-Hudaybiyya the Prophet said of a man who would strike necks for the religion that he was "the one who sews the sandal", and Ali was sewing a sandal for the Prophet. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.877-878} The Prophet sent him to Yemen as judge and prayed that God would make his tongue firm and guide his heart; Ali said he never doubted a judgement between two people after that. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.875}

## Knowledge and fatwa
Ali related much from the Prophet. His sons al-Hasan, al-Husayn, Muhammad and Umar related from him, with Ibn Mas'ud, Ibn Umar, Ibn Abbas and other Companions, and among the Successors a group such as Sa'id ibn al-Musayyab, Alqama and al-Sha'bi. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.875-876} It is related from Ibn Abbas that Ali was given nine parts of knowledge in ten; Umar used to seek refuge from a question "for which there is no Abu al-Hasan". {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.875-876}

## Asceticism and justice
Sayings on asceticism are related from him, among them that the world is a carcass and whoever seeks it must endure the company of dogs. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.876} Reports of his way of life are related: a loincloth he bought for five dirhams, and dirhams that remained of his expenses in Yanbu'. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.877} He spent by night and by day, in secret and in public. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.877-878}

In his governance of the land-tax he forbade striking a man with a whip for the collection of one dirham, and said: "We were only ordered to take from them what is easy." {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.877}

Ibn al-Athir says that al-Hasan related that he left at his death only six hundred dirhams, with which he had bought a slave-servant. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.876-877}

## The caliphate
Ibn al-Athir says that the khalifa after Uthman was given to Ali in the mosque of the Prophet in Medina in Dhu al-Hijja 35 AH. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.881} Ali related that he thought himself more entitled to the affair, yet he listened and obeyed those who had ruled before him: Abu Bakr, then Umar, then Uthman. When Uthman was killed people gave him the allegiance, and then they withdrew it, and he said he found after that only the sword or disbelief in what was sent down. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.881}

It is mentioned that Talha and al-Zubayr gave him the allegiance, and that Ibn Umar, Sa'd, Usama and others stayed back from the allegiance, and he did not compel them, saying that they "sat back from the truth and did not support falsehood". The people of Syria with Mu'awiya did not give him the allegiance and fought him. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.882} {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.883}

## The troubles
Reports are related in which the Prophet orders fighting "the oath-breakers, the transgressors and the renegades", and in which Ammar ibn Yasir is killed with Ali. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.882} Ibn al-Athir says that Ibn Umar said at his death that he had not fought with Ali against the rebel group, and that Masruq repented to God of staying back from fighting with Ali. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.883} The reports here refer to al-Kamil for the details of the fighting with the Kharijites and others. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.883}

## His death
Ali related that the Prophet told him he would be struck on the head and his beard would be dyed, and that the most wretched of the last people is the one who would strike him. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.882-884} Ibn al-Athir relates from Abd Allah ibn Salam that he advised him not to enter Iraq, and Ali said the Prophet had told him this. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.882}

Three Kharijites, Ibn Muljam al-Muradi, al-Burak ibn Abd Allah and Amr ibn Bakr, conspired to kill Ali, Mu'awiya and Amr ibn al-As, and Ibn Muljam undertook to kill Ali. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.884-885} On the morning of Friday in Ramadan 40 AH, as he went out for the dawn prayer in the mosque of Kufa, Ibn Muljam struck him on the head, and the sword reached his brain; Shabib ibn Bajra, who struck with him, escaped. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.884-885} Ali told his family that if he lived he was the master of his blood, to pardon or to exact retribution, and if he died he was to be followed by the killer. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.885}

He remained through Friday and Saturday, and died on the night of Sunday, eleven nights before the end of Ramadan 40 AH. His sons al-Hasan and al-Husayn and Abd Allah ibn Ja'far washed him; he was shrouded in three garments without a shirt; al-Hasan prayed over him and he was buried at dawn. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.886} {cite:marifat-sahaba-abu-nuaym p.2002}

Ibn al-Athir relates that al-Hasan brought Ibn Muljam out of prison to be retaliated against after the burial; the report details his killing, which is not set out here. {cite:usd-al-ghaba p.886}

## A conversation with Umar
Ibn Umar relates that Umar ibn al-Khattab asked Ali three questions, among them that souls are conscripted hosts, which become acquainted and agree when they meet, and are strangers and differ when they are estranged; and that the dream which comes true is the one whose owner does not wake until the Throne, while the one he wakes before does not come true. {cite:marifat-sahaba-abu-nuaym p.2002-2003}

## In the Encyclopaedia of Islam: early life and family
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, describes Ali as the cousin and son-in-law of [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] and the fourth caliph, and as one of the first to believe in his mission. Whether he was the second believer after [[person/khadija|Khadija]] or the third after Khadija and [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] was, the article says, much disputed between Shi'is and Sunnis. At that time he was about ten or eleven at most, and Muhammad had taken him into his own household to relieve his father [[person/abu-talib|Abu Talib]], who had fallen into poverty {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 403}.

The article calls the narrative of Ali occupying the Prophet's bed on the night of the departure from Mecca open to criticism on several counts. According to that narrative the conspirators found the young cousin asleep there. After returning the objects Muhammad had held in trust, Ali rejoined him at [[place/quba|Quba]]. Some months later he married [[person/fatima|Fatima]], and [[person/hasan-ibn-ali|al-Hasan]] and [[person/husayn-ibn-ali|al-Husayn]] were born of the marriage. He took no other wife during Fatima's lifetime {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 403}.

## Campaigns and services under the Prophet
The article reports that in Muhammad's lifetime Ali took part in almost all the expeditions, often as standard-bearer, and only twice as commander, at [[place/fadak|Fadak]] in 6/628 and in Yemen in 10/632. His courage, it says, later became legendary: at [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]] he killed a large number of Qurayshites, and at [[event/battle-of-khaybar|Khaybar]] he used a heavy door as a shield, and the article attributes the Muslim victory over the Jews there to his ardour. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 403}.

He also served the Prophet as one of his secretaries and was entrusted with missions that the article calls diplomatic {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 403}. After the Prophet's death, the article says, he took part in no military expedition, for reasons unknown. It mentions a report that [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] kept the Qurayshites from going out to the provinces and that [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]] removed the obstacles, and it suggests that Ali may not have wished to leave Medina or that his health kept him back, although several feats are attributed to him at the Camel and Siffin, when he was already sixty according to the article {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 403}.

## The succession and the first caliphs
During the election of Abu Bakr, the article says, Ali stayed in the Prophet's house with [[person/talha-ibn-ubaydullah|Talha]], [[person/zubayr-ibn-al-awwam|al-Zubayr]] and other Companions to watch over the body and prepare the burial. Although urged by [[person/abbas-ibn-abd-al-muttalib|al-Abbas]] and, it is said, by [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-harb|Abu Sufyan]], he made no effort to keep leadership of the community in the hands of the [[group/banu-hashim|Hashimites]]. He maintained his refusal to recognise Abu Bakr for six months. His position was complicated by a question of inheritance: Fatima claimed the lands held by her father, and Abu Bakr rejected the claim on the ground of the saying that prophets have no heirs {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 403} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 404}.

The article doubts that Ali really hoped to succeed Muhammad, noting that the Arabs as a rule chose mature chiefs and that Ali was a little over thirty in 11/633 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 404}.

Under the caliphs before him, the article says, Muslim authors describe Ali as a valued counsellor, and it judges it probable that he was consulted on legal matters because of his knowledge of the Qur'an and the sunna. On the army register he held a view opposed to Umar's, recommending that the whole revenue be distributed without holding anything in reserve. During the reigns of Umar and Uthman he held no office except the lieutenancy of Medina while Umar travelled to Palestine and Syria, and for that reason, the article notes, he alone was absent from the meeting at al-Jabiya. In the received tradition of the consultative council, asked by [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-awf|Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf]] whether he would follow the conduct of the preceding caliphs together with the Qur'an and the sunna, Ali gave an evasive answer, which the article takes as a sign that he was not in full agreement with the policies of Abu Bakr and Umar {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 404}.

## Relations with Uthman
The article reports that under Uthman, Ali and other Companions, notably Talha and al-Zubayr, often accused the caliph of departing from the Qur'an and the sunna, especially in applying the fixed penalties. Ali was among those who demanded the penalty for drinking against [[person/al-walid-ibn-uqba|al-Walid ibn Uqba]], governor of Kufa, and in some accounts carried out the flogging himself. With Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf he reproached Uthman for innovations such as four rak'as at Arafat and Mina in place of two {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 404}.

On political matters the article lists four episodes. Ali went with his sons to bid farewell to [[person/abu-dharr-al-ghifari|Abu Dharr]] when he was exiled, despite Uthman's prohibition. He was an intermediary when the rebels from Egypt negotiated with Uthman. When the rebels besieged the house they asked him to lead them; he refused, but the article judges that his attitude encouraged them and suspects that he agreed with their demand for the caliph's abdication, while excluding any share by him in the bloody end. After his own election, his supporters included persons known to have been hostile to the government on economic questions, among them [[person/malik-al-ashtar|al-Ashtar]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 404}.

The article adds that on becoming caliph he distributed all the sums he found in the treasuries of Medina, Basra and Kufa, which it reads as consistent with the view he had given Umar, and that he is said to have wished to distribute the Sawad but refrained for fear of legal disputes. It sees no ground for calling him an extremist: it says he was hostile to the followers of Abd Allah ibn Saba, and rid himself of them when they exalted him beyond measure. It judges that his insistence on early conversion and service to Islam over birth and administrative ability set the Quraysh against him, while he had the support of most of the [[group/ansar|Ansar]], of other non-Qurayshite Arabs among the early believers and of the provincial fighters {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 404}.

## Election as caliph
After Uthman was killed, the Umayyads fled Medina and the opposition stayed in control; Ali was invited to succeed as the person they respected most. The most commonly accepted date of his election is 18 Dhu al-Hijja 35/17 June 656, and the article says the traditions disagree about his willingness to accept. Some did not yield to pressure and left Medina, among them [[person/abdullah-ibn-umar|Abd Allah ibn Umar]], [[person/saad-ibn-abi-waqqas|Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas]], [[person/al-mughira-ibn-shuba|al-Mughira ibn Shu'ba]], [[person/muhammad-ibn-maslama|Muhammad ibn Maslama]] and [[person/usama-ibn-zayd|Usama ibn Zayd]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 404}.

Against the objection that the election was invalid because a minority made it, the article reports Ali's reply that choosing the caliph was the right of those present in Medina at the time, whether Ansar, Muhajirun or veterans of Badr. The article judges it an error that he let himself be nominated also by rebels with Uthman's blood on their hands, since this exposed him to charges of complicity. He removed Uthman's governors where he could and replaced them with his own, and satisfied the people with distributions of money that the article calls equitable {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 405}.

## The Camel
[[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya]], governor of Syria and a cousin of Uthman, accused Ali of complicity with the murderers and refused homage. [[person/aisha|A'isha]], who had been on pilgrimage at Mecca during the siege, returned there on hearing of Ali's election and campaigned against him; some months later Talha and al-Zubayr joined her and the three set out for Iraq with several hundred troops. Ali followed, but could not overtake them. The article says he had to stop them from taking Iraq, since Syria obeyed only Mu'awiya and Egypt was in anarchy. The three proclaimed that the fixed penalties must apply to all alike and that reform must be carried out; the article finds their motives and their demand for vengeance obscure and thinks social and economic fears more convincing than personal feeling {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 405}.

After the insurgents occupied [[place/basra|Basra]] and massacred many of the besiegers of Uthman's house, Ali raised forces in [[place/kufa|Kufa]] and marched there. An agreement was negotiated, but a brawl provoked by extremists on his side grew into the [[event/battle-of-the-camel|Battle of the Camel]], on 15 Jumada II 36/9 December 656, in which Talha and al-Zubayr died. Ali had A'isha sent back to Medina under escort {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 405}.

## Conflict with Mu'awiya and Siffin
Negotiations with Mu'awiya failed. He demanded the surrender of Uthman's murderers, citing the right of a murdered man's next of kin to vengeance, with a verse of sura 17 as his ground. The article says the sources are vague on Ali's answer, except that Nasr ibn Muzahim reports him holding that Uthman was killed by people outraged at his arbitrary actions, so that the killers were not liable to retaliation. The article judges the real issue to be the pre-eminence of Syria or Iraq and two conceptions of governing the state {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 405}.

The armies, each tens of thousands strong, met on the plain of [[event/battle-of-siffin|Siffin]]. After a truce in Muharram 37/June-July 657 and a week of combat came the violent fighting of the night of clamour, 10 Safar 37/28 July 657. [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn al-As]] then advised Mu'awiya to have Qur'an copies raised on lances, which the article says was not a surrender but an invitation to settle the matter by consulting the Qur'an. The sources swell the dead to 70,000 or more. The article says Ali was forced by his followers, among whom were many Qur'an reciters, to accept arbitration and to choose his arbitrator from the neutrals {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 405}.

## The arbitration and the Kharijites
A convention drawn up at Siffin in Safar 37/657 named [[person/abu-musa-al-ashari|Abu Musa al-Ash'ari]] for Ali and Amr ibn al-As for Mu'awiya, to consult the Qur'an and then the sunna. The article, following Veccia Vaglieri, says their task was to decide whether Uthman's acts were arbitrary departures from divine law; if so his killing could be seen as just, and if not Mu'awiya's claim to vengeance was justified, with the loss of the caliphate for Ali following a verdict for Mu'awiya {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 405} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 406}.

Some men had already protested at Siffin with the cry that judgment belongs to God alone. They cited sura 49, which they read as requiring Ali to go on fighting the rebellious party. On the return to Kufa a group of some thousands stopped at Harura, which gave them the name Harurites. Ali visited their camp and reconciled them, the article thinks by concessions, but when Abu Musa was sent to the meeting a group of 3,000 or 4,000 left Kufa and some hundreds left Basra, gathering at al-Nahrawan; these were the [[group/khawarij|Kharijites]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 406}.

Mu'awiya arrived first at the meeting place in Ramadan 37/February 658, and Ali sent Abu Musa with his cousin [[person/abdullah-ibn-abbas|Ibn Abbas]], excusing himself because of the dissidents. The sources are vague or contradictory on place and date; the article, following Veccia Vaglieri, believes a first meeting of six persons took place at [[place/dumat-al-jandal|Dumat al-Jandal]] and a second at [[place/adhruh|Adhruh]] in Sha'ban 38/January 659. It reasons that the arbitrators agreed at Dumat al-Jandal that Uthman had committed no breach of trust and agreed to keep silent. The Syrians pledged to Mu'awiya in Dhu al-Qa'da 37/April 658, and Ali publicly protested against both arbitrators and declared their sentence contrary to the Qur'an and the sunna {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 406}.

Ali then marched against the dissidents at al-Nahrawan. Their demand that he confess to impiety was refused, and after offering safety to those who submitted he attacked them on 9 Safar 38/17 July 658. The article calls it a massacre rather than a battle, says contemporary opinion condemned it, and says Ali seems to have been the first to regret it; defections increased. In the same month Mu'awiya took Egypt {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 406}.

At Adhruh the article reports from the sources that Abu Musa and Amr agreed to depose both Ali and Mu'awiya and leave the choice to a committee, but that Amr in his public speech declared Ali deposed and confirmed Mu'awiya. The article accepts the scene, although some modern historians reject it, and calls Amr's declaration a disloyal act that later opinion judged a treacherous trick. The conference ended without a decision on the caliphate {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 406} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 407}.

## Last years, death and burial
Ali was still regarded as caliph by his supporters and Mu'awiya by his. In 39/659 the article describes Ali as confined to Kufa and passive, in 40/660 as without authority in the two holy cities and unable to stop Mu'awiya's attack on Yemen. A Kharijite, [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-muljam|Ibn Muljam]], struck him with a poisoned sword at the door of the mosque of Kufa, to avenge the men killed at al-Nahrawan, and he died about two days later aged 62 or 63. The article calls questionable the tradition that Ibn Muljam was one of a group planning to kill three men held responsible for the civil war {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 407}.

His burial place was kept secret, evidently for fear that the body would be dug up and profaned. In the time of [[person/harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]] it was announced that the tomb had been identified a few miles from Kufa, where a sanctuary arose and the town of [[place/najaf|al-Najaf]] grew, surrounded by a great cemetery, which the article attributes to pious Shi'is' wish to be buried near their Imams {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 407}.

## Family, writings and character
The article says he had two nicknames, Haydara, meaning lion, and Abu Turab. It thinks the second was probably given contemptuously by his enemies and later reinterpreted as an honorific through invented episodes; the report in Sahih Muslim cited in the sections above, which has the Prophet giving the name, presents it differently. He is credited with fourteen sons and nineteen daughters by nine wives and several concubines. Only three sons, al-Hasan, al-Husayn and [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-hanafiyya|Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya]], played a historical role, and five in all left descendants {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 407}.

He was reputed to have a deep knowledge of the Qur'an and to be among its best reciters; the article rejects the statement that he compiled a recension. Speeches, sermons, letters and sayings are ascribed to him and found in the [[work/nahj-al-balagha|Nahj al-Balagha]], compiled in the fifth/eleventh century by [[person/al-sharif-al-radi|al-Sharif al-Radi]]. The article judges his gifts as an orator remarkable, but not his poetry {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 407}.

## How scholars assess him
The article calls his personality difficult to grasp, because his will was paralysed or modified by events and by pressure from his followers, his speeches are obscure and hard to separate from forgeries, and the sources are tendentious or reticent. It rejects the hostile judgment of Lammens and calls Caetani's milder view vague, and holds that neither brought out Ali's religiosity. It treats the many reports of his austerity, observance and scruples as no reason for suspicion, and cites his preventing the enslavement of the vanquished women and children after the Camel and his grief and prayer over the dead. Its own judgment is that obedience to the divine law was the keynote of his conduct, that his excessive rigorism made him inflexible, lacking the political flexibility of Mu'awiya, and that his programme was utopian rather than uncertain {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 407} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 408}.

## Differing positions
**Sunni position.** Sunni tradition counts Ali as the fourth of the Rashidun caliphs, as the sections of this page drawn from Ibn al-Athir and Abu Nu'aym set out.

**Shi'i position, as the article reports it.** The article says that Shi'is have always maintained that the Prophet meant the succession to pass to his cousin and son-in-law, relying on sayings they invent or interpret in the light of their beliefs; it refers readers to its article on the Shi'a for their doctrines and legends. It also records the Sunni-Shi'i dispute over the order of the first believers {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 403} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 404}.

**Academic view, the article's own.** It states that it is certain the Prophet did not voice the wish for Ali to succeed him in his last illness, and it traces the half-divine aureole around Ali, with Caetani, partly to his relationship with Muhammad and partly to the personal impression he left on contemporaries {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 404} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 408}.

## For young visitors
Ali ibn Abi Talib was the cousin of the Prophet. He fought at Badr as a young man, and became the fourth caliph. He was killed in the mosque of Kufa while going out to the dawn prayer, and was buried at night.

## Notes for researchers
- The year of Ali's death, 40 AH, is given by Abu Nu'aym and Ibn al-Athir; some later writers say 34 AH, which Abu Nu'aym considers a mistake. The dispute is recorded as reported here and not settled.
- The day of the wound differs: Abu Nu'aym says he was struck on the nineteenth night of Ramadan, Friday; Ibn al-Athir says he stayed through Friday and Saturday and died on the night of Sunday, eleven nights before the end of the month. Both place the death on the night of Sunday.
- Ali's age at death is given as sixty-three by al-Waqidi, whom Ibn al-Athir says he finds "more established"; another report, from Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya, gives something close to sixty-five, though he had outlived his father's age.
- On who was first to embrace Islam, the reports conflict as Ibn al-Athir gives them; we have not weighed them against each other.
- The virtues reports ("I am the city of knowledge", "You are my brother", the cloak, and the report of Ghadir) are given by Ibn al-Athir with their chains, and he notes some objections to the chains. We have not judged any of these hadiths here, nor set out their different interpretations; they will be set out in their place with the other sources.
- The fighting of Ali at the Camel, Siffin and Nahrawan is a matter on which the community differs, and it will need a fuller set of attributed views when it is written.
- The full biography of Ali in Ibn al-Athir ("Usd al-Ghaba", pp. 872–886) has been read here. The history and biography books (al-Tabari, Ibn Hisham, Ibn Sa'd) have not yet been read; their details will be added when extracted.

## On the secret call (Al-Rahiq al-Makhtum, pp. 75, 77)
The book counts him among the four who embraced Islam on the first day of the call, when he was a boy raised in the Prophet's care. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.75} {cite:rahma-lil-alamin 1/50} Ibn Hisham relates that [[person/abu-talib|Abu Talib]] saw the Prophet and Ali praying once, spoke to them about it, and when he understood the matter he ordered them to persist ([[concept/al-salat-qabl-al-isra|prayer before the Night Journey]]). {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.77} {cite:ibn-hisham-sira 1/247}

## At the Prophet's last illness
Ali left the Prophet on the day of his death, and when people asked him about his condition he said: "He has risen this morning, by God's praise, healed." Al-Abbas said to him later: "You will be a slave of the staff within three days", and asked that the Prophet be asked about the affair after him. Ali refused and said: "If we ask him and he refuses us, no one after him will give it to us." {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/304} Ibn Ishaq relates from Aisha that Ali and al-Fadl ibn al-Abbas walked between the Prophet and Aisha's house. {cite:ibn-hisham-saqqa 4/298} See [[event/death-of-the-prophet|the death of the Prophet]].

## Sleeping in the Prophet's bed (Al-Rahiq al-Makhtum p. 163)
On the night of the departure the Prophet told him to sleep in his bed and wrap himself in the green Hadrami cloak, so the besiegers saw him asleep until morning ([[event/hijra-night-escape|the Prophet's departure from his house]]). {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.163} Then the Quraysh struck him and held him for an hour, to learn the news. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.165}

## Commander of the Muhajirun at Badr (Al-Rahiq al-Makhtum pp. 205, 210)
The Prophet put him over the battalion of the Muhajirun, and sent him with al-Zubayr and Sa'd to scout the caravan, and they seized two boys ([[event/badr-expedition-setup|the preparation before Badr]]). {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.205} {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.210}

## For Sprouts
Ali ibn Abi Talib was the Prophet's cousin, brave and wise, the fourth righteous caliph who loved justice and truth.

## For Explorers
Ali ibn Abi Talib was the cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet, married to Fatima al-Zahra, and the first youth to embrace Islam. Renowned for supreme courage, profound eloquence, and judicial wisdom, he served as the fourth Rightly Guided Caliph and holds an honored place in the hearts of all Muslims.

## For Scholars
Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Abi Talib (c. 23 BH – 40 AH / 600–661 CE), the Prophet's cousin, son-in-law married to Fatima al-Zahra, the fourth Rightly Guided Caliph (35–40 AH) in Sunni tradition, and the first Imam in Shi'i tradition. The first male youth to accept Islam, he risked his life in the Prophet's bed on the night of the Hijra. Famed for valor at Badr, Uhud, and Khaybar, and acclaimed for judicial wisdom and supreme Arabic eloquence, his caliphate emphasized rigorous social justice, strict administrative accountability, and personal asceticism.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lectures: God-consciousness and piety

The Sheikh relays a definition of piety attributed to Ali: that God does not find you where He forbade, and does not miss you where He commanded (needs_source). {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-naba-al-safi/ep03 00:06:00}
