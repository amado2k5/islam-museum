## Summary
Sharaf al-Din Muhammad ibn Sharif ibn Yusuf, known as Ibn al-Wahid, was born in or around Damascus, perhaps at Zar'a in the Hawran, as his epithet al-Zar'i suggests. He travelled to Baalbek and then to [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]], where he learned all six round scripts from [[person/yaqut-al-mustasimi|Yaqut]], then moved to [[place/cairo|Cairo]], entered the service of the amir and later sultan [[person/baybars-al-jashankir|Baybars al-Jashankir]] and became legal secretary (katib al-shari'a) at the [[building/mosque-of-al-hakim|Mosque of al-Hakim]], earning the epithet al-Misri. Besides his skill with the pen he was a good linguist and a poet; he died in 1311 aged 61. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 385} {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 386}

## The Baybars Qur'an
His most famous work is the [[object/baybars-al-jashankir-quran|seven-part Qur'an]] written in gold for Baybars al-Jashankir. Blair notes that he followed in many respects the traditions of Yaqut's followers in Iran and Iraq — using half-baghdadi sheets as [[person/ahmad-al-suhrawardi|Ahmad al-Suhrawardi]] had — but departed from them in the seven-part division and the even number of lines (six), perhaps following a Syrian tradition such as the four-line Qur'an made for Nur al-Din ibn Zangi at Damascus in 562/1166–7. His team was larger than those of his Baghdad contemporaries: the illuminators Abu Bakr, known as Sandal, and Muhammad ibn Mubadir, and the outliner Aydughdi ibn Abdallah al-Badri. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 386} {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 388}

## His script
His hand is rounded, with many upturned and sometimes reversed tails; words are crowded without spaces and do not sit on a baseline; alif has a triangular projection at the top and a foot below, final ha' is left open, and there are many unauthorised connections. Mamluk sources call this hand thuluth ash'ar or ash'ar, a hybrid said to combine muhaqqaq, thuluth and naskh, which makes the manuscript one of the rare examples of a hand labelled by contemporaries. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 385} {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 386} {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 388} {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 389}
