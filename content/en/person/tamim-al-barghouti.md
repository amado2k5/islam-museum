## Summary
Tamim al-Barghouti is a Palestinian-Egyptian poet, columnist and political scientist — the man his audiences call "the Poet of Jerusalem" — and one of the most widely read and heard poets in the Arabic language. The Muslim 500 lists him in Arts & Culture; Wikipedia records his Boston University doctorate and his position among the Arab world's most popular poets. {cite:muslim500-tamim-al-barghouti} {cite:wikipedia-tamim-al-barghouti}

## A poet's lineage
Al-Barghouti was born in Cairo in 1977 into a literary family: his father is the Palestinian poet Mourid Barghouti, his mother the Egyptian novelist and scholar Radwa Ashour — a household in which Arabic letters were the air itself. He grew up between Cairo and exile, absorbing both the classical Arabic poetic tradition and the Palestinian experience of dispossession that his parents' generation carried. {cite:wikipedia-tamim-al-barghouti}

## Two callings
He took his doctorate in political science from Boston University in 2004 and has held academic posts — including at the American University in Cairo and Georgetown — alongside a daily practice as a columnist for Egyptian and pan-Arab newspapers and a poet whose performances fill stadiums. The scholar and the poet run in the same man: his political analysis is widely read, but it is the poetry, delivered in his own resonant voice, that draws the crowds. {cite:wikipedia-tamim-al-barghouti} {cite:muslim500-tamim-al-barghouti}

## The voice and the poems
Al-Barghouti writes in classical Arabic forms with a modern ear, and his recitations — notably the poem al-Quds — became anthems of Arab public life, their lines carried in protests, weddings and broadcasts across the Arab world. Poems like Qamar, on love, and his epic-length works on Jerusalem and Egyptian politics circulate among audiences who memorise rather than merely read. {cite:muslim500-tamim-al-barghouti} {cite:wikipedia-tamim-al-barghouti}

## Significance
In the museum's record, al-Barghouti stands for the living public poet of Arabic — the heir of two poets who became the voice of a third generation's longings, whose verse demonstrates that in the Arab world poetry still moves mass audiences as it did a thousand years ago. {cite:muslim500-tamim-al-barghouti} {cite:wikipedia-tamim-al-barghouti}

## The poet in the square
Al-Barghouti's significance is also institutional: he belongs to the generation of Arab poets who reclaimed the public square after the 2011 uprisings, when his poem al-Quds was recited in Cairo's Tahrir and across the Arab world by demonstrators who had never read his books. That movement — the return of the classical Arabic ode to revolutionary use — is the phenomenon his record documents; the museum keeps it alongside the academic career and the family lineage as the three dimensions of a poet whose verse travels further than his name. {cite:muslim500-tamim-al-barghouti} {cite:wikipedia-tamim-al-barghouti}

## The poet as citizen
Al-Barghouti's record is the museum's example of the poet who treats verse as a form of citizenship: the Palestinian-Egyptian who understood that the classical Arabic ode was the only form that could carry the weight of his people's history, and who made the case in a voice that fills stadiums. The verse is the story; the voice is the instrument; and the result — a Muslim poet whose lines are recited by crowds who have never read his books — is the profile his Muslim 500 entry records. The museum notes the career and leaves the recitation to those who carry it. {cite:muslim500-tamim-al-barghouti}
