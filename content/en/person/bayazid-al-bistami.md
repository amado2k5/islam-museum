## Overview
Abu Yazid Tayfur ibn Isa ibn Surushan al-Bistami, one of the leading figures of Islamic Sufism. He was born around 188 AH (804 CE) and died around 261 AH (875 CE). {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba}

The encyclopaedia says that his influence was great on his contemporaries and on those who came after them. {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba}

## Encyclopaedia of Islam account
The Encyclopaedia of Islam (2nd ed.), unsigned, gives his full name as Abu Yazid Tayfur b. 'Isa b. Surushan al-Bistami. He spent much of his life in Bistam, in the province of Kumis, after a period when orthodox theologians' hostility forced him to live away from home. He died there in 261/874 or 264/877-8. The Ilkhanid Uljaytu Muhammad Khudabanda is said to have had a dome built over his grave in 713/1313. He wrote nothing, but some five hundred of his sayings were handed down, in part extremely daring and expressing an experience of the self merged with God. His disciple and attendant Abu Musa 'Isa b. Adam, son of his elder brother, first collected them, and al-Junayd received such sayings in Persian and translated them into Arabic. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 181}

Other transmitters were 'Isa's son Musa, known as 'Ammi, and the "lesser Tayfur" b. 'Isa. Among visitors who recorded his sayings were Abu Musa al-Dabili of Dabil in Armenia and Abu Ishaq Ibrahim al-Harawi, known as Istanba, a pupil of Ibrahim b. Adham. Abu Yazid was a friend of Dhu 'l-Nun al-Misri, and al-Junayd wrote a commentary on his utterances, of which parts survive in al-Sarraj's Luma'. The fullest source is the Kitab al-Nur fi Kalimat Abi Yazid Tayfur by Abu 'l-Fadl Muhammad b. 'Ali al-Sahlaji al-Bistami (born 389/998-9), an incomplete edition of which exists. Al-Sahlaji's authorities include Ibn Baboya, who met him in 419/416. The Kitab al-Qasd ila 'llah of the pseudo-Junayd embroiders Abu Yazid's "journey to heaven". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 182}

His teacher in Sufism was an unlettered mystic called Abu 'Ali al-Sindi, whom he taught the Quranic verses needed for prayer and who in return introduced him to mysticism; the article finds Indian influence possible. Unlike Abu Ishaq al-Kazaruni or Abu Sa'id b. Abi 'l-Khayr, he did not engage in social work, but he would have been ready to save humanity from hell by vicarious suffering, and he criticised the punishment of the damned. His sense of the numinous was strong, with a sense of horror before God, and his aspiration was to free himself from every barrier between him and God. He described this process in autobiographical sayings. Once he had shed his "I" in annihilation and reached the goal, his changed self-consciousness produced the famous ecstatic utterances, "Glory be to me! How great is my majesty!" and "I am the throne and the footstool". A later legend has him solve riddles put to him in a Christian monastery, which converted to Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 182}

## His ecstatic sayings in Talbis Iblis and al-Luma'
In the section on ecstatic sayings and claims of [[work/talbis-iblis|Talbis Iblis]], [[person/ibn-al-jawzi|Ibn al-Jawzi]] relates with his chain from Tayfur the Younger, from his uncle, Abu Yazid's servant, that he heard him say "Glory be to me, glory be to me, how great is my state"; he comments that if it is authentic he may have been quoting God, and he gives other claims attributed to him whose ugliness, he says, is not hidden. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 347} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 350} On the other side, [[person/abu-nasr-al-sarraj|Abu Nasr al-Sarraj]] defends Abu Yazid in [[work/kitab-al-luma-fi-al-tasawwuf|al-Luma']], saying he visited Bistam, where Abu Yazid's family denied the story, and that the scholars of his region seek blessing at his grave. {cite:luma-sarraj-jadir-dar-al-fath p. 526} All the views are set out in [[question/shath-abu-yazid|the card on Abu Yazid's "Glory be to me"]].

## In Hilyat al-Awliya
[[person/abu-nuaym-al-asbahani-ahmad|Abu Nu'aym]] gives him notice 458 in the tenth volume of [[work/hilyat-al-awliya|Hilyat al-Awliya]]. He opens in rhymed prose, describing him as a wanderer lost to all limits, and says that his expressions are veiled: a reassurance to those who understand them and a trial to those who reject them. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/33-34}

The first report is a prayer in which Abu Yazid marvels not at his own love for God, since he is a poor servant, but at God's love for him, since He is a mighty King. Ibrahim al-Harawi then relates that he heard him list four mistakes of his early days: he thought that it was he who remembered, knew, loved and sought God, and at the end he saw that God's remembrance, knowledge, love and seeking had come first. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/34}

Asked why he did not travel, he answered that his Companion does not travel and he stays with Him. He then gave the image of rivers: they roar while they run, and when they reach the sea and mix with it they fall silent, and the sea is neither increased nor lessened by them. Abu Musa relates that for thirty years he rinsed his mouth whenever he wished to mention God, out of reverence. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/34-35}

[[person/yahya-ibn-muadh-al-razi|Yahya ibn Mu'adh]] relates his saying that he roamed the field of tawhid until he came out to the house of tafrid (isolation of the One), and then drank a draught after which he never thirsted. The transmitter Yusuf ibn al-Husayn adds that he used to hear something close to this from [[person/dhu-al-nun-al-misri|Dhu al-Nun]]. The notice also has his saying that he was absent from God for thirty years, his absence being his own remembering of Him, and that when he left that off he found Him in every state. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/35}

A man came asking for counsel. Abu Yazid told him to look at the sky and asked who created it, then said that its Creator sees him wherever he is, so he should be wary of Him. Told "we hear you pass through the air", he said that a carrion-eating bird passes through the air and a believer is nobler than a bird. Ahmad ibn Harb sent him a mat to pray on at night; he wrote back that he had gathered the worship of the people of the heavens and the earth, put it in a pillow and laid it under his cheek. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/35-36}

On criticism of the self he says that as long as a servant thinks anyone in creation is worse than himself, he is proud. He says he strove for thirty years and found nothing harder than knowledge and following it, and that the disagreement of the scholars is a mercy except in pure tawhid. His wife relates his saying that he had treated everything and found nothing harder to treat than his own self. He names three people as the most veiled from God: the ascetic by his asceticism, the worshipper by his worship and the scholar by his knowledge. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/36-37}

On gnosis he says that the knower's concern is what he hopes for and the ascetic's concern is what he eats. Asked when a man reaches the rank of men in this matter, he said: when he knows the faults of his own self. Asked for God's greatest name, he answered that it has no fixed limit; it is the emptying of the heart for His oneness. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/37} {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/39}

His position on marvels is plain in the notice. Walking on water is no wonder, and if you see a man given marvels until he rises into the air, do not be taken in by him until you see how he stands at command and prohibition, at keeping the limits and performing the law. Yahya ibn Mu'adh wrote to him that he was drunk from how much he had drunk of the cup of love; he replied that another had drunk the seas of the heavens and the earth and was not yet quenched. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/39-40}

Abu Nu'aym closes by saying he has kept to this much of his speech because of its deep allusions, which only one who dives into his sea can grasp. He then says that hadith transmission from him is not preserved. He quotes a hadith on weak certainty that a preacher he met in Baghdad and Basra, known as Abu al-Fath ibn al-Himsi, related with a chain passing through Abu Yazid, and he warns that this hadith was assembled and attached to Abu Yazid, that the fault lies with Abu al-Fath, and that Abu al-Fath was caught with other hadiths he had assembled. He adds that the shaykhs of the East were treated by [[person/al-sulami|Abu Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami]] in [[work/tabaqat-al-sufiyya-sulami|Tabaqat al-Sufiyya]], and that he includes the best known of them in brief. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/41-42}
