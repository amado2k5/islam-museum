## Overview
Abu Yazid Tayfur ibn Isa ibn Surushan al-Bistami, one of the leading figures of Islamic Sufism. He was born around 188 AH (804 CE) and died around 261 AH (875 CE). {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba}

The encyclopaedia says that his influence was great on his contemporaries and on those who came after them. {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba}

## Encyclopaedia of Islam account
The Encyclopaedia of Islam (2nd ed.), unsigned, gives his full name as Abu Yazid Tayfur b. 'Isa b. Surushan al-Bistami. He spent much of his life in Bistam, in the province of Kumis, after a period when orthodox theologians' hostility forced him to live away from home. He died there in 261/874 or 264/877-8. The Ilkhanid Uljaytu Muhammad Khudabanda is said to have had a dome built over his grave in 713/1313. He wrote nothing, but some five hundred of his sayings were handed down, in part extremely daring and expressing an experience of the self merged with God. His disciple and attendant Abu Musa 'Isa b. Adam, son of his elder brother, first collected them, and al-Junayd received such sayings in Persian and translated them into Arabic. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 181}

Other transmitters were 'Isa's son Musa, known as 'Ammi, and the "lesser Tayfur" b. 'Isa. Among visitors who recorded his sayings were Abu Musa al-Dabili of Dabil in Armenia and Abu Ishaq Ibrahim al-Harawi, known as Istanba, a pupil of Ibrahim b. Adham. Abu Yazid was a friend of Dhu 'l-Nun al-Misri, and al-Junayd wrote a commentary on his utterances, of which parts survive in al-Sarraj's Luma'. The fullest source is the Kitab al-Nur fi Kalimat Abi Yazid Tayfur by Abu 'l-Fadl Muhammad b. 'Ali al-Sahlaji al-Bistami (born 389/998-9), an incomplete edition of which exists. Al-Sahlaji's authorities include Ibn Baboya, who met him in 419/416. The Kitab al-Qasd ila 'llah of the pseudo-Junayd embroiders Abu Yazid's "journey to heaven". {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 182}

His teacher in Sufism was an unlettered mystic called Abu 'Ali al-Sindi, whom he taught the Quranic verses needed for prayer and who in return introduced him to mysticism; the article finds Indian influence possible. Unlike Abu Ishaq al-Kazaruni or Abu Sa'id b. Abi 'l-Khayr, he did not engage in social work, but he would have been ready to save humanity from hell by vicarious suffering, and he criticised the punishment of the damned. His sense of the numinous was strong, with a sense of horror before God, and his aspiration was to free himself from every barrier between him and God. He described this process in autobiographical sayings. Once he had shed his "I" in annihilation and reached the goal, his changed self-consciousness produced the famous ecstatic utterances, "Glory be to me! How great is my majesty!" and "I am the throne and the footstool". A later legend has him solve riddles put to him in a Christian monastery, which converted to Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 182}

## His ecstatic sayings in Talbis Iblis and al-Luma'
In the section on ecstatic sayings and claims of [[work/talbis-iblis|Talbis Iblis]], [[person/ibn-al-jawzi|Ibn al-Jawzi]] relates with his chain from Tayfur the Younger, from his uncle, Abu Yazid's servant, that he heard him say "Glory be to me, glory be to me, how great is my state"; he comments that if it is authentic he may have been quoting God, and he gives other claims attributed to him whose ugliness, he says, is not hidden. {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 347} {cite:talbis-iblis-muniriyya-1928 p. 350} On the other side, [[person/abu-nasr-al-sarraj|Abu Nasr al-Sarraj]] defends Abu Yazid in [[work/kitab-al-luma-fi-al-tasawwuf|al-Luma']], saying he visited Bistam, where Abu Yazid's family denied the story, and that the scholars of his region seek blessing at his grave. {cite:luma-sarraj-jadir-dar-al-fath p. 526} All the views are set out in [[question/shath-abu-yazid|the card on Abu Yazid's "Glory be to me"]].

## In Hilyat al-Awliya
His notice in *Hilyat al-Awliya* (volume 10, no. 458, pp. 33-42) opens with verses by Abu Nu'aym describing him as "the lost one, the lone ecstatic, the unique of Bistam, Abu Yazid," then with his saying: "There is no wonder in Your keeping me alive while I am a poor servant; the wonder is Your loving me while You are the Omnipotent King." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/33-34}

Ibrahim al-Harawi relates of him: "I erred in my beginnings in four things: I imagined I remembered Him, knew Him, loved Him and sought Him; when I had finished, I saw His remembrance preceded my remembrance, His knowledge preceded my knowledge, His love was prior to my love, and His seeking me came first — until He sought me." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/34}

Among his most famous sayings in the notice: "I was absent from God for thirty years, and my absence from Him was remembrance of Him; when I stole away from Him I found Him in every state, as though He were I"; and "I kept roaming the field of tawhid until I came out to the house of tafrid, then kept roaming the house of tafrid until I came out to da'wa, and I drank from its cup a draught after which I never thirsted again for remembrance of Him." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/34-35}

He relates that a man asked him for counsel, looked up at the sky, and was told "Who created this?" He said "God," and Abu Yazid replied: "He who created it was not looking at you where you were — beware Him." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/35}

On annihilation he says: "I cast the world off three times, and called to Him for succour: 'My God, I call on You with a call that has none left but You'; when He knew the truth of the call from my heart and my soul's despair, the first thing that came upon me of the answer to that call was that He made me forget myself utterly and placed all creatures before me while I turned away from them." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/36}

On knowledge he rules: "Knowledge in the Essence of the Real is ignorance; learning in the reality of knowledge is wrongdoing; the allusion of the alluder is association in allusion"; "The knower's concern is what he hopes for, the renouncer's concern is what he eats — blessed is he whose two concerns are one"; "The knower is above what he says and the scholar below what he says"; "Hunger is a cloud, and when the servant hungers, wisdom rains upon his heart"; and "Look out for the hour when you see none in heaven but Him and none on earth but you." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/37-39}

On marvels (karamat) he says: "If you see a man given marvels until he is raised into the air, do not be deceived by him until you see how he fares at command and prohibition, keeping the limit and performing the law"; and "When you stand before God, make yourself as a Magian about to cut the sacred thread before Him." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/40}

Abu Nu'aym closes the notice saying he confined himself to this much "because of the deep allusions it contains, which only one who dives into its sea and drinks from the purity of its breast's waves can reach," then relates a hadith from him on the weakness of certainty and notes that "this hadith was grafted onto Abu Yazid — the inclination in it belongs to our shaykh Abu al-Fath." {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/41}
