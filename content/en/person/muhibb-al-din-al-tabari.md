## Summary
Muhibb al-Din al-Tabari was shaykh of the Meccan sanctuary, head of the Shafi'is and hadith master of the Hijaz; he wrote a large six-volume work on legal hadith (al-Ahkam) and took it to Yemen, where he read it to its sultan. His books include al-Riyad al-Nadira on the virtues of the ten, Dhakha'ir al-'Uqba on the Prophet's kin, al-Simt al-Thamin on the Mothers of the Believers, al-Qira li-Qasid Umm al-Qura and a commentary on al-Tanbih; al-Dimyati, Ibn al-Attar, Ibn al-Khabbaz and al-Birzali transmitted from him. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 743}

## Name, lineage and family
He was Ahmad ibn Abdullah ibn Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr ibn Muhammad ibn Ibrahim, a Meccan Shafi'i, known by the kunyas Abu Ja'far and Abu al-Abbas and the title Muhibb al-Din. He is said to have first been called Muhyi al-Din and to have disliked it; on a visit to Medina he praised the Prophet in a poem and asked that his reward be the removal of that title — the story is in al-'Iqd al-Thamin. {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.31} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.32} "Al-Tabari" refers to Tabaristan; according to several Meccan historians, such as Umar ibn Fahd, the family traced its descent to al-Husayn, and for some six centuries it held teaching posts, judgeships, the Friday sermon and the imamate of the Station of Abraham in Mecca. {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.32} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.33} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.34}

## His age and birth
He lived in Mecca between 615 and 694 AH, a period that saw the end of the Ayyubid state and the rise of the Mamluks in 648 and the sharifs' struggles over the emirate of Mecca. {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.15} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.16} One report puts his birth on 15 Jumada II; al-Birzali, on the authority of Amin al-Din al-Wani, said he was born in 617. {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.36}

## Studies and teachers
He grew up in a learned Meccan family and, according to al-Isnawi, studied law at Qus with Majd al-Din al-Qushayri. {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.39} In Mecca he heard the Sunan of Abu Dawud from Ibn al-Muqayyar of Baghdad, al-Bukhari's Sahih from his father's uncles, Muslim's Sahih and al-Tirmidhi's Jami' from Ya'qub ibn Abi Bakr al-Tabari and Ibn Hibban's Sahih from Sharaf al-Din al-Mursi; he read the Shafi'i manual al-Tanbih with Najm al-Din al-Tabrizi, shaykh of the sanctuary, and studied law with him; scholars of Baghdad, Syria and Egypt gave him licences. {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.39} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.40} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.41} His editor holds that Mecca's role as a meeting place of scholars spared him the usual travel in search of knowledge. {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.43}

## In Yemen
News of him reached [[person/muzaffar-yusuf-rasulid|al-Malik al-Muzaffar Yusuf ibn Ali]], ruler of Yemen, who invited and honoured him; he stayed about two years, wrote poems there longing for Mecca, and was then allowed to return. {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.43} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.44} Al-Muzaffar paid him fifty dinars a month for teaching at the Mansuriyya madrasa in Mecca. {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.36} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.37}

## Students and standing
Those who learned from him include his son Jamal al-Din Muhammad, judge of Mecca, [[person/al-dimyati|Sharaf al-Din al-Dimyati]], Ala' al-Din Ibn al-Attar, [[person/al-birzali|Alam al-Din al-Birzali]], [[person/abu-hayyan-al-gharnati|Abu Hayyan]] and Ibn al-Khabbaz, and he sent his transmissions in writing to al-Dhahabi. {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.42} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.47} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.48} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.49} Abu al-Yumn Ibn Asakir said he never saw him except in prayer, circumambulation, supplication, teaching or writing, and al-Qutb al-Halabi said there was no one like him in the Meccan sanctuary in his time. {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.49} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.50}

## Criticism
His editor notes that Ibn Kathir praised his book on legal hadith while observing that he sometimes cited weak hadiths without saying so, and that Abu Hayyan reproached him for an error in a chain and for including weak and fabricated reports on virtues without warning or chains. {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.50} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.51}

## Works
His editor counts more than forty works, mostly hadith and then law, among them: in Qur'anic studies al-Kafi fi Gharib al-Qur'an; in hadith Safwat al-Qira on the Prophet's pilgrimage; in law al-Ahkam in large, middle and small versions, al-Muharrar written for al-Malik al-Muzaffar, and a ten-volume commentary on al-Tanbih; in sira and virtues [[work/al-riyad-al-nadira|al-Riyad al-Nadira]], Dhakha'ir al-'Uqba, al-Simt al-Thamin and Khulasat al-Siyar; and a diwan of poetry and a book on the orthography of the Medinan Uthmanic codex. {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.52} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.53} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.54} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.55} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.56}

## Death
He died in Mecca in Jumada II 694 AH on the soundest report, at about seventy-nine, and was buried at al-Ma'la; other months of that year are also given, and his son Jamal al-Din Muhammad died a few days before him. {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.37} {cite:riyad-nadira-dar-al-gharb-1996 p.38}
