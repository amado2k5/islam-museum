## Overview
[[person/umar-khayyam|Umar Khayyam]], who is Umar ibn Ibrahim al-Khayyam, Abu al-Fath, a mathematician, astronomer, philosopher and poet {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba p.628}. The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, presents him as a scholar and poet of the [[polity/seljuk-empire|Seljuk]] period, with his life and the quatrains attributed to him treated by one author and his mathematics, astronomy, mechanics and philosophy by another. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 882} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 886} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 889}

## Life
The encyclopedia gives his birth as 439 AH and about 1048 CE, and his death as 526 AH and about 1131 CE. Little is known of his life {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba p.628}.

This is one chronology, not agreement among references: Unat gives birth proposals between1039 and1048 and deaths between1123 and1132, while Stanford narrows death to1124–1129. The older data remain marked as a tradition rather than silently harmonized {cite:tdv-omer-hayyam-unat pp.66–68} {cite:sep-umar-khayyam sec.1}.

## Contemporary accounts and dates

The Encyclopaedia of Islam notes that he is called al-Imam Abu Hafs Umar ibn Ibrahim al-Khayyami in the Mizan al-hikma that [[person/al-khazini|Abd al-Rahman al-Khazini]] composed in 515/1121, and that al-Khazini had personal links with him. The article declines to speculate on whether the name points to a family of tent-makers, and adds that catalogues file him under several initial letters. The kunya Abu Hafs given here differs from the Abu al-Fath of the older entry above, and this page keeps both without choosing {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 882}.

For his life the article relies on two contemporaries. Nizami-i Arudi-i Samarqandi, who wrote in about 551/1156, says that in 506/1112, while in Khayyam's service, he heard his master foretell his own death. He places Khayyam's reputation at Marw in 508/1114 above that of all the astrologers and astronomers at the sultan's court. In 530/1135 he visited the tomb of his former master, four years after the death according to one manuscript and longer according to another. The article reads these accounts as inspired by admiration {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 882}.

Ali ibn Zayd al-Bayhaqi, born in 499/1105 and so much younger, wrote the first biography. He recalls going with his father in 507/1113 to a gathering led by Khayyam, who put a question on an astronomical topic. He describes a man expert in philosophy, in all branches of mathematics and in medicine, born in [[place/nishapur|Nishapur]] like his father and grandfather, a skilled teacher and author, strict in character and proud of his learning. He says Khayyam was influenced by [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]] through one of that philosopher's disciples, was often consulted, and answered a question from [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]]. He adds that Khayyam often travelled to [[place/isfahan|Isfahan]] to visit the observatory that [[person/malikshah|Malik Shah]] built after 467/1076 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 882} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 883}.

On the dates the article reports that Bayhaqi gives a horoscope of Khayyam's birth, from which Swami Govinda Tirtha calculated 18 Dhu al-Qada 439/18 May 1048. A date for his death of 12 Muharram 526/14 December 1131 rests on a later work of 867/1460, and Mu'in doubted it. The article judges 439 entirely plausible and 526 dubious, and says that general opinion places the death around 517/1123. It adds that after Malik Shah's death he seems to have lived away from the court {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 883}.

Further traces are two treatises dated 469/1077 and 472/1080, and a remark by [[person/al-zamakhshari|al-Zamakhshari]] that Khayyam liked to frequent his circle, knew the thought of the poet al-Maarri and was of obstinate character. The poet [[person/sanai|Sana'i]] wrote to him from Nishapur, asking for help as a man of influence in the town. The article concludes that early sources show an important scholar with as yet unsuspected poetic talent, and that later biographers such as al-Shahrazuri and al-Qifti amplified the early reports, sometimes imaginatively {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 883}.

## Samarkand and mathematics

MacTutor associates his1070 move to [[place/samarqand|Samarkand]] with Abu Tahir's support and the algebra treatise, then describes Malik Shah's patronage and the observing/calendar project at Isfahan {cite:khayyam-mactutor life}. That context does not authenticate every court story: MacTutor challenges the childhood-schoolfriend legend involving [[person/nizam-al-mulk|Nizam al-Mulk]] and [[person/hasan-i-sabbah|Hasan-i Sabbah]] through their different ages; patronage does not establish that classroom friendship {cite:khayyam-mactutor childhood legend}.

In his [[invention/khayyam-geometric-cubics|geometric cubic methods]], he organized fourteen irreducible forms under positive-coefficient conventions using conic intersections, aware of particular cases solved earlier {cite:sep-umar-khayyam sec.4.1}. Stanford names al-Mahani, al-Khazin and al-Layth, explaining that this was not a general radical formula and that multiple-root analysis was incomplete {cite:sep-umar-khayyam sec.4.1}. Woepcke's1851 translation offers a circle/parabola construction and proportional proof at32–34: this is the primary passage read here, not a claim to have collated all manuscripts {cite:khayyam-woepcke-1851 pp.32–34}.

Unat also describes work on difficulties in Euclid, proportional theory and philosophical writings, so the biography is not reducible to a poet who happened to know arithmetic {cite:tdv-omer-hayyam-unat pp.66–68}. MacTutor cautions against making lost work on roots and binomial coefficients proof of the first such triangle; al-Karaji's earlier contribution is not displaced by another celebratory story {cite:khayyam-mactutor mathematics}.

## Mathematics in the Encyclopaedia of Islam

The article places his main scientific contributions in mathematics and astronomy. His first surviving mathematical work, preserved in one manuscript in Tehran, is a treatise on dividing a quadrant of a circle. It reduces the geometric problem to the cubic equation x cubed plus 200x equals 20x squared plus 2000, solves it by the intersection of an equilateral hyperbola and a circle, and also gives an approximate numerical solution {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 886} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 887}.

His full theory of cubic equations is in an Arabic treatise on proofs in algebra and almucabala, written in Samarkand for the judge Abu Tahir and published by Woepcke from manuscripts in Paris, Cairo, London, Leiden and the Vatican. It classifies 25 equations with positive coefficients: one linear, five quadratic, five cubic reducible to lower degree and fourteen cubic not reducible. For the quadratics he follows [[person/al-khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]] and [[person/thabit-ibn-qurra|Thabit ibn Qurra]]. For the irreducible cubics he uses conic sections, with cases of one, two or no positive roots {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 886} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 887}.

Earlier he wrote a treatise on arithmetic, now lost, in which he says he proved the Indian rules for roots and extended them to roots of any integer power. The article infers that it contained the binomial formula, and corrects Minorsky's earlier claim that a manuscript survives at Leiden: the title Mushkilat al-hisab is only written on the title page of a Leiden copy of his geometric treatise {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 887}.

His commentary on difficulties in the introductions to Euclid's book was finished at Isfahan in 469/1077, which differs by one year from the date given in the older sections above. It has three chapters, on parallel lines, on the definition of ratio and on compound ratios. On parallels he states five principles taken from Aristotle, the fourth of which is equivalent to Euclid's fifth postulate, and builds on it a quadrilateral with equal sides, two right angles at the base and two equal upper angles. He refutes the acute and obtuse hypotheses and keeps the right angle. The article says his argument avoids the circular reasoning of many earlier proofs, that it in effect proves first theorems of non-Euclidean geometries, and that the figure was later used by [[person/nasir-al-din-al-tusi|Nasir al-Din al-Tusi]], Wallis and Saccheri {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 887}.

On ratios he is said to have rediscovered Theaetetus's definition and proved it equivalent to Eudoxus's. For compound ratios he introduced generalised numbers, which the article equates with real numbers, and it judges the commentary important for later European mathematics. These are the article's judgements {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 887}.

## Astronomy and the calendar

The Jalali calendar project belongs to Malik Shah's patronage and collaborative observing work, not an achievement isolated from scientific and political conditions {cite:khayyam-mactutor calendar} {cite:tdv-omer-hayyam-unat pp.66–68}. We import no numerical modern-calendar comparison or everlasting precision claim from a summary, and do not infer that cubic geometry directly caused the reform.

The Encyclopaedia of Islam adds its own account. [[person/ibn-al-athir-izz-al-din|Ibn al-Athir]] is cited for a congress of scholars that Malik Shah convened from 465/1072 to reform the calendar, fix the date of Nawruz and plan an observatory at Isfahan. The scholars named include Khayyam, al-Isfizari and Maymun ibn al-Najib al-Wasiti, so the article thinks Nizam al-Mulk and Hasan-i Sabbah did not attend, contrary to a legend. In its astronomy section it says Khayyam was invited in 466/1074, that the spring equinox of 471/1079 began the Maliki or Jalali era, and that the reform was never finally enforced. It gives a leap-year pattern and a claimed divergence of one day in 5,000 years, against 3,333 for the Gregorian calendar. The observatory was destroyed after Malik Shah's death in 485/1092 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 883} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 887} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 888}. The event is recorded at [[event/jalali-calendar-reform-467|the Jalali calendar reform]].

There he compiled astronomical tables for Malik Shah, of which only a catalogue of 100 bright stars survives in an anonymous manuscript in Paris. Afterwards he wrote the Nawruz-nama in Persian, which the article thinks was meant to draw Malik Shah's successors to the calendar and the observatory {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 888}.

## Mechanics, natural sciences and music

When the Seljuk capital moved to [[place/marw|Marw]], his works on mechanics were written. His pupil al-Khazini worked there at the court of [[person/sanjar|Sultan Sanjar]], and his Balance of Wisdom contains two of Khayyam's treatises on the level balance. In the first, Khayyam determines the amounts of gold and silver in an alloy by weighing it in air and in water; the second treats a balance with a movable weight. The article also lists a treatise on musical kinds formed by fourths, and notes that Bayhaqi and al-Tatawi mention works on nature and on places. It adds that he was a physician who treated the sons of Malik Shah {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 888}.

## Philosophy

Besides the quatrains, the article says, he wrote philosophical treatises as a pupil of Ibn Sina, an adherent of eastern Aristotelianism. A treatise on being and obligation, of 472/1080, answers the judge and imam Abu Nasr al-Nasawi on God's wisdom in creation and on the duty to pray; the article suggests the request may have come from a wish to free Khayyam of suspicion of heresy. Khayyam argues for God as the final cause that ends the chain of causes, and explains prayer as a way of remembering God and obeying his laws {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 888} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 889}.

A second treatise answers three questions, on the necessity of contradiction in the world, on determinism and on permanence. On evil, he holds that even an all-powerful God cannot act without it and that refusing a great good for a small evil would itself be a great evil. On universals he admits two modes of existence, in things and in the mind, where Ibn Sina held three. Three further treatises follow, on the light of intelligence about universal knowledge, on existence, and, in Persian, on the chain of order, written for the son of the vizier Muayyid al-Mulk. The last classifies seekers of truth as theologians, philosophers and scientists, Ismailis and Sufis, and, following al-Ghazali, gives the highest place to the Sufis {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 888} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 889}.

## The quatrains attributed to him

The article opens this part by saying that the recent fame of the quatrains attributed to Khayyam is inversely related to the authenticity of many of them. It adds that the study of authenticity should not hide the Khayyamian tradition, which it traces to an original nucleus that can scarcely be the work of anyone but one individual, who certainly seems to be Khayyam. No reference text of his poetry exists. Sparse quotations collected by anthologists form a small coherent corpus, and later came imitations, quatrains by other poets and fabricated old manuscripts {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 883}.

The attribution has been disputed for over a century. After Cowell found a fifteenth-century Oxford manuscript of 158 quatrains in 1856, Fitzgerald published five English versions, the last in 1889, which the article says ran to 310 editions. In 1897 Zukovski questioned 82 quatrains, and Ross and Browne took up the question, Browne concluding with others that no adequate answer could be found. Christensen in 1927 accepted a quatrain only if five texts of the best manuscript group contained it, and arrived at 121 {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 883} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 884}.

The article therefore looks at early quotations. It treats the absence of Khayyam's name as a sign of antiquity, since the pen name came into general use in the thirteenth century. It lists, among others, four Arabic verses in the anthology of [[person/imad-al-din-al-katib-al-isfahani|Imad al-Din al-Katib al-Isfahani]] of 572/1176; a Persian quatrain in a work of Ahmad-i Sam'ani from before 534/1139; an anecdote of [[person/farid-al-din-attar|Farid al-Din Attar]] about Khayyam's tomb; and five quatrains in Zahiri-i Samarqandi's Sindbad-nama. [[person/fakhr-al-din-al-razi|Fakhr al-Din al-Razi]] names Khayyam as author of a quatrain in 600/1203. [[person/najm-al-din-razi|Najm al-Din Daya]] repeats it in 620/1223 and adds a second, to express his indignation as a believer at such unbelief {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 884} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 885}.

Later sources cited include the anthology of Khalil-i Shirwani, with a chapter of 31 quatrains under his name of which the article calls five problematic; the anthology of al-Jajarmi of 741/1340, with 13; and the historians [[person/ata-malik-al-juwayni|Ata Malik al-Juwayni]] and [[person/hamdallah-mustawfi|Hamd Allah Mustawfi]]. Collections grew from 56 to 315 quatrains in fifteenth-century manuscripts, and beyond 500 later. Manuscripts of the thirteenth century that came to light in 1947 to 1959 named him sole author of 252. The article counts about 25 quatrains found in texts between the sixth/twelfth and eighth/fourteenth centuries that could be his, fewer than ten named as his, leaving aside Shirwani. It holds that the 252 is out of proportion {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 885} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 886}.

It describes the tradition's themes as the world's precariousness, the earth as dust from proud humans, pleasure as the answer to guilt, and a poet between total certainty and total doubt, and notes its influence on [[person/hafiz-al-shirazi|Hafiz]]. It also reports the late tale of three childhood friends, Khayyam, Nizam al-Mulk and Hasan-i Sabbah, found in [[person/rashid-al-din-hamadani|Rashid al-Din]], and calls it fictitious {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 885} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 886}. This page reports the article's account of the dispute and gives no verdict on any quatrain.

## The calendar: narrative and witness

The [[invention/jalali-calendar|Jalali reform]] anchors New Year to the equinox, but his personal design role remains qualified: MacTutor reports participation, while Thomann separates anonymous reformers from named observers and does not necessarily identify him as designer {cite:khayyam-mactutor calendar} {cite:thomann-jalali-calendar-2021 sec.4}. We present the later1079/471 epoch alongside the earlier-epoch/Hijri-day disagreements, not a verified day of individual invention {cite:thomann-jalali-calendar-2021 sec.4} {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy Jalali subsection}. Modern month lengths/rules are not all eleventh-century rules; the1804 Berlin folio witnesses parallel calendars, not an autograph or proof of comprehensive fiscal use {cite:iranica-calendars-abdollahy Solar-Hijri subsection} {cite:thomann-jalali-calendar-2021 sec.5 fig.5.1} {cite:berlin-astronomical-tables-211 metadata,image,rights}.
