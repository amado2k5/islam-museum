## Summary
Amr ibn Uthman al-Makki, one of the Sufi shaykhs of the third century. He heard from Yunus ibn Abd al-Ala and al-Rabiʿ ibn Sulayman, and Jaʿfar al-Khuldi related from him. {cite:muntazam-ibn-al-jawzi p. 205}.

When he was appointed judge of Jeddah, al-Junayd cut off relations with him and said: "I will not speak to one who used to show asceticism and then was seen to expand his pursuit of worldly wealth." {cite:muntazam-ibn-al-jawzi p. 205}.

He died in Baghdad in the year 297 AH; it is also said 291 AH, but the first date is more sound. {cite:muntazam-ibn-al-jawzi p. 205}.

## In al-Risala al-Qushayriyya
In [[work/al-risala-al-qushayriyya|al-Risala al-Qushayriyya]] al-Qushayri gives a notice of Abu 'Abd Allah 'Amr ibn 'Uthman al-Makki: he met Abu 'Abd Allah al-Nibaji and kept company with [[person/abu-said-al-kharraz|Abu Sa'id al-Kharraz]], was "the shaykh of the Sufis and leader of the community in principles and the path", and died in Baghdad in 291 AH. {cite:risala-qushayriyya-minhaj-2017 p. 168} He quotes his teaching that whatever the heart imagines or occurs to thought — beauty, splendour, light, a figure or an image — God is far from it, citing the verses that deny God any likeness or equal; his saying, "Knowledge leads and fear drives, and the self is a balky mount between them: tend it with the governance of knowledge and drive it with the threat of fear"; and: "No expression falls upon ecstasy, for it is God's secret with the believers." {cite:risala-qushayriyya-minhaj-2017 p. 168}

## In Hilyat al-Awliya
[[person/abu-nuaym-al-asbahani-ahmad|Abu Nu'aym]] gives him notice 573 of [[work/hilyat-al-awliya|Hilyat al-Awliya]]. He gives his kunya as Abu Abd Allah and describes him as a clear-sighted gnostic and a well-informed scholar who travelled widely. Every text in the notice comes to Abu Nu'aym through his teacher Abu Muhammad Abd Allah ibn Muhammad ibn Ja'far, who heard Amr and wrote down his dictation. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/291}

**A dictated answer on God's oneness.** He dictated to a questioner that he should weigh his self with his intellect and flee to God when stray thoughts intrude. The first station of the people of faith, he says, is to hold that the acts of lordship are God's alone: none withholds, gives, benefits or harms but He. He then lays down that whatever the heart imagines of beauty, radiance or form, God is other than that; the servant must stop at what God has said of Himself, submitting without probing, since even the angels nearest to God could not describe how He is. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/291-292}

**The discipline of the self.** He describes the way of those who answered God's call. They begin by calling the self to account for what is past and binding it to repentance and asking forgiveness. They then give it hunger for fullness, wakefulness for sleep and toil for rest, and they stop it at every glance and passing thought. Through all this striving he makes the real reliance a turning to God and asking His help against the self. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/293-294}

**Watchfulness, modesty and gratitude.** He says that the sincere among the scrupulous examine their hearts in every movement and stillness, and God gives them watchfulness. Lasting watchfulness brings modesty before God; modesty fills the heart with reverence for what God has made sacred, and this world grows small in it. He defines gratitude as the heart's joy turning from the blessing itself to the One who gave it. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/295-296}

Abu Nu'aym closes by saying that Amr had a large share in the branches of knowledge and that his works, with their chains and reports, were well known. He transmits one hadith from him, through Yunus ibn Abd al-A'la from Ibn Uyayna, and calls it rare by that route. {cite:hilyat-al-awliya-dar-al-kutub-al-ilmiyya-1988 10/296}
