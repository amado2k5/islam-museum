## Summary
Professor Teepu Siddique is a Pakistani-American neurologist and neurogeneticist whose laboratory transformed the genetics of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis — ALS, the progressive motor-neuron disease also known as Lou Gehrig's disease. The Muslim 500 lists him in Science & Technology, citing his discovery of primary genetic and molecular mechanisms causing the disease. {cite:muslim500-teepu-siddique}

## Career at Northwestern
Born in Pakistan, Siddique trained in medicine and neurology before joining Northwestern University in Chicago, where he holds the Les Turner ALS Professorship and built one of the world's leading centres for the study of neurodegenerative disease — combining a clinic that sees ALS patients with a laboratory that hunts the genes that cause it. {cite:muslim500-teepu-siddique}

## Science
His laboratory's contributions helped turn ALS from a clinical mystery into a molecular disease: work on the genetic linkage and mutations underlying familial ALS, including landmark studies identifying the UBQLN2 gene and other causal and disease-modifying genes, and on the protein pathways — TDP-43 aggregation among them — that connect genetic forms of the disease to the far commoner sporadic cases. The research runs from gene to cell to clinic: identifying mutations in families, engineering them into models, and testing what goes wrong in the motor neuron. {cite:muslim500-teepu-siddique}

## Honours and influence
Siddique is among the most decorated figures in neurogenetics, with recognitions from international neurological and scientific societies and decades of continuous federal funding; his trainees populate laboratories across the United States. His listing in The Muslim 500 records a Pakistani-born physician-scientist whose discoveries are among the foundations on which the current wave of ALS drug development stands. {cite:muslim500-teepu-siddique}

## Significance
In the museum's record, Siddique stands for the physician who stays at the bench: a neurologist who took a fatal disease apart gene by gene over four decades, giving families an explanation and drug developers a target — the long patience of laboratory medicine applied to one of neurology's cruelest diagnoses. {cite:muslim500-teepu-siddique}

## A lab built on loss
The reason Siddique's lab became the world's most prolific ALS-genetics centre is partly technical — access to families, to tissue, to sequencing at scale — and partly biographical: he has spoken of losing relatives to the disease and of carrying its heredity as a personal motivation. The combination of a geneticist's tools and a family's stake is a recurring pattern in medicine's great lab stories, and it explains why a Pakistani-American neurologist at Northwestern became the field's most cited name in inherited motor-neuron disease. The Muslim 500 lists him for that record. {cite:muslim500-teepu-siddique}

## The family as laboratory
Siddique's record is the museum's example of the geneticist who treats the family as a research instrument: the neurologist who understood that the rarest diseases are also the most informative, and who built a career collecting the families that carried them. The pattern — clinical access converted into genetic discovery — is the one his Muslim 500 entry records; the museum keeps it as the profile of a researcher whose laboratory was built on the families that trusted him with their inheritance. {cite:muslim500-teepu-siddique}
