## Summary
Mu'adha bint Abd Allah al-Adawiyya, known as Umm al-Sahba', was a devout woman of Banu Adi in [[place/basra|Basra]]; Ibn al-Jawzi opens his "chosen women ascetics of Basra" with her in [[work/sifat-al-safwa|Sifat al-Safwa]] (entry 584). {cite:sifat-al-safwa-tartusi-2012 p.704} She met [[person/aisha|Aisha]] and transmitted from her, and [[person/al-hasan-al-basri|al-Hasan al-Basri]], [[person/abu-qilaba-abd-allah-ibn-zayd|Abu Qilaba]] and Yazid al-Rishk transmitted from her. {cite:sifat-al-safwa-tartusi-2012 p.706}

## Her devotion
From Muhammad ibn Fudayl, from his father: when day came she would say, "This is the day I shall die," and not sleep until evening; when night came, "This is the night I shall die," and not sleep until morning; and in the cold she wore thin clothes so the cold would keep her awake. {cite:sifat-al-safwa-tartusi-2012 p.704} {cite:sifat-al-safwa-tartusi-2012 p.705} A woman who served her said she spent the night in prayer, and when sleep overcame her she walked about the house telling her soul that its sleep in the grave would be long; Asiya bint Amr al-Adawiyya reported that she prayed six hundred rak'as each day and night, and she used to say, "I wonder at an eye that sleeps when it knows how long the sleep in the darkness of the graves will be." {cite:sifat-al-safwa-tartusi-2012 p.705} Abu al-Sawwar al-Adawi said Banu Adi were the most strenuous people of Basra in devotion: Abu al-Sahba' did not sleep his nights or break his fast by day, and his wife Mu'adha had not raised her head to the sky for forty years. {cite:sifat-al-safwa-tartusi-2012 p.705}

## The day her husband and son fell
Her husband was [[person/sila-ibn-ashyam|Sila ibn Ashyam]], Abu al-Sahba'. Ibn al-Jawzi relates from Thabit al-Bunani that Sila was on a campaign with a son of his, sent the son forward to fight until he was killed, and then advanced himself and was killed; when the women gathered at Mu'adha's she said, "If you have come to congratulate me, welcome; if you have come for anything else, go back." {cite:sifat-al-safwa-tartusi-2012 p.705} Al-Baladhuri in Futuh al-Buldan says Sila fell with Yazid ibn Ziyad at Junza in the Kabul campaign. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407} Al-Hasan reported that she never again lay on a bed after Abu al-Sahba' until she died, and Umm al-Aswad bint Zayd al-Adawiyya — whom Mu'adha had nursed — reported her saying that she did not love remaining in the world for its pleasures but to draw near to her Lord, hoping He would unite her with Abu al-Sahba' and his son in Paradise. {cite:sifat-al-safwa-tartusi-2012 p.705}

## Her counsel and death
She counselled a girl she had nursed to meet God with both fear and hope, then wept until weeping overcame her. {cite:sifat-al-safwa-tartusi-2012 p.705} From Afira the devout: at her death she wept and then laughed; asked why, she said she wept at parting from fasting, prayer and remembrance, and laughed because she saw Abu al-Sahba' coming into the courtyard in two green robes, and that she did not think she would live to another obligatory prayer; she died before the prayer time came. {cite:sifat-al-safwa-tartusi-2012 p.705} The editor refers for her to Ibn al-Jawzi's al-Muntazam under the year 83 AH, and to Ibn Sa'd's Tabaqat, al-Kashif and Tahdhib al-Tahdhib. {cite:sifat-al-safwa-tartusi-2012 p.704}

## For young visitors
Mu'adha was a woman of Basra who spent her nights in prayer; when her husband and son fell in battle she was patient, and she said she loved life only to worship God more.
