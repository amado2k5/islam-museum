## Zayn al-Abidin of Kashmir

Qutb al-Din Zayn al-Abidin (c. 1401 – 12 May 1470), known to Kashmiris as Budshah, the Great King, was the ninth sultan of the Kashmir Sultanate and the most celebrated ruler of the Shah Mir dynasty. Handed the government in 1418 during his brother Ali Shah's absence, he lost the throne and won it back in the civil war of 1419–1420, then reigned until his death — a span the chroniclers counted at fifty-two years and remembered as a golden age of peace, religious tolerance and prosperity {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en}. His reign is unusually well documented for medieval Kashmir: Jonaraja's continuation of the Rajatarangini covers its first thirty-five years, and Jonaraja's pupil Srivara carried the Sanskrit chronicle through the rest {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en}.

### Early life

He was born at Srinagar, with the name Shah Rukh, and was known as a prince by the name Shahi Khan. He was the second son of Sultan Sikandar Shah and of Mira Khatun, daughter of Firuz, the chief of Ohind, whom Sikandar had married after defeating him early in his reign. The prince was tutored by Maulana Kabir, a Kashmiri scholar and polymath, some of whose liberal outlook is said to have marked his pupil {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en}. The English article gives his birth as c. 1401, on the authority of Srivara and Firishta; Wikidata gives the year 1401 {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en} {cite:wikidata-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir}.

As a youth Shahi Khan was sent to Samarkand, to Timur's court, and spent seven to eight years there, observing the city's administration, crafts and architecture. After Timur's death in 1405 he returned to Kashmir, and later accounts say he came back accompanied by artisans and craftsmen — paper-makers, bookbinders, carpet weavers and saddle makers among them — who seeded industries his reign would foster {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en}.

### Accession and civil war

Back home he assisted his father and then his elder brother Ali Shah. On 30 December 1416 he killed the rebellious vizier Hamsabhatta at the Eidgah, and Ali Shah made him wazir in his place. When Ali Shah left on pilgrimage toward Mecca, he entrusted the administration to Shahi Khan, who received on this occasion the title Zayn al-Abidin, Ornament of the Worshippers {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en}.

Ali Shah abandoned the pilgrimage at the court of his father-in-law, the Raja of Jammu, raised an army from Jammu and Rajauri, and marched to reclaim his throne. Zayn al-Abidin gave up his new authority without resistance — a surrender historians still puzzle over — then withdrew to Sialkot and allied with its ruler Jasrat Khokhar. Jasrat defeated Ali Shah at Thanna, and again at Uri when Ali Shah counter-attacked with the Raja of Jammu's help; whether Ali Shah died in battle, in captivity or by execution the accounts do not agree {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en} {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-shah-mir-ar}. Zayn al-Abidin was crowned for his second and main reign in 1420, though Abu'l Fazl dates the coronation to 1422 {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en}.

### The long reign

To restore the sultanate's integrity he campaigned on the frontiers, recovering Ladakh and Baltistan and regaining Ohind, all of which had broken away under Ali Shah, and when the king of Kashgar sent an invading force toward the valley, Kashmiri arms repelled it after several days of battle {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en} {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-shah-mir-ar}.

His administration made village communities collectively answerable for crimes committed within their bounds, regulated the prices of commodities, stabilised a currency debased under earlier rulers, and forbade provincial governors to impose unauthorised taxes on the peasantry. Public works ran through the reign: irrigation canals, bridges and causeways, and whole new towns — Zaynapur, Zaynagir and Zaynakot — above all his new capital Naushahr, part of the larger foundation of Zainanagar, where his palace, the Zaina Dab, was described by Mirza Haidar Dughlat as twelve storeys of fifty rooms each under a gilded dome. He completed Srinagar's Jamia Masjid, begun under his father, built the Zaina Kadal bridge, and in 1443–1444 reclaimed the drowned island of Zaynalank in Wular Lake, raising on it a palace, a mosque and gardens behind a stone embankment {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en}.

### Religious policy

Against the memory of his father's persecuting reign, Zayn al-Abidin set a policy of studied tolerance. He abolished the jizya imposed on Kashmir's non-Muslims, encouraged Hindus who had fled earlier persecution to return, restored stipends and state patronage to learned Brahmins, allowed temples to be rebuilt and Hindu personal law to be followed, and regulated cattle slaughter, with a ban on killing cows recorded in the Arabic article {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en} {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-shah-mir-ar}. In laying out Naushahr, abandoned places of worship inside the new capital were repaired rather than demolished. The sources name no school of law for him in their prose — the English article's infobox gives Sunni Islam with a note naming Hanafi jurisprudence, while the Arabic article and Wikidata give Islam without a school — so the museum records his own affiliation as unattested and his policies, which are documented in full, as the record of his religion in government {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en} {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-shah-mir-ar} {cite:wikidata-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir}.

### Patronage of learning and craft

His court joined Muslim and Hindu scholars. Near the palace at Naushahr he founded a residential seat of learning, the Dar-ul-Ulum, under his old tutor Maulana Kabir, and a state translation bureau, the Dar-ul-Tarjuma, which by his order rendered the Mahabharata and Kalhana's Rajatarangini from Sanskrit into Persian, the Rajatarangini translation taking the title Bahr-ul-Asmar. Jonaraja and Srivara continued the Rajatarangini itself into the sultanate period; Soma composed a biography of the sultan, the Zaina Charita; and the sultan, proficient in Persian, Sanskrit and Tibetan, wrote Persian verse under the pen name Qutb {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en}.

Crafts were state policy. Royal workshops organised papier-mâché and rag paper-making on Samarkand lines, Persian carpet weaving with its coded taleem pattern system, khatamband woodwork and pinjrakari lattice, stone polishing and cutting, metalwork and bottle making; artisans and scholars were drawn from Iran, Turan, Turkistan and Hindustan, and Kashmiris were sent abroad to learn industries {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en}.

### Death and legacy

In his last years the succession tore his house: after his consort Taj Khatoon died in 1468, his sons Adam Khan, Haji Khan and Bahram Khan disputed the throne and rebelled. He suppressed them, but his health failed — chroniclers describe confusion and a fear of poisoning that made him refuse food prepared at court — and he withdrew from government {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en}.

He died at Naushahr at noon on Friday, 12 May 1470, the date given in the Julian calendar from Srivara's record, aged sixty-nine, and was buried in the Mazar-e-Salatin, the Shah Mir royal cemetery at Srinagar, near his father {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en} {cite:wikidata-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir}. Kashmir mourned him for days. His son Haji Khan succeeded him under the regnal name Haider Shah {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en}. Mohibbul Hasan's verdict stands at the head of his modern reputation: of all the sultans who sat on the throne of Kashmir, "Zainu'l-Abidin was undoubtedly the greatest" {cite:wikipedia-zayn-al-abidin-kashmir-en}.
