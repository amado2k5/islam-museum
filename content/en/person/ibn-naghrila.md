## Overview
Ibn Naghrila, written Ibn al-Naghralla or Ibn Nagrella in the Encyclopaedia of Islam and called Samuel ha-Nagid in Hebrew sources, was a Jew of Granada who served as vizier. The Encyclopaedia of Islam notes that the viziers of al-Andalus were always Muslim until the age of the party kings, the muluk al-tawa'if, when there were also a few Jewish viziers, and it names him as the most famous of them, with his death in 448/1056-7. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 199} The article adds that in al-Andalus the rank of vizier stood above every other civil office except that of the chamberlain, the hajib. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 199}

The Encyclopaedia has no entry of its own for him. This page therefore gathers what its other articles say, and it does not go beyond them.

## Vizier under the Zirids of Granada
He served the [[polity/zirid-dynasty|Zirids]] of [[place/granada|Granada]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 742} The article on the rabbi Nissim ibn Ya'qub of Qayrawan records that Nissim's daughter was unhappily married to a son of Samuel ha-Nagid of the Banu al-Naghralla, and that Nissim himself visited Granada and taught there. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 12 p. 742} The same article is the source for the family name Banu al-Naghralla.

## The first Alhambra
The article on Granada says that the small fortress of al-Hamra, on the Sabika plateau, had been abandoned during the last years of the caliphate and the first half of the 5th/11th century. It was rebuilt and, without doubt, enlarged by the Jewish vizier Samuel Ibn Nagrello between 443/1052 and 447/1056, and the Zirid amir Abd Allah later improved it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1058} The article describes this earlier castle as small; the Nasrid Alhambra was begun on the same plateau only after 1238. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 1058}

## Poetry
The article on the cup-bearer in Arabic poetry gives his dates as 993 to 1056 and lists his Hebrew Divan among the works of Hebrew poets who, it says, imitated the Arabic wine-song in every point, taking over the figure of the cup-bearer and its stock motifs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 949}

## A polemic with Ibn Hazm
In its article on [[person/ibn-hazm|Ibn Hazm]], the Encyclopaedia calls Ibn Naghrila a Jew of Granada and cites Ibn Hazm's polemic with him as proof that Ibn Hazm was well informed about Judaism. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 828} A passage from Ibn Hazm's refutation of him is quoted in the article on creation in kalam, where Ibn Hazm reads the two days in which the heavens were made as the last two of the four days in which provisions were distributed on the earth. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 1023}

## His son, and the later controversy
The article on [[person/abu-ishaq-al-ilbiri|Abu Ishaq al-Ilbiri]] says that this Granadan jurist and poet protested, under the Zirid ruler Badis ibn Habbus, against the growing influence of Jews in the kingdom and against the functions given to the vizier Samuel ha-Nagid ibn Nagrella and to his son Joseph, who succeeded him in the office in 448/1056-7. It judges that al-Ilbiri's political poem was one of the factors behind the pogrom in Granada on 9 Safar 459/30 December 1066, in which, it reports, Joseph and 3,000 of his co-religionists were killed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 149} The article on the Yahud adds that satires against Jews circulated while the Ibn Naghrelas held the vizierate in Zirid Granada, and that popular anger could spill into violence against a whole community, though it calls such anti-Jewish outbursts exceptional. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 251}

## What is not covered
The Encyclopaedia gives no account of his birth, upbringing, his Hebrew and Arabic writings or his military role, and the museum does not supply them here. A fuller biography awaits a source that treats him directly.
