## Overview
Abu'l-Qasim Sulayman b. Ahmad b. Ayyub b. Mutayr al-Lakhmi al-Tabarani, the hafiz, musnad of the age and of the horizons; his first hearing was in 273 at Tiberias, to which he is ascribed, then he travelled to Jerusalem in 274 and to Caesarea in 275 where he heard the companions of al-Firyabi, then on to Homs, Jabala and the cities of Syria; he made the hajj, entered Yemen, returned to Egypt, then travelled to Iraq, Isfahan and Fars — Ibn Khallikan counted a thousand shaykhs for him. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 30}

## The three Mu'jams
His are the three Mu'jams — the Great, the Middle and the Small — the most famous of his books; Ibn Nasir al-Din called him musnad of the horizons, and he used to say of the Middle one: "it is my soul, for I toiled over it"; he settled at Isfahan and died there. {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 30}

## His life at the head of al-Mu'jam al-Awsat

The editors of al-Mu'jam al-Awsat reproduce his biography from Siyar A'lam al-Nubala': he was Abu al-Qasim Sulayman ibn Ahmad ibn Ayyub ibn Mutayr al-Lakhmi al-Shami al-Tabarani, author of the three Mu'jams, born in Acre in Safar 260 AH to a mother from Acre; he first heard hadith in 273 AH, and his father, himself a hadith man, took him travelling from 275 AH, a journey that lasted sixteen years. {cite:mujam-awsat-haramayn-1415-v1 p.14} He heard hadith in Syria, the two Sanctuaries, Yemen, Egypt, Baghdad, Kufa, Basra, [[place/isfahan|Isfahan]] and Khuzistan, then settled in Isfahan for some sixty years, teaching and writing; al-Dhahabi notes that he reached Iraq only after Egypt, Syria, the Hijaz and Yemen, and had he gone there first he would have gained very high chains. {cite:mujam-awsat-haramayn-1415-v1 p.16} Among those who related from him were Abu Nu'aym al-Isfahani, Ibn Manda and Ibn Marduya, and among his own teachers who related from him were Abu Khalifa al-Jumahi and Ibn Uqda. {cite:mujam-awsat-haramayn-1415-v1 p.16} Asked how he came to have so many hadiths, he said he had slept on reed mats for thirty years. {cite:mujam-awsat-haramayn-1415-v1 p.17}

## What was said of him

Al-Dhahabi quotes Ahmad ibn Mansur al-Shirazi, who wrote three hundred thousand hadiths from him, calling him trustworthy except that he mistook the name of an Egyptian teacher, one of the two sons of al-Barqi; he reports Abu Ali al-Naysaburi's poor opinion of him and that Ibn Marduya bore him some grudge, and answers that Ibn Marduya named and weakened a number of people in his history of Isfahan but did not weaken al-Tabarani. {cite:mujam-awsat-haramayn-1415-v1 p.20} {cite:mujam-awsat-haramayn-1415-v1 p.21} Besides the Mu'jams, the biography lists his Tafsir, al-Awa'il, Dala'il al-Nubuwwa, the Musnad of Aisha, Ma'rifat al-Sahaba and others, most of them musnads of leading memorisers. {cite:mujam-awsat-haramayn-1415-v1 p.22}
