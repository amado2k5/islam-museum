## Overview
As'ad Pasha al-Azm (c. 1706 – March 1758) was the Ottoman governor of Damascus from 1743 until his deposition in 1757, and the most prominent figure of the al-Azm family, which held sway over the provinces of Ottoman Syria in the first half of the eighteenth century. He is remembered for crushing the Damascus janissaries in 1746, for protecting the Syrian Hajj caravan year after year, and for building the Azm Palace in Damascus and Khan As'ad Pasha, landmarks of eighteenth-century Arab architecture. {cite:wikipedia-asad-pasha-al-azm-en} {cite:wikipedia-asad-pasha-al-azm-ar}

## Family and origins
As'ad was born in 1706 in Ma'arrat al-Nu'man in Ottoman Syria; the Arabic article, citing the historian Ibn Miru, gives his birth in 1117 AH, while its own lead and Wikidata date him from 1701 — the 1706 reading, which agrees with the English article, is recorded in his data, with the 1701 reading preserved in the note. His grandfather Ibrahim al-Azm was a rural notable sent to Ma'arrat al-Nu'man in the mid-seventeenth century to restore order; on his death As'ad's father, Ismail Pasha al-Azm, and his uncle, Sulayman Pasha al-Azm, completed the task and were rewarded with hereditary tax farms in Homs, Hama and Ma'arrat al-Nu'man, so that by 1725 the al-Azm family controlled much of Ottoman Syria. His brother Sa'deddin Pasha al-Azm was another leading member of the family. {cite:wikipedia-asad-pasha-al-azm-en} {cite:wikipedia-asad-pasha-al-azm-ar} {cite:wikidata-asad-pasha-al-azm}

## From Hama to the governorship of Damascus
As'ad governed Hama as a tax farmer for a number of years and built the Azm Palace there as his residence. When his uncle Sulayman Pasha, governor of Damascus, died in 1743, As'ad succeeded him — some formulations in the English article say he "succeeded his father", but the article's own family account dates his uncle's death in office to 1743, and that chronology is followed here. Throughout the first half of the century the janissary corps of Damascus had challenged the family's rule; in 1746 As'ad crushed the local janissaries and thereby secured his authority over the city. {cite:wikipedia-asad-pasha-al-azm-en}

## The Hajj caravan and the buildings
He was favoured by the authorities in Istanbul for his success in protecting the Syrian pilgrim caravan that left each year for Mecca and Medina: in dealing with the Bedouin tribes along the route through the Syrian Desert and the Hejaz he either subdued them by force or bought them off, and the caravan's safety mattered greatly to the Ottoman sultan as Protector of the Two Holy Sanctuaries, especially after the janissaries' repeated failures. When locust swarms devastated the harvests of interior Syria, he used the disaster as a pretext to raid Druze communities in the Bekaa Valley, plundering their crops and placing them on the market in Damascus. At the same time he relaxed restraints on Arab Christians, allowing them, for instance, to drink alcohol in public. {cite:wikipedia-asad-pasha-al-azm-en}

He spent his family's great wealth on building: the Azm Palace in Damascus, raised in 1750 as a combined residence and guesthouse and a monument of eighteenth-century Arab architecture, and the famed Khan As'ad Pasha, built under his patronage in 1752. His rule marked the apex of al-Azm influence in the Levant, when members of the family administered Damascus, Aleppo, Hama, Tripoli, Sidon and, briefly, Mosul. {cite:wikipedia-asad-pasha-al-azm-en}

## Deposition and death
Despite his success with the caravan, the Grand Vizier Raghib Pasha deposed him in 1757 after fourteen years in office, denouncing him as a "peasant son of a peasant" after a deal between them faltered; the Ottoman state was also interested in confiscating the great wealth he had accumulated. He was assigned to Aleppo, and a month later appointed governor of Egypt. In late 1757 the Hajj caravan was annihilated, and the Ottomans accused As'ad of inciting the Bedouin to attack it in revenge for his deposition, so Raghib Pasha ordered his execution. {cite:wikipedia-asad-pasha-al-azm-en}

The stories of his death differ. One has him executed in March 1758 aboard a ship ostensibly transporting him to exile in Crete. The Encyclopaedia of Islam of the Turkey Religious Foundation recounts instead that he was appointed to Aleppo on 21 January 1757 and to Sivas on 25 September 1757 and exiled toward Ruse in Bulgaria, and that, having reached Ankara on the way, he was executed there; his tomb in Ankara — the Azimi (İsmail Paşazade Hacı Esad) Türbesi — bears on its stone the year 1171 AH and his fourteen years as commander of the Hajj caravan, while his head was taken to Istanbul and buried in the Tunusbağı cemetery in Üsküdar. After his death, his Georgian mamluk Uthman Pasha al-Kurji led the authorities to his master's treasures and was rewarded with the governorship of Damascus in 1760. {cite:wikipedia-asad-pasha-al-azm-en} {cite:wikipedia-asad-pasha-al-azm-ar}
