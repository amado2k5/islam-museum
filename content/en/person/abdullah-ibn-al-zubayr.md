## Overview
'Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr was the son of [[person/zubayr-ibn-al-awwam|al-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam]] and [[person/asma-bint-abi-bakr|Asma bint Abi Bakr]]. He is remembered as the Meccan claimant to the caliphate during the [[event/second-fitna|Second Fitna]]. According to al-Mas'udi, the people of Mecca and Iraq gave him their allegiance as caliph, and he lived through the strife of al-Harra and the rebuilding of the Ka'ba. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 104-105} The Encyclopaedia of Islam, 2nd edition, heads its article with the label anti-caliph, an editorial heading that is not the museum's own wording, and places him in the 'Abd al-'Uzza clan of Quraysh. It also notes that his mother was a sister of [[person/aisha|'A'isha]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 73} His rule is described in the museum under the [[polity/zubayrid-caliphate|Zubayrid caliphate]].

## Birth and family
The article places his birth at [[place/medina|Medina]], twenty months after the hijra, about Dhu al-Qa'da of the second year, or May 624. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 73} It says that some sources, among them Ibn Qutayba and Ibn Habib, call him the first child born to the Muhajirun at Medina. Through his father and mother he was closely related to the Prophet's family, and the article regards this kinship as one factor in building his reputation, both against the Umayyads and, it suggests, against the 'Alids. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 73}

## Early campaigns
The article reports that he was present with his father at the battle of [[event/battle-of-yarmuk|al-Yarmuk]], still a boy, and went with him to join [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|'Amr ibn al-'As]] in Egypt in 19/640. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 73} He took part in the campaign of [[person/abdullah-ibn-sad-ibn-abi-sarh|'Abd Allah ibn Sa'd ibn Abi Sarh]] against the Byzantines in [[place/ifriqiya|Ifriqiya]] in 26-27/647, and is said to have killed the exarch Gregory himself. Returning to Medina with news of the victory, he is credited with an eloquent account of it, which the article traces to the Aghani. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 73} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74}

He then accompanied [[person/said-ibn-al-as|Sa'id ibn al-'As]] on his campaigns in northern Persia in 29-30/650. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74} The article adds that [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|'Uthman]] named him to the commission charged with the official recension of the Qur'an. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74} The Sahih of al-Bukhari likewise places him among the four men who copied the codices, with Zayd ibn Thabit, Sa'id ibn al-'As and 'Abd al-Rahman ibn al-Harith. {cite:sahih-al-bukhari 4987}

## The first civil war
After the killing of 'Uthman, the article says, he went with his father and 'A'isha to [[place/basra|Basra]] and commanded the infantry at the [[event/battle-of-the-camel|battle of the Camel]], on 10 Jumada II 36, which it gives as 4 December 656. He returned to Medina with 'A'isha afterwards and took no further part in the war, apart from attending the arbitration at Dumat al-Jandal, or rather Adhruh, where he is said to have advised 'Abd Allah ibn 'Umar to bribe 'Amr ibn al-'As. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74} The museum describes that meeting at [[event/arbitration-meeting-at-dumat-al-jandal|the arbitration meeting at Dumat al-Jandal]].

## Refusing allegiance to Yazid
The article says that under [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya]] he stayed in the background, biding his time, having inherited a considerable fortune from his father, but would not swear allegiance to [[person/yazid-ibn-muawiya|Yazid]] as heir. On Mu'awiya's death in 60/680 he and [[person/husayn-ibn-ali|al-Husayn ibn 'Ali]] again refused, and to escape the threats of Marwan they fled to [[place/mecca|Mecca]], where they were left unmolested. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74}

After the expedition and death of al-Husayn at [[event/battle-of-karbala|Karbala]], Ibn al-Zubayr, according to the article, began to enrol supporters in secret. A small force sent from Medina under his brother 'Amr to arrest him was defeated; 'Amr was taken prisoner, beaten and held until he died, and his body was exposed on a gibbet in 61/681. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74} Ibn al-Zubayr then publicly declared Yazid deposed. The Ansar at Medina followed and chose [[person/abdullah-ibn-hanzala|'Abd Allah ibn Hanzala]], known as Ibn al-Ghasil, as their chief. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74}

## The first siege of Mecca
Yazid sent a Syrian army under [[person/muslim-ibn-uqba-al-murri|Muslim ibn 'Uqba]]. It defeated the people of Medina at the battle of [[event/waqat-al-harra|al-Harra]], on 27 Dhu al-Hijja 63, given as 27 August 683. The army then went on to besiege Ibn al-Zubayr at Mecca from 26 Muharram 64, or 24 September 683, even after Muslim's death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74} The museum has a separate record of this [[event/siege-of-mecca-683|siege of Mecca]]. Sixty-four days later the news of Yazid's death arrived and the Syrian forces withdrew. Their commander, [[person/al-husayn-ibn-numayr|al-Husayn ibn Numayr]], tried to persuade Ibn al-Zubayr to come back to Syria with them, but he chose to stay in Mecca. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74}

## The claim to the caliphate and its limits
The article says the confusion in Syria after Yazid's death gave him his opening. He proclaimed himself commander of the believers, and opponents of the Umayyads in Syria, Egypt, southern Arabia and [[place/kufa|Kufa]] recognised him as caliph, although in the article's view his authority remained almost wholly nominal. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74} It then lists the setbacks that put his supporters on the defensive. These were the victory of [[person/marwan-ibn-al-hakam|Marwan]] at [[event/battle-of-marj-rahit|Marj Rahit]] at the end of 64/July 684 and the revolt of [[person/al-mukhtar-ibn-abi-ubayd|al-Mukhtar]] at Kufa fifteen months later. It adds that al-Muhallab's support for [[person/musab-ibn-al-zubayr|Mus'ab ibn al-Zubayr]] at Basra and the victory over al-Mukhtar in 67/687 restored a Zubayrid government in Iraq, though Mus'ab was in effect an independent ruler. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74}

The article also describes the Bakri Kharijites, who had parted from Ibn al-Zubayr after Yazid's death and established themselves in eastern Najd. They occupied the province of Bahrayn, then in 68/687-8 seized Yemen and Hadramawt, and the next year took al-Ta'if, so that he was cut off in the Hijaz. At the pilgrimage of 68/688, the article says, four different leaders presided over their own groups of partisans, among them Ibn al-Zubayr, a Kharijite, an Umayyad and Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74}

## The final siege of Mecca and his death
After the Umayyads retook Iraq in 72/691, the article says, 'Abd al-Malik sent al-Hajjaj against Mecca. The siege began on 1 Dhu al-Qa'da 72, or 25 March 692, and lasted more than six months, with the city and the Ka'ba under bombardment. When his supporters gave way and even his own sons surrendered to al-Hajjaj, Ibn al-Zubayr, urged on by his mother, went back into the fight and was killed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74} The article gives the date of his death as 17 Jumada I or II 73, which it converts to 4 October or 3 November 692, and says the fighting was against the Syrian troops under al-Hajjaj. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 73} His body was placed on a gibbet where his brother 'Amr had been displayed. Some time later 'Abd al-Malik ordered it returned to his mother, who buried it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74}

## The siege of Mecca and the Ka'ba: the accounts side by side
Two accounts in the museum's sources describe the final siege differently, and this page reports both without choosing between them.

- Al-Mas'udi, in the Meadows of Gold, says al-Hajjaj besieged him in Mecca for fifty nights from Dhu al-Qa'da 72, until he was killed. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 104-105} Al-Mas'udi also mentions the rebuilding of the Ka'ba in connection with him. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 104-105}
- The Encyclopaedia of Islam article, by contrast, puts the siege at more than six months, with the city and the Ka'ba bombarded. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74} It does not describe the Ka'ba's rebuilding or repair, and the museum's record of [[event/siege-of-mecca-692|the siege of Mecca in 692]] and of [[building/kaaba|the Ka'ba]] should be consulted for what other sources say.

Whether the difference lies in the length counted, in the stage of the siege or in the sources used is not settled by the article. Neither account is given priority here beyond the order in which they appear.

## How he is remembered
The article describes him as the main representative of the second generation of the noble Muslim families of Mecca, who resented the Umayyad house taking the caliphate and the gap in power this created between the clan of 'Abd Shams and the other Meccan clans. It says this resentment is visible as an undercurrent in the many anecdotes about his dealings with Mu'awiya. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74}

The sources differ in how they present him, and the article names both views. Later Muslim tradition idealised the challenger of Umayyad rule as a model of piety, and the article cites the Hilyat al-Awliya' as the leading example. The author of the article, H. A. R. Gibb, reads this idealisation as having transformed a courageous man who, in his judgement, was at heart self-seeking and indulgent. He adds that many sources describe Ibn al-Zubayr as avaricious, jealous and ill-natured, and reproach him especially for harshness towards his brother Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya and towards 'Abd Allah ibn 'Abbas. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 74} These are the views of the sources and of the article's author, and the museum does not adopt either as its own.

## His speech after the killing of Mus'ab
When his brother [[person/musab-ibn-al-zubayr|Mus'ab]] was killed, he went up to the pulpit and said that the news had grieved him and gladdened him at once, and that his family do not die in their beds but by spears and under the shade of swords. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 104-105}

This page follows al-Mas'udi's "Meadows of Gold" and the Encyclopaedia of Islam for the matters above; other details need an additional source.

## His officials in Khalifa ibn Khayyat
[[person/khalifa-ibn-khayyat|Khalifa ibn Khayyat]] closes his account of this reign by naming its governors and office-holders. They are given here in his order; lines the editor restored from later authors are marked as such. {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 269–270}

- **Judgeship of Basra:** Hisham ibn Hubayra ibn Fadala al-Laythi {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 269}
- **Judgeship of Kufa:** Sa'id ibn Nimran al-Hamdani, appointed by Mus'ab when Shurayh withdrew and then dismissed; then [[person/abdallah-ibn-utba-ibn-masud|Abd Allah ibn Utba ibn Mas'ud]], until Mus'ab was killed {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 269}
- **Chamberlain:** Abd Allah ibn Sa'd, mawla of Hatib ibn Abi Balta'a {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 270}
- **Over all his affairs:** Abd Allah ibn Safwan ibn Umayya ibn Khalaf {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 270}

## From Omar Abdelkafy's series on the Hikam of Ibn Ata Allah
