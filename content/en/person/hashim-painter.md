## Summary
All works ascribable to Hashim are portraits, most of them single figures on a plain background; only one is dated by inscription, so Milo Cleveland Beach dates the rest by the age of the sitter. {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 126} Among the earliest attributed to him are the two portraits of [[person/jahangir-mughal|Jahangir]] with a globe in the [[object/minto-album|Minto Album]], about 1620, and a portrait of Sultan Muhammad Qutb Shah of Golconda. {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 127}

## Style
His technique grew ever more secure: the portraits of [[person/shah-jahan-mughal|Shah Jahan]], [[person/dara-shikoh|Dara Shikoh]] and An Abyssinian are executed with breathtaking control but little warmth, and for Beach he is an even more detached observer than [[person/bichitr|Bichitr]], unlike [[person/govardhan-painter|Govardhan]]. {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 126} In An Abyssinian there is no background to divert the eye, and the rendering of sash and dagger becomes a major visual element; Stuart C. Welch has suggested the sitter is Fath Khan, son of Malik Ambar. {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 25} {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 126} Shah Jahan in Old Age, about 1650–55, is for Beach the culmination of the trend: an icon of the emperor that projects his perfected general image, not his individuality, like a temple sculpture. {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 25} {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 26} Because so many inscriptions name him and his range is so narrow, his lesser authentic works are hard to tell from faithful later copies. {cite:beach-grand-mogul-1978 p. 126}
