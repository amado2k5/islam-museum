## Overview
Vahan was the general under whom, according to the Encyclopaedia of Islam, Byzantine forces came from Hims, ancient Emesa, to meet the Muslims at the Yarmuk. The article suggests he was probably the Magister Militum per Orientem, the master of soldiers for the East, and names alongside him [[person/theodore-trithurios|Theodore Trithurios]], the Sakellarios or treasurer. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302} Elsewhere in the same article he is called the Armenian Byzantine general Vahan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302} The only article in the Encyclopaedia of Islam found to speak of him is the one on the Yarmuk, so this record is short.

## Command at the Yarmuk
The article dates the battle to 636 and says it ended on 12 Rajab 15 AH, 20 August 636, after a month of manoeuvres that began near al-Jabiya, the old base of the Ghassanids. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302} It says the emperor [[person/heraclius|Heraclius]] had gathered a very large Byzantine and Christian Arab force in early 636 to drive the Muslims from Syria and Palestine, though he was not personally present. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 301} Alongside the forces under Vahan and Theodore, [[person/jabala-ibn-al-ayham|Jabala ibn al-Ayham]], king of the Ghassanids, led Ghassanid troops, and other Christian Arabs recruited by Heraclius also took part. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302}

The article describes the Muslim side, under [[person/abu-ubayda-ibn-al-jarrah|Abu Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah]], as drawing the Byzantines into an attack on their camp near Dayr Ayyub, and says that a night raid under [[person/khalid-ibn-al-walid|Khalid ibn al-Walid]] seized the bridge over the Wadi al-Rukkad, the only viable retreat route, so that the Byzantine army was cut off and most of it was killed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302} The article does not say what became of Vahan himself in this fighting. See [[event/battle-of-yarmuk|the battle of the Yarmuk]].

## An army of mixed peoples
The article remarks that the Byzantine force included heterogeneous ethnic elements, many of them with no experience of the region, and that there was probably mistrust, misunderstanding and friction between Greeks, Armenians and Christian Arabs within it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302} It says the Byzantines also had supply problems before the battle and were suffering from a series of recent defeats. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302} These remarks concern the army as a whole and are not tied to Vahan in particular.

## Blame after the defeat
According to the article, the reigning Heraclian dynasty appears to have tried to shift responsibility for the disaster from itself to others. One of the explanations it names is an alleged abortive rebellion of the Armenian Byzantine general Vahan. The others are a failure to follow Heraclius's injunctions about Arab ambushes and the adverse climate. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302} The article calls the rebellion alleged and abortive, and does not claim that it happened. It adds that no documentary records or eyewitness accounts of the battle survive, and that the best account is the one preserved by Ibn Asakir in his history of Damascus. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 11 p. 302}

## Names and what the sources leave open
The Encyclopaedia of Islam writes the name Vahan. The articles read give no Arabic form of it, no dates for his life, no account of his earlier career and no account of his end. The Arabic form is left open until it is checked in an Arabic chronicle of the conquest.
