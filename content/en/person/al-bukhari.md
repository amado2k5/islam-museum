## Overview
Abu Abd Allah Muhammad ibn Isma'il ibn Ibrahim ibn al-Mughira ibn Bardizbah al-Bukhari al-Ju'fi, described in the source as the Commander of the Faithful in hadith and one of the trustworthy hadith scholars. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.321-322}

## His works
Ibn al-Nadim lists among his books: "al-Tarikh al-Kabir", "al-Tarikh al-Saghir", "al-Tarikh al-Awsat", "al-Asma' wa al-Kuna", "al-Du'afa'", "al-Sahih", "al-Sunan" in jurisprudence, "al-Adab", "Khalq Af'al al-Ibad", and "al-Qira'a khalf al-Imam". {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.321-322}

The account the introduction to the Sahih gives through the History of Damascus (the period of composition, the number of hadiths selected, and the practice at the Sacred Mosque) is not included here: the local copies of the Sahih do not show this text, and it will be added from a documented source in a later batch.

## For Sprouts
A revered scholar of Hadith who traveled widely and compiled the Prophet's authentic sayings in his monumental Sahih al-Bukhari.

## For Explorers
Muhammad ibn Ismail al-Bukhari was the master of Hadith scholars, born in Bukhara with an early passion for prophetic traditions. He traveled across the Islamic world to collect and critically verify narrations with rigorous scrutiny, authoring his celebrated Sahih al-Bukhari, universally regarded as the most authentic Hadith collection.

## For Scholars
Abu Abd Allah Muhammad ibn Ismail al-Bukhari (194–256 AH / 810–870 CE), acclaimed as the supreme authority in Hadith criticism. Born in Bukhara, he embarked on sixteen years of extensive scholarly journeys across Khorasan, Iraq, the Hijaz, Syria, and Egypt, studying under master traditionists like Ahmad ibn Hanbal and Ali ibn al-Madini. Applying rigorous methodological criteria demanding both contemporaneous verification and documented direct transmission, he authored 'al-Jami' al-Sahih' (Sahih al-Bukhari), revered in Sunni scholarship as the most authoritative canonical compilation of prophetic traditions, alongside seminal biographical works like 'al-Tarikh al-Kabir' and 'al-Adab al-Mufrad'.

## In the 'Al-Mubdi'un' programme (part 1 of 2)
Tareq al-Suwaidan dedicates two episodes to al-Bukhari, declaring his greatest secret to be a rare talent — a powerful memory — nurtured from childhood and invested, adding that one who finds no patron can still carve his own way {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:07:30}.
He recounts: as a boy under ten he attended the jurists' circles at Merv and wrote; when the attendants laughed that he had written only two hadiths, a shaykh said 'do not laugh, for he may laugh at you one day'; and at sixteen he visited Basra, wrote nothing for sixteen days, then recited from memory the fifteen thousand hadiths his companions had collected — until they corrected their books from his memory {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:09:30}.
He cites his saying 'I memorise a hundred thousand sound hadiths and two hundred thousand unsound ones' and that he distilled the Sahih from six hundred thousand; and he pairs it with an incident he himself witnessed at Shaykh al-Albani's, aged seventeen — al-Albani spotting an interrupted hadith by naming the volume, page, transmitters and their dates — to show this memory is a real human capacity, not legend {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:10:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:23:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:12:30}.
Of his travels: he heard al-Zuhri at Mecca, entered Baghdad eight times sitting each time to Imam Ahmad until Ahmad said at parting 'do you leave knowledge and the people and go to Khurasan?' — and journeyed to Mecca, Medina, Egypt and Syria gathering every route of each hadith {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:20:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:21:00}.
He transmits his saying 'I wrote from one thousand and eighty men, every one a hadith scholar, all holding that faith is word and deed, increasing and decreasing' — the formula of the Sunnis {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:17:00}.
And the making of the Sahih: hearing a companion of Ishaq ibn Rahawayh wish for 'a concise book of the Prophet's sunnas', it settled in his heart and he began collecting at eighteen, finishing in sixteen years, entering no hadith without first washing and praying two rak'as, and leaving many sound ones so the book would not grow long {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:22:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:23:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:24:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:25:00}.
He draws from him that one who splits his life into taking and giving, focuses on a field suiting his gift, and rubs against the great scholars reaches the summit — with balance that neglects neither worship nor character {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep09 00:18:00}.

## In the 'Al-Mubdi'un' programme (part 2)
Al-Suwaidan continues al-Bukhari's life, describing the labour of authorship: a student counted him rising eighteen times in one night to light the lamp, recall and write, and al-Warraq reports fifteen to twenty risings a night while travelling — for creativity is 'one percent inspiration and ninety-nine percent effort' {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:06:30}.
He explains the Sahih's greatness: not only the soundness of its hadiths but the fiqh of its chapter-headings, the ordering, and his own comments; and he would not write from a man until he asked his name, kunyah, lineage and stock of hadiths, tested his understanding and went back to the man's source {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:08:30}.
The Baghdad test: the hadith scholars scrambled a hundred hadiths — sound isnads on sound but unconnected texts — split among ten memorisers; al-Bukhari heard them all, said 'I do not know these', then returned them correctly ordered, 'the first isnad belongs to the seventh text' — whereupon Ibn Sa'id called him 'the butting ram' {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:11:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:12:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:13:30}.
Of his scrupulousness in jarh wa-ta'dil: he used to say 'I hope to meet God without being called to account for backbiting anyone', so instead of 'liar' he would say 'his hadith needs examining' — meaning suspect, weak — except rarely for fabricators {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:15:30}.
And when a scholar claimed he did not know how to pray he answered 'by God, if I wished I would narrate for you now ten thousand hadiths on prayer' {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:16:00}.
His early brilliance: at eleven he corrected his shaykh — 'Zayd did not narrate from Ibrahim, but Sulayman' — and the shaykh rose, fetched his source and corrected the book; while the people of Basra gathered to hear him at seventeen, his beard not yet full {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:18:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:10:30}.
His father Isma'il said on his deathbed 'I know not a dirham of my wealth from the unlawful nor a dirham of doubt' — and al-Bukhari remarks: a man is most truthful at death {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:21:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:21:30}.
Of the scholars' testimony: Qutayba's 'had he been among the Companions he would have been a sign', Ahmad's 'none like Muhammad ibn Isma'il came to us from Khurasan', and Ibn Khuzayma's 'under heaven's canopy I have seen none more knowing or more memorising of the Prophet's hadith' {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:22:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:23:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:23:30}.
He died at sixty-two; the lesson drawn: a single book can immortalise a man — 'a scholar's book is his immortal child' — with focus on one field and no distraction {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:23:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:24:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep10 00:24:30}.
