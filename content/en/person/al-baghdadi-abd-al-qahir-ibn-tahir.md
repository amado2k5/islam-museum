## Summary
Abd al-Qahir ibn Tahir ibn Muhammad ibn Abdallah al-Tamimi, Abu Mansur al-Baghdadi (d. 429/c. 1037), a Shafi'i jurist, theologian of the principles, man of letters, poet, grammarian and arithmetician, described as "the complete imam, master of many fields" {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba pp. 634-635} {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi pp. 265-266}.

## From Baghdad to Nishapur
He was born and raised in Baghdad, then travelled with his father Abu Abdallah Tahir — a man of wealth and honour — to Khurasan, and they settled at Nishapur; the son spent his father's fortune on the people of learning and hadith and never made money from his own scholarship {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba p. 634} {cite:tabaqat-shafiiyya-subki p. 238}.

## Teachers and pupils
He studied under the master Abu Ishaq al-Isfarayini, the Ash'ari jurist-theologian whose school at Nishapur was famous, reading the principles of religion under him and mastering many fields, above all arithmetic; he also heard Abu Amr ibn Nujayd, Muhammad ibn Ja'far ibn Matar, Abu Bakr al-Isma'ili and Abu Ahmad ibn Adi {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba p. 634} {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 265}. After his teacher's death in 418 he took his place dictating in the mosque for years, and the imams thronged to read under him — among them al-Qushayri and Nasir al-Marwazi — while al-Bayhaqi read jurisprudence under him and Abd al-Ghaffar ibn Shiruya transmitted from him {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba pp. 634-635} {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 265}.

## Standing
He taught in seventeen fields, a man of dignity and means; Abu Uthman al-Sabuni said he was among the imams of the principles and the leading men of Islam by the consensus of the people of merit {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 265}, and Fakhr al-Din al-Razi said he coursed against opponents like gazelles across the plains, and that the Takmila on arithmetic alone would have sufficed him {cite:tabaqat-shafiiyya-subki p. 238}.

## Works
A Qur'an commentary, Fada'ih al-Mu'tazila, al-Farq bayna al-Firaq, al-Fasl in legal theory, Tafdil al-Faqir al-Sabir, Fada'ih al-Karramiyya, Ta'wil Mutashabih al-Akhbar, a concise al-Milal wa'l-Nihal "without equal in its kind", Nafy Khalq al-Qur'an, al-Sifat, al-Iman wa-Usuluh, Bulugh al-Mada, Ibtal al-Qawl bi'l-Tawallud, al-'Imad in inheritance law, al-Takmila fi'l-Hisab, and a refutation of Abu Abdallah al-Jurjani's argument for Abu Hanifa's school {cite:tabaqat-shafiiyya-subki p. 239}. His Usul al-Din expounds the Ash'ari creed in fifteen principles covering theology, prophecy, the imamate and the rulings on the sects {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba p. 635}.

## Last days
The Turkmen disturbance forced him out of Nishapur in 429 — "among the calamities of Nishapur was that a man like him was forced to leave" — and he went to Isfarayin, whose people rejoiced at his coming beyond description; he stayed only a little while before dying there in 429, and the scholars agreed to bury him beside the master Abu Ishaq, so that their two graves stand side by side {cite:tabaqat-shafiiyya-subki pp. 238-239} {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba p. 635}. Ibn al-Najjar's date of 427 is a scribal error {cite:tabaqat-shafiiyya-subki p. 239}, and Ibn Shakir transmits 420 {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba p. 635}.

## In the study by the editor of al-Farq bayn al-Firaq
Muhammad Uthman al-Khisht, in the introduction to his edition of [[work/al-farq-bayna-al-firaq|al-Farq bayn al-Firaq]], says Abd al-Qahir ibn Tahir al-Baghdadi was born and raised in Baghdad, then as a youth travelled with his father to Khurasan and settled in Nishapur, where he attached himself to Abu Ishaq al-Isfara'ini and learned the principles of religion from him; Abu Ishaq had learned from Abu al-Hasan al-Bahili, a student of [[person/al-ashari|Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari]], so his scholarly lineage goes back to al-Ash'ari. {cite:farq-bayn-al-firaq-khisht-ibn-sina p.7} After his teacher's death in 411 AH he succeeded him in teaching at the Aqil mosque in Nishapur, where students gathered around him, among them Nasir al-Marwazi and Abu al-Qasim al-Qushayri; wealth left by his father allowed him to devote himself to learning. {cite:farq-bayn-al-firaq-khisht-ibn-sina p.7} He taught and wrote in Nishapur until the Turkmen troubles of 429 AH forced him to leave for Isfara'in, where he died the same year; the editor quotes al-Subki: among Nishapur's regrets was that a man like him had to leave it. {cite:farq-bayn-al-firaq-khisht-ibn-sina p.7} {cite:farq-bayn-al-firaq-khisht-ibn-sina p.8} The works the editor names include Usul al-Din, Fada'ih al-Qadariyya, al-Iman wa-Usuluh, al-Milal wa-l-Nihal, al-Sifat, Nafy Khalq al-Qur'an, Fada'ih al-Karramiyya, Ibtal al-Qawl bi-l-Tawallud and Tafsir Asma' Allah al-Husna. {cite:farq-bayn-al-firaq-khisht-ibn-sina p.8}
