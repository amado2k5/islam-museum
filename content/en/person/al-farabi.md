## Overview
Abu Nasr Muhammad ibn Muhammad ibn Tarkhan al-Farabi, known in medieval Latin texts as Alfarabius or Avennasar, was one of the most renowned Muslim philosophers. He became known as the "second teacher", the first being Aristotle. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 808} The entry in Kashf al-Zunun names him in full as Abu Nasr Muhammad ibn Muhammad ibn Tarkhan al-Turki al-Farabi, the sage, who died in 339 AH. {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p.290} The Encyclopaedia of Islam stresses that very little is known of his life, since he left no autobiography and no report by a contemporary survives. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 808}

## Origins and life
Al-Farabi was of Turkish origin. He was born in Turkestan at Wasidj, in the district of the town of Farab on the middle Jaxartes, and is said to have died aged eighty or more in 339 AH, 950 CE, in [[place/damascus|Damascus]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 808} His father is described as an army officer and may have belonged to the Turkish guard of the caliph, so al-Farabi may have reached [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] with him early in life. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 808}

In Baghdad he lived for many years as a private person, belonging neither to court society nor to the secretarial class. For reasons that are unknown, he accepted in 330/942 an invitation from the Hamdanid ruler [[person/sayf-al-dawla|Sayf al-Dawla]], and lived in his entourage, mainly in [[place/aleppo|Aleppo]], among other men of letters until his death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 808}

## Teachers and intellectual setting
The article names his teacher in philosophy as a Christian, Yuhanna ibn Haylan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 808} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 809} [[person/ibn-abi-usaybia-ahmad|Ibn Abi Usaybi'a]] and [[person/al-masudi|al-Mas'udi]] both link this line of teaching to a branch of the Greek philosophical school of Alexandria, which they say continued after the Arab conquest, moved to Antioch, and then spread to [[place/marw|Marw]], [[place/harran|Harran]] and Baghdad. Yuhanna is reported to have come from Marw to Baghdad after 295/908, so the author does not rule out that he taught al-Farabi in Marw. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 809}

Al-Farabi was also in touch with the translator and commentator [[person/matta-ibn-yunus|Matta ibn Yunus]], who died in 329/940 and was a leading figure of the Baghdad school of Christian Aristotelians, and he strongly influenced Yahya ibn Adi, who died in 362/972 and was that school's main representative in the next generation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 809} The article holds that his surviving philosophical works show his dependence on the tenth-century syllabus of Christian Aristotelian teaching in Baghdad and the impact of the late Alexandrian reading of Greek philosophy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 809}

## Philosophy and revelation
According to the article, al-Farabi was convinced that philosophy had come to an end elsewhere and had found a new home in the world of Islam. It says he held human reason to be superior to religious faith, and so gave the revealed religions a secondary place: in his view they offer non-philosophers a road to truth through symbols. Philosophical truth is valid everywhere, whereas the symbols differ from nation to nation. They are the work of philosopher-prophets, of whom, the author says, Muhammad was one. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 809} This is the article's account of his position and not a view the museum adopts. The article contrasts him with [[person/al-kindi|al-Kindi]], who made philosophy a handmaiden of revealed truth, and with [[person/al-razi-abu-bakr|al-Razi]], who condemned the prophets as impostors; al-Farabi instead gave organised religion, as Plato did, an important and indispensable function. The author adds that there is some evidence he reached this view gradually. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 809}

The article describes his aim as showing how Greek philosophy, which reached him as an almost closed system of truth, could answer the major questions of contemporary Islamic debate. Greek natural theology, in his hands, explains God as the first cause of emanation, divine inspiration as the result of the supreme perfection of the human mind, creation and providence as shown in the ordered hierarchy of the universe, and immortality as not granted to every human being. Philosophy also guides the freedom of moral choice and the good life. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 809}

He also had to rethink the existing Islamic sciences and give them a new place in his philosophy. The article lists a grammar adaptable to every language, a dialectical theology, kalam, serving a particular religion, and a jurisprudence restricted to service of the particular religion; the true lawgiver is the metaphysician, as Plato had shown in the Laws. Rhetoric and poetics are, in his scheme, the best means of bringing truth home to the majority of people by working on their imagination. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 809} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 810}

## Main doctrines
The article says that, like many later Greek thinkers, he believed Plato and Aristotle ultimately agreed. He followed Aristotle, as read by the late Greek commentators, in logic, natural science, psychology and metaphysics, the last developed along moderate Neoplatonic lines. In political science he preferred Plato's Republic and Laws, as understood by the middle Platonists, holding that Plato's analysis of imperfect states and his answers to political problems stayed valid. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 810}

On the first cause, the article says he combined the eternal creator of an eternal world of Plotinus with the Aristotelian divine mind. His active intellect is neither the first cause nor part of the human soul, but a transcendent entity mediating between the higher world, the world below the moon and the human mind. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 810} Prophecy is, for him, an indispensable ingredient of human perfection but is tied to the faculty of imagination, which is inferior to reason. It is neither possession by supernatural powers nor a mystical state, and divine inspiration may be granted to the perfect man who has reached the highest philosophical level together with the highest prophecy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 810}

## The perfect city and the ruler
In the article's summary, the perfect man, the philosopher, ought also to be the sovereign, and philosophy alone shows the path to reform of the caliphate. Al-Farabi envisages a perfect city, a perfect nation and a perfect world state. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 809} The article counts a group of major works as aimed at giving philosophy the sovereign place in thought and at organising the perfect society under a philosopher-king, and notes that he deliberately leaves the reader to work out the application. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 810} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 811}

## Works
The Arab bibliographers attribute more than a hundred works of varying size to him, not all of them genuine. One treatise, the Fusus fi'l-hikma, is by [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]], and its wrong attribution to al-Farabi has hidden how basic the differences are between the two most influential Islamic philosophers. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 810}

The article first lists large commentaries on Aristotle, continuing the tradition of the late Greek schools. They seem to have been used by [[person/ibn-bajja|Ibn Bajja]] and especially [[person/ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd]], and were largely superseded by their commentaries. One, on On Interpretation, was edited in Beirut in 1960. The article reports similar commentaries on the rest of the Organon, on the Physics, which he is said to have read more than forty times, on On the Heavens, the Meteorology and parts of the Nicomachean Ethics. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 810}

It also lists shorter introductory monographs in logic, physics, metaphysics and ethics and politics. Examples are paraphrases of Porphyry's Isagoge and of Aristotle's Categories and Prior Analytics, a treatise on the canons of poetry, On the Vacuum, Against Astrology, On the Intellect, the Reminder of the Way to Happiness, the Aphorisms of the Statesman and a summary of Plato's Laws. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 810} It further notes the Harmony between the Views of Plato and Aristotle, Answers to Questions and Main Questions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 810}

Three refutations the author could not trace help define his position: one against [[person/jalinus|Galen]], rejecting Galen's attacks on Aristotle's first cause; one, again in defence of Aristotle, aimed by implication at al-Kindi and others who hold that the world was created from nothing; and one against Muhammad ibn Zakariyya al-Razi, presumably over atoms and creation in time. A treatise against [[person/ibn-al-rawandi|Ibn al-Rawandi]] may have concerned his rejection of prophecy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 810}

The article names as his major works the Survey of the Sciences, Ihsa' al-Ulum; a three-part work on attaining happiness and on the philosophies of Plato and Aristotle; the work on the principles of the views of the inhabitants of the excellent state, [[work/ara-ahl-al-madina-al-fadila|The Views of the People of the Virtuous City]]; and On Political Government. Gerard of Cremona translated the Survey into medieval Latin. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 811} The Kashf al-Zunun entry also lists The Views of the People of the Virtuous City among his works. {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p.290}

## Reception and influence
The article calls al-Farabi's importance for later philosophers considerable. It judges his effect on tenth-century authors such as the [[group/ikhwan-al-safa|Ikhwan al-Safa]], al-Mas'udi, [[person/ibn-miskawayh|Miskawayh]] and Abu'l-Hasan al-Amiri undeniable, says Ibn Sina seems to have known his works intimately, and says Ibn Rushd follows him in essentials. It adds that Maimonides appreciated him highly, that a few treatises reached the Latin Schoolmen, and that his political ideas enjoyed a belated and lasting success from the thirteenth century. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 810}

## In the 'Al-Mubdi'un' programme
Tareq al-Suwaidan presents al-Farabi as "the Second Teacher" after Aristotle, a philosopher of the Arabs despite his Persian Farabi origin, since his culture was Arabic and his hundred or more works were all in Arabic, and as a master musician besides. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:04:30}
Of his life, the programme says he was born in Farab in 260 AH amid political turmoil while the civilisational current flowed on, and lived as an ascetic aloof from strife. It says he studied grammar under Ibn al-Sarraj in Baghdad while teaching him logic, and philosophy under Yuhanna ibn Khaylan in Harran, then surpassed his teachers. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:02:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:07:00} These details of birth date and teachers differ from the Encyclopaedia of Islam, which gives no birth date and places his teacher Yuhanna in Marw or Baghdad; the museum reports both.
How he entered philosophy: a student deposited Aristotle's books with him as a trust, letting him read them, and he threw himself upon them and mastered them alone. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:08:00}
Of his testimonies, the programme quotes Ibn Sina saying "I journeyed seeking Abu Nasr and never found him" and that he read the Metaphysics forty times without understanding it until he read al-Farabi's writings. It adds the saying that but for al-Farabi Aristotle would have been lost, his lost works recovered through al-Farabi's commentaries, and the orientalist Diago calling him of greater allure than Ibn Sina, his soul richer in flow. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:10:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:11:30}
Of his asceticism: he refused Sayf al-Dawla al-Hamdani's lavish gifts, content with four dirhams a day for necessity, spending most days alone composing in gardens without books, teaching the hardest questions, while counselling "do not take my life as your model". {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:12:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:14:30}
Of his philosophy: logical sequence and firm interconnection. In Ara' Ahl al-Madina al-Fadila he treats the First Existent as negating partner and opposite, in a manner the speaker finds consonant with the faith in its fundamentals, with caveats on some creedal points, the speaker's note. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:24:00}
His analysis of the virtuous city: man's need for man and cooperation, sixteen qualities of its leader, and its opposites, cities of license, appetites and materialism. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:22:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:22:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:23:00}
His reconciling project: reconciling Plato and Aristotle as differing only superficially while one in essence, so that religion might face a single philosophy, proving no contradiction between revelation and reason. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:18:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:19:30}
Of his works: al-Jam' bayna Ra'yay al-Hakimayn, Ara' Ahl al-Madina al-Fadila, Kitab al-Musiqi al-Kabir, Ihsa' al-'Ulum, and a treatise on the rules of poetry, forty surviving of over a hundred. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:21:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:21:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:22:00}
He died in Damascus in 339 AH, the recording saying 950 by error, corrected to 339 AH, after a life of austerity focused on a single field. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:23:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep26 00:24:00}
