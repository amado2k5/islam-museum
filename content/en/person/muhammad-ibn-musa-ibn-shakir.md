## Summary
Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Musa ibn Shakir was the eldest of the Banu Musa, the three sons of the astronomer Musa ibn Shakir who made Baghdad's House of Wisdom a centre of translation and original science in the ninth century. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en} {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-ar} The sources date him only "before 803" for his birth; his death in 873 is the one firm year in the brothers' lives — Ibn al-Nadim's Fihrist places it in Rabi' I of 259 AH. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en} {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim} The museum's queue figure of 805 for his birth is attested in no source consulted: the articles give the terminus "before 803", and his Wikidata item offers two competing unsourced values, 800 and 803, while recording no death date at all. {cite:wikidata-muhammad-ibn-musa-ibn-shakir} {cite:wikidata-banu-musa} Muhammad was the most productive of the brothers and, late in life, the most political. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en}

## Formation
The brothers' father Musa had been a highwayman and then an astronomer in Khurasan, where he befriended the future caliph al-Ma'mun at Merv and entered his service as astrologer and astronomer. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en} {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-ar} When Musa died, al-Ma'mun took charge of the orphaned boys, placed them under the guardianship of the official Ishaq ibn Ibrahim al-Mus'abi, and enrolled them in the House of Wisdom, where they were trained by the court astrologer Yahya ibn Abi Mansur and lived in Baghdad for the rest of their lives. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en}

## Patronage, translation and measurement
Under al-Ma'mun and his successors the brothers used their wealth to hunt Greek science: they sent agents to Byzantium, travelled there themselves, and paid a circle of translators — Hunayn ibn Ishaq among them — at rates the sources put near 500 dinars a month. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en} {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-ar} On a journey back from Byzantium Muhammad met a Harran money-changer named Thabit ibn Qurra, recognised his talent and brought him to Baghdad, where Thabit became one of the century's leading mathematicians and astronomers. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en} The brothers' own fieldwork included the measurement of a degree of latitude in the desert near Sinjar, repeated at Kufa, from which they obtained a value of about 24,000 miles for the Earth's circumference, and a programme of observations recorded between about 847 and 869 — stellar altitudes, the sun's extremes, and simultaneous sightings of a lunar eclipse from Samarra and Nishapur to fix their difference of latitude. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en} {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-ar}

## Court politics
Employment on caliphal engineering projects drew the brothers into politics. Under al-Mutawakkil they were given the Ja'fariyya canal; when their agent's error threatened to drain it, the caliph's engineer Sanad ibn Ali certified the work sound — knowing the caliph, whose death the astrologers had predicted, would not live to see the truth — and the assassination of al-Mutawakkil saved them. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en} They also turned court influence against al-Kindi, whose library was confiscated through their hostility before Sanad ibn Ali secured its return. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en} Muhammad's own peak came after al-Mutawakkil's death, when Turkish commanders were taking control of the state: he helped al-Musta'in secure the caliphate, and when al-Musta'in's brother besieged Baghdad, Muhammad was sent first to estimate the besieging army and then to learn the terms on which al-Musta'in would abdicate. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en} {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-ar}

## Works and death
Of the nearly twenty books the brothers wrote, most are lost. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en} Their joint Book on the Measurement of Plane and Spherical Figures — which treated area and circumference numerically where the Greeks had used ratios, and added theorems the Greeks did not have — was used by medieval mathematicians for centuries, in Gerard of Cremona's Latin version among others. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en} {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-ur} Muhammad's own writings include a recension of Apollonius's Conics, the Book on the First Motion of the Celestial Sphere, a treatise on the visibility of the crescent that cites his kunya Abu Ja'far, and a lost Book on the Beginning of the World. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en} {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim} He died in 873 — the Fihrist gives Rabi' I 259 AH; the English article's lead says February of that year and its Deaths section, following Bir, says January, so this record keeps the year. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en} {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim} No source consulted names a religion or legal school for him, and his faith is recorded as unattested. {cite:wikipedia-banu-musa-en} {cite:wikidata-muhammad-ibn-musa-ibn-shakir}
