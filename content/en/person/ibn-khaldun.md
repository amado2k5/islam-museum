## Overview
Wali al-Din Abu Zayd Abd al-Rahman ibn Muhammad ibn Khaldun al-Hadrami, author of the [[work/al-muqaddima|Muqaddima]] and the Kitab al-Ibar, was born in Tunis in 732 AH (1332 CE) and died in 808 AH (1406 CE) {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun p. 3} {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba vol. 8 p. 407} {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 4/145}. He wrote for Sultan [[person/abu-inan-faris|Abu Inan]] at Fez, was imprisoned in 758 AH until the sultan died, and then became confidential secretary to his brother Abu Salim {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.2058-2060} {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 4/145}. He completed the Muqaddima in five months ending in the middle of 779 AH, then revised it and attached to it the histories of the Arabs and the Berbers {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun p.652}. He opens it by calling history a noble art of many benefits, and he makes the subject of his science umran, the necessary human association {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun p.22} {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun p. 48}.

He reached Egypt in 784 AH, taught at al-Azhar, and was made Maliki judge by [[person/barquq|al-Zahir Barquq]] in 786 AH {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 4/146}. In 803 AH he went out with the army to Syria and was brought before [[person/timur-lang|Timur]] at Damascus, where the jurist Abd al-Jabbar ibn al-Nu'man al-Khwarazmi interpreted between them {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.2121}. He died suddenly, still a judge, in Ramadan 808 AH and was buried in the Sufi cemetery outside Bab al-Nasr {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 4/146}.

[[person/al-sakhawi|Al-Sakhawi]] quotes the judgement of [[person/al-maqrizi|al-Maqrizi]] that nothing like his Muqaddima had been made, and with it views critical of his judgeship and his history {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 4/146} {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 4/147} {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 4/148}. The Mawsu'at A'lam al-Ulama' wa-l-Adaba' calls him the author of the first book on the philosophy of history and human society among the scholars of Arab-Islamic civilisation {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba vol. 8 p. 407}.

## Family and education at Tunis
Ibn Khaldun was born at [[place/tunis|Tunis]] on 1 Ramadan 732, 27 May 1332, into an Arab family that came originally from the [[place/hadramawt|Hadramawt]] and had settled at [[place/seville|Seville]] at the start of the Muslim conquest, playing an important political role there. The family left Seville for [[place/ceuta|Ceuta]] just before the Christian reconquest, then moved to [[place/ifriqiya|Ifriqiya]] and settled in Tunis under the [[polity/hafsid-dynasty|Hafsid]] ruler Abu Zakariyya. His great-grandfather, who wrote a treatise on the secretary's craft, was put in charge of finances under Abu Ishaq and was strangled by a usurper after torture and the seizure of his property. His grandfather held posts at Bougie and Tunis, and his father avoided politics and lived as a jurist and man of letters. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 857}

The father made sure the boy received a thorough classical education, based on the Qur'an, hadith, the Arabic language and jurisprudence, with courses from the famous teachers of Tunis, whom Ibn Khaldun describes at length in his autobiography. The [[polity/marinid-dynasty|Marinid]] invasion of 748-50 brought many scholars to Tunis with the sultan Abu al-Hasan, and under the supervision of [[person/al-abili-muhammad-ibn-ibrahim|al-Abili]] the young man learned about philosophy and the main problems of Arabo-Muslim thought. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 857}

The Marinid occupation ended in disorder, and the [[event/ibn-khaldun-parents-die-black-death-1349|Black Death]] killed many people in Tunis, among them his parents, when he was seventeen. The article calls this his first traumatic experience, one that left its mark on his Autobiography and the Muqaddima and, it judges, on the direction of his thought. When the Marinid scholars left, an intellectual vacuum remained, and his aim became to reach [[place/fez|Fez]], then the most brilliant capital of the Muslim West. His elder brother Muhammad dissuaded him, but not for long. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 857} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 858}

## Fez, Granada and Bougie
Not yet twenty, towards the end of 751 AH, he was appointed by the chamberlain Ibn Tafragin as writer of the ruler's official signature for the sultan Abu Ishaq, a post he apparently did not intend to keep long. An invasion of Ifriqiya in 753/1352 gave him the chance to leave, and he went by Ebba, Tebessa and Gafsa to [[place/biskra|Biskra]], where he spent the winter among friends of the Banu Muzni. The article notes that this was the first of many changes of direction for which most students of his life have criticised him, but suggests it may not have been a mistake, since Ifriqiya was disintegrating. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 858}

After the death of Abu al-Hasan, his son [[person/abu-inan-faris|Abu Inan]] took power at Fez and reduced Bougie again to submission. Ibn Khaldun offered him his services, was received at Bougie by the Marinid chamberlain Ibn Abi Amr, and was then summoned to Fez, where he joined the sultan's literary circle and his secretariat, without enthusiasm, since such a post was not in the family tradition. He kept studying under the teachers of the Maghrib and of Spain who were then at Fez. The article says that he may have joined a plot to free the former amir of Bougie, which he himself denies, citing intrigue and jealousy. He was imprisoned for two years, 758-9/1357-8, until the death of Abu Inan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 858}

Freed amid disorder over the succession, he changed allegiance, as was common, and in Sha'ban 760 became secretary of the chancellery for the new sultan [[person/abu-salim-ibrahim-marinid|Abu Salim]]. He even took up court poetry and quotes long extracts of his panegyrics, but his fortune declined. Two years later he moved to a judicial post, the redress of grievances, and after another change of sultan, feeling unjustly deprived of the fruits of victory, he made enemies and eventually obtained leave to go to Granada. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 858}

At Granada he was received with the highest honours, thanks to the friendship formed at Fez with the vizier [[person/ibn-al-khatib|Ibn al-Khatib]]. At the end of 765/1364 he was sent to Seville on a delicate peace mission to Pedro the Cruel, a contact with the Christian world that the article says influenced him. On his return the Nasrid amir showered favours on him, but because Ibn al-Khatib resented his success he chose not to exploit the position. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 858}

In 766/1365 his friend Abu Abd Allah Muhammad regained the kingdom of Bougie and made him chamberlain, then the most important office in the state, and also teacher of jurisprudence and preacher. The success was brief: the amir of [[place/constantine|Constantine]] defeated and killed Muhammad in the following year, and Ibn Khaldun handed the town over in Sha'ban 767 and served the conqueror for a short time before resigning. He refused the offer of the chamberlain's post at [[place/tlemcen|Tlemcen]] and sent his brother instead, explaining that he was cured of the temptation of office and wished to devote himself to study and teaching. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 858} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 859}

## Retreat and the castle of Ibn Salama
At Biskra he corresponded at length with Ibn al-Khatib, but the article says he could not resist intrigue, and he supported one alliance after another while trying to raise tribal forces behind a great power. There were too many claimants and no winner in the West of that century. His Banu Muzni hosts began to object to his activities, and a retreat to the ribat of Abu Madyan and a failure at Fez in 774/1372 followed, after which he was permitted to withdraw to Muslim Spain in 776/1375. There, in the article's words, he had become a political personality too well known to be trusted. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 859}

Practically ordered to leave Granada, he returned to the Maghrib and settled at Tlemcen in Shawwal 776/March 1375, while Ibn al-Khatib, whom he had tried in vain to save, was strangled in prison at Fez. The sultan of Tlemcen wished to use him again, so Ibn Khaldun slipped away to the Awlad Arif, and for four years, 776-80/1375-9, he lived in the castle of Ibn Salama, south-west of present-day Frenda in Oran. The article calls this the decisive turning point: enclosed for the first time in his ivory tower, he says that he worked out the Muqaddima there on an original plan that came to him in retirement. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 859}

## Tunis again and Cairo
Needing documentation for his work, he asked permission to return to Tunis from the Hafsid ruler Abu al-Abbas, arrived in Sha'ban 780/1378, taught, and finished a first version of the Ibar, which he presented to the sultan with a long panegyric. His teaching, which some thought subversive, and the ruler's favours earned him enemies, among them a cabal led by the famous [[person/ibn-arafa|Ibn Arafa]]. Using the pilgrimage as a pretext, he left for Alexandria by ship on 15 Sha'ban 784/24 October 1382. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 859}

In [[place/cairo|Cairo]] he was at first dazzled. Students flocked to his courses at [[place/al-azhar|al-Azhar]], and he was named teacher of Maliki jurisprudence at the Qamhiyya madrasa and, in Jumada II 786/1384, Maliki chief judge. His family, allowed to join him through the intervention of the sultan [[person/barquq|al-Zahir Barquq]], was shipwrecked off [[place/alexandria|Alexandria]], and his intransigence and the intrigues of enemies angry that so high an office had gone to a foreigner led to his dismissal in 787/1385. In 789/1387 he was named to the Zahiriyya madrasa, then, after the pilgrimage, to teach hadith at the [[building/madrasa-of-sarghatmish|madrasa of Sarghatmish]], where his inaugural lectures on the Muwatta of Malik are preserved. He was also placed at the head of the [[building/khanqah-of-baybars-al-gashankir|khanqah of Baybars]], which the article calls the most important Sufi convent in Egypt. After fourteen years of teaching he was appointed judge again on 15 Ramadan 801/1399. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 859}

Dismissed in Muharram 803, he was obliged to accompany the sultan al-Nasir on the expedition to relieve [[place/damascus|Damascus]], threatened by [[person/timur-lang|Timurlang]], who was already master of [[place/aleppo|Aleppo]]. Left in the besieged city when al-Nasir departed, he played a part in its surrender on a false promise of safety and gave a detailed account of his interviews with the Mongol leader. The article says he may have seen in Timur a man with enough group feeling to reunite the Muslim world and give history a new direction. He wrote a description of the Maghrib for Timur, witnessed the burning and sacking of Damascus, and was stripped and robbed by brigands on the road back. He was nonetheless well received at court and was appointed judge and dismissed four more times. His sixth and last appointment came in Sha'ban 808/1406, a few weeks before his death on 26 Ramadan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 859} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 860}

In Cairo he kept his Maghribi dress, a dark burnous, encouraged exchanges of gifts between the sultans of Egypt and the Maghrib, sent a copy of the Ibar to the Marinid Abu Faris, and kept up correspondence with friends, preserving letters from the Granadan poet [[person/ibn-zamrak|Ibn Zamrak]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 860}

## Judgements on his life
The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls him one of the strongest personalities of Arabo-Muslim culture in the period of its decline, generally regarded as a historian, sociologist and philosopher. It adds that studies of his life and work have produced very varied and even contradictory interpretations. It divides his life into three parts: about twenty years of childhood and education, twenty-three of study and political adventure, and thirty-one as scholar, teacher and magistrate. The first two periods were spent in the Muslim West, and the third was divided between the Maghrib and Egypt. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 857}

The article reports that his life has been judged variously and rather severely, and that he himself openly records his successive changes of allegiance, so that he has been accused of fickleness and lack of patriotism. It argues that such charges presuppose an idea of allegiance to a country that scarcely existed then, that treachery was commonplace at every rank, and that he should be judged by the standards of his own time. It also suggests, without claiming proof, that his adventures could be read as a calculated search for a group feeling strong enough to save a civilization he thought dying, and adds that his Autobiography gives no way to know his real intentions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 860}

## Early and minor works
At about twenty, under al-Abili, he summarised the theological and philosophical summa of al-Razi, the [[work/muhassal-afkar-al-mutaqaddimin|Muhassal afkar al-mutaqaddimin]], under the title Lubab al-Muhassal; the autograph is dated 29 Safar 752/1351. Between 752 and 765 he wrote, according to Ibn al-Khatib, a commentary on the Burda of [[person/al-busiri|al-Busiri]], an outline of logic, a treatise on arithmetic, summaries of works by [[person/ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd]] and a commentary on a poem by Ibn al-Khatib on legal theory. All are lost and seem to have been forgotten quickly. Later works include an autobiography that stops in Dhu al-Qa'da 807/1405 and the Shifa al-sa'il, a treatise on mysticism whose authenticity the article says is not finally settled. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 860}

The article holds that nothing up to then showed he would be remembered as a founder of disciplines; his genius flowered at the castle of Ibn Salama, where traditional learning met the political experience that had made him aware of the meaning, ibar, of history. The first draft of the Muqaddima and large parts of the history were written in 776-80/1375-9, and he went on rewriting them until the end of his life. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 860}

## The Muqaddima and its ideas
The article describes the Muqaddima as an introduction to the historian's craft and an encyclopaedic synthesis of the methods and cultural knowledge a historian needs. Starting from questions of method, Ibn Khaldun was led to what he called a new science, which implicitly holds the seeds of the philosophy of history, sociology and economics. In his preface he defines history broadly, to include social, economic and cultural aspects, criticises predecessors for lack of method, and bases criticism on conformity with reality, meaning the probability of reported facts. This leads to the need to find the laws of historical change, which he assigns to the science of [[concept/umran|umran]], human civilization. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 861}

The main body is set out in six long chapters: a general account of human society with the influence of environment; nomadic civilization; states, government and institutions; urban civilization; crafts and economic affairs; and learning, literature and culture. The article judges that the central concern is the aetiology of decline, the causes of the death of civilizations, and ties this to the author's sense that he was witnessing a tremendous change, a new creation in his own words. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 861} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 862}

Observation is his main tool. The article stresses his realism, his use of logic and induction, his distrust of speculative reasoning and, after a radical criticism of knowledge, his refutation of philosophy, replaced by an empiricism tied to concrete facts. It also finds in his treatment of conflict and contradiction a dialectical movement, and says that his well-known cyclical scheme, not original in itself, should be read inside this wider view. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 862}

Specialists have found in it the beginnings of several modern disciplines. The article quotes Lacoste on history as a science, calls his new science essentially a system of sociology, and singles out [[concept/asabiyya|asabiyya]] as his most fruitful concept, both it and umran being much debated by modern scholars. It reports that he saw differences between generations as expressions of different economic ways of life, a sentence often compared with Marx, but judges it difficult to regard him as a forerunner of materialism, because his explanations are also psychological. It also mentions economic doctrines, a philosophy of history, and ethnographic and demographic information of value. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 862}

## The Ibar, reception and legacy
The Ibar attracted less interest. The article says the usual criticism, that it does not fulfil the promises of the Muqaddima, is obvious but unavoidable, and adds that Ibn Khaldun shows surprising gaps of learning and few precise dates. It nevertheless cites Brunschvig, who calls it an incomparable tool for the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, and says it is most valuable for the Muslim West. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 861}

According to the article, the Ottoman historian [[person/mustafa-naima|Naima]] praised him, and a first Turkish translation of part of the Muqaddima was made in 1143/1749, yet it was in Europe that the work's importance was realised: through d'Herbelot, de Sacy, von Hammer-Purgstall and Quatremere, who produced the first complete edition in 1858, and de Slane, who published the French translation Les Prolegomenes in 1863-8. Rosenthal's English translation of 1958 follows the Istanbul manuscript that bears a note in Ibn Khaldun's hand. No truly critical edition of either work yet existed. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 861}

The article concludes that he has been considered, since his discovery in Europe, an authentic genius, a solitary one who belonged to no definite current of Arabo-Muslim thought. It reports Brunschvig's view that he had no forerunners or successors in Arabic until the contemporary period, and that in his native Barbary he met incomprehension and hostility. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 862} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 863}

## In his own words: the Muqaddima
He opened his introduction by saying that the art of history is a noble discipline with many benefits, which makes known the conditions of past nations, prophets and kings. He took as the subject of his new science *al-'Umran*, that is, the necessary human association. {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun p.22}

In its conclusion he reports that he completed it in five months, finishing in the middle of 779 AH, and then revised it and attached to it the histories of the Arabs and the Berbers in the *Ibar*. {cite:muqaddima-ibn-khaldun p.652}

## On writing for sultans
In the conclusion of the *Ibar* he relates, in the first person, his attachment to the sultan Abu Inan at the end of 757 AH and his employment in writing for him. He was imprisoned on 18 Safar 758 AH until the sultan died. He then wrote for the sultan Abu Salim, and entered the new city with him in the middle of Sha'ban 760 AH. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.2058-2060}

## Meeting Timur
In the *Tadhkira* of the *Ibar* he says that he asked the sultan al-Nasir Faraj for permission to visit the mosque, and he visited [[place/al-khalil|Hebron]] at the end of Ramadan 802 AH. When Timur advanced on Syria, he travelled with the army in 803 AH, and remained in Damascus after the sultan fled. He was brought before Timur, and the jurist Abd al-Jabbar ibn al-Nu'man al-Khwarazmi interpreted between them. Ibn Khaldun describes Timur as very shrewd and much given to inquiry. {cite:tarikh-ibn-khaldun-ibar p.2118-2126}

## In al-Sakhawi's al-Daw' al-Lami'
[[person/al-sakhawi|Al-Sakhawi]] gives an entry for Abd al-Rahman ibn Muhammad Ibn Khaldun, Wali al-Din Abu Zayd al-Hadrami, of Sevillian origin, of Tunis and later Cairo, the Maliki: born at Tunis at the start of Ramadan 732. {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 4/145} He reached Egypt in Dhu al-Qa'da 784, taught at al-Azhar, was made Maliki chief judge by al-Zahir Barquq in Jumada al-Akhira 786, and met Timur, who honoured him, and came away safely. {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 4/146} He died suddenly in office on Wednesday, four nights before the end of Ramadan 808, and was buried in the Sufi cemetery outside Bab al-Nasr. {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 4/146} Al-Sakhawi quotes divergent views of him, including sharp criticism of his judgeship and his history, as others' reports. {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 4/147} {cite:daw-lami-jil-1992 4/148}

## At the khanqah of Baybars al-Jashankir
Yeomans writes that Ibn Khaldun, born in Tunis, served the courts of Fez, Granada, Tlemcen and Tunis before settling in Cairo, and met the great figures of his day, among them Tamerlane, for whom he wrote a brief history of North Africa. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.150} He counts him the first historian to develop a sociological explanation of history, analysing geographical, climatic, social, economic and moral factors in the Muqaddima. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.150} He came to Egypt in 1384, was made professor of Maliki law and then Maliki chief judge by al-Zahir Barquq, directed the khanqah of Baybars al-Jashankir, and died in 1406, buried in the Sufi cemetery outside Cairo. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.150} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.151}
