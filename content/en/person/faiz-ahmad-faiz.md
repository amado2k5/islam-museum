## Summary
Pakistani poet and journalist (1911–1984), among the greatest Urdu poets of the twentieth century. He joined classical ghazal to politically committed verse; his collections include Naqsh-e-Faryadi, Dast-e-Saba and Zindan-Nama. He won the Lenin Peace Prize in 1962 and spent years in exile in London and Beirut. {cite:wikipedia-faiz-ahmad-faiz}

## Life and career

Faiz Ahmad Faiz was born in 1911 in Sialkot, Punjab (later Pakistan), learned the Qur'an, Persian and Urdu, then took master's degrees in Arabic and English at Lahore and taught at the Anglo-Arabic College in Amritsar. {cite:wikipedia-faiz-ahmad-faiz}

He joined the socialist movement and the Communist Party of Pakistan, became editor of the Pakistan Times, and was imprisoned in the 1951 Rawalpindi 'conspiracy case', during which he wrote some of his most famous poems. {cite:wikipedia-faiz-ahmad-faiz}

## Poetry

He published collections in Urdu and English, including Naqsh-e-Faryadi (1941), Dast-e-Saba (1952), Zindan-Nama, Dast-e-Tah-e-Sang and Sar-e-Wadi-e-Sina, joining the ghazal tradition to themes of freedom, the poor and revolution. {cite:wikipedia-faiz-ahmad-faiz}

His style was known for merging romance and resistance — love poems carrying double political meanings — and his line 'Bol, ke lab azad hain tere' (Speak, for your lips are free) became a slogan of freedom in South Asia. {cite:wikipedia-faiz-ahmad-faiz}

## Legacy

After his release Faiz spent years in exile, working in London and then in Beirut as editor of the journal Lotus; he won the Lenin Peace Prize in 1962 and the Lotus Prize for Afro-Asian literature, and was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature. {cite:wikipedia-faiz-ahmad-faiz}

He died in Lahore in 1984; his poetry is still recited and sung in Pakistan, India and the diaspora, and he stands with Iqbal and Ghalib among the pillars of Urdu poetry, one of the most translated poets in modern South Asian literary history. {cite:wikipedia-faiz-ahmad-faiz}
