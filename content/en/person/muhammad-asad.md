## Summary
Muhammad Asad (born Leopold Weiss; 2 July 1900 – 20 February 1992) was an Austro-Hungarian-born Pakistani writer, journalist, traveller, political theorist, linguist and diplomat. Born into a Jewish family of rabbinic descent at Lemberg in Galicia, he converted to Islam in Berlin in 1926 and took the name Muhammad Asad. He served the new state of Pakistan as Director of the Department of Islamic Reconstruction and as Pakistan's envoy to the United Nations, and became one of the most influential European Muslims of the twentieth century. His best-known works are his autobiography *The Road to Mecca* (1954) and *The Message of the Qur'an*, his English translation and commentary of the Qur'an, published after seventeen years of work and ranked alongside the translations of Pickthall and Yusuf Ali. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-asad-en} {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-asad-ar}

## Early life
Leopold Weiss was born on 2 July 1900 at Lemberg, then in the Austro-Hungarian Empire and now Lviv in Ukraine. He was descended from a long line of Jewish rabbis, but his father Akiva Weiss broke with the tradition and became a lawyer. Leopold received a religious education, was proficient in Hebrew from an early age and familiar with Aramaic, and studied the Bible, the Talmud, the Mishna and Gemara, and biblical exegesis and the Targum. By his mid-twenties he could read and write in English, French, Persian and Arabic besides his native German and Polish. At fourteen he escaped school and tried to join the Austrian army under a false name; he was found within about a week and returned. He studied philosophy at the University of Vienna before turning to journalism. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-asad-en} {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-asad-ar} {cite:wikidata-muhammad-asad}

## Journalism and conversion
Weiss travelled in Mandatory Palestine as a journalist and argued publicly with Zionist leaders, including Chaim Weizmann, voicing reservations about aspects of the Zionist movement. His engagement with Islam deepened through his travels in the Arab world, and in 1926, in Berlin, he converted to Islam before the head of the city's Muslim community and adopted the name Muhammad Asad — Asad, "lion", rendering the root of his given name Leo. The English article describes the conversion as a conversion to Sunni Islam, and the Institute of Objective Studies' conference concept note (New Delhi, 2022) independently states that "he accepted Sunni Islam in 1926 (in Berlin)". The Urdu article adds that he accepted Islam at the hands of Abdul Jabbar Kheri. No source read for this record names a legal school (madhhab) for him, so the museum records his affiliation as Sunni on the basis of those two texts, without a school. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-asad-en} {cite:institute-of-objective-studies-asad} {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-asad-ur}

## Arabia and India
In Saudi Arabia Asad spent time among the Bedouin and enjoyed the close company of the state's founder, Ibn Saud; he also carried out a secret mission for Ibn Saud to trace the sources of funding for the Ikhwan revolt. Western writing of the time dubbed him "Leopold of Arabia". In India he became friends with the poet-philosopher Muhammad Iqbal, who persuaded him to give up his eastward travels and "help elucidate the intellectual premises of the future Islamic state". He edited the Hyderabad journal *Islamic Culture* in 1937–1938, and at the outbreak of the Second World War the British government interned him for some five years as an enemy alien. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-asad-en}

## Pakistan
On 14 August 1947, the day Pakistan gained independence, Asad received Pakistani citizenship. He served as Director of the Department of Islamic Reconstruction, then as Deputy Secretary (Middle East Division) in the Foreign Ministry, and then as Pakistan's envoy to the United Nations in New York. After leaving government service he devoted himself to writing. {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-asad-en} {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-asad-ar}

## Works
His first book after his conversion, *Islam at the Crossroads*, appeared in 1934. His autobiography *The Road to Mecca* (1954) became a best-seller in the West. His magnum opus, *The Message of the Qur'an* — a translation of the Qur'an into English with commentary, the fruit of seventeen years of scholarly research — is regarded, with the translations of Pickthall and Yusuf Ali, as one of the most influential Qur'an translations of the modern era. An ardent proponent of rationality and independent reasoning (ijtihad) in interpreting religious texts, he dedicated his works "to People who Think". His later essays include *This Law of Ours* (1987). {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-asad-en}

## Death and recognition
Asad died on 20 February 1992 at Mijas in Spain — Wikidata also carries a conflicting date of 23 February, and the museum records the article's date — and he was buried in the Muslim cemetery of Granada. In 2008 the entrance square to the United Nations Office in Vienna was named Muhammad Asad Platz in his honour, commemorating his work as a "religious bridge-builder". His biographers have described him as "Europe's gift to Islam" and "a mediator between Islam and the West". {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-asad-en} {cite:wikipedia-muhammad-asad-ar} {cite:wikidata-muhammad-asad}
