## Overview
Ahmad ibn Tulun, a ruler who governed Egypt. The sources mention him in the events of the Abbasid caliphate. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.579}

## With al-Musta'in
The "History of the Caliphs" says that [[person/al-mutazz|al-Mu'tazz]] sent to him to go to [[person/al-mustain|al-Musta'in]] and kill him. He said: "By God, I will not kill the sons of the caliphs." {cite:tarikh-khulafa-minhaj-1434 p.556}

Al-Mas'udi says he was assigned to guard al-Musta'in when he was brought down to Wasit, before his governorship of Egypt. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.545}

Ibn al-Jawzi says that he accompanied al-Musta'in and treated him well, and that he refused to kill him. After the killing he washed and shrouded the body and buried it. Ibn al-Jawzi relates from him that he said God had compensated him for the governorship of Wasit with the governorship of Egypt and Syria. {cite:muntazam-ibn-al-jawzi j.5 p.76-77}

## Death
Al-Mas'udi says he died in Egypt on Saturday, ten days into Dhu al-Qa'da 270 AH, at the age of sixty-five. His rule lasted seventeen years. When he despaired of himself, he gave the allegiance to his son Abu al-Jaysh [[person/khumarawayh|Khumarawayh]] as his successor. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.579}

Ibn al-Jawzi relates reports about his spending on the mosque and his almsgiving, and that he left much wealth. His figures are numerous and need to be compared with a second source. {cite:muntazam-ibn-al-jawzi j.5 p.76-77}

## In al-Ya'qubi
[[person/al-yaqubi|Al-Ya'qubi]], in his account of al-Mu'tamid's caliphate, names the Turk Ahmad ibn Tulun as governor of Egypt and says he sent the reserve of Egypt's treasury to al-Mu'tamid, 2,100,000 dirhams together with horses, embroidered textiles and wax, weighing it out himself until it was handed over. Al-Mu'tamid wrote appointing him to Alexandria, and he went there in Ramadan 257 AH. {cite:tarikh-yaqubi-houtsma-1883 vol. 2 p. 621} {cite:tarikh-yaqubi-houtsma-1883 vol. 2 p. 622}
That year, al-Ya'qubi says, he sent a Turk with a thousand horsemen alongside the Egyptian pilgrims, ordered to enter Medina, Mecca and Arafat armed and in battle order; he also sent the Talibids out of Egypt to Medina, and had a descendant of al-Abbas ibn Ali who tried to leave for the Maghrib flogged. Among the events of 258 AH he records that Ibn Tulun took an oath of allegiance to himself from the army, the shakiriyya, the clients and the people at large, binding them to be enemies of his enemies and friends of his friends. {cite:tarikh-yaqubi-houtsma-1883 vol. 2 p. 622} {cite:tarikh-yaqubi-houtsma-1883 vol. 2 p. 623} {cite:tarikh-yaqubi-houtsma-1883 vol. 2 p. 624}

## His career and building in Yeomans
Yeomans writes that Ahmad ibn Tulun (835-884) studied the Qur'an, Arabic, theology and jurisprudence in Baghdad and Tarsus, then underwent extensive military training in Samarra, the new Abbasid capital, whose cultural atmosphere left an indelible mark on him. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.28} In 868, aged thirty-three, he was appointed to govern Egypt as deputy for his stepfather Bayakbak, while finance and the post were held by his rival Ibn al-Mudabbir; when Ibn al-Mudabbir was transferred to Syria four years later he consolidated his power and built an army of Turkish, Sudanese and Greek slave troops. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.28} After Bayakbak the fief of Egypt passed to his father-in-law Yarjukh and then to the caliph's son Ja'far al-Mufawwad, and under each Ibn Tulun was confirmed in office. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.28}

When al-Mu'tamid divided the empire between his son al-Mufawwad in the west and his brother al-Muwaffaq in the east, Ibn Tulun exploited their rivalry by withholding Egypt's revenues from Baghdad, then saw off an army sent by al-Muwaffaq and invaded Syria, so that Egypt became independent and annexed Abbasid territory. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.29} He built [[place/al-qatai|al-Qata'i]] and [[building/masjid-ibn-tulun|his mosque]] on the model of Samarra and restored the [[building/nilometer-of-rawda|Nilometer]], erasing al-Mutawakkil's name from it; in Yeomans's view he changed the face of art and architecture in Egypt. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.28} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.29} {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.30} He died in May 884 before his dispute with al-Muwaffaq was resolved and was succeeded by his twenty-year-old son [[person/khumarawayh|Khumarawayh]]. {cite:yeomans-islamic-cairo-2006 p.39}
