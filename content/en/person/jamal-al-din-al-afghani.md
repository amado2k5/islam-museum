## Overview
Muhammad ibn Safdar al-Husayni, Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (1838–1897 CE). Al-Zirikli describes him as "the philosopher of Islam in his age", and as one of those on whose shoulders the modern renaissance of the East was raised. {cite:al-aalam tarjama}

## His life
He was born in Asadabad in Afghanistan, and travelled to India, where he excelled in the rational and transmitted sciences. He made the pilgrimage in 1273 AH, then settled in Istanbul. He went to Egypt, where he breathed the spirit of reform into it, and [[person/muhammad-abduh|Muhammad Abduh]] became his pupil. {cite:al-aalam tarjama}

The Egyptian government exiled him to Paris, where he founded with his pupil the newspaper *al-Urwa al-Wuthqa*. He then made many long journeys, and was invited to Istanbul, where he died. {cite:al-aalam tarjama}

Al-Zirikli says that he spoke seven languages well, and that he did not produce many writings. Among his works is *Risala al-Radd 'ala al-Dahriyyin* (The Refutation of the Materialists), which his pupil Muhammad Abduh translated into Arabic. {cite:al-aalam tarjama}

## In The Oxford Dictionary of Islam
The Oxford Dictionary of Islam differs from the record on where he came from. It says al-Afghani was born and brought up in Iran and that a claim of Afghan origin was made for him; it adds that he studied in Iran and Iraq, went to India and met British colonial rule there, spent time in Afghanistan, Istanbul and Egypt, and was expelled for his anti-British stance and for teachings said to be heretical. With [[person/muhammad-abduh|Muhammad Abduh]] he issued the journal al-Urwa al-Wuthqa, which stressed pan-Islamism and active opposition to British rule across Muslim countries. {cite:oxford-dictionary-of-islam e54}

The Dictionary lists his positions as a call for Sunni-Shii unity, trust in reason and natural law (while he gave ordinary people conventional religion), and a push for internal reform and technical and scientific schooling. It counts him among the first modern reformers to turn Islam into mainly a political programme, and names his means: speeches, newspaper articles, a Masonic lodge, opposition to foreign concessions, secret groups, leaflets, and a role in the assassination of the shah of Iran. {cite:oxford-dictionary-of-islam e54} The two accounts of his birthplace are set side by side and not settled: the record follows al-Zirikli, who gives Asadabad.
