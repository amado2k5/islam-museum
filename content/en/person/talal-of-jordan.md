## Summary
Talal (1909–1972) was King of Jordan from the assassination of his father, Abdullah I, in July 1951 until his forced abdication in August 1952. Born in Mecca as the eldest son of Abdullah bin Hussein, he was educated privately in Amman and trained as an officer in the Arab Legion and at Sandhurst. His short reign is remembered above all for Jordan's modern constitution of 1952, which made the government responsible to parliament. Deposed because of a mental illness reported as schizophrenia, he spent the remaining twenty years of his life at a sanatorium in Istanbul. {cite:wikipedia-talal-of-jordan-en} {cite:wikidata-talal-of-jordan}

## Early life and education
Talal was born in Mecca on 26 February 1909, the eldest child of Abdullah, then an Arab deputy of Mecca in the Ottoman parliament, and his wife Musbah bint Nasser. His grandfather, Sharif Hussein of Mecca, led the Great Arab Revolt of 1916, and his father established the Emirate of Transjordan in 1921. Talal spent his early years with his mother during his father's absences, received a private education in Amman, and joined the Arab Legion as a second lieutenant in 1927. He served as aide to his exiled grandfather in Cyprus, attended the Royal Military College at Sandhurst, graduating in 1929, and had risen to general by 1948. In November 1934 he married Zein al-Sharaf bint Jamil; their first son, Hussein, was born in 1935. When his father became king of the newly independent Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan, Talal became crown prince. {cite:wikipedia-talal-of-jordan-en} {cite:wikipedia-talal-of-jordan-ar}

## Reign
On 20 July 1951 Abdullah I was assassinated at the al-Aqsa Mosque in Jerusalem, where the fifteen-year-old Hussein, at his grandfather's side, also narrowly escaped death; Talal ascended the throne. His most enduring act was the liberalised constitution ratified on 1 January 1952, which made the government collectively, and ministers individually, responsible to the Jordanian parliament, rendering the kingdom a constitutional monarchy. He is also credited with smoothing Jordan's previously strained relations with Egypt and Saudi Arabia. Contemporary Egyptian and Syrian papers praised him as a patriotic anti-imperialist, in contrast to his half-brother Naif, who also sought the throne. {cite:wikipedia-talal-of-jordan-en}

## Forced abdication and death
Within a year of his accession, Talal was reported by the British resident, by Queen Zein al-Sharaf and by Prime Minister Tawfiq Abu al-Huda to be suffering from mental illness, and he was flown to Paris for treatment. In May 1952 his wife sought refuge in the British embassy in Paris, claiming he had threatened her and one of his younger children. Parliament forced his abdication on 11 August 1952 in favour of his eldest son Hussein, then still a minor. Talal spent the rest of his life at a sanatorium in Istanbul and died there on 7 July 1972. The stamps printed for his accession had already been withdrawn and burned after his abdication, a small measure of how completely his brief reign was set aside. His filiation is recorded once in his own data record, as are the sources for what is — and is not — recorded about his religion: no biography names a school for him, and this museum records none. {cite:wikipedia-talal-of-jordan-en} {cite:wikidata-talal-of-jordan}
