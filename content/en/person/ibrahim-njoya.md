# Ibrahim Njoya

## Summary

Ibrahim Njoya (c. 1860 – 1933) was the seventeenth ruler of the Bamum kingdom of western Cameroon, reigning from 1886 or 1887 until his death. He is remembered above all as the inventor of the Bamum script, a writing system he developed in stages over some fifteen years and taught in schools he founded, and as the builder of the great palace at Foumban. His long reign spanned the German and French colonial periods and three religious turns: a conversion to Christianity, a national religion of his own devising, and a conversion to Islam in 1916, after which he placed himself under the authority of the Sokoto Caliph.{cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-njoya-en}{cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-njoya-ar}

## Accession and the regency

Njoya succeeded his father Nsangu, who had been killed in battle against the Nso; the Nso kept the dead king's head, an object of ritual importance to the Bamum, and the Germans later helped the young king recover it. During his minority his mother Njapdunke acted as regent with the great servant Gbetnkom Ndombouo. When Njoya came of age he dismissed his tutor, who rebelled, and a civil war followed (1892–1895); on his mother's advice he obtained cavalry help from the Fulani lamido of Banyo, a victory that bound the Bamum kingdom to the Muslim north and preceded his own conversion to Islam. With the Germans, who were advancing into the interior, he kept good relations, welcoming them with great festivals at Foumban and exchanging gifts with Kaiser Wilhelm II, whose gift of a suit of armour is still displayed in the Foumban palace museum.{cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-njoya-fr}{cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-njoya-ar}

## The Bamum script

Njoya's most durable achievement was his script. Beginning as a pictographic system of some 700 signs, it passed through successive simplifications — the stages are named Lerewa, Mbima, Nyi Nyi Fa Fu, Ri Nyi Fa Fu, Ri Nyi Fu Fen and finally Akauku, completed in February 1910 with about 83 signs plus numerals — until it could write the Bamum language efficiently. He founded schools in which Bamum children learned the script alongside some German, used it for his administration's registers, taxation, justice and archives, and had a history and customs of his people, the *Histoire et Coutumes des Bamum*, written in it. After the old wooden palace burned in 1913, he built a new palace at Foumban in 1917 in a brick style influenced by Prussian architecture; it remains the seat and museum of the Bamum kings.{cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-njoya-en}{cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-njoya-fr}

## Religion

Njoya's religious life moved through three phases, and the sources do not describe them in quite the same order. The English article reports that under the influence of a German missionary he converted to Christianity, then created a new syncretic religion based on Christianity and traditional Bamum religion, and finally, in 1916, converted to Islam along with much of his court; he accepted the authority of the Sokoto Caliph in northern Nigeria and asked the caliph to send him an emir's flag and Muslim teachers. The French article places his conversion to Islam earlier, in the years of Fulani support during the civil war of the 1890s, dates his creation of a national religion — drawn from Islam, Christianity and traditional beliefs, with its principles set down in a book — to about 1915, and says that from 1920 he returned to Islam, which then spread rapidly. No source names a school or branch of Islam for Njoya personally, and the museum records his affiliation as unknown, with this itinerary preserved in the data record.{cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-njoya-en}{cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-njoya-fr}

## Final years

France took over Cameroon in 1916, and the Bamum kingdom lost the relative independence it had kept under the Germans. Njoya remained at Foumban and continued to act as king in practice, though the French never recognised him officially. In 1931 the French administration placed him under house arrest at Yaoundé, where he died in 1933. He was succeeded by his son Seidou Njimoluh Njoya. A British officer who met him in 1914 reported that he had some 600 wives and 149 children by 1915; other accounts give far larger figures, and the English article notes he may have had around 350 children in his lifetime.{cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-njoya-en}{cite:wikipedia-ibrahim-njoya-ar}{cite:wikidata-ibrahim-njoya}
