## Summary

Sheikh Abdul Karim Saleem Khasawneh is a Jordanian jurist who twice served as Grand Mufti of the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan — from February 2010 to January 2017, and again from November 2019 to November 2023 — after a long career as the senior religious officer of the Jordanian Armed Forces. He also served as the kingdom's Chief Islamic Justice and remains a member of the Jordanian Ifta Council and of the board of the World Islamic Sciences and Education University ⁦(WISE)⁩ in Amman. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-karim-khasawneh} {cite:muslim500-2026 p. 148}

## Life and formation

He was born in 1944 in Eydoun, a town in the Irbid Governorate of northern Jordan, into the Khasawneh family, a family long associated with scholarship and public service in the region. In 1973 he graduated from the Faculty of Sharia at Damascus University in Syria, where he also worked for a time before returning home. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-karim-khasawneh} {cite:khasawneh-info-abdul-karim}

His chosen path was unusual for a sharia graduate of his generation: on 27 December 1973 he entered the Jordanian Armed Forces as a religious guide ⁦(murshid dini)⁩, the corps of military chaplains responsible for instruction, preaching and pastoral care among soldiers, and he remained in uniform for more than thirty years. {cite:khasawneh-info-abdul-karim}

## Mufti of the armed forces

On 1 April 2004 he was appointed Mufti of the Jordanian Armed Forces, with the rank of major general, holding the post until 31 October 2009, when he became religious adviser in the General Command. In this capacity he supervised the monthly magazine al-Tadhkira, issued by the military ifta directorate, oversaw the fatwas published in it and in the Arab Army's radio broadcasts, and published research in military and cultural periodicals. In 2005 he attended the International Islamic Conference in Amman — the gathering that produced the Amman Message on the mutual recognition of the legal schools of Islam. {cite:khasawneh-info-abdul-karim} {cite:wikipedia-abdul-karim-khasawneh}

## The grand muftiate

On 23 February 2010 a royal decree appointed him Grand Mufti of the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan, succeeding Sheikh Nuh al-Qudah. The grand mufti heads the General Ifta Department, the body that issues the kingdom's official fatwas, and chairs the Council of Ifta, Research and Islamic Studies. He held the office until 22 January 2017, when he was appointed Chief Islamic Justice ⁦(qadi al-qudat)⁩ — the head of the sharia judiciary — succeeding Ahmad Hilayel; Mohammad Khalaileh followed him as grand mufti. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-karim-khasawneh} {cite:khasawneh-info-abdul-karim}

He was brought back to the grand muftiate on 11 November 2019 and served a second term until 5 November 2023, when he resigned and was replaced by Ahmad Hasanat. Across his two terms — nearly a decade in the kingdom's highest religious office — he dealt with the ordinary hard questions of a modern muftiate: medical and financial fiqh, family law, and the religious guidance of a state that hosts large refugee populations. {cite:wikipedia-abdul-karim-khasawneh}

## Boards and continuing work

Beyond the muftiate, Khasawneh sits on the Jordanian Ifta Council, the collective body that deliberates on the kingdom's most consequential fatwa questions, and on the board of the World Islamic Sciences and Education University ⁦(WISE)⁩, the Amman university devoted to Islamic studies. He is also associated with the Royal Aal al-Bayt Institute for Islamic Thought, the Council of Preaching and Guidance, and the King Abdullah II centre for training imams. {cite:khasawneh-info-abdul-karim} {cite:muslim500-2026 p. 148}

## Assessment

Khasawneh's career reads as the Jordanian model of the establishment jurist: sharia formation at Damascus, decades inside the armed forces' religious service, then the two crowning offices of the kingdom's religious establishment — grand mufti and chief justice of the sharia courts. The Muslim 500 lists him among its scholarly entries as a figure whose influence runs through Jordan's official religious institutions rather than through popular preaching or media. {cite:muslim500-2026 p. 148}
