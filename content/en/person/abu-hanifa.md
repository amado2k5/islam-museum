## Overview
Al-Nu'man ibn Thabit al-Kufi, Abu Hanifa, a Sunni jurist and scholar from Iraq. His birth is given as 80 AH (699 CE) and his death as 150 AH (767 CE). He is the founder of the [[group/hanafi-madhhab|Hanafi school]]. {cite:mawsuat-a3lam-ulama-adaba} {cite:duha-islam}

## Lineage and upbringing
Ibn al-Nadim says that he was al-Nu'man ibn Thabit ibn Zawta, and that he was a silk-merchant in Kufa. Zawta was a client of the Banu Taym Allah ibn Tha'laba, and was from Kabul; it is said he was a client of the Banu Tha'laba. Ibn al-Nadim says that he was of the Successors and met a number of the Companions. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.284-285}

## Death and works
Ibn al-Nadim relates that he died in 150 AH at the age of seventy, and was buried in the cemetery of al-Khayzuran in the camp of al-Mahdi on the east side, and al-Hasan ibn Imara prayed over him. Attributed to him are the book "al-Fiqh al-Akbar", his letter to al-Busti, the book "al-Alim wa al-Muta'allim", and the book "Refutation of the Qadariyya". {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.284-285}

## In the Encyclopaedia of Islam: life
The article in the Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, by J. Schacht, presents Abu Hanifa al-Nu'man ibn Thabit as a theologian and religious lawyer, the eponym of the [[group/hanafi-madhhab|Hanafi school]]. It says he died in 150 AH, 767 CE, at the age of 70, so that he was born about 80 AH, 699 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142}

His grandfather Zuta is said to have been brought as a slave from [[place/kabul|Kabul]] to [[place/kufa|Kufa]] and freed by a member of the Arab tribe of Taym Allah ibn Tha'laba. He and his descendants thus became clients of that tribe, and Abu Hanifa is occasionally called al-Taymi. The article says very little is known of his life, except that he lived in Kufa as a manufacturer and merchant of a kind of silk material called khazz. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142}

It holds as certain that he attended the lectures of [[person/hammad-ibn-abi-sulayman|Hammad ibn Abi Sulayman]], who died in 120 AH and taught religious law in Kufa. Perhaps on the occasion of a pilgrimage, he also attended those of [[person/ata-ibn-abi-rabah|Ata ibn Abi Rabah]] in [[place/mecca|Mecca]], who died in 114 or 115 AH. The long lists of authorities from whom later biographers say he heard traditions are, in the article's judgement, to be treated with caution. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142}

After Hammad's death, the article continues, Abu Hanifa became the foremost authority on questions of religious law in Kufa and the main representative of the Kufan school of law. He gathered a large number of private disciples and taught them his doctrine, but he was never a judge. He died in prison in [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]], where he is buried, and a dome was built over his tomb in 459 AH, 1066 CE. The quarter around the mausoleum is still called al-A'zamiyya, since al-Imam al-A'zam is Abu Hanifa's customary epithet. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142}

## The judgeship and the imprisonment
According to the article, the biographical legend has it that the Abbasid caliph [[person/al-mansur|al-Mansur]] summoned him to the newly founded capital, wished to appoint him a judge, and imprisoned him because of his steady refusal. A variant has the Umayyad governor [[person/yazid-ibn-umar-ibn-hubayra|Yazid ibn Umar ibn Hubayra]], under Marwan II, offer him the post of judge in Kufa and have him flogged to make him accept it, again without success. Parts of the OCR of this passage are cut off. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142}

The author judges that such stories are meant to explain his end in prison and the fact, surprising to later generations, that the master was not a judge. In his view the truth is probably that Abu Hanifa compromised himself by unguarded remarks at the time of the rising of the Alid [[person/muhammad-al-nafs-al-zakiyya|al-Nafs al-Zakiyya]] and his brother Ibrahim, and was in 145 AH transported to Baghdad and imprisoned there. The article refers here to al-Khatib al-Baghdadi, though the OCR of the reference is incomplete. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142}

## Sources for his doctrine
The article states that Abu Hanifa did not himself compose any works on religious law. He discussed his opinions with his disciples and dictated them to them. The works of those disciples are therefore the main sources for his doctrine. It names the Ikhtilaf Abi Hanifa wa-Ibn Abi Layla and the Radd ala Siyar al-Awza'i by [[person/abu-yusuf-al-qadi|Abu Yusuf]], and the Hujja and al-Shaybani's version of the Muwatta' of Malik. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142}

The article adds that the formal chain al-Shaybani, Abu Yusuf, Abu Hanifa, which occurs in many of [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-hasan-al-shaybani|al-Shaybani]]'s works, only designates the general relationship of pupil and master and is of no value for this purpose. For the doctrine that Abu Hanifa himself received from Hammad, it names the Athar of Abu Yusuf and the Athar of al-Shaybani as the main sources. Comparing his successors with his predecessors, it says, lets one assess his achievement in developing Muslim legal thought. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142}

## Legal thought
In the author's assessment, Abu Hanifa's legal thought is in general much superior to that of his contemporary [[person/muhammad-ibn-abd-al-rahman-ibn-abi-layla|Ibn Abi Layla]], the judge of Kufa, who died in 148 AH. Compared with him and with contemporary legal reasoning in Kufa, Abu Hanifa appears to the author as a theoretical systematizer who achieved considerable progress in technical legal thought. Not being a judge, he was less restricted by considerations of practice. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142}

The article describes his doctrine as a rule systematically consistent, so full of new and explicit legal thought that an appreciable part of it was found defective and rejected by his disciples. It calls his thought more thoroughly applied, technically more developed and more refined than that of his older contemporaries, and finds a high degree of reasoning, often somewhat ruthless and unbalanced and with little regard for practice, typical of it as a whole. These are the author's judgements. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142}

Abu Hanifa used personal judgment, called [[concept/ray-fiqh|ray]], and analogy, called [[concept/qiyas|qiyas]], to the extent customary in the schools of his time. Like the representatives of other schools, the Medinese for example, he was not inclined to abandon the traditional doctrine for the sake of isolated traditions from the Prophet, meaning traditions related by single individuals, which were beginning to become current during his lifetime in the first half of the second century AH. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142}

## Later criticism and the hostile tradition
The article says that when this kind of tradition gained official recognition two generations later, mainly through the work of [[person/al-shafii|al-Shafi'i]], Abu Hanifa was for adventitious reasons made the scapegoat for resistance to the traditions of the Prophet and for the exercise of personal judgment in the ancient schools. Many sayings shocking to later taste were attributed to him. It names al-Khatib al-Baghdadi, who died in 463 AH, 1071 CE, as the mouthpiece of this hostile tendency. The legal devices, called hiyal, that he developed in the course of his technical reasoning were criticized too, but later became one of his titles to fame. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142}

The article adds that his later opponents, to discredit him, charged him with extravagant opinions derived from the principles of the [[group/murjia|Murji'a]] and with heretical doctrines it judges he could not possibly have held. As examples it gives the doctrine that hell is not eternal, which it describes as a doctrine of the [[group/jahmiyya|Jahmis]] against whom he ranged himself explicitly in the Fiqh al-Akbar, and the opinion that revolt against rulers is lawful, which it says runs straight against his own tenets as expressed in the al-Alim wa'l-Muta'allim. It reports that he was even called a Murji'ite who believed in the sword, which it calls a contradiction in terms and suggests may be deduced from his attitude at the time of the revolt of al-Nafs al-Zakiyya. Parts of this passage are garbled in the OCR. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 143}

## Theology
The article says Abu Hanifa also exercised considerable influence as a theologian. He is the eponym of a popular tradition of dogmatic theology that stresses the community of the Muslims, its unifying principle the [[concept/sunna|sunna]], and the majority of the faithful who follow the middle of the road and avoid extremes, and that relies on scriptural rather than rational proofs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142}

This tradition is represented, the author says, by the al-Alim wa'l-Muta'allim, wrongly attributed to Abu Hanifa, and by the Fiqh al-Absat, both of which originated in the circle of his disciples. Later it is represented by Hanafi theologians, among them the creed of [[person/al-tahawi|al-Tahawi]], who died in 321 AH, 933 CE, and the catechism of [[person/abu-l-layth-al-samarqandi|Abu'l-Layth al-Samarqandi]], who died in 383 AH, 993 CE, which has always been very popular in Malaya and Indonesia, territory that is solidly Shafi'i in religious law. The article says this dogmatic tradition arose from the popular background of the Murji'a movement, to which Abu Hanifa himself belonged. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142}

## Works attributed to him
The article says the only authentic document by Abu Hanifa we possess is his letter to Uthman al-Batti, in which he defends his Murji'ite views in an urbane way. It was printed in Cairo in 1368 AH, 1949 CE, together with the al-Alim wa'l-Muta'allim and the Fiqh al-Absat. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 142} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 143}

Another title ascribed to him is [[work/al-fiqh-al-akbar|al-Fiqh al-Akbar]]. The article reports Wensinck's finding that only the so-called Fiqh al-Akbar I is relevant. It exists only embedded in a commentary wrongly attributed to al-Maturidi, and consists of ten articles of faith setting out the orthodox position against the Kharijites, the Qadaris, the Shiites and the Jahmis. Propositions against the Murji'a and against the [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazila]] are lacking, which the author takes to mean that its author was a Murji'ite who lived before the rise of the Mu'tazila. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 143}

All but one of its theses also occur in the Fiqh al-Absat, which consists of statements by Abu Hanifa on theology in answer to questions from his disciple [[person/abu-muti-al-balkhi|Abu Muti al-Balkhi]], who died in 183 AH, 799 CE. The article therefore holds the contents of the Fiqh al-Akbar I to be authentic opinions of Abu Hanifa, though nothing shows he composed the short text himself. It judges the so-called Fiqh al-Akbar II and the Wasiyya of Abu Hanifa not to be his, calls the authenticity of other short texts ascribed to him not yet investigated and at least doubtful, and says the Wasiyya to his disciple Yusuf ibn Khalid al-Samti al-Basri represents the ethics of Iranian courtiers and cannot be imagined as the work of a specialist in religious law. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 143}

## Descendants, pupils and the Musnads
Among his descendants, the article says, his son [[person/hammad-ibn-abi-hanifa|Hammad]] and a later descendant distinguished themselves in religious law; the OCR of the second name is unreadable. Among his more important pupils it lists [[person/zufar-ibn-al-hudhayl|Zufar ibn al-Hudhayl]], who died in 158 AH, 775 CE; [[person/dawud-al-tai|Dawud al-Ta'i]], who died in 165 AH, 781-2 CE; Abu Yusuf; Abu Muti al-Balkhi; al-Shaybani; Asad ibn Amr, who died in 190 AH, 806 CE; and Hasan ibn Ziyad, who died in 204 AH, 819-20 CE. Among the traditionists, [[person/abd-allah-ibn-al-mubarak|Abd Allah ibn al-Mubarak]], who died in 181 AH, 797 CE, esteemed him highly. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 143}

Under the growing pressure of traditions, the article says, his followers, beginning with Abu Yusuf, collected the traditions from the Prophet that Abu Hanifa had used in his legal reasoning. As spurious information grew, so did their number, until Abu'l-Mu'ayyad Muhammad ibn Mahmud al-Khwarizmi, who died in 655 AH, 1257 CE, gathered fifteen versions into one work, the Jami' Masanid Abi Hanifa. The author says the several versions can still be distinguished and compared, but judges that none is an authentic work of Abu Hanifa. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 143}

## On almsgiving
Al-Baladhuri says that Abu Hanifa, together with Sufyan al-Thawri, al-Awza'i, Malik, Ibn Abi Dhi'b and Abu Yusuf, held that double the amount taken from a Muslim should be taken from a Taghlibi in his land, livestock and property. The people of Iraq, including the companions of Abu Hanifa, held that double the alms should be taken from the land of a child or an imbecile, but not from his livestock. This relates to [[event/treaty-with-taghlib|the peace with Taghlib]]. {cite:futuh-buldan-maarif-1407 p.252}

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Tafsir lectures (episode 3)
The Sheikh says that a former president, king or emperor is called "former" once he is gone, whereas scholars are not spoken of in that way; one does not say "the former Abu Hanifa", although Abu Hanifa died in the year 150 of the Hijra (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep03 00:53:30}.

He holds that rulers die while the people of knowledge live on, and that though people try to kill their memory it stays in people's hearts; he gives Abu Hanifa and Abu Hurayra as examples of men whom people still remember and love {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy tafsir-al-quran-al-qareem/ep03 00:54:00}.

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lectures on Sharh Kitab al-Fawa'id

The Sheikh narrates that Abu Hanifa declined the judgeship when al-Mansur asked him to take it. He told the caliph that if he was lying, the caliph would be appointing a liar as judge, and that if he was truthful about being unable to do the work, he asked to be excused (needs_source: narration with no source given in the lecture). {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep02 01:04:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep02 01:04:30}

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's lecture series Al Dinu Al Qayem

Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy places Abu Hanifa's youth before his jurisprudence became widely known, and dates it to around 80 AH (needs_source: the dates and his age are the sheikh's own) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep01 00:12:30}.

He recounts a judgement in which two parties disputed a jar of gold found in a house that one of them had bought. Abu Hanifa ruled that the children of the two parties should be married to each other, with the jar as the dowry (needs_source: the story as the sheikh tells it) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep03 00:54:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep03 00:55:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep03 00:55:30}. He recounts that Abu Hanifa refused to sell a cloth for four hundred when it was worth one hundred; he took the cloth back to his shop while the buyer followed him, thinking he was a thief (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep08 00:40:30}.

He recounts a neighbour who drank wine and sang at night. When Abu Hanifa noticed the man was missing, he went at dawn to a police station to intercede for him, the man was released, repented, and prayed the noon prayer behind Abu Hanifa (needs_source: the story as the sheikh tells it) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep09 00:25:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep09 00:25:30} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep09 00:26:00}.

He recounts Abu Hanifa's principle in disagreement: our view is correct but may be wrong, and the view of others, which we judge to be wrong, we bear with (needs_source) {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy al-dinu-al-qayem/ep01 00:19:30}.

## Reported supervision in brick reckoning
Within Baghdad-construction narratives,Le Strange transmits a report placing Abu Hanifa in supervision connected with brick-quantity reckoning through measurement-based estimation rather than one-by-one counting,as detailed in [[invention/abu-hanifa-brick-stack-reckoning|the invention record]] {cite:lestrange-baghdad-1900 p.17}. In this record it is handled as a bounded professional role report,not as a standalone mathematical treatise or a monopolized technical invention claim.

## Jurist and practical administration
This addition does not displace Abu Hanifa's primary juristic standing; it shows that scholarly figures may appear in practical organizational episodes where state and construction intersect. It also does not erase conflict narratives preserved elsewhere for the period. The balanced reading is dual: major legal authority,plus a specific transmitted operational role.

## Attribution limits in this file
The file does not claim that Abu Hanifa invented counting or invented measurement. Attribution is limited to a reported practical improvement in a Baghdad works context: estimating large quantities through measured units. The source frame remains a historical reconstruction narrative,not a complete original workshop ledger. Therefore attribution is retained at evidence scale and left open to future enrichment.

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "A Story and an Idea"
[[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] gives the twenty-eighth episode of his programme [[work/kissa-wa-fikra-program|"A Story and an Idea"]] to Abu Hanifa, under the idea of "reason and religion". {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:02:00} What follows is his account; the anecdotes are introduced as reports.

A man was spreading the claim that [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman ibn Affan]] was a Jew. Abu Hanifa came to ask for his daughter's hand on behalf of a noble, rich and generous man who knew the Qur'an by heart and prayed at night, but who was a Jew; when the man protested that a Muslim woman cannot marry a Jew, Abu Hanifa asked how, then, the Prophet had married two of his daughters to Uthman. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:00:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:01:30} He calls Abu Hanifa the imam of reason and analogy (qiyas) and head of the school of opinion; his name was al-Nu'man ibn Thabit, and he grew up in Kufa when it was full of sects and debates on creed, ideas and law. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:02:00}

He would put a question and let his students argue it with evidence, sometimes for days, while he stayed silent; at the end he confirmed, corrected or added, judged the hadiths cited and said who had been right, so that a ruling reached this way was hard to fault. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:04:30} [[person/abu-yusuf-al-qadi|Abu Yusuf]] and [[person/zufar-ibn-al-hudhayl|Zufar]], he says, once debated from the dawn prayer to the call to the noon prayer, and Abu Hanifa then endorsed Abu Yusuf. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:05:30} He cites Abu Hanifa's own account of his method: the Book, then the Sunna, then the view of a Companion, never leaving the Companions' views for others'; and once a question reached Successors such as Ibrahim, al-Sha'bi, al-Hasan, Ibn Sirin and Sa'id ibn al-Musayyib, his own ijtihad as they had done theirs. Transmitted text comes first, he sums up, and reason works where the text is silent. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:07:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:09:30}

He relates that [[person/muhammad-al-baqir|Muhammad al-Baqir]] once accused Abu Hanifa of changing his grandfather's religion by analogy; Abu Hanifa seated him on a chair, sat on the ground before him, and put three questions in each of which opinion alone would have reached the opposite of the revealed rule, saying he had followed the text, and al-Baqir rose and kissed his head. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:11:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:11:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:13:30} The lesson he draws is to check a rumour by asking the person or reading his views. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:10:00}

He describes the school of tradition, centred in the Hijaz, which used opinion only when forced and did not rule on cases not yet arisen, and the school of opinion, centred in Iraq, which used ijtihad wherever no sound hadith existed and answered realistic cases in advance; both, he says, came from religious caution, one fearing to lie about the Prophet, the other fearing to declare things lawful or forbidden by opinion. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:18:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:20:00} Opinion did not begin with Abu Hanifa, he says: [[person/abdullah-ibn-masud|Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud]] gave rulings by his own opinion rather than risk a lie about the Prophet. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:20:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:20:30} Abu Hanifa widened analogy and excelled in istihsan, and al-Suwaidan adds that al-Shafi'i wrote in his *Risala* that analogy and ijtihad are two names for one thing. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:21:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:21:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:22:00} His closing lesson: with a clear method there is no conflict between transmitted text and sound reason; he ends with the saying he attributes to Abu Hanifa, that this is our opinion, the best we could reach, and whoever brings better is closer to the right. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:23:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:24:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep28 00:25:00}

## From Sheikh Omar Abdelkafy's Sharh Ketab Al-Fawaed lectures (episode 10)

The Sheikh relates an anecdote that Abu Hanifa used to live near the Kaaba in Mecca. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-ketab-al-fawaed/ep10 00:08:30} ⁦(needs_source: the recording wording is partly garbled)⁩
