## Overview
Yaqut al-Musta'simi, the Baghdadi scribe-adib, al-Musta'sim's mamluk — the last to head the munasab script school, writing in Ibn al-Bawwab's manner {cite:shadharat-dhahab p. 774}.

## His life according to Sheila Blair
[[person/sheila-blair|Sheila Blair]] calls Abu'l-Majd Jamal al-Din Yaqut the important calligrapher of his age, later regarded as one of the most famous of all time, and notes that few details of his life are certain and much is legend. He was born in the first or second decade of the thirteenth century, probably at [[place/amasya|Amasya]] in Anatolia, and was likely a convert, since he signed simply Yaqut ibn Abdallah, a genealogy that fits his name ("ruby"), common for a slave. Brought as a boy to [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]], he served the last Abbasid caliph, [[person/al-mustasim-billah|al-Musta'sim billah]], whence his epithet. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 282}

## In Baghdad after 1258
He studied calligraphy with [[person/safi-al-din-al-urmawi|Safi al-Din Abd al-Mu'min al-Urmawi]] and, like [[person/ibn-muqla|Ibn Muqla]], became a secretary in the Abbasid chancery. After Hulegu took Baghdad in 1258 he stayed, and his career flourished under Mongol patronage; he was friendly with [[person/ata-malik-al-juwayni|Ata-Malik al-Juvayni]], historian and governor of the city for the Mongols, and taught calligraphy to Juvayni's sons and to his brother Shams al-Din, head of the chancery. Like [[person/ibn-al-bawwab|Ibn al-Bawwab]] he was a librarian, at the [[building/mustansiriyya-madrasa|Mustansiriyya Madrasa]] under the historian [[person/ibn-al-fuwati|Ibn al-Fuwati]]. The most convincing death dates are 697 or 698 (1297–9), and all sources agree he was buried in Baghdad near the tomb of [[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]]. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 282}

## The difficulty of knowing his hand
Blair warns that textual descriptions of his style are brief and metaphorical: [[person/dust-muhammad-haravi|Dust Muhammad]], in his album preface of 951/1544, credits him with perfecting the round naskh developed by Ibn Muqla. Because of his prestige, many manuscripts and specimens bear his signature, authentic and otherwise, and no scholar has yet established a method for telling genuine work from later fakes. His three main followers in Iraq were [[person/ahmad-al-suhrawardi|Ahmad al-Suhrawardi]], Arghun al-Kamili and Nasrallah Tabib; another pupil, [[person/ibn-al-wahid|Ibn al-Wahid]], carried the Six Pens to Cairo. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 282} {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 283} {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 333} {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 386}
