## Overview
Sultan Suleiman al-Qanuni, whose name was Suleiman ibn Selim I, one of the sultans of the Ottoman state. He ruled from 926 to 974 AH (1520-1566 CE) and was the son of [[person/selim-i-ottoman|Selim I]] {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.200-202}.

## Upbringing and accession
He was born in Trabzon, where his father was governor. al-Salabi reports that his father took great care of him, and he grew up loving knowledge, literature, scholars and jurists. He came to the throne at the age of twenty-six, and he was deliberate in his affairs, and once he had made a decision he did not go back on it {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.200-202}.

## The early revolts
al-Salabi counts four revolts in his first years: the revolt of Jan-Berdi al-Ghazali, governor of Syria; the revolt of Ahmad Shah in Egypt in the year 930 AH, when he declared himself sultan and was killed by the state's soldiers; the revolt of Baba Dhu al-Nun, a Shi'i, in the year 926 AH; and the revolt of Qalandar Jalabi at Konya and Marash with some thirty thousand men, which was defeated after the grand vizier Ibrahim Pasha won over some of his men {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.200-202}.

## The conquests
He conquered the island of Rhodes in the year 1522 CE after a great war, and gave the Knights of Saint John the right to move to Malta. He then launched a great raid on Hungary and won the victory at the battle of Mohács in the year 1526 CE, entered Buda on 11 September, and continued his pressure until the siege of Vienna {cite:dawlat-uthmaniyya-salabi p.200-202}.

## In the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The second edition of the Encyclopaedia of Islam calls him the tenth and most illustrious of the Ottoman sultans, with a reign of 926 to 974 AH, 1520 to 1566 CE. It notes a Western tradition that numbers him Suleiman II, and says this rests on a mistaken idea that the earlier prince Suleiman Chelebi, a son of Bayezid I, was a legitimate sultan. The epithet "the Lawgiver" is first recorded, it says, at the start of the 18th century in the work of Dimitrie Cantemir, while in the West he was known as the Magnificent, the Great Turk or the Great Lord. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 857}

## Early years and accession
He was born at [[place/trabzon|Trabzon]], where his father, the future [[person/selim-i-ottoman|Selim I]], resided as a district governor. The article lists three proposed birth dates: 6 November 1494, 27 April 1495, and April or May 1496. It reports a tradition that his mother Hafsa was a daughter of the Crimean khan Mengli Giray, and adds that a document about a mosque founded in her name at [[place/manisa|Manisa]] shows she was a convert to Islam, which contradicts the story. In childhood he was taught by a certain Khayr al-Din Efendi and, according to Evliya Chelebi, learned goldsmithing from a Greek master. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 858}

As a prince he first governed at Kefe in the Crimea, and in 1513 became governor of Manisa, a post he held until his accession. Twice his father called on him in his absence: in 1514 to 1515, during the Persian campaign, he kept the lieutenancy in Istanbul, and in 1516 to 1518, during the campaign against the Mamluks, he was in charge of the defence of Edirne. He returned to Manisa when his father died, and eight days later reached Istanbul, where he acceded without trouble since no brother stood to contest it. His reign began on 17 Shawwal 926 AH, 15 September 1520, and was the longest in Ottoman history, coinciding with the empire's zenith. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 858}

## A reign seen as a whole
The article says later Ottoman observers of decline looked back on the reign as a golden age and a reference point, though an old opinion divides it into two halves, and Koci Beg thought the threat of decline could be traced to its later part. At the outset the new sultan was little known and overshadowed by his formidable father, but was seen as a just and peace-loving young man. The beglerbeg of Syria and Palestine, Janbirdi al-Ghazali, a former Mamluk chief, rebelled and was quickly put down. From spring 1521 there followed a series of imperial campaigns in which the sultan took part in person, ten in Europe and three in Asia. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 858}

## Belgrade, Rhodes and Hungary
The sultan turned first to the west. After the ill treatment of his envoy, he forced the surrender of Belgrade on 29 August 1521, the "key to Hungary", where his great-grandfather [[person/mehmed-ii|Mehmed II]] had failed in 1456. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 858} Next came [[place/rhodes|Rhodes]], held by the Knights of St John and used as a base for piracy. A fleet of some 235 vessels was sent against it, the siege ran into winter, and the Knights capitulated on 21 December 1522 after five months. The sultan kept his promise that they could leave freely, which added to his reputation for reliability. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 858}

In April 1526 he and his grand vizier Ibrahim Pasha marched on Hungary; weather delayed the army so that it reached Belgrade only in July. The Hungarian heavy cavalry was cut down by Ottoman artillery fire, and the sultan pursued the survivors to [[place/buda|Buda]], which he entered unopposed on 11 September. He stayed about ten days, hurried back because of Turkoman revolts in Cilicia and Karaman, which were not crushed until summer 1528, and kept only two counties south of the Danube, Szerem and Valko, with the booty of Buda. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 858} The voivode of Transylvania, Janos Szapolyai, was elected king on 11 November 1526, while Ferdinand of Habsburg was also enthroned. On the advice of Ibrahim and Alvise Gritti the sultan chose Szapolyai, making him a vassal in February 1528. Ferdinand's troops took Buda, which forced a third Hungarian campaign. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 858}

The sultan left Istanbul on 10 May, reached Belgrade on 17 July, and on 18 August received Szapolyai at Mohacs, a place now symbolic for him. He retook Buda without difficulty and reached Vienna on 27 September. The famous siege was lifted on 14 October after four vain assaults and the early arrival of winter. The article ascribes the failure to climate and logistics and says Ottoman propaganda deliberately masked it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 859}

## The Habsburg rivalry
The article presents Hungary as one stake in a deeper antagonism with the [[polity/habsburg-monarchy|Habsburgs]]. Seeing himself as unique master of the world, Suleiman refused them any imperial title, calling [[person/charles-v|Charles V]] only the king of Spain and Ferdinand the king of Vienna. His fourth European campaign, called the German campaign against the king of Spain, was marked by triumphal arches, great pomp and a tiara made by Venetian goldsmiths, all meant to announce Ottoman claims to Christendom. Militarily it was less brilliant, with a laborious siege of Guns and devastating raids into Styria and Slavonia, yet it frightened the Habsburgs into seeking a truce. He granted it in July 1533 on the basis of the existing division of Hungary between Ferdinand and Szapolyai, who both became tributaries. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 859}

## Campaigns from 1537 to 1555
Over the last thirty years of his life he led seven more campaigns, and the fleet grew in importance. Against the Habsburg admiral [[person/andrea-doria|Andrea Doria]] he welcomed the corsair [[person/khayr-al-din-barbarossa|Khayr al-Din Barbarossa]], whom he made kapudan pasha in 1533. The same period saw the alliance with [[person/francis-i-france|Francis I]] of France, whose plans for joint action against Charles V did not go as expected: the French fleet arrived late, and the sultan abandoned his attack on Naples and laid siege to Venetian Corfu instead, lifting it when the French fleet came. Barbarossa then took most of the Aegean islands still held by Venetians, and on 28 September 1538 won the greatest Ottoman naval success at Preveza over the joint fleets of Spain and Venice under Doria. A treaty of 2 October 1540 gave Venice peace, while the sultan gained Nauplia, Monemvasia, Vrana, Nadin and a number of Aegean islands. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 860}

In Hungary, Szapolyai died in July 1540 leaving an infant son. When Ferdinand besieged Buda in 1541, Suleiman reoccupied it at the end of July, made the central part of the kingdom an Ottoman province, and assigned Transylvania to the child king, which meant a tribute from 1543 of 10,000 gold pieces, later 15,000. His careful campaign of summer 1543 took Esztergom and Szekesfehervar, and a five-year peace of June 1547 confirmed the status quo with a tribute of thirty thousand ducats a year from the archduke. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 860} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 861}

The Persian front reopened with the flight to Istanbul of Elkas Mirza, brother of Shah [[person/tahmasp-i-safavi|Tahmasp]]. In spring 1548 the sultan marched to Tabriz without resistance, besieged Van, which fell on 25 August, and wintered in Aleppo, returning to Istanbul on 21 December 1549. Later Safavid raids led to a campaign led by the grand vizier Rustem Pasha, which Suleiman declined to join; the article says this strengthened the army's feeling that his age no longer fitted the role of commander. A rumour that his son Mustafa meant to take over led the sultan to set out for Anatolia in August 1553, and on 6 October near Eregli he had Mustafa executed as a presumed rebel and dismissed Rustem. In 1554 the Nakhchivan campaign ended in a truce, and the peace of Amasya of May 1555 fixed the position: the Ottomans kept Iraq, much of Kurdistan and eastern Armenia, and gave up Erivan and Nakhchivan. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 861}

## At sea and in the Indian Ocean
In 1543 Barbarossa besieged Nice and wintered at Toulon as the guest of the French king. Tripoli fell in 1551 to Murad Agha and Torghud Reis. In 1560 Piyale Pasha won the last great naval success at Djerba, but the great siege of Malta in 1565 failed, and in 1566 Piyale took Chios, the last Genoese possession in the archipelago. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 861} Against the Portuguese, Hadim Suleiman Pasha sailed from Suez with seventy-two ships, took Aden and besieged Portuguese Diu without success; in 1552 Piri Reis plundered Muscat, failed at Hormuz and was executed, and in 1554 Sidi Ali Reis fought the one serious Ottoman-Portuguese clash before a storm scattered his fleet. The article concludes that Suleiman kept the trade of the Red Sea and Persian Gulf from complete ruin but did not dislodge the Portuguese from the Sea of Oman or north-western India. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 861}

## The man and his household
Commentators agree on his physical majesty and private virtues, listing frugality, temperance, modesty, loyalty, generosity, faithfulness to his word and piety, with a leaning to mysticism encouraged in his Manisa youth by the Khalwati shaykh Merkez Efendi. Early victories gave him firm confidence in divine support, and the article says that in his first years some saw him as a messianic figure, the sahib-qiran, alongside a taste for splendour and a Renaissance-like aestheticism shared by Ibrahim Pasha. After Ibrahim's removal his mood darkened with age and his piety turned to a strictness bordering on puritanism. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 862} The article reads his reliance on successive relatives as a sign of weak character, yet also notes his ability to delegate, including the long viziership of Rustem Pasha in 1544 to 1553 and 1556 to 1561, accused of avarice but credited with progress in finance and trade. It names his concubine Gulbahar and the Ruthenian slave known in the West as Roxelana, Khurrem Sultan, who became his legal wife in 1534. Of eight sons, three died young and Jihangir died in 1553; Mehmed died in 1543 and is commemorated by the Shehzade mosque, leaving Mustafa, Selim and Bayezid. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 862}

## Law and religion
The article describes Suleiman as a universal monarch modelled on the "mirrors of princes", with order and justice resting on two sources of law, the shari'a and the sultanic kanun, and says this is why Turkish tradition remembers him as Kanuni. A general code is attributed to him, but the article says, following Halil Inalcik, most of its provisions go back to the code of Mehmed II, so his legislation is marked by compilation, systematisation, adaptation and diffusion, led by the chancellor Jelalzade Mustafa. The kanun moved towards centralisation, with meticulous land surveys that make his reign the golden age of Ottoman land registration, and with codes of provincial law for Hungary, Syria, Egypt, Diyarbakir and Erzurum. The article adds that he gave unprecedented weight to the shari'a and the Sunni character of the state, while giving the caliphal title only a limited place in his rhetoric. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 862} He played the part of servant of the two holy sanctuaries through pious bequests to [[place/mecca|Mecca]] and [[place/medina|Medina]] and protection of the pilgrimage, and proclaimed pre-eminence over other rulers in his titles and inscriptions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 862} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 863}

The article ties the stress on orthodoxy partly to rivalry with the Safavids and their influence over the Kizilbash Turkomans, visible in the revolts of Baba Dhu al-Nun, dated there to 1526, and Shah Kalender in 1527. Note that this dating differs from al-Salabi's 926 AH for the first revolt. The sultan's right hand was [[person/abu-al-suud-afandi|Abu al-Su'ud Efendi]], mufti of Istanbul from 1545 to 1574, whose fatwas guided judges in applying the Hanafi school, and whose work reduced the gap between kanun and shari'a by restating the kanun on shari'a grounds. The sultan founded many madrasas and strengthened the learned class as a state-linked hierarchy with the mufti at its head. A firman of 944 AH, 1537 to 1538, ordered a mosque in every village. A policy of persecution was pursued on a large scale, the article says, but it did not touch Christians and Jews, protected as dhimmis, and aimed at heresy or dissidence within Islam, for example the trial of Molla Kabiz in 1527 and actions against Melami-Bayrami and Gulsheni shaykhs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 863}

## Patronage and the title Magnificent
Christian observers, struck by his conquests and the splendour of his court, gave him the name the Magnificent. Before the austerity of old age he was a great patron, supporting craftsmen at Topkapi palace and commissioning work in Bursa, Iznik, Venice and Brussels, in calligraphy, bookbinding, illumination, metalwork, wood and stone carving and gem setting. He collected Chinese porcelain, and composed a Turkish divan, with some Persian pieces, under the pen name Muhibbi, dominated by the figure of his beloved Khurrem. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 863} His most constant patronage was architecture, most of it by [[person/mimar-sinan|Mimar Sinan]], chief architect from 1539 to 1588. The Suleymaniye complex, built between 1550 and 1558, cost 897,350 gold florins, one tenth of the empire's budget for 1527 to 1528, according to the article. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 863}

## Szigetvar and death
His health had worsened from the late 1540s, and rumours of his death circulated. The article says that his last grand vizier, [[person/sokollu-mehmed-pasha|Sokollu Mehmed Pasha]], seems to have urged a new Hungarian campaign to efface the failed Malta siege of 1565 and to answer the new emperor Maximilian II. In pain and irritable, Suleiman led it himself. On the night of 20 to 21 Safar 974 AH, 6 September 1566, he died in his tent beneath the fortress of Szigetvar, defended by Miklos Zrinyi; the siege had begun on 5 August and the fortress fell the next day. His body was eviscerated and secretly buried under the tent. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 863} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 864} Sokollu then ran the posthumous arrangements, and after a dispute at Belgrade between Selim and the Janissaries he sent the body quickly to Istanbul under escort. Suleiman was buried as he wished in the Suleymaniye cemetery, in a mausoleum by Sinan beside Khurrem, and seven stanzas of an elegy by Baki were read, which Selaniki says made the whole nation lament. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 9 p. 864}

## For Scholars
Sultan Suleiman the Lawgiver, Suleiman son of Selim I, of the Ottoman sultans; he ruled from 926 to 974 AH (1520–1566 CE), son of Selim I. Born at Trebizond where his father was governor, al-Sallabi says his father cared for him so he grew up loving learning, letters, scholars and jurists; he took the throne at twenty-six, deliberate in his affairs and never backing down from a decision once made. Al-Sallabi counts four revolts in his early years: the governor of Syria Janbirdi al-Ghazali; Ahmad Shah in Egypt in 930 AH who declared himself sultan and was killed by the state's soldiers; the Shi'i Baba Dhu al-Nun in 926 AH; and Qalandar Chalabi in Konya and Marash with some thirty thousand, beaten after Grand Vizier Ibrahim Pasha won over some of his men. He took the island of Rhodes in 1522 after a great war and gave the Knights of St John leave to move to Malta; then launched a great campaign on Hungary, won the battle of Mohács in 1526, entered Buda on 11 September, and pressed on to the siege of Vienna.

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "A Story and an Idea"
[[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] gives the fourteenth episode of his programme [[work/kissa-wa-fikra-program|"A Story and an Idea"]] to Suleiman, under the idea of "achievements of civilisation": works of civilisation, not political conflict, are what make a ruler endure. He frames the episode around the box the sultan ordered buried with him. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:00:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:08:30} The dates and figures below are his.

He says Suleiman was born in Trabzon in 900 AH / 1495, where his father was governor, and took power in 926 AH at twenty-six after the death of [[person/selim-i-ottoman|Selim I]]. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:02:30} He put down the revolts of governors in Syria and Egypt, took Belgrade and parts of Hungary including Budapest, fought the Safavids in three great campaigns, and opposed the Portuguese in the Indian Ocean and the Gulf. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:04:30} The navy reached its height under him: al-Suwaidan says that in 935 AH [[person/khayr-al-din-barbarossa|Khayr al-Din Barbarossa]] made seven voyages carrying seventy thousand Muslims fleeing the Inquisition from Spain to the Maghrib, and that Spain suffered its worst defeat at Preveza in 945 AH. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:06:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:07:30}

He describes Suleiman as a poet and first-rate calligrapher who spoke several languages, Arabic among them, knew gemstones and loved building. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:09:00} His works include fortifications at Rhodes and Belgrade and mosques, cisterns and bridges in Damascus, Mecca, Baghdad and Istanbul; al-Suwaidan's principle is that spending on monuments is right only once people's needs are met, "people before stones". {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:10:30} [[person/mimar-sinan|Sinan]], whom he calls the most famous architect in Islamic history, built the Süleymaniye in 964 AH. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:11:00} Suleiman carried out major works at the Haram in Mecca and improved the Syrian Hajj road, and his wife restored the water channel of [[person/zubayda-bint-jafar|Zubayda]] to Mecca; al-Suwaidan's point is that worship, and the Hajj above all, drove civilisation. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:12:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:12:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:13:00} He says he has visited the Süleymaniye Library, that it holds eighty thousand manuscripts not yet printed, and that Suleiman laid its nucleus and set rules for its librarian and for lending. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:14:30}

His greatest fame, al-Suwaidan says, comes from the laws he issued with the Shaykh al-Islam [[person/abu-al-suud-afandi|Abu al-Su'ud Efendi]], author of the tafsir *Irshad al-'Aql al-Salim*, fitted to each region and kept within the Shari'a. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:15:30} Mehmed II had laid the first laws down and Bayezid II and Selim developed them, and Suleiman edited and ordered them best: more than two hundred laws on trade, finance and customs, turning legal rulings into clear statutes because the books of fiqh differ. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:16:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:17:00} On Abu al-Su'ud's advice he sometimes chose a weaker opinion that served people better, under the maxim that "the ruler's ruling removes disagreement". {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:18:00} Europeans called him "the Magnificent"; Ottomans preferred "al-Qanuni", which stands for justice in applying the law. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:19:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:20:00} Al-Suwaidan adds that he copied the whole Qur'an by hand eight times, the copies kept in the Süleymaniye. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:20:30}

Though he had gout and could not ride, at seventy-four he led the army himself against the Habsburgs, leaving on 9 Shawwal 973 AH / April 1566, and told his doctor he would love to die a ghazi in God's path. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:21:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:22:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:22:30} During the siege of Szigetvár a Muslim cannonball struck the fortress's powder store, the walls were breached and it fell; he praised God and said, "now death is sweet". He died in Safar 974 AH / September 1566. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:24:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:25:00} At his funeral the scholars, fearing the box held money or jewels, which may not be buried, opened it and found it full of the scholars' fatwas he had turned into laws; Abu al-Su'ud wept and said, "you have saved yourself, Suleiman", for if the fatwas were wrong the burden now fell on the scholars. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:26:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:27:00} He was called, al-Suwaidan says, "the sultan of the learned and the learned one among sultans". {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan kissa-wa-fikra/ep14 00:27:30}
