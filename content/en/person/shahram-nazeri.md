## Summary
Shahram Nazeri — the "Persian Nightingale" and, to the Western press, "Iran's Pavarotti" — is an Iranian Kurdish tenor who carried Sufi music from shrine and ceremony to the world's concert stages. The Muslim 500 lists him in Arts & Culture as the master of classical Persian, Kurdish and Sufi music and the first vocalist to set Rumi's poetry to traditional Iranian music; Wikipedia records his career and his French honours. {cite:muslim500-shahram-nazeri} {cite:wikipedia-shahram-nazeri}

## Formation in Kermanshah
Nazeri was born in 1950 into a musical Kurdish family in Kermanshah, western Iran, and was raised inside the sound of Sufi ceremony: he sang in Sufi ensembles from the age of eight, performed on Iranian national television at eleven, and studied the classical radif — the canon of Persian art music — under masters including Abdollah Davami, Nourali Boroumand and Mahmoud Karimi. {cite:wikipedia-shahram-nazeri}

## Rumi set to music
His signature achievement is the marriage of Rumi to the radif: in the mid-1970s he became the first vocalist to set Mowlana Jalal al-Din Rumi's mystical poetry to the classical Persian repertoire, and the resulting albums — among them Gol-e Sad Barg — made Sufi poetry a recording genre in its own right and carried it to a new generation. Albums and concerts followed for four decades, singing in Persian and Kurdish with Iran's leading ensembles. {cite:wikipedia-shahram-nazeri} {cite:muslim500-shahram-nazeri}

## World stage
Nazeri toured Europe, North America and the Arab world, performing with his son, the composer Hafez Nazeri, and drawing audiences far beyond the Iranian diaspora — the New York Times heard "impassioned singing of Sufi mysticism"; the Christian Science Monitor called him Iran's Pavarotti. France named him Chevalier des Arts et Lettres and awarded him the Ordre national du Mérite. His 2014 Kermanshah concert, where he sang in Kurdish, also drew the attention of Iranian authorities — a fact the museum records attributed, without verdict. {cite:wikipedia-shahram-nazeri}

## Significance
In the museum's record, Nazeri is the voice of Persian Sufism for the world's ears: the Kurdish singer who took Rumi out of the tekke and onto the radif — mystical devotion carried not in a sermon but in a tenor that crosses languages. {cite:muslim500-shahram-nazeri} {cite:wikipedia-shahram-nazeri}

## The voice of Kurdistan
Nazeri's biography is Kurdish before it is Iranian: born in Kermanshah, trained in the Persian classical radif and in the Kurdish musical tradition, he became the first singer to record Rumi's poetry in a fully classical Persian style — the 1980s albums that made his voice the standard by which Persian sacred song is measured. He has sung for Kurdish audiences through the region's difficult decades, in concerts that have become civic events; and his voice is also the voice of Persianate culture's highest reach — a Sunni Kurdish singer of Rumi, in a Twelver-Shia-majority state, carrying a tradition that crosses every boundary the modern Middle East draws. {cite:wikipedia-shahram-nazeri} {cite:muslim500-shahram-nazeri}

## The singer as bridge
Nazeri's record is the museum's example of the singer who treats his voice as a bridge: the Kurdish-Iranian who understood that the tradition he carried — the Persian classical radif, the Kurdish folk song, the poetry of Rumi — was a language that crossed every boundary the modern Middle East draws. The tradition is the story; the voice is the instrument; and the result — a Sunni Kurdish singer whose voice is the sound of Persianate culture's highest reach — is the profile his Muslim 500 entry records. The museum notes the career and leaves the boundaries to those who draw them. {cite:muslim500-shahram-nazeri}
