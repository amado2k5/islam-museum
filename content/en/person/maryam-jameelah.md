## Summary
Maryam Jameelah (born Margaret Marcus; 23 May 1934 – 31 October 2012) was an American-born writer who lived in Pakistan from 1962. Born into a Jewish family at New Rochelle, New York, she embraced Islam on 24 May 1961 at the age of 27, and became known for books and essays that defended a conservative reading of Islam and criticised modernism, secularism and the West. {cite:sailanmuslim-maryam-jameelah-obituary-2012 obituary} {cite:wikipedia-maryam-jameelah-en Biography}

## Background
Her parents were of German Jewish descent and she spent her early years in Westchester County. She studied drawing at the Art Students League of New York in the autumn of 1952, entered New York University in 1953 and left in 1956 without a degree, exploring several religious traditions in those years. She was hospitalised from 1957 to 1959; the English Wikipedia article, drawing on a New York Public Library finding aid and a volume by Esposito and Voll, gives the diagnosis as schizophrenia. The obituary adds that she was raised in a secular environment and began to take an interest in religion at nineteen. {cite:wikipedia-maryam-jameelah-en Biography} {cite:sailanmuslim-maryam-jameelah-obituary-2012 obituary}

## The path to Islam
The obituary says her search was shaped by Marmaduke Pickthall's translation of the Qur'an and by the writings of [[person/muhammad-asad|Muhammad Asad]], himself a convert from a Jewish family, and that she embraced Islam in New York on 24 May 1961. The English article names the Qur'an and Asad's *The Road to Mecca*, and adds that a university course on the influence of Judaism on Islam, taught by Abraham Katsch, drew her further. She took the name Maryam Jameelah. {cite:sailanmuslim-maryam-jameelah-obituary-2012 obituary} {cite:wikipedia-maryam-jameelah-en Biography} See also [[person/muhammad-marmaduke-pickthall|Marmaduke Pickthall]].

## After embracing Islam
She began writing for the *Muslim Digest* of Durban, where she came to know the work of [[person/abul-ala-maududi|Abul Ala Mawdudi]], and corresponded with him. On his invitation she travelled to Pakistan in 1962 and first lived with his family in [[place/lahore|Lahore]]. In 1963 she married Muhammad Yusuf Khan, a member of the Jamaat-e-Islami, as his second wife; they had five children, the first of whom died in infancy. Her books include *Islam and Modernism*, *Islam Versus the West*, *Why I Embraced Islam* and *Islam and the Muslim Woman Today*, and have been translated into Urdu, Persian, Turkish, Bengali and Indonesian. She argued against secularism, materialism and modernisation, held that veiling, polygamy and purdah are required, and, on Mawdudi's advice that drawing living beings was un-Islamic, gave up drawing for writing. {cite:sailanmuslim-maryam-jameelah-obituary-2012 obituary} {cite:wikipedia-maryam-jameelah-en Writings}

## Difficulties and reactions
The sources read do not report how her family or American society reacted to her conversion. The obituary says she suffered illness for several years before her death and was hospitalised at times. The Express Tribune column of 12 June 2011, a critical review of Deborah Baker's book *The Convert*, reports that Mawdudi's attitude toward her cooled during her stay, that she was sent to a friend in Pattoki and was committed to a Lahore psychiatric hospital, and, on the word of Mawdudi's son Haider Farooq, that her 1963 marriage was not arranged by Mawdudi and angered him. These are the reviewer's statements and are attributed to him. {cite:sailanmuslim-maryam-jameelah-obituary-2012 obituary} {cite:express-tribune-khaled-ahmed-maryam-jameela-2011 column}

## Criticism and debate
This section records criticism as the sources state it, without a verdict. Her opposition to modernist Muslim thinkers drew the column's objection: the reviewer lists Ziya Gokalp, Syed Ahmed Khan and Taha Husain among those she attacked, reads the absolutism of her writing as bound up with her illness, and notes that when asked how the sharia should be applied she deferred to the scholars. The same column calls her, in closing, an admirable survivor. Other sources read give no criticism. {cite:express-tribune-khaled-ahmed-maryam-jameela-2011 column} {cite:wikipedia-maryam-jameelah-en Writings}
