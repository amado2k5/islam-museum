## Molla Khosrow

Molla Khosrow — in Turkish Molla Hüsrev, in scholarly transliteration Mullā Khusraw — was an Ottoman Hanafi jurist who rose to the head of the empire's learned hierarchy, dying at Istanbul in 885/1480 while mufti of the capital, the office that came to be known as Shaykh al-Islam {cite:tdv-molla-husrev}. The tradition states his school plainly: the Arabic encyclopedia summary calls him "a Hanafi jurist, of Rumi origin" {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-ar}, and Ibn al-Imad's Shadharat al-Dhahab heads his entry with the name "al-Hanafi" {cite:shadharat-dhahab}.

### Origins, and the two accounts of his father

His given name was Mehmed, son of Ferâmurz. The colophon he wrote at the end of his Dürerü'l-hükkâm gives his grandfather's name as Ali, and a sale deed of early 843/1439 already names "Mehmed b. Ferâmurz b. Hoca Ali" {cite:tdv-molla-husrev}. Modern scholarship, working critically through the biographical tradition, places his family among the Varsak Turkmen of the Amasya–Tokat–Sivas region and his birth — possibly in 1397 — in a village called Karkın between Sivas and Tokat, though some sources point instead to a Karkın near Yerköy by Yozgat {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-en} {cite:tdv-molla-husrev}.

An older account, carried by the Shadharat al-Dhahab, makes his father a commander of the Farasikha who embraced Islam, and has the boy raised by the Khosrow of that household {cite:shadharat-dhahab}. The TDV encyclopedia shows how that story arose: Taşköprülüzâde had written that Ferâmurz was of the "ümerâ-i Ferâsiha" — the commanders of the Varangians — and the phrase was later misread and mistranslated as "of the Franks", so derivative sources turned the father into a converted Frankish or Rum amir {cite:tdv-molla-husrev} {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-tr}. What the accounts agree on is the name: after his father's early death the boy was raised in the household of his brother-in-law Hüsrev, was first called "Hüsrev kaynı" — Hüsrev's in-law — and the byname stuck as Molla Hüsrev {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-tr}.

### Education and career

He studied at Bursa and received his ijaza from Yûsuf Bâlî, qadi of Bursa and a son of Molla Fenârî; the English article adds that he may also have studied at Edirne under Burhâneddin Haydar of Herat, a student of al-Taftazani, while the TDV entry's apparatus names Molla Yegân as his teacher {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-en} {cite:tdv-molla-husrev}. His first posts were teaching ones at Edirne — the Şah Melek Madrasa, then in 1435–36 the Halebî (Çelebi) Madrasa {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-en}. The Turkish article dates his appointment as kazasker, the empire's chief military judge, to 1429, before the Battle of Varna; the English article places it in 1444, in the setting of Murad II's abdication in favour of Mehmed II {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-tr} {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-en}.

After the conquest of Constantinople he stood among the scholars around Mehmed II, and when the city's first qadi, Hızır Bey, died in 863/1459, Molla Khosrow succeeded him as qadi of Istanbul, with the judgeships of Galata and Üsküdar added and the teaching post at the Hagia Sophia madrasa {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-tr} {cite:shadharat-dhahab}. The Shadharat tradition preserves colour the modern accounts pass over: a stipend of a hundred dirhams a day, mosques built in Constantinople, and his habit of copying two pages a day from the books of the early scholars in a fine hand despite his household of servants {cite:shadharat-dhahab}.

### Bursa, the Dürer, and the muftiate

At some point between 1460 and 1473 he withdrew to Bursa — the story told is of a slight: the sultan had seated Molla Gürânî at his right hand and Molla Khosrow at his left {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-tr}. In Bursa's Zeyniler quarter, near the Emir Sultan Mosque, he built his own madrasa, and there he composed the work on which his fame rests: the Gurerü'l-ahkâm, a concise manual of Hanafi law, with his own commentary, the Dürerü'l-hükkâm, begun in 1472 and finished in 1478 and presented to Mehmed II {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-tr}. For centuries the Dürer was read in Ottoman madrasas together with its commentaries, and it functioned for Ottoman judges as a semi-official reference for resolving disputed points of law {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-tr}.

Mehmed recalled him to Istanbul and appointed him mufti of the capital — probably in 874/1469 on the TDV entry's dating, after 1473 on the English article's — and he held the post until his death {cite:tdv-molla-husrev} {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-en}. Mehmed is reported to have said of him, "the Abu Hanifa of our time" {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-en}.

### Death and legacy

He died at Istanbul in 885/1480 — so the TDV entry and the Arabic tradition pair the years; the Shadharat gives 886, and the English article allows 1480–81, possibly October 1480 {cite:tdv-molla-husrev} {cite:shadharat-dhahab} {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-en}. By his will his body was carried to Bursa and buried in the burial ground of his madrasa {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-tr}. His students included Zenbilli Ali Efendi, later himself Shaykh al-Islam, and Fenârî Hasan Çelebi {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-en}. Beyond the Dürer, his writings include the Mirqat al-wusul on legal theory with its commentary the Mir'at al-usul, a commentary on al-Pazdawi's usul, and glosses on al-Taftazani's Mutawwal and Talwih and on the opening of al-Baydawi's Qur'an commentary {cite:wikipedia-molla-husrev-tr} {cite:shadharat-dhahab}.
