## Brief
Abdullah ibn Abbas was the Prophet's young cousin — a boy when the Prophet died, and later the tradition's founding authority on what the Qur'an means.

## Life
Born about 619 CE, three years before the Hijra, Ibn Abbas grew up in Medina in the Prophet's extended household {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}. The traditions describe the Prophet praying that God teach him the interpretation — and describe the boy's hunger to learn from every Companion he could find.

Under the caliphs he became a teacher and jurist; under [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]] he served as governor of Basra, then withdrew to the Hijaz after Ali's death {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}. Generations of students memorized his explanations of Qur'an passages, his legal judgments, and his store of Arabic poetry; nearly every later commentary cites him. He died at Taif about 688 {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

## Scholar and teacher, according to the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, calls Ibn Abbas, whose full name is Abd Allah ibn al-Abbas and whose kunya is Abu al-Abbas, one of the greatest scholars of the first generation of Muslims, and says that some would call him the greatest. It gives him the nicknames al-Hibr, "the doctor", and al-Bahr, "the sea", because of his learning, and describes him as the father of Qur'anic exegesis, who worked at a time when the text had to be brought into line with the demands of a society that had undergone a deep transformation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 59}

The article places his birth three years before the [[event/hijra|hijra]], when the Hashimite family, the [[group/banu-hashim|Banu Hashim]], was living shut up in the Ravine, al-Shi'b. His mother had become a Muslim before the hijra, so he too was regarded as a Muslim from the start. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 59}

From youth he showed a taste for careful research. The article says he thought early of gathering information about the Prophet by questioning the Companions, and that while still young he became a master around whom people crowded to learn. It reports that his knowledge rested not only on memory but on a large collection of written notes, and that he gave public classes following a weekly programme: interpretation of the Qur'an, judicial questions, the Prophet's expeditions, pre-Islamic history and ancient poetry. According to the article, his habit of quoting lines of verse to explain Qur'anic words is the reason ancient Arabic poetry gained its recognised importance for Muslim scholars. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 59}

His competence led people to ask him for fatwas. The article singles out his authorisation of [[concept/muta|muta marriage]] as especially famous, and adds a remark about what he later had to do concerning it, which is left out here because the printed text is partly unreadable. His explanations of the Qur'an were soon gathered in special collections whose chains of transmission go back to one of his immediate pupils, with a reference to the Fihrist, and his fatwas were collected too. Many manuscripts and several editions of a tafsir, or of several tafsirs, are attributed to him today; the article states that whether the attribution is right cannot be said, because no study of this material had yet been made. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 59}

## In the political and military events of his time
The article warns that Ibn Abbas's part in the politics and wars of his age should not be exaggerated, as his Muslim biographers have tended to do, in its view because he was the grandfather of the Abbasids. It lists campaigns he followed with the Muslim armies: into Egypt between 18 and 21 AH, into [[place/ifriqiya|Ifriqiya]] in 27, into [[place/jurjan|Jurjan]] and [[place/tabaristan|Tabaristan]] in 30, and in 49 on the expedition of Yazid against [[place/constantinople|Constantinople]], together with [[person/abdullah-ibn-umar|Abd Allah ibn Umar]] ibn al-Khattab. At the battles of the [[event/battle-of-the-camel|Camel]], in 36, and of [[event/battle-of-siffin|Siffin]], in 37, he commanded a wing of Ali's troops. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 59}

According to the article, later biographers present him as a counsellor highly valued by the caliphs [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] and [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman]], and as a counsellor of Ali and of Ali's son al-Husayn whose advice was little heeded. The author judges that he entered political life only after Ali came to power and took an active part for three or four years at most. The one official mission Uthman gave him was to lead the pilgrimage to [[place/mecca|Mecca]] in the year the caliph was besieged in his house in [[place/medina|Medina]], which is why he was not in the capital when Uthman was killed. When he returned some days later, he paid homage to Ali. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 59}

From then on Ali gave him important missions, and after the occupation of [[place/basra|Basra]] in 36 he appointed him its governor. The article lists him among the signatories of the Siffin agreement of 37, which handed the quarrel between Ali and [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Muawiya]] to two arbitrators, and says that in a discussion with the Harurites he argued for the legal validity of that [[event/arbitration-of-siffin|arbitration]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 59}

## The break with Ali
The article reports that relations between Ibn Abbas and Ali suddenly became strained, that Ibn Abbas withdrew to Mecca and left his seat of government, and that Ali no longer regarded him as his representative at Basra. The sources give the date as 38, 39 or 40; the author finds good reason to prefer 38. He does not find credible the traditions that say Ibn Abbas stayed faithful to Ali until the caliph's death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 59}

On the reason, the article gives two accounts. Some Arabic sources say Ibn Abbas took offence because Ali reproached him for misappropriations he was alleged to have committed as governor. The author instead ties the withdrawal, which coincided with that of many other supporters of Ali, to larger events: the killing of the Kharijites at [[event/battle-of-nahrawan|al-Nahrawan]], which Ibn Abbas, according to certain men, had condemned, and what the author calls the false position of Ali, who kept his claim to the caliphate after the arbitrators' verdict, when most Muslims no longer recognised it. The OCR of this sentence is damaged, so the wording of that second explanation is given only in outline. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 59}

## The treasury of Basra and the years of al-Hasan
The article says Ibn Abbas then took the provincial funds of Basra, probably when he returned to the town some time after leaving it. The author notes that the exact circumstances are unknown, and observes that the act did not lessen the esteem of the Muslim community for him, from which he supposes there were fairly valid reasons. Events just after Ali's death are also described as far from clear. Al-Hasan appointed him general of his troops, but Ibn Abbas made contact with Muawiya, whether on his own initiative or at al-Hasan's invitation being obscure. The author suggests he may have helped bring about the agreement between the two claimants to the caliphate, and says Ibn Abbas maintained that Muawiya, as a reward, recognised his right to keep the money he had taken. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 59} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 60}

According to the article, some transmitters found these dealings unworthy of such a figure and transferred them, wrongly in the author's view, to his brother Ubayd Allah. During Muawiya's long reign Ibn Abbas lived in the [[place/hijaz|Hijaz]] and went fairly often to the court at [[place/damascus|Damascus]], mainly, it seems to the author, to defend the interests of the Hashimites, which were also his own. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 60}

## Conflict with Ibn al-Zubayr and death
After the deaths of the first two Umayyad rulers, the article says, Ibn Abbas was drawn back, perhaps against his will, into politics. The information is fragmentary, but it suggests that [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr]], having raised revolt at Mecca, was angered with Ibn Abbas and with the son of Ali called [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-hanafiyya|Ibn al-Hanafiyya]], who refused to recognise him as caliph. Both were banished from Mecca, returned in 64 and kept up their opposition, and the article says they were imprisoned. Informed of their danger, [[person/al-mukhtar-ibn-abi-ubayd|al-Mukhtar]] sent a large troop of horse from [[place/kufa|Kufa]] that freed them by surprise attack, and the article credits Ibn Abbas with avoiding bloodshed in the holy city on that occasion. Under the troop's protection they went to [[place/mina|Mina]] and then to [[place/taif|al-Ta'if]], where Ibn Abbas died some time later, in 68, which the article gives as 686 to 688 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 60}

## Assessments
The article notes that the verdicts of the modern scholars Caetani and Lammens on Ibn Abbas contrast with the respect Muslims of all periods have shown him. The author, L. Veccia Vaglieri, holds that Caetani's arguments can be answered by careful criticism, warning especially against confusing accounts drawn from Muslim biblical history with hadiths about the Prophet, and casts grave doubt on how closely a portrait sketched by a further writer resembles the original. The end of this passage is cut off in the source text, so that writer's name is not recoverable. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 60}

## Legacy
The whole genre of Qur'an commentary — tafsir — traces its ancestry to his teaching. Wherever a commentator writes "Ibn Abbas said," the chain claims to start here.

## In Ahmad ibn Hanbal's Fada'il al-Sahaba
[[person/ahmad-ibn-hanbal|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]] gives Ibn Abbas two chapters. The first has Ibn Mas'ud's "What an excellent interpreter of the Qur'an Ibn Abbas is" (1556, 1558) and "Had Ibn Abbas reached our age, none of us would come to a tenth of him" (1559); Ibn Abbas's own remark, on the verse about the number of the Companions of the Cave ([[passage/quran-18-22|al-Kahf 18:22]]), that he was one of the few who know it (1557); al-Zuhri's report that the Emigrants asked Umar why he did not invite their sons as he invited Ibn Abbas, and he said, "That is the youth among the elders: he has a questioning tongue and an understanding heart" (1555, weak for a break, since al-Zuhri did not meet Umar, according to the editor [[person/wasi-allah-abbas|Wasi Allah Abbas]]); and the prayer "O God, give him understanding of the religion and teach him interpretation" through Muhammad ibn Ali (1560, weak for an unnamed transmitter and *irsal*). {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1066} {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1067} {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1068} The second chapter has Ibn Abbas's report that the Messenger of God hugged him and said, "O God, teach him the Book" (1835, sound); Shu'ayb ibn Yasar's report that al-Abbas sent him to the Prophet, he saw a man with him, and the Prophet called him, sat him in his lap, stroked his head and prayed for him to have knowledge (1836); and Tawus's words: "I never saw anyone who revered God's sanctities more than Ibn Abbas; whenever I remember him I could weep if I wished" (1837, sound up to Tawus). {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1203} {cite:fadail-sahaba-ahmad-wasi-allah-1999 p.1204}

## For young visitors
Ibn Abbas was only a boy when the Prophet died, but he spent his life asking the elders everything they remembered — and became the great explainer of the Qur'an.

## For Sprouts
Ibn Abbas was a wise companion for whom the Prophet prayed for understanding, becoming the scholar who explained the Qur'an.

## For Explorers
Abdullah ibn Abbas was the Prophet's young cousin, acclaimed as the Sage of the Community and the Interpreter of the Qur'an. Blessed by the Prophet's prayer for deep comprehension, he studied with senior companions, mastering Arabic poetry, vocabulary, and circumstances of revelation, establishing celebrated teaching circles in Mecca and Basra.

## For Scholars
Abu al-Abbas Abdullah ibn Abbas (3 BH – 68 AH / 619–687 CE), acclaimed as the Sage of the Community (Habr al-Umma) and Interpreter of the Qur'an (Tarjuman al-Qur'an). Born in Mecca shortly before the Hijra, he was blessed by the Prophet's prayer for deep understanding of religion and the interpretation of scripture. Following the Prophet's passing, he rigorously collected traditions and linguistic usage from senior companions, pioneering systematic Qur'anic exegesis (tafsir) by utilizing classical Arabic poetry, lexical analysis, and contexts of revelation (asbab al-nuzul), founding the renowned Meccan school of commentary through students like Mujahid and Sa'id ibn Jubayr.

## In Abd al-Rahman Mansour's programme 'Ahalina'
[[person/abd-al-rahman-mansour|Abd al-Rahman Mansour]] quotes Ibn Abbas in his programme [[work/ahaleena|'Ahalina']] as saying that he liked to adorn himself for his wife as she adorned herself for him, and makes this the ground for a husband's care for his appearance and cleanliness. {cite:assabile-abd-al-rahman-mansour ahaleena/ep02 00:03:30} He mentions a report that [[person/mujahid-ibn-jabr|Mujahid]] relates: Ibn Abbas saw a man and spoke of what was in the man's heart towards him, and when asked how he knew, said that he himself loved the man, and mentioned that souls are conscripted soldiers. {cite:assabile-abd-al-rahman-mansour ahaleena/ep03 00:12:30} {cite:assabile-abd-al-rahman-mansour ahaleena/ep03 00:13:00}

## His place in al-Tabari's Jami' al-Bayan
One of the chains for Ibn Abbas's exegesis that recurs most in [[work/jami-al-bayan|Jami' al-Bayan]] is that of [[person/atiyya-al-awfi|Atiyya al-Awfi]] and his descendants: Muhammad ibn Sa'd, from his father, from his uncle, from al-Husayn ibn al-Hasan, from his father, from his grandfather Atiyya, from Ibn Abbas; [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]] quotes it in some 1,560 places according to Fuat Sezgin, as Ahmad Khalid Babakr cites. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.48}

## In al-Tabari's commentary
Al-Tabari gives a report from Ibn Abbas, through Abu Rawq and al-Dahhak, that "al-hamd lillah" means thanks to God, submission to Him, and acknowledgment of His blessing, guidance and origination; the editor grades the chain as weak. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 135}
Al-Tabari also gives the view of Ibn Abbas and Sa'id ibn Jubayr that "the worlds" are each kind of creation and each generation within it, which he says is the common view of the exegetes. {cite:tafsir-tabari-shakir-v01 p. 143}
