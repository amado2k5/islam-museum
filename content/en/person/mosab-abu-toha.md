## Summary
Mosab Abu Toha is a Palestinian poet, essayist and librarian born in 1998 in the Jabalia refugee camp in the northern Gaza Strip. He founded Gaza's first English-language public library and became, during the war that began in 2023, one of the most widely read witnesses of life under bombardment. {cite:muslim500-mosab-abu-toha} {cite:wikipedia-mosab-abu-toha}

## The library
Raised in Jabalia camp and educated at the Islamic University of Gaza, where he studied English, Abu Toha began collecting books after the 2014 war. With donations from readers abroad he opened the Edward Said Public Library in Gaza City — a free lending library that grew to two branches, both damaged or destroyed in the later war. {cite:wikipedia-mosab-abu-toha}

## Writing
His debut collection, Things You May Find Hidden in My Ear ⁦(2022)⁩, won the Palestine Book Award and an American Book Award; his poems and essays appeared in The New Yorker, The New York Times, The Atlantic and Poetry, and in 2025 his New Yorker columns on Gaza earned the Pulitzer Prize for commentary. {cite:wikipedia-mosab-abu-toha}

## War and exile
In November 2023 he was detained by Israeli forces at a checkpoint while leaving Gaza with his family, then released after international attention; he resettled in the United States and taught at Syracuse University. His second book, Forest of Noise ⁦(2024)⁩, gathers poems written amid the destruction. {cite:wikipedia-mosab-abu-toha}

## Assessment
Abu Toha writes a spare, documentary lyric — poems of neighbours, children, books and olive trees under siege — received by readers as testimony rather than ornament. Debate attaches mainly to the politics around his reporting; the museum reports positions attributed, without verdict. {cite:wikipedia-mosab-abu-toha} {cite:muslim500-mosab-abu-toha}


## Themes and style
His poems move between documentary detail and elegy — tomatoes on a windowsill, a neighbour's olive press, a classroom register — insisting that Gaza's ordinary life, not only its destruction, belongs in literature; critics have likened the register to Mahmoud Darwish's later plain style. {cite:wikipedia-mosab-abu-toha}

## Pulitzer and debate
The 2025 Pulitzer for his New Yorker columns was celebrated by free-expression groups and attacked by some pro-Israel commentators who challenged his reporting; prize bodies defended the award. The museum reports these positions attributed to their sources, without verdict. {cite:wikipedia-mosab-abu-toha}

## The library's afterlife
Supporters abroad crowdfunded rebuilding of the Edward Said Library after its destruction, and Abu Toha has spoken of the library as his life's project — books, he has written, being the proof that people in Gaza read. {cite:wikipedia-mosab-abu-toha} {cite:muslim500-mosab-abu-toha}

## The Forest of Noise and the Pulitzer
His 2024 collection Forest of Noise brought his Gaza poetry to international readers and in 2025 he won the Pulitzer Prize for Commentary for New Yorker essays on the war — recognition press coverage framed as unprecedented for a Gazan poet writing in English. {cite:wikipedia-mosab-abu-toha}

## Teaching and institutions
He has taught in Gaza and held fellowships and residencies abroad — including at Syracuse and Harvard-affiliated programmes — a career arc that carried him from UNRWA-school English teacher to the centre of Anglophone literary attention. {cite:wikipedia-mosab-abu-toha}

## Detention and deportation coverage
His December 2023 detention by Israeli forces while evacuating his family, and his later writings about it, were covered internationally; he has also written on book destruction in Gaza, including the loss of his own library. {cite:wikipedia-mosab-abu-toha} {cite:muslim500-mosab-abu-toha}

## Poetry under siege
Abu Toha's writing developed inside the particular pressures of Gaza: recurrent wars, the destruction of buildings and books, and the everyday work of living under blockade. His English-language poems — many first published in American magazines and then collected in Things You May Find Hidden in My Ear ⁦(2022)⁩ — treat ordinary objects, streets and memories as the material of a literature that insists on the ordinary humanity of a place usually reported only as a war zone. Critics describe the work as documentary in register but lyrical in method. {cite:wikipedia-mosab-abu-toha}

## The Muslim 500 listing
The Muslim 500 lists him among the most influential Muslims in arts and literature, citing his role in bringing the experience of the Gaza war to a global literary readership and the extraordinary recognitions his work received during the conflict itself — including the Pulitzer Prize, awarded for essays written while the war was ongoing around him. {cite:muslim500-mosab-abu-toha}
