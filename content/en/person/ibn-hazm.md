## Overview
The hafiz Abu Muhammad Ali ibn Ahmad ibn Hazm al-Andalusi, imam of the Zahiri school. Author of [[work/tawq-al-hamama|The Ring of the Dove]] and *al-Fasl fi al-Milal wa al-Ahwa' wa al-Nihal* (The Decisive Account on Religions, Sects and Schools). {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari}

The Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, describes him as born in [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]] in 384 AH/994 CE and dead at Manta Lisham in 456 AH/1064 CE: an Andalusian poet, historian, jurist, philosopher and theologian, whom it counts among the greatest thinkers of Arabo-Muslim civilization. The article says he set out the [[group/zahiri-madhhab|Zahiri]] doctrine in a systematic form and applied its method to all the Qur'anic sciences. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 822}

## The Banu Hazm
The same encyclopaedia gives a short account of the family so that its members are not confused. Ali's father, Abu Umar Ahmad ibn Said ibn Hazm, was a dignitary at the court of the chamberlain [[person/al-mansur-ibn-abi-amir|al-Mansur ibn Abi Amir]] and of his son [[person/abd-al-malik-al-muzaffar-ibn-abi-amir|al-Muzaffar]]. He was hard hit by the grave events of 399 AH/1009 CE and died on 28 Dhu al-Qada 402 AH/21 June 1012 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 822}

Ali's elder brother Abu Bakr, known only by his kunya, was born in 379 AH/989 CE and died at the age of twenty-two in the plague that struck Cordoba in Dhu al-Qada 401 AH/June 1011 CE. Ibn Shuhayd dedicated his *Risalat al-Tawabi wa al-Zawabi* to him, which the article uses to date that work. Ali's son Abu Rafi al-Fadl entered the service of the Abbadids of Seville and was killed at the battle of al-Zallaqa in Rajab 479 AH/October 1086 CE; he wrote a historical work on the genealogy of the Abbadids. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 822}

A cousin, Abu al-Mughira Abd al-Wahhab ibn Ahmad ibn Abd al-Rahman ibn Hazm, was a secretary and wit. He was made vizier by al-Mustazhir in 414 AH/1023 CE, later served the petty kings of Saragossa, was imprisoned when that town fell in 431 AH/1040 CE, and died in 438 AH/1046 CE. The article says Ibn Hayyan thought highly of him as a poet and prose writer, and that the Kayrawani Ibn al-Rabib addressed to him a well-known epistle criticising the Andalusians for not preserving the memory of their famous men; Ali Ibn Hazm also answered that letter, and his reply survives. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 822}

## Life and the politics of his time
The article quotes E. García Gómez to the effect that Ibn Hazm lived through the most tragic moments of Muslim Spain. It calls his family origins obscure and says the most probable view is that he descended from Christian converts to Islam; this differs from the Persian origin reported by Ibn Ghalib in the section below, and the museum records both as attributed. His grandfather Said settled in Cordoba and his father rose to the vizierate under al-Mansur and al-Muzaffar. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 822} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 823}

According to the article, he spent his early years among the women of the harem, which Asin Palacios and García Gómez held to have deeply marked him, though the article cautions against calling him neurotic. Until fourteen he lived a pampered life. Then the struggles among Andalusians, Berbers and Slavs reached the family: his father fell into disgrace when the Amirids fell and [[person/hisham-ii-al-muayyad|Hisham II]] was replaced, was later imprisoned and stripped of his goods by the Slav general Wadih, and died in 402 AH/1012 CE. The family house at Balat Mughith was destroyed in 403 AH/1013 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 823}

Ibn Hazm took refuge at [[place/almeria|Almeria]] until 407 AH/1016 CE, when he was suspected of pro-Umayyad propaganda, imprisoned for some months and banished. With his friend Muhammad ibn Ishaq he found refuge at Hisn al-Qasr, then joined the Umayyad claimant [[person/abd-al-rahman-iv-al-murtada|Abd al-Rahman IV al-Murtada]], became his vizier and fought in his army before [[place/granada|Granada]]; he was taken prisoner and released. Around 412 AH/1022 CE, retired to [[place/shatiba|Jativa]], he began the *Tawq al-Hamama*, which the article says contains many autobiographical passages about these events. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 823}

When the Berbers lost Cordoba in 414 AH/1023 CE, the new caliph [[person/abd-al-rahman-v-mustazhir|Abd al-Rahman V al-Mustazhir]] appointed him vizier, but was assassinated after seven weeks and Ibn Hazm was again imprisoned. He reappears at Jativa in 418 AH/1027 CE and, on the report of al-Jayyani given by Yaqut, served again as vizier under [[person/hisham-iii-al-mutadd|Hisham III al-Mu'tadd]]. The article says that political experience destroyed the ideals of his youth, and that from then on he devoted himself, in semi-retirement, to study, writing and teaching. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 823}

His sharp attacks on the [[group/maliki-madhhab|Maliki]] jurists, whom the article describes as always backing those in power, his stand against the Abbadids of Seville and his general non-conformity brought him the hatred of the official scholars and the hostility of rulers in the age of the petty kingdoms. He withdrew to his family's country at Manta Lisham. The article says that measures were taken to silence him: groups of students were forbidden to form around him, and only a few disciples, among them the historian al-Humaydi, dared to attend him. He died in his village, leaving, according to his son Abu Rafi, four hundred works. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 823}

## Teachers and education
The article says he received a thorough education and names teachers drawn from the Tawq and from his treatise on the merits of Muslim Spain: Abd al-Rahman ibn Abi Yazid al-Azdi al-Misri, for rhetoric, traditions, grammar, lexicography, dialectic and theology; the jurisconsult Abu al-Khiyar al-Lughawi, for law; Abu Said al-Fata al-Jafari, who commented on early poetry in the Great Mosque of Cordoba; Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn al-Jasur, for hadith; and, for philosophy, Abu Abd Allah Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Madhhiji. It also notes a collection of epistles to friends on religious and intellectual subjects, and his *Risala fi Maratib al-Ulum*, which it describes as a complete plan of education arguing that the sciences are related, that they demand renouncing a life of pleasure, and that their purpose is success and well-being in the next world. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 823}

## Character as the article describes it
The article's view is that for a modern reader he remains the most alive and vigorous author of Arab Islam. It follows Asin Palacios in ascribing to his early years a heightened sensitivity that became a sensitive conscience, with a repugnance for falseness and deceit and a distrust of the hidden recesses of the human soul. Experience of those in power confirmed in him what it calls a mournful scepticism, and he held that there is no refuge from human vice except in God. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 823} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 824}

It says that he valued friendship as the source of truth, frankness and sincerity, and that this is linked to his respect for plain language, on which his Zahiri doctrine rests. In the article's account he regarded the supposedly rational arguments of philosophers, theologians and some jurists as passions and prejudices given a semblance of legitimacy, and this led to a logic in which reason has a defined and subordinate place. The article calls him a fighter and man of action rather than a recluse, and records an objection that he did not try to understand his opponents thoroughly and sometimes used specious arguments; it explains that he did not regard them as people with whom a real dialogue was possible. It also points to his observation of the concrete: remarks on agriculture when treating the zakat, enquiries about the size of the plant grown from a mustard seed, and the tracing of a mudd measure through successive owners back to Medina in the manner of an isnad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 824}

## Psychologist and moralist
The article singles out two works. *Tawq al-Hamama* is a treatise on love and lovers, in a genre it says had predecessors such as [[person/al-jahiz|al-Jahiz]] and Ibn Dawud al-Isfahani and soon became a stock of clichés and quotations. It judges that Ibn Hazm gradually leaves the stereotypes behind, that the work grows more serious, with moral and religious reflection, personal examples and direct observation, and that it probably was not written all at one time. It points to the chapter on the ugliness of disobedience for autobiographical detail and for notes on feminine psychology. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 824} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 825}

The article reads the Tawq and the *Kitab al-Akhlaq wa al-Siyar* together: in its reading, Ibn Hazm exposes the way speech can conceal and deceive, turns away from the depths of human consciousness, and directs people toward the revealed text, understanding which is the only union with God he allows. It quotes the Akhlaq as saying that there is one object to seek, the banishing of anxiety, reached only through the service of God, and concludes that his moral science centres on action purged of inner motives. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 825}

## Language and logic
On the article's account, Ibn Hazm held that language was instituted by God, that it carries a truth, and that the evil in speech lies in using it for personal ends. Perfect speech, above all the Word of God, must be understood through its apparent sense, the zahir; seeking a hidden meaning, the batin, leaves room for passion and arbitrary judgement. He opposed theories of hidden meanings in grammar and treated the meaning of an utterance as independent of the speaker's or hearer's intentions. The article adds that he treated the origin of languages with detachment and made no categorical statement, and that his chief valuation of Arabic is that it is the language of the Qur'an. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 825}

His logic, the article says, was built to analyse revealed texts at the level of the zahir. He believed in reason but allowed it a limited role: it grasps necessary intuitions such as the principle of contradiction, is tied to the senses and to speech, and can distinguish true from false, but cannot discover or create a truth, nor judge good and evil. In the article's phrase, reason is a worker and not a ruler. Ijtihad, on this view, adds nothing to knowledge but leads to a more secure understanding of the texts. He did not build a metaphysical system from the logical categories and used terms such as species and nature in their ordinary linguistic sense, except in argument with opponents. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 826}

His *Kitab al-Taqrib* presents a summary of Aristotelian logic, which he treated as common to all languages and so useful for studying Arabic. The article adds that some Muslim writers on logic have claimed that his grasp of Aristotle was not authentic; it itself suggests that he did not wish to give logic so wide a scope. In its summary, the Zahiri view of language led to a Zahiri logic, and that to a Zahiri theology and law. Its basic rule is that elements of a text are first taken in their general sense: an imperative means an obligation, and a prohibition a ban, unless another text gives a clear indication that something weaker is meant. Where two texts conflict, the particular gives way to the general. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 826} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 827}

## Law and the sources of law
The article says his reading of the Qur'an is literal and wide at once: from verses of seemingly narrow bearing he drew broad principles on which he built parts of his legal system, while in other places, such as loans and pledges, he read the terms very narrowly. He applied severe standards to hadith and rejected most of those on which his opponents relied. He rejected analogy, [[concept/qiyas|qiyas]], arguing that resemblance is vague and the choice of a point of analogy arbitrary, and in al-Muhalla he stresses the inconsistencies analogy produces. He also narrowed [[concept/ijma|ijma]] to the consensus of the Companions, the only one he held possible and certain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 827}

The article calls him the most representative figure of the Zahiri school. It says that he opposed Hanafism fiercely and Shafi'ism less so, that his chief adversary in theory and practice was Malikism, strong in Spain in his day, and that he may have embraced Zahirism as a means of opposing the Maliki jurists, so that in this sense his legal work was a liberation of the faithful. In its summary he recognised five categories of ruling, and an act can be placed in one of the first four only by citing a text in its apparent sense; otherwise it is permitted. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 827}

Examples the article gives of his simplifying the law: in al-Muhalla he retains, for the zakat on crops, only wheat, barley, dates and raisins; he sharply limits forms of partnership on the principle that each person keeps the fruit of his labour; his treatise on sale returns to forms of commerce for which the Prophet laid down rules; and on marriage he takes what the article calls a liberal view, defending women's rights and greatly reducing the guardian's power of compulsion. It explains that he did not see a need to develop the law in step with social change, since he saw it first as a religious reality through which people obey God and which binds the community. In his view every Qur'anic rule is itself a principle, and no common cause, [[concept/illa|illa]], may be drawn from a particular case to extend it. The article observes that this risks detaching the law from the present, while crediting him with stressing the right to the fruits of labour, the duty to develop one's possessions for the common good, and respect for the law of land, and with leaving a wide field for the permitted. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 827}

## Historian of religious ideas
The article follows Asin Palacios in calling him a historian of religious ideas and describes *al-Fisal* as an encyclopaedia of religious knowledge. It judges that his information is full and accurate, that he read widely and made some enquiries himself, and that his quarrel with Ibn Naghrila, a Jew of Granada, shows this. His plan runs from questions of knowledge and the possibility of truth, rejecting the Sophists, through the eternity of the world, to monotheism and revelation through prophets, and then to criticism of astral religions, dualism, metempsychosis, the Trinity and Jewish and Christian ideas of prophecy, before turning to a heresiography of Muslim groups that concentrates on theological and juridical ideas and settles disputes by Zahiri principles. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 827} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 828}

The article also lists limits it perceives. It says he approached other religions only through the points that let him compare them with Islam, such as asking the Jews about abrogation, and argued that the Gospels lack the marks of a revealed text by the Qur'anic standard and lack even an isnad. It describes him as presenting opponents' positions honestly, but as never trying to grasp why their problems mattered to them, and as aiming to trap them by dialectic, for instance accusing the [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazila]], who limit God's power, of ascribing to God a weakness greater than that of bugs, fleas or worms. The article attributes this tone to anger more than temperament, and to a Zahiri conviction. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 828}

## His creed as summarised in the article
The article draws on the summary of his creed at the opening of al-Muhalla, and what follows is Ibn Hazm's position as it reports it, with no judgement from the museum. The basic obligation is to know with certainty in the heart, and to say with the tongue, that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is His Messenger; the heart's part is an act of the soul accompanying outward actions, and Ibn Hazm does not demand cultivating inner purity of intention. He argues from time that the world is created and has one Creator, who made it without any necessitating cause. Prophecy is a means of true knowledge; Muhammad is the seal of the prophets, whose milla cancels the others, and belief in the other prophets, named or not, is obligatory. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 828} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 829}

On the next world he holds that Paradise and Hell are created, that no believer remains eternally in Hell, and that the descriptions in the Qur'an are literal and not metaphorical, to be affirmed without asking how. One who rejects a single letter of the Qur'an in Muslim hands is called an unbeliever in his statement, and he holds that no one possesses a secret concerning religion. He sets out how good and bad deeds are recorded. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 829}

On the divine names and attributes, he teaches that God may be named only by the names He has used of Himself, and that derivation of new names is not allowed: God is seated on the Throne, but may not be called the Seated. The Mu'tazila, he says, wrongly identify God's knowledge with His essence, and the Ash'aris, who derive attributes from names, remained within the Mu'tazili framing of the problem. He holds that the names are descriptions and not derived from attributes, and that the vision of God by the faithful comes from a faculty they lack in this world. God creates human actions, including free choice, will and knowledge, and in al-Fisal he says God created the capacity to act. The article adds that he extended Zahiri method from legal theory to theology, and that this lets him dismiss the embarrassments of kalam; it notes his epistle on faith to Ibn al-Hawwath, which takes up the question of [[concept/taqlid|taqlid]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 829} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 830}

## Politics, Arabism and genealogy
The article describes him as by tradition and experience a legitimist and partisan of the Umayyads, and suggests that his reasons were chiefly personal loyalty and friendship, together with a view of the Umayyads as representing Arabism. It points to his *Jamharat Ansab al-Arab*, where he justifies genealogy by a Qur'anic verse on nations and tribes, and distinguishes obligatory kinds, such as the Prophet's lineage, the Qurashi descent of the caliph and the prohibited degrees of marriage, from those that are a communal duty. It adds that his letter on the merits of the people of al-Andalus, preserved by al-Maqqari, shows some chauvinism and a call to Spain to stop admiring the East. On its reading his three ideals were Zahirism, Arabism and Umayyad Spain, and when the political one was lost he gave his energies to the other two. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 830}

## Reception after his death
After his death he was attacked by the judge [[person/abu-bakr-ibn-al-arabi-qadi|Ibn al-Arabi]], who was much influenced by al-Ghazali, in *al-Qawasim wa al-Awasim*, and in the sixth century AH/twelfth century CE by Maliki theologians, among them Abd al-Haqq ibn Abd Allah and Ibn Zarqun, who wrote a book against al-Muhalla. The botanist Ibn al-Rumiyya and the mystic of Murcia [[person/ibn-arabi-muhyi-al-din|Ibn al-Arabi]] supported him; the latter wrote a summary of al-Muhalla and was Zahiri in law, and the article says the concept of the zahir plays an important part in his mystical anthropology. The article notes renewed interest in Ibn Hazm in Muslim countries through studies and manuscript editions. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 830}

## Origin and birth
Ibn Ghalib says that the Banu Hazm are a house other than the one from which Ibn Hazm the Zahiri came, and that they were of Persian origin. {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari p.327}

He was born in Córdoba on the last day of Ramadan in 384 AH, and died in the village of Mant Lusham in Sha'ban 456 AH. {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari p.80-81}

## His doctrine and learning
Ibn Hayyan and others said that he was a man of hadith, jurisprudence and disputation, and that he wrote books on logic and philosophy. He was first a Shafi'i and then became a Zahiri, and he remained so until he died. Ibn Hayyan says that the jurists reviled him, and that the kings drove him away. {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari p.80-81}

Sa'id says in his history that he gathered together the learning of Islam more fully than any other man in al-Andalus. His son al-Fadl relates that about four hundred volumes in his father's hand were gathered at his home. Al-Ghazali said that he found a book by him on the names of God, which shows the greatness of his memory. {cite:nafh-tib-maqqari p.80-81}

## His works
Haji Khalifa attributes to him [[work/al-ihkam-fi-usul-al-ahkam|al-Ihkam fi Usul al-Ahkam]] (The Firm Principles of the Rulings). {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan}

## In «Asdaf al-Lu'lu'» by Abu Ishaq al-Heweny
[[person/abu-ishaq-al-heweny|Abu Ishaq al-Heweny]] says his beginnings in fiqh were with Ibn Hazm's «al-Muhalla», and that he always advises students not to begin with Ibn Hazm because of his harsh wording. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep25 00:25:30} He says Ibn Hazm could persuade you that a red-brick wall is gold, that he spread his school in the face of the Malikis of al-Andalus, and that perhaps he took his sharpness from his opponents, though he was sharp by nature. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep25 00:26:00} He calls «al-Ihkam fi Usul al-Ahkam» more than wonderful, except on analogy (qiyas), where he differs from Ibn Hazm. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep25 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep26 00:06:30} He mentions that he read in «al-Muhalla», alongside al-Albani's research, that a prayer deliberately abandoned is not made up, and Ibn Hazm's division of acts of worship by their timing, and took this to the lesson of his teacher [[person/muhammad-najib-al-mutii|al-Muti'i]]. {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep08 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-abu-ishaq-alheweny asdaf-al-loaloa/ep08 00:16:30}

## In the introduction to the Dar Ibn Hazm edition of al-Muhalla

The editors of [[work/al-muhalla-ibn-hazm|al-Muhalla]] give his life: Abu Muhammad Ali ibn Ahmad ibn Sa'id ibn Hazm ibn Ghalib, Persian by origin, Andalusian of Cordoba, client of the emir Yazid ibn Abi Sufyan; his ancestor Khalaf ibn Ma'dan was the first of the family to enter al-Andalus, in the company of Abd al-Rahman al-Dakhil. He was born in [[place/cordoba|Cordoba]] in 384 AH, and wrote to the judge Sa'id ibn Ahmad that he was born in the suburb of Munyat al-Mughira on its eastern side. {cite:muhalla-dar-ibn-hazm-2016-v1 p.24} He grew up in comfort; his father was one of the notables of Cordoba and a vizier of the Amirid state, and he himself served as vizier in his youth, first excelling in letters, history, poetry and logic. {cite:muhalla-dar-ibn-hazm-2016-v1 p.25} On the age at which he began to study law see [[question/when-did-ibn-hazm-begin-studying-fiqh|When did Ibn Hazm begin to study law?]].

The editors note that he held views on the divine names and attributes and on some questions of faith that do not match his method of taking texts at their apparent sense, and they attribute this partly to his work in philosophy, logic and refuting the materialists; they comment on some of these views in the Book of Divine Unity of al-Muhalla. This is their assessment. {cite:muhalla-dar-ibn-hazm-2016-v1 p.31} {cite:muhalla-dar-ibn-hazm-2016-v1 p.32}

Among his trials: expulsion from Cordoba when the Hammudids took it; captivity after the murder of al-Murtada and the defeat of his army near Granada; imprisonment after the killing of al-Mustazhir bi-llah, whose vizier he became in 414 AH; and the burning of his books in Seville under al-Mu'tadid ibn Abbad, which the editors ascribe to jurists stirring rulers against him for attacking Malik and the imams. They quote his verses on it and Ibn Hayyan's account that the jurists of his time combined against him until he ended up in the countryside of [[place/labla|Niebla]], teaching whoever came to him. {cite:muhalla-dar-ibn-hazm-2016-v1 p.80} {cite:muhalla-dar-ibn-hazm-2016-v1 p.81} {cite:muhalla-dar-ibn-hazm-2016-v1 p.82} He died on the evening of Sunday, two nights before the end of Sha'ban 456 AH, aged seventy-one years and some months. {cite:muhalla-dar-ibn-hazm-2016-v1 p.82}

## His life in the introduction to the al-Fisal edition
The life by Abd al-Rahman Khalifa at the start of the Maktabat al-Salam edition of [[work/al-fisal-ibn-hazm|al-Fisal]] says that his father, Abu Amr Ahmad ibn Sa'id ibn Hazm, was a vizier of al-Mansur ibn Abi Amir and of his son al-Muzaffar; that Ibn Hazm was born in Cordoba after the dawn prayer on the last day of Ramadan 384 AH; and that he served as vizier to Abd al-Rahman al-Mustazhir and then Hisham al-Mu'tadd. {cite:fisal-ibn-hazm-salam-v1 p.7} It relates that he reached twenty-six without knowing how to make good a prayer, and that he took up law after attending a funeral: he sat in the mosque without praying its greeting prayer, then prayed at a time when prayer is not made, was embarrassed, and went to the jurist Abu Abd Allah ibn Dahhun, beginning with the Muwatta'. {cite:fisal-ibn-hazm-salam-v1 p.7} It says he abandoned the vizierate for learning, wrote al-Taqrib li-Hudud al-Mantiq under his teacher in logic Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Madhhiji known as Ibn al-Kattani, adopted the school of Dawud ibn Ali in denying analogy and causal reasoning, and that his son Abu Rafi' al-Fadl said his works came to some four hundred volumes. {cite:fisal-ibn-hazm-salam-v1 p.8} The books it names include al-Isal ila Fahm al-Khisal in twenty-four volumes, al-Ihkam li-Usul al-Ahkam, al-Muhalla, a book on consensus, al-Imama wa-l-Siyasa, Akhlaq al-Nafs and Kashf al-Iltibas between the Zahiris and the people of analogy. {cite:fisal-ibn-hazm-salam-v1 p.8} {cite:fisal-ibn-hazm-salam-v1 p.9} It quotes Abu al-Abbas ibn al-Arif that Ibn Hazm's tongue and al-Hajjaj's sword were twins, and says his opponents warned rulers against him until he settled in his village near Onuba, and that some of his books were burned in Seville, on which he wrote: burn the paper if you will, you will not burn what it holds - that is in my breast. {cite:fisal-ibn-hazm-salam-v1 p.9} {cite:fisal-ibn-hazm-salam-v1 p.10} It gives his death at the end of Sha'ban 456 AH, citing Sa'id in Akhbar al-Hukama'. {cite:fisal-ibn-hazm-salam-v1 p.11}
