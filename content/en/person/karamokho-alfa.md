## Summary
Karamokho Alfa (c. 1700 – c. 1751), born Ibrahima Musa Sambeghu, was the Fula scholar who led the jihad that created the Imamate of Futa Jallon in what is now Guinea — the first Muslim state founded by the Fulbe in West Africa. Elected leader by the nine ulama of the new state, he ruled from Timbo as its first almami, always constrained by their council, and was remembered for his learning and piety rather than for war: the fighting was led by his cousin Ibrahim Sori, who succeeded him. {cite:wikipedia-karamokho-alfa-en} {cite:wikipedia-karamokho-alfa-fr}

## Formation and the call to jihad
The French article places his birth about 1700 at Diari, in the Labé region; he learned the Qur'an with his uncle at Sombily and from the age of twelve pursued Islamic studies in Mali. The English article adds his study at Fugumba under the famous scholar Tierno Samba, and that he was hereditary ruler of Timbo and a maternal cousin of Maka Jiba of Bundu. His father, Alfa Ba, formed a coalition of Muslim Fulbe and called for the jihad in 1725, but died before the struggle began; the jihad was launched about 1726–1727. By tradition, the young Ibrahim Sori symbolically opened the war in 1727 by cutting down the great ceremonial drum of the Yalunka with his sword. {cite:wikipedia-karamokho-alfa-fr} {cite:wikipedia-karamokho-alfa-en}

## The founding at Talansan
After the victory at Talansan, where a small Muslim force defeated a far larger one, the state was established at a meeting of nine Fulbe ulama, each representing a province of the Futa Jallon. Ibrahima Sambeghu, one of the nine, was elected leader of the jihad and took the title almami. Under him Futa Jallon became the first Fulbe Muslim state, and disadvantaged men — gangs of young men, outlaws and slaves — were enlisted to its cause. {cite:wikipedia-karamokho-alfa-en}

## Ruler of a loose federation
The new state was a tenuous confederation. Alfa ruled the diwal of Timbo directly, with his political capital there, while the religious capital was Fugumba, seat of the council of ulama, and some of the other eight ulama held more secular power than he did. He was known for Islamic scholarship and piety, and for respecting the rights of the old masters of the soil — "it was Allah who had established them" — though the imams reserved the right to reassign land held in trust, and the old owners now paid zakat as a form of rent. The French article adds that he made Labé the capital of his own province, built its mosque, and devoted himself there to teaching and to spreading Islam through the kingdom. {cite:wikipedia-karamokho-alfa-en} {cite:wikipedia-karamokho-alfa-fr}

## Last years and succession
Alfa was not the movement's soldier — Ibrahim Sori was. In 1748 his excessive devotions caused him to become mentally unstable, and Sori was selected as de facto leader; Alfa Saliu, Alfa's son, was passed over as too young. Karamokho Alfa died around 1751 and was formally succeeded by Ibrahim Sori. The French article places his death in 1751 at Labé and his burial behind the great mosque there, where a mausoleum later received some of his descendants as well. He came to be thought of as a saint, and a miracle story is told of his tomb. {cite:wikipedia-karamokho-alfa-en} {cite:wikipedia-karamokho-alfa-fr}
