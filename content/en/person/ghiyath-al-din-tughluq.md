## Summary
Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq Shah, known before his accession as Ghazi Malik (died 1 February 1325), was the founder of the Tughluq dynasty and Sultan of Delhi from 1320 to 1325. A frontier commander of the Khalji sultans, he made his name defending the north-western borderlands against the Mongols as governor of Multan and Dipalpur, then marched on Delhi in 1320, overthrew the usurper Khusrau Khan and took the throne. In five years of rule he founded the fortress-city of Tughluqabad, annexed Warangal and campaigned in Bengal, before dying on the road home when a wooden pavilion built for his reception collapsed on him. {cite:wikipedia-ghiyath-al-din-tughluq-en} {cite:wikipedia-ghiyath-al-din-tughluq-ar}

## Origins and early career
The literary sources differ on his ancestry in detail but not in substance. He belonged to the Qara'unas, a Mongol tribe of Central Asia; Ibn Battuta and Shams-i Siraj Afif describe him in broader terms as a Turk or Mongol, and several sources indicate that his family had settled in Hindustan — his father served under the sultan Balban, and his mother was of the Hindu Jat community of the Punjab — so that Firishta portrays a mixed Turk–Jat lineage. "Tughluq" was the sultan's personal name, not originally a dynastic one. Amir Khusrau's Tughluq Nama presents him describing himself as an unimportant man in his early career; he began, the sources say, as a menial servant keeping horses for a merchant before entering Khalji service. {cite:wikipedia-ghiyath-al-din-tughluq-en}

## The frontier commander
Tughluq first distinguished himself in the early 1290s at the siege of Ranthambore under Jalaluddin Khalji. Under Alauddin Khalji he rose as a personal attendant of the sultan's brother Ulugh Khan, fought at Amroha in 1305 when the Khalji army defeated a Chagatai Mongol force, and led the vanguard against the Mongol invasion of 1306. Alauddin appointed him governor of Multan and then of Dipalpur, the frontier provinces through which the Mongol invasions came — a trust that itself testifies to his martial reputation. Khusrau credits him with eighteen victories over the Mongols, Barani with twenty, and Ibn Battuta reports an inscription at the Jama mosque of Multan recording twenty-nine; the victories were probably, in the main, border successes rather than set-piece battles. After Alauddin's death in 1316 he navigated the brief Kafur regency and Mubarak Shah's reign without committing himself against Delhi. {cite:wikipedia-ghiyath-al-din-tughluq-en}

## The march on Delhi
In July 1320 Mubarak Shah was murdered and his general Khusrau Khan seized the throne. Tughluq refused to recognise him, but it was his son Fakhruddin Jauna — the future Muhammad bin Tughluq — who organised the conspiracy from inside Delhi and summoned his father from Dipalpur. Tughluq canvassed the neighbouring governors by letter, gathered such support as came, and declared, in the English article's summary of his stated case, that he fought for the glory of Islam, from loyalty to Alauddin's house and to punish the criminals in Delhi. His army beat Khusrau Khan's forces at Saraswati and Lahrawat; Khusrau was captured and killed, and Tughluq was proclaimed sultan on 6 September 1320. The Arabic article tells the same story in outline: the frontier commander, with his son, assembled the forces of Sindh and Multan and ended Khusrau Khan's rule. {cite:wikipedia-ghiyath-al-din-tughluq-en} {cite:wikipedia-ghiyath-al-din-tughluq-ar} {cite:wikipedia-ghiyath-al-din-tughluq-ur}

## Reign
His policy towards the Mongols was harsh: he had killed envoys of the Ilkhan Abu Sa'id and punished Mongol prisoners severely. In 1323 he sent his son Jauna against the Kakatiya capital Warangal; the siege annexed Warangal and ended the Kakatiya dynasty, and in the same year he formally designated Jauna his heir, taking a written undertaking from the ministers and nobles. He began the construction of Tughluqabad, the fortified city that still stands in ruins in south Delhi. In 1324 he campaigned in Bengal, then in civil war: he left Nasiruddin on the throne of West Bengal as a vassal, annexed East Bengal, and on the way back defeated the Raja of Tirhut and annexed his territory. {cite:wikipedia-ghiyath-al-din-tughluq-en} {cite:wikipedia-ghiyath-al-din-tughluq-ar}

## Death
February 1325 ended the reign abruptly. On the return march, the wooden pavilion prepared for his reception collapsed, killing him and his second son, Prince Mahmud Khan, and he was succeeded by his eldest son, Muhammad bin Tughluq. The sources read for this record place the collapse differently: the English article's narrative puts it at Kara-Manikpur in February 1325 and its lead dates his death to 1 February, while the Arabic article puts it at Afghanpur and dates it to July 1325 (725 AH). Ibn Battuta claimed the collapse was no accident but a conspiracy hatched against him — by his vizier, in one telling, with his heir's foreknowledge implied in others; the museum records the claim as a claim, attributed to Ibn Battuta, and the two datings side by side. No source read gives his birth year, and none names a religious school or affiliation for the man himself; the record therefore carries his affiliation as unknown, with Khusrau's "glory of Islam" framing noted as a court poet's description of a campaign rather than an attestation. {cite:wikipedia-ghiyath-al-din-tughluq-en} {cite:wikipedia-ghiyath-al-din-tughluq-ar} {cite:wikidata-ghiyath-al-din-tughluq}
