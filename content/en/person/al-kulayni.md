## Overview
Muhammad ibn Ya'qub al-Kulayni, Abu Ja'far, the Imami traditionist from the village of [[place/kulayn|Kulayn]], 38 km south-west of [[place/rayy|Rayy]] in the Pashapuya district; its Persian name Kolen was arabicised as Kulayn and Kulin {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5 p. 400-401}.

## His studies
Since his chief teachers were scholars of [[place/qumm|Qumm]], the EI2 concludes he studied there for a prolonged time, most likely in the last decade of the 3rd century AH (903-913); he transmitted from scholars of Rayy including his maternal uncle [[person/muhammad-ibn-ali-alan|Muhammad ibn Ali 'Alan']], al-Najashi calls him the shaykh of the Imamiyya of Rayy in his time, and he heard [[person/muhammad-ibn-ismail-al-naysaburi|Muhammad ibn Isma'il al-Naysaburi]], transmitter of the traditions and views of [[person/al-fadl-ibn-shadhan|al-Fadl ibn Shadhan]] {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5 p. 400-401}.

## Baghdad and al-Kafi
He moved to [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] probably in the first decade of the 4th century (913-923), living and teaching in Darb al-Silsila near the Bab al-Kufa on the Tigris's west bank; there he completed [[work/al-kafi|al-Kafi]] after twenty years' work — a book arranged by subject in Usul, Furu' and the Kitab al-Rawda, meant as a guide to authoritative Imami doctrine in theology and fiqh {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5 p. 401}. In Ahmad Amin's words, among the Shi'a he is like al-Bukhari among the Sunnis; they call him Thiqat al-Islam, and the book holds sixteen thousand hadiths {cite:duha-islam p. 850, 891}.

## His death
He died at Baghdad in 328/939-40 or 329/940-41, most likely Sha'ban 329/May 941 as [[person/shaykh-al-tusi|al-Tusi]] specifies in the Rijal. The funeral prayer was led by the Hasanid [[person/abu-qirat-muhammad-ibn-jafar|Abu Qirat Muhammad ibn Ja'far]] and he was buried near the Bab al-Kufa {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 5 p. 401}. Tarikh al-Islam lists him under 329: 'he was at Baghdad and died there, and his tomb is visible with a tablet over it'; Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Saymari transmitted from him {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 250}.

The museum classes him among the Twelver Shi'a; al-Dhahabi records his entry in Tarikh al-Islam from his own Sunni standpoint, describing him as one of the heads of the Shi'a and their authoring jurists {cite:tarikh-islam-dhahabi p. 250}.
