## Brief
Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib, the uncle of the Prophet and one of the fiercest horsemen of Quraysh. His Islam strengthened the first community, and his death at Uhud in 625 CE made him, in the eyes of Muslims, the Master of the Martyrs.

## Life
Close to the Prophet in age {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}, he was a hunter and one of the strong men of Mecca. The narrative tells that he embraced Islam around 616 CE out of anger at what he had found done to his nephew. His Islam was public, so that the opponents of the community feared it afterwards {cite:ibn-ishaq-guillaume}.

He migrated to Medina and fought with distinction at [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]]. At [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]], in March 625 CE, he was killed by [[person/wahshi-ibn-harb|Wahshi ibn Harb]], a slave who was freed on account of it {cite:ibn-ishaq-guillaume}. After the battle the dead were mutilated, and Hamza's body was among those so treated. The sources record this in a dry tone, and it stayed in memory {cite:watt-1956-medina}.

## The Encyclopaedia of Islam account
The Encyclopaedia of Islam (second edition) gives a short entry for Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib. It calls him a paternal uncle of the Prophet and the son of Hala bint Wuhayb {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 162}.

The article says he played a part in the negotiations with Khuwaylid ibn Asad, the father of [[person/khadija|Khadija]], over the Prophet's marriage. On his conversion, it says, he became one of the bravest champions of Islam, having earlier been counted among those who opposed the new religion {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 162}.

The article lists several episodes from his Medinan years. He defended the Prophet against the insults of [[person/abu-jahl|Abu Jahl]]. He took part in the action against the Jewish clan of [[group/banu-qaynuqa|Qaynuqa]]. He led an expedition of thirty Emigrants to the sea coast at al-Is; the party met the followers of Abu Jahl, but there was no fighting because Majdi ibn Amr al-Juhani intervened {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 162}. At Badr in 2 AH, 624 CE, the article says, he fought with great courage and distinguished himself in single combat against many of the polytheists {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 162}.

The article dates his death to the following year, at Uhud, where it says he was slain while fighting heroically by the Abyssinian slave Wahshi, who gained his manumission by it. After he fell, it adds, [[person/hind-bint-utba|Hind bint Utba]] mutilated his body and chewed his liver. The author judges this to have been a survival of prehistoric animism; that interpretation is the article's own, and the museum reports it without adopting it {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 162}.


## Legacy
The title that stayed with him in Muslim memory is "Master of the Martyrs" (sayyid al-shuhada), an uncle whose strength stood between his nephew and those who wished him harm.

## For young visitors
Hamza was the Prophet's uncle and his strongest protector. When he died in the battle, the whole community wept for him.


## The Prophet's foster-brother twice over (al-Raheeq al-Makhtum p.55)
[[person/thuwayba|Thuwayba]], freedwoman of Abu Lahab, nursed Hamza before she nursed the Prophet. Hamza was also nursed in [[group/banu-sad-ibn-bakr|Banu Sa'd ibn Bakr]], where his mother there nursed the Prophet one day while the Prophet was with [[person/halima-al-sadiyya|Halima]]. So Hamza was the Prophet's foster-brother in two ways: through Thuwayba and through the woman of Sa'd {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.55} {cite:talqih-fuhum-ahl-al-athr p.4} {cite:zad-al-maad 1/19}. The book counts him the fifth of the ten sons of [[person/abd-al-muttalib|Abd al-Muttalib]] {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.52}.


## The expedition of Sif al-Bahr (al-Raheeq al-Makhtum p.197)
The Prophet put him in command of thirty men of the emigrants who were to intercept a caravan of Quraysh carrying [[person/abu-jahl|Abu Jahl]]. When the two groups met, Majdi ibn Amr al-Juhani came between them, and they did not fight. Hamza's banner was the first banner the Prophet tied ([[event/expedition-of-sif-al-bahr|the expedition of Sif al-Bahr]]) {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.197}.

## For Sprouts
Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib was the Prophet's uncle and the bravest horseman of Quraysh. One day he heard that Abu Jahl had harmed the Prophet, so he answered him and declared his Islam — and his faith strengthened the first Muslims. He fought as a champion at Badr and was martyred at Uhud in 625 CE; Muslims call him the master of martyrs.

## For Explorers
Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib, the Prophet's uncle, was the fiercest man of Quraysh before his Islam. Ibn Hisham tells how he came back from hunting and heard that Abu Jahl had insulted the Prophet, so he struck him with his bow and declared the religion on the spot — and his conversion gave strength to the vulnerable early Muslims. At Badr in 2 AH he fought in the front, and at Uhud in 3 AH (625 CE) Wahshi threw his spear from afar and he was martyred; his body was mutilated in the incident that grieved the Muslims. Muslims call him the Lion of God and the master of martyrs.

## For Scholars
Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim, the Prophet's uncle and foster-brother, was one of Quraysh's hardest men before Islam and among its earliest converts of the elders. The sira tells his conversion: returning from the hunt he heard from a slave woman that Abu Jahl had insulted the Prophet, went to him, struck him with his bow and said 'do you curse him when I am on his religion?' — declaring a faith not yet settled in his heart, which God then made true in the historians' reading. He emigrated to Medina, where the Prophet paired him in brotherhood with Zayd ibn Haritha; at Badr in 2 AH he fought among the champions and received a banner. At Uhud in 3 AH (625 CE) Wahshi ibn Harb — promised freedom by Jubayr ibn Mut'im for Hamza's death — speared him from a distance and he was martyred; Hind bint Utba then mutilated his body in the act the sira treats as its bitterest wound for the Prophet and his companions. For Muslims he is 'the Lion of God and of His messenger' and 'master of the martyrs'; his grave at Uhud is visited today.
