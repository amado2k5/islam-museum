## Overview
Uthman ibn Affan was the third of the Rightly Guided Caliphs (644–656 CE). He married two daughters of the Prophet one after the other, ordered an authoritative codex of the Qur'an, and died under siege in his house in Medina at the hands of rebels.

## Life
Uthman was one of the wealthy men of the [[group/banu-umayya|Banu Umayya]], and he was among the first to become Muslim through [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]]. {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley} He married two daughters of the Prophet one after the other, Ruqayya and then Umm Kulthum, and so he was called “Dhu al-Nurayn” (the Possessor of Two Lights). He emigrated during the [[event/hijra|Hijra]], and he was the envoy of the Muslims to Mecca at [[event/treaty-of-hudaybiyya|Hudaybiyya]]. {cite:ibn-ishaq-guillaume}.

The shura council that Umar appointed chose him, and he ruled for twelve years. During his rule the conquests extended to Armenia and North Africa. In the most lasting part of his work, a committee headed by [[person/zayd-ibn-thabit|Zayd ibn Thabit]] produced the authoritative codex, and copies were sent to the regional capitals. {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}. In his last years, complaints grew about the appointment of his relatives and about his financial policy. In June 656 CE, groups of rebels from Egypt and Iraq besieged him and killed him in Medina, and with this the First Civil War began. {cite:watt-1956-medina}.

## Family, conversion and marriages
The Encyclopaedia of Islam article places Uthman in the great Meccan family of the Banu Umayya, in the branch of his grandfather Abu al-As. It describes him as a very rich merchant and accomplished man of the world, and says that what drew him to a cause of such obscure origin remains a mystery. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1002}

Part of the tradition links his conversion to his marriage to [[person/ruqayya-bint-muhammad|Ruqayya]], the Prophet's daughter; other sources, which the article thinks probably more correct, place the marriage after his conversion. The article calls him the first socially high-ranking convert, says his conversion caused a stir, and adds that his own personal action was never remarkable. Tradition ascribes to him a lively faith and great generosity, and also an indolent temperament, which the article's authors regard as a real trait. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1002}

He is thought to have joined both emigrations to Abyssinia, among them [[event/first-migration-to-abyssinia|the first]], and later the emigrants to Medina. The article says he apparently missed Badr because he was tending his sick wife, and that the Prophet nevertheless counted him present and assigned him a share of the booty. After Ruqayya died, the tie was renewed by his marriage to another daughter, [[person/umm-kulthum-bint-muhammad|Umm Kulthum]]. The article holds that Lammens's doubts about this marriage are not justified, since the early papyrus of Ibn Lahi'a attests it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1002}

## Election by the council
The article says Uthman played an unobtrusive part under the Prophet, Abu Bakr and [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]], which raises the question of why the [[event/shura-council-of-uthman|council]] Umar set up on his deathbed chose him. It notes that Catani analysed the sources minutely, and judges that the secret debate will always elude historical criticism. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1002}

The article then gives its own reading. In its view the strongest candidates cancelled one another out: [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]], whose election would have meant repudiating Umar's policies, and [[person/zubayr-ibn-al-awwam|al-Zubayr]] and [[person/talha-ibn-ubaydullah|Talha]], whom it calls opponents of Umar suspected of ambition. Among [[person/saad-ibn-abi-waqqas|Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas]], [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-awf|Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf]] and Uthman, it supposes that his Umayyad clan, besides his marriage ties to the Prophet, decided the matter. It warns against picturing [[person/abu-sufyan-ibn-harb|Abu Sufyan]] as a hidden director of early politics, and says Umayyad influence rested on the talent of some members more than on noble birth. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1002}

It says Umar's strong personality held opposed factions and the older Companions in balance, and that this was not so under Uthman, who, following Wellhausen and Catani, only continued and developed Umar's policies. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1002} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1003}

## The grievances against him
The article says his bloody end embarrassed the Arab historians, who had to accept the list of grievances and admit that the caliph had sinned against the law, or hold that his accusers, some of them venerated, had lied or erred. It reports that the orthodox tradition escaped the dilemma through the notion of an excusable error and other distinctions, and that the grievances were thereby preserved in detail, for example by Muhibb al-Din al-Tabari and al-Baladhuri. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1003}

The first grievance, perhaps the most serious in the article's words, was the appointment of relatives as governors. [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Muawiya]] long held Syria. At Basra and Kufa Uthman replaced [[person/abu-musa-al-ashari|Abu Musa al-Ash'ari]] and Sa'd with [[person/abd-allah-ibn-amir|Abd Allah ibn Amir]] and his half-brother [[person/al-walid-ibn-uqba|al-Walid ibn Uqba]]; after al-Walid was deposed in a scandal, [[person/said-ibn-al-as|Sa'id ibn al-As]] followed. Egypt, first conquered by [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn al-As]], went to [[person/abdullah-ibn-sad-ibn-abi-sarh|Abd Allah ibn Sa'd ibn Abi Sarh]], who was not an Umayyad. His counsellor was his cousin [[person/marwan-ibn-al-hakam|Marwan ibn al-Hakam]], whose father he had recalled from the exile to which the Prophet had condemned him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1003}

The article concedes a certain nepotism, but asks that his deeper motive be recognised: unity of administration, threatened by the independence of the governors. Umar pursued the same aim and achieved it through his prestige; Uthman expected officials bound to him by blood to achieve it, and did not succeed. The caliph came under his relatives' influence, perhaps less than the official historiography implies. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1003}

## Conquests, booty and finance
The article explains that the diwan system required ever more booty, since the taxes on the [[concept/ahl-al-dhimma|ahl al-dhimma]] could not pay the salaries of new recruits, and that this lay behind the expeditions of the reign: the last provinces of the dynasty whose last king, [[person/yazdegerd-iii|Yazdagird III]], was murdered, the occupation of [[place/armenia|Armenia]], and incursions along North Africa into Nubia and Asia Minor and by sea into the eastern Mediterranean. It judges these conquests less forceful than Umar's, yet the close of the first period of the Arab empire and the prelude to the second. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1003}

A further grievance was that he kept back part of the limited booty for his governors and family and extended the land grants, [[concept/iqta|qata'i]], which Umar had already practised. The article suggests this may also be read as an attempt to create state property, not only to enrich relatives. It calls his plan of ordered administration adequate and in line with Umar's ideal, though he may be reproached for his methods, and adds that the economic crisis forced cuts in military allowances, which swelled the discontented. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1003}

## Opposition in the provinces
The article names among the religious opponents the old Companions of simple or servile origin, [[person/ammar-ibn-yasir|Ammar ibn Yasir]], [[person/abu-dharr-al-ghifari|Abu Dharr]] and [[person/abdullah-ibn-masud|Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud]]. The sentence naming the measure that fed their opposition is cut off by a page break in the scan, so it is not reported. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1003}

At [[place/kufa|Kufa]], in 32 to 33/652 to 654, the readers, the kurra, whose religious character merged with politics according to the article, brought about the deposition of Sa'id ibn al-As; Abu Musa al-Ash'ari, whom it calls a pietist and an adversary of Uthman, replaced him, and Kufa was thereafter beyond the central government's reach. In [[place/misr|Egypt]], Ibn Abi Sarh yielded to a group led by the young [[person/muhammad-ibn-abi-hudhayfa|Muhammad ibn Abi Hudhayfa]]. The article suggests that Amr ibn al-As, dismissed and living in Palestine, secretly supported the movement, and notes that the Ibn Lahi'a papyrus shows the Egyptians besieging the caliph outnumbered the others. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1004}

## The sieges
At the end of 35/656, the article reports, rebel troops of the three provinces advanced on Medina, the Egyptians first, under Abd al-Rahman ibn Udays. The grievances were stated in very harsh terms, but the caliph's humility and conciliatory attitude disarmed them: he consented to their demands and promised to repeal his measures and change his governors, and the Egyptians left satisfied. At al-Arish on the way back, a messenger of Uthman was stopped and a letter taken from him, addressed to Ibn Abi Sarh and ordering that the movement's leaders be killed or mutilated. The Egyptians returned. Uthman denied the letter and suggested his enemies had forged it. The article notes that the official tradition tends to blame Marwan, that other versions exist, and that one, kept only by al-Baladhuri, says Uthman suspected Ali. It adds that forged documents were a common practice and that, whatever one thinks of the episode, other causes were already in motion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1004}

By the papyrus, a second siege followed. Uthman spoke in the presence of Abu Thawr al-Fahmi, in what the article calls the oldest known self-defence of the caliph in its original form. He calls the accusations false, recalls being the fourth to adopt Islam, mentions his oral collection of the Qur'an from the Prophet's lifetime and his marriage to two of the Prophet's daughters, and speaks of his exemplary spiritual life before and after Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1004} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1005} The article says the older Companions, Ali among them, neither joined the violent deposition nor helped him, which its authors judge harshly as a malevolent neutrality; that is their opinion. It reports that [[person/aisha|Aisha]] avoided the issue by going on pilgrimage to Mecca. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1005}

## The killing
Uthman refused to abdicate. After a siege whose length the sources estimate differently, men entered his house in the last days of 35, June 656, led by [[person/muhammad-ibn-abi-bakr|Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr]], who seized him. The article says it is unclear who struck the last blow, that tradition cites several names, and that the affair was obscure from the start. It reports that his blood fell on the copy of the Qur'an he was reading, that his wife Na'ila bint al-Furafisa was wounded, and that he was buried secretly that night by her and a few friends. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1005} For the event as a record, see [[event/murder-of-uthman|the killing of Uthman]] in [[place/medina|Medina]]. Troops Muawiya sent from Syria turned back on hearing the news; the Egyptian killers were later imprisoned by him at Lydda, and their leader Ibn Udays was recaptured and beaten to death. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1005}

## Aftermath and positions
The article says the next election took place in tumult and terror and, with Catani, that no earlier agreement existed among leading Companions. It attributes Ali's election to the support of the Ansar more than to his family ties, and says his regime was attacked from the start by disappointed rivals and by Muawiya, after which the political and soon the religious unity of Islam was over. It judges Uthman's reign and end a turning point that gave him an importance his personality, which it calls mediocre, did not merit; that is the authors' judgement. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1005}

The positions are kept apart. The Sunni position, as the article describes the orthodox tradition, handled the accusers' dispute with Uthman through the idea of an excusable error, while preserving the list of grievances. The academic position is the article's own, weighing the grievances against his aim of administrative unity and his continuity with Umar. The article does not report a Shi'i position, so none is stated here; this record's data carries a separate labelled summary of Twelver Shi'i criticism from another source. No verdict is given. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1003} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 10 p. 1005}

## Legacy
Every Qur'an codex opened today goes back to the script established by his committee. Every argument about the limits of the caliph's duty and the rights of his subjects passes through his killing.

## For young visitors
Uthman gathered the scattered pages of the Qur'an into a single authoritative book, which Muslims read today. His killing began the first civil war in the community.

## In the secret call (The Sealed Nectar p.76)
The book counts him among those who became Muslim through [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] during the years of the [[event/al-dawa-al-sirriyya|secret call]], one of the eight who went before the people and formed the first vanguard and the front line of Islam. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.76} {cite:ibn-hisham-sira 1/245-262}. The book refers to him by his Umayyad lineage. {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.76}.

## For Sprouts
Uthman ibn Affan was the third righteous caliph, known for modesty and generosity, who unified the Qur'an into one standard text.

## For Explorers
Uthman ibn Affan, titled Dhu al-Nurayn, was the third caliph and an early convert to Islam. Renowned for his vast generosity, he outfitted the expedition of Tabuk and bought the well of Rumah as an enduring public endowment. His most significant service as caliph was standardizing the Qur'an into a unified text distributed across the regions.

## For Scholars
Abu Abd Allah Uthman ibn Affan (c. 47 BH – 35 AH / 576–656 CE), titled Dhu al-Nurayn (Possessor of the Two Lights), the third Rightly Guided Caliph (23–35 AH). An early convert who participated in both migrations to Abyssinia and Medina, he was famed for his immense philanthropic endowments, financing the Tabuk campaign, purchasing the well of Rumah for public use, and expanding the Prophet's Mosque. Elected caliph following the council appointed by Umar, his era saw major territorial expansions and the creation of the first Islamic navy. His crowning historical legacy remains the authoritative compilation and distribution of the standard Uthmanic codex of the Qur'an.

## His officials in Khalifa ibn Khayyat
[[person/khalifa-ibn-khayyat|Khalifa ibn Khayyat]] closes his account of this reign by naming its governors and office-holders. They are given here in his order; lines the editor restored from later authors are marked as such. {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 178–180}

- **Mecca:** Ali ibn Adi ibn Rabi'a; then Khalid ibn al-As ibn Hisham al-Makhzumi (in office when Uthman was killed) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 178}
- **Medina:** [[person/zayd-ibn-thabit|Zayd ibn Thabit]] (his deputy when he went on Hajj) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 178}
- **Basra:** [[person/abu-musa-al-ashari|Abu Musa al-Ash'ari]] (confirmed for four years, then dismissed); then [[person/imran-ibn-husayn|Imran ibn Husayn]] (Abu Musa's deputy when he campaigned); then [[person/ziyad-ibn-abi-sufyan|Ziyad]] (sometimes Abu Musa's deputy; later Ibn Amir's deputy); then [[person/abd-allah-ibn-amir|Abd Allah ibn Amir ibn Kurayz]] (took office in 29 AH) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 178}
- **Kufa:** [[person/al-mughira-ibn-shuba|al-Mughira ibn Shu'ba]] (confirmed briefly, then dismissed); then [[person/saad-ibn-abi-waqqas|Sa'd ibn Malik]] (then dismissed); then [[person/al-walid-ibn-uqba|al-Walid ibn Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt]] (then dismissed); then [[person/said-ibn-al-as|Sa'id ibn al-As]] (expelled by the Kufans); then [[person/abu-musa-al-ashari|Abu Musa al-Ash'ari]] (installed by the Kufans and confirmed by Uthman) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 178}
- **Egypt:** [[person/amr-ibn-al-as|Amr ibn al-As]] (dismissed); then [[person/abdullah-ibn-sad-ibn-abi-sarh|Abd Allah ibn Sa'd ibn Abi Sarh]] (until Uthman was killed) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 178}
- **Syria:** [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan]] (confirmed; in (( )) in the edition: restored by the editor from later authors quoting Khalifa) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 178}
- **Khurasan:** [[person/qays-ibn-al-haytham|Qays ibn al-Haytham al-Sulami]] (Ibn Amir's deputy, 33 AH); then [[person/abdallah-ibn-khazim-al-sulami|Abd Allah ibn Khazim al-Sulami]] (killed Qarin; Ibn Amir confirmed him until Uthman was killed; in (( )) in the edition: restored by the editor from later authors quoting Khalifa) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 179}
- **Sijistan:** [[person/al-rabi-ibn-ziyad-al-harithi|al-Rabi' ibn Ziyad al-Harithi]] (appointed by Ibn Amir); then Abd al-Rahman ibn Samura ibn Habib (33 AH) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 180}
- **Sind:** [[person/hakim-ibn-jabala|Hukaym ibn Jabala al-Abdi]] (went to Mukran and came back; Uthman sent no one after him) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 180}
- **Bahrayn:** [[person/abdallah-ibn-sawar|Abd Allah ibn Sawwar al-Abdi]] (sent by Ibn Amir); then [[person/marwan-ibn-al-hakam|Marwan ibn al-Hakam]] (also among his governors there) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 180}
- **Summer campaign:** Yazid ibn al-Hurr al-Absi; then Abd al-Rahman ibn Khalid ibn al-Walid (then dismissed); then Sufyan ibn Awf al-Ghamidi (with Junada ibn Abi Umayya as his deputy at sea); then Abd al-Rahman ibn Khalid ibn al-Walid (again after Sufyan died); then Ubayd Allah ibn Rabah {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 180}
- **Police:** Abd Allah ibn Qunfudh (of Taym; Uthman was the first to appoint a police chief) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 179}
- **Chamberlain:** [[person/humran-ibn-aban|Humran ibn Aban]] {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 179}
- **Treasury:** Abd Allah ibn Arqam (then asked to resign and was released) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 179}
- **Secretary:** [[person/marwan-ibn-al-hakam|Marwan ibn al-Hakam]] {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 179}
- **Judgeship of Basra:** [[person/kab-ibn-suwar|Ka'b ibn Sur]] (later reappointed by Ibn Amir until killed at the Camel); then [[person/abu-musa-al-ashari|Abu Musa al-Ash'ari]] (ordered by Uthman to judge) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 179}
- **Judgeship of Kufa:** [[person/shurayh-al-qadi|Shurayh]] (until Uthman was killed) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 179}
- **Judgeship of Yemen:** [[person/yala-ibn-umayya|Ya'la ibn Umayya]] (over San'a) {cite:tarikh-khalifa-umari-1985 p. 179}
