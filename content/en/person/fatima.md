## Brief
Fatima, the youngest daughter of the Prophet Muhammad by [[person/khadija|Khadija]], the wife of [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]], and the mother of [[person/hasan-ibn-ali|al-Hasan]] and [[person/husayn-ibn-ali|al-Husayn]]. Through her two sons the Prophet's lineage continues.

## Life
She was born in Mecca at some point between about 605 and 615 CE; the sources differ {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}. She is recorded as a girl washing from her father the harm that the opponents in Mecca used to throw on him. She migrated to Medina at the [[event/hijra|hijra]] and married Ali around 624 CE. Their household lived in hardship and patience {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

After the Prophet's death in June 632 CE, the early sources record her grief and her dispute with the new leadership over an inheritance she claimed from her father's estate at Fadak. She died a few months after him, between three and six months according to differing reports, and was buried in Medina in an unmarked grave, as the tradition relates {cite:madelung-1997}.

## Fatima in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The article on Fatima in the Encyclopaedia of Islam, second edition, calls her the only one of the Prophet's daughters to enjoy great renown, and says all Muslims hold her in great veneration. It suggests possible reasons: she lived closest to her father and outlived his other children, she gave him numerous descendants while the lines of his other children were short or died out, the standing of her husband and sons was reflected on her, and with time extraordinary qualities were attributed to her. It adds that it was above all the Shi'a who surrounded her with a halo of beliefs, some centuries after her death, and that she stayed on the margin of the great events of early Islam, so the historical sources give her little attention. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 879}

Two European orientalists are reported as holding opposite judgements. Henri Lammens drew a gloomy portrait of a colourless, sickly and little-esteemed woman, a thesis criticised by G. Levi Della Vida and C. H. Becker. Louis Massignon raised her to a sublime position comparable to that of the Virgin Mary among Christians, seeing in her the hostess of the Prophet's freedmen and of non-Arab converts, a figure of the beginnings of universal Islam. The article says Massignon's reading offers a psychological and religious explanation of how the legend arose, but that a historian will object that it subordinates the facts to beliefs which appeared only later. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 879} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 880}

For the historical Fatima the article draws on sources of the second/eighth and third/ninth centuries and the first half of the fourth/tenth, chiefly al-Baladhuri, Ibn Sa'd and the hadith collections Sunnis regard as canonical. It leaves aside later works, and remarks that Ibn Hisham and the other historians had little occasion to treat her, so obscure was the life she led. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 880}

## Birth, childhood and the move to Medina
The date of her birth is uncertain. The year the article calls most probable is that of the rebuilding of the Kaaba, five years before the start of the Prophet's mission, which would make her over eighteen at marriage; a later birth, reported by al-Ya'qubi, would mean [[person/khadija|Khadija]] was over fifty. Her place among the daughters is also not wholly settled; they are generally listed as [[person/zaynab-bint-muhammad|Zaynab]], [[person/ruqayya-bint-muhammad|Ruqayya]], [[person/umm-kulthum-bint-muhammad|Umm Kulthum]] and Fatima. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 880}

Only two episodes of her Meccan years are related. She was overcome with grief at her mother's death, and her father consoled her with the report that Jibril had told him God had built for Khadija a pavilion of pearl in Paradise. And she removed the refuse that [[person/uqba-ibn-abi-muayt|Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt]], one of the Quraysh most hostile to the Prophet, had thrown over him while he prayed, cursing the offender in her indignation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 880}

After the hijra, in one version, the Prophet sent his adopted son [[person/zayd-ibn-haritha|Zayd ibn Haritha]] and Abu Rafi' with two camels and money to bring his daughters Fatima and Umm Kulthum and his wife [[person/sawda-bint-zama|Sawda bint Zam'a]] from Mecca to Medina. A different version has [[person/abbas-ibn-abd-al-muttalib|al-Abbas]] escorting them, and says al-Huwayrith ibn Nukayz ibn Wahb prodded their camels and threw the women to the ground, for which he is said to have been killed after the occupation of Mecca. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 880}

## Betrothal and marriage to Ali
The sources give much information on the betrothal but do not wholly agree. [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] and [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]] both asked for her hand, and the Prophet refused, saying he awaited the moment fixed by destiny, according to Ibn Sa'd. Ali did not dare to propose because of his poverty, and the Prophet eased his task by reminding him of a breastplate he owned. Selling it, with some other objects and a camel or a ewe, raised about 480 dirhams for the bridal gift, which on the Prophet's advice went partly on perfumes and partly on household needs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 880}

On Fatima's response the reports differ. Ibn Sa'd says she was silent and the Prophet took this as consent; other sources say she protested and was consoled with the words that he had married her to the most learned and wise member of the family, the first to embrace Islam. The year and month of the marriage are disputed: the first or second year after the hijra, more likely the second, with consummation postponed in some accounts for days or months, or until after Ali's return from [[event/battle-of-badr|Badr]]. A feast was prepared, the [[group/ansar|Ansar]] contributed grain and Ali slaughtered a sheep, while [[person/aisha|Aisha]] and [[person/umm-salama|Umm Salama]], wives of the Prophet, arranged the house. It is said that Ali was twenty-five and Fatima between fifteen and twenty-one. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 880} The sources also describe a rite of the wedding night in which the Prophet washed his hands in a jar of water, sprinkled it on Ali and Fatima and invoked God's blessing on them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 881}

## The household and the children
The article reports that the household was very poor. Their covering was an old piece of striped Yemeni cloth too small for both feet and head, their pillow leather stuffed with palm fibre, and their trousseau a water skin, a sieve, a duster and a cup. Having no servant, Fatima ground the grain herself and got blisters, while Ali drew water for wages and complained of pains in the chest. When the Prophet received some captives and a servant was requested, he refused, saying he could not let the poor of the ahl al-suffa go hungry. He later visited the couple and taught them short formulas of praise to repeat each night. The article would limit this poverty to the first years of the marriage, since conditions improved after Khaybar, when they received shares of its produce. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 881}

Of the children, [[person/hasan-ibn-ali|al-Hasan]] was born in 2هـ/624م or 3هـ/625م, in Ramadan, and [[person/husayn-ibn-ali|al-Husayn]] was conceived fifty days later and born in 4هـ/626م, in the first days of Sha'ban. A third son, Muhassin or Muhsin, was stillborn. There were also two daughters, named Umm Kulthum and Zaynab after their aunts. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 881}

## Disputes in the household
The article says Ali and Fatima did not always live in harmony, that Ali at times treated her harshly and that she went to complain to her father, and it describes hadiths showing the Prophet intervening. The most serious dispute arose when the Banu Hisham ibn al-Mughira suggested that Ali marry one of their women. The Prophet defended his daughter, and the reports give his words in several forms, among them that she is a part of him and that whoever offends her offends him. Ali seems also to have been asking for a daughter of [[person/abu-jahl|Abu Jahl]]; the Prophet spoke against this from the pulpit and added that Ali must first divorce Fatima if he wished to proceed. Some authors have concluded that monogamy was among her particular privileges. One explanation of Ali's name Abu Turab, "father of dust", connects it with these quarrels: rather than answer his wife in anger, Ali would go out and put dust on his head. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 881}

## Events of the Prophet's lifetime
The article lists what it can gather of her part in public events. After [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]] she tended the Prophet's wounds, cleaned the bloodstained swords, and made it her custom to pray at the graves of those killed there. Abu Sufyan, foreseeing the occupation of Mecca, sought her and Ali's help with the Prophet. She received a share of the produce of [[place/khaybar|Khaybar]]. At the occupation of Mecca Abu Sufyan begged her for protection and she refused, and refused to let her child grant it, the Prophet having forbidden this; in 10هـ/632م she made the umra. With her husband and sons she took an important part in the [[concept/mubahala|mubahala]], an episode with strong repercussions among the Shi'a. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 881}

## Fatima and the people of the house
A verse in sura 33 is addressed to the People of the House, the [[group/ahl-al-bayt|ahl al-bayt]]. The verses before it address the Prophet's wives in the feminine plural while this one uses the masculine plural, from which it has been argued that it concerns more than the wives. The article says the privilege led many relatives, and even groups such as the Ansar or the whole community, to claim a place among the ahl al-bayt. It then reports a story, found in many traditions, in which the Prophet gathered under his cloak his grandsons, his daughter and his son-in-law, so that these five are called the ahl al-kisa, the people of the mantle. Efforts have been made to include the wives, but generally the number is limited to the five. According to the Shi'a without exception, and also to pro-Alid Sunnis, the ahl al-bayt are identical with the people of the mantle. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 881} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 882}

## The Prophet's last illness
Fatima loved her father greatly and wept during his illness. Aisha relates in many hadiths that she saw Fatima weep when her father spoke to her in secret and then smile. After his death Fatima explained that he had told her that Jibril brought him the Qur'an once a year but had come twice that year, from which he inferred that his end was near, and that she would be the first of his family to join him. She wept, and he asked whether she was not pleased to be the sayyida of the women of this people; the hadiths give variants such as the women of the believers, of the world or of Paradise. She smiled. The article notes that this story was greatly developed in Shi'i tradition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 882}

## After the Prophet's death: succession and inheritance
The article describes Fatima as a woman who had never taken part in political matters and was drawn indirectly into the events after her father's death. The accounts below are those it cites, each with the work that reports it, and the museum rules on none of them.

**Reports of the pledge of allegiance.** According to al-Ya'qubi, after Abu Bakr's election he went with some companions to Fatima's house, where some Ansar and supporters of Ali had gathered. Ali came out with his sword drawn; Fatima, once her husband had been disarmed and the party was about to enter, cried out and threatened to uncover her hair, so that Abu Bakr withdrew. Al-Baladhuri has her seeing a firebrand in Umar's hand and asking whether he meant to burn her door. The book al-Imama wa'l-siyasa, whose attribution to Ibn Qutayba the article considers wrong though the work is early, gives a fuller version in which Umar had wood brought and threatened to burn the house even with Fatima in it. In it those inside came out and gave their pledge except Ali, Fatima reproached them for deciding without consulting the family, and when they returned she refused them entry until Ali let them in. The same book says she rode with Ali to the Ansar's meeting places seeking support and was told they were already committed to Abu Bakr. The article's author judges that these episodes, even if enlarged with invented detail, rest on fact, and that for the Shi'a they add a further reason for hostility towards Umar. The article gives no Sunni assessment of these reports, and the museum supplies none. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 882}

**The estate, as Sunni tradition has it.** After her father's death Fatima asked Abu Bakr for the possessions he was holding. The dispute concerned the land of [[place/fadak|Fadak]] and her share of Khaybar. Abu Bakr refused, saying he had heard the Prophet say that he had no heirs and that all he left would be sadaqa. The article adds that Muslims, finding the dispute disagreeable, have tried to lessen its gravity, saying for example that Fatima meant to give the revenues to the poor, and they like to depict Abu Bakr as grieved to refuse the Prophet's daughter but bound by the Prophet's own conduct. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 882} See also the record of the [[event/fatima-inheritance-claim-fadak|claim to Fadak]].

**The estate, as Shi'i tradition has it.** According to the article the Shi'a do not forgive the caliph for not believing Fatima, who held that Fadak had been given to her by her father, and have argued the matter for centuries. Shi'i sources, it adds, say she meant the revenue for the freed clients as well as the poor. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 882}

**The academic reading.** The article says it is unclear whether the property the convert Mukhayriq had given the Prophet was in dispute, and thinks probably not. It is also unclear whether the claim came from Fatima alone or with al-Abbas; from many hadiths the author infers two attempts, the first probably by both and the second by Fatima alone, on different arguments. The author describes Fatima as yielding, as she had to, to the head of state, but unwillingly, and as refusing to accept the validity of Abu Bakr's decision. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 882} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 883}

## Illness, death and burial
Fatima fell ill soon after her father's death. According to some reports she was reconciled during her illness with Abu Bakr, who had asked to visit her, but according to the majority she remained angry to the end. A frequently repeated story says she prepared for death by washing and putting on coarse garments, and charged her sister-in-law [[person/asma-bint-umays|Asma' bint Umays]], widow of Ja'far ibn Abi Talib, that no one should uncover her body. Asma' prepared a bier for her, with which she was content. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 882} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 883} Other accounts conflict with this calm picture: al-Ya'qubi has her rebuking the Prophet's wives and the Quraysh women who visited, and al-Tabari says her wish to hide her form came from shame at her extreme thinness. Reports differ on whether Ali washed the body or she asked him to, and the article finds it difficult, if not impossible, to choose among them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 883}

She died in the year 11, though the month is doubtful, the commonest report being six months after the Prophet. Her death was kept secret and she was buried at night; in most versions neither Abu Bakr nor Umar was informed, though some accounts say Abu Bakr performed the prayer at her grave. Nearly all sources place the burial in [[place/al-baqi|al-Baqi]], some near the Masjid Rukayya at the corner of the house of Aqil, Ali's brother, while others say the exact spot was soon lost. Al-Mas'udi alone reports a tomb with an inscription naming her and three Alids, and al-Muqaddasi lists her tomb among places of disagreement, noting she might have been buried in the room. Today Shi'i pilgrims visit three places: her house, al-Baqi, and the space in the Great Mosque between the rawda and the Prophet's tomb. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 883}

## Name, appearance and character in the early sources
She bore an unusual kunya, Umm Abiha, "mother of her father". The article suspects a Shi'i origin because it appears only in later sources such as the Usd al-ghaba, and notes an Imami explanation that she learned by revelation that her last descendant would be named Muhammad. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 883}

On her appearance the sources are silent, except that they report she resembled the Prophet in her gait. The article argues against Lammens that she cannot have been weak and sickly, since she bore five children, did arduous household work and made two journeys to Mecca. It finds her character hard to assess because accounts contradict one another, but judges that she did her tasks diligently and patiently and liked to help others. The Prophet's wives used her as a spokeswoman over his preference for Aisha, but when he asked whether she loved what he loved, she answered that she loved Aisha too, and the wives had to find another advocate. In the article's view her bold support for her husband against Abu Bakr rules out timidity, while she was meek and submissive towards the Prophet and it was she who prevented Ali from taking a second wife. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 883}

## Fatima in Shi'i tradition and legend
The article states that no systematic study of the Fatima of legend exists and limits itself to three early Shi'i works: the Dala'il al-imama of Ibn Rustam al-Tabari, a fourth/tenth-century author; a work begun in 448هـ/1056م by Husayn ibn Abd al-Wahhab; and the Manaqib Al Abi Talib of Ibn Shahrashub, who died in 588هـ/1192م. What follows is Shi'i tradition as the article summarises it, not the museum's account of her life. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 883}

In these works her birth is accompanied by wonders: women from Paradise assist Khadija, light spreads over the earth, which explains the title al-Zahra', and she speaks at birth. Her marriage is said to have been concluded first in heaven, with the bridal gift and trousseau described in detail, and a book is said to have come to her from heaven after her father's death, passing later to Ali and the imams. She is said to be the first to enter Paradise after the Resurrection, where she asks God for justice for those guilty of the deaths of her sons. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 884} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 885}

These works also give her many names, among them al-Siddiqa, al-Tahira, al-Muhaddatha, al-Batul and al-Sayyida, compare her with Mary, and call her the sayyida par excellence of the women of Paradise. The article adds that Shi'i exegetes read a number of Qur'anic passages, among them in suras 3 and 55, as allusions to Ali and Fatima. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 885} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 886}

Episodes of her legend appear in the Persian ta'ziya passion plays, such as her trousseau, the dispute over Fadak, her last illness and death, and her visit to al-Husayn's camp on the tenth of Muharram. Among the Shi'a the only canonical feast in her honour is that of the mubahala, on 21, 24 or 25 Dhu'l-Hijja, while other feasts are held privately. The article observes that among the Imamis the Fatima of legend almost always keeps links with the Fatima of history, whereas among the Isma'ilis and some other groups other beliefs arose, with a greater assimilation of themes of Christian devotion to Mary. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 886} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 887}

## What the article leaves for further study
The article ends by listing gaps: the references to Fatima in Shi'i hadith collections such as al-Kulayni's, al-Majlisi's compilation, Isma'ili and Fatimid-era ideas, Persian lithographs and Sufi anecdotes all still need study. Its author expects that in most cases the themes will prove to connect with details preserved in hadiths and with genuinely Islamic ideas, even where they have grown into wholly fantastic stories. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 2 p. 887}

## Legacy
Every lineage traced back to the Prophet, including the sharifs and honoured notables across the Islamic world, goes back to Fatima and Ali. Her name is borne by towns, mosques, a whole Fatimid dynasty and millions of women.

## For young visitors
Fatima was the daughter of the Prophet. Her two sons were the Prophet's only grandchildren who grew up, so everyone who today says he is a descendant of the Prophet is a descendant of hers.

## On the marriage to Khadija (The Sealed Nectar p. 61)
The Sealed Nectar, in its chapter on the marriage, says that Fatima was among the children of [[person/khadija|Khadija]], the fifth in order after al-Qasim, Zaynab, Ruqayya and Umm Kulthum. All of the Prophet's daughters died during his lifetime except Fatima, who came after him six months later and then joined him {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p. 61} {cite:ibn-hisham-sira 1/190-191} {cite:fiqh-al-sira-al-ghazali p. 60}.

## For Sprouts
Fatima bint Muhammad is the daughter of the Prophet and Khadija; she married Ali ibn Abi Talib and their children were Hasan, Husayn, Zaynab and Umm Kulthum. The Prophet loved her dearly and she resembled him in simple living, and the hadiths tell of her standing with him.

## For Explorers
Fatima al-Zahra, daughter of the Prophet Muhammad and Khadija bint Khuwaylid, was his youngest daughter and the only one to outlive him. She married Ali ibn Abi Talib after Badr, and their children were Hasan, Husayn, Zaynab and Umm Kulthum; through her children spread the line of the Prophet's household across the Muslim world. She kept a house of ascetic work; the sira books record her mourning her brother, serving her house, and dying a few months after the Prophet in 11 AH. The Prophet called her 'the mother of her father' and spoke of her standing, and accounts differ on her age at death.

## For Scholars
Fatima, daughter of the Prophet Muhammad and Khadija bint Khuwaylid, his youngest daughter on the common account and the only one to survive him, is called al-Zahra, al-Batul and 'the mother of her father'. She married Ali ibn Abi Talib after Badr; their children Hasan, Husayn, Zaynab and Umm Kulthum spread the household's line through the Muslim world. The sira and hadith books record her household of ascetic work, her hand marked by the millstone, and accounts of nearness to the Prophet that most exegetes place within the people of the house in the purification verse, and the mubahala hadith counts her in many readings. She died about six months after her father in 11 AH, with her age reported variously between eighteen and twenty-eight or more. The sources also tie her to the succession debates of Abu Bakr's caliphate — Fadak and the inheritance question — where jurists and historians differ by school: Sunni writing frames her within love of the Prophet's household, while Shia traditions make her central to larger doctrines.

## From Omar Abdelkafy's series on the Hikam of Ibn Ata Allah
Omar Abdelkafy relates that Fatima asked her father for a servant, and the Prophet directed her and Ali instead to glorify, praise and magnify God thirty-three times each at bedtime. {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep04 00:53:00} {cite:assabile-omar-abdelkafy sharh-al-hekam-al-ataeya/ep04 00:53:30}

## In Mosaad Anwar's series 'Those Given Glad Tidings of Paradise'
In episodes 7 and 8 of [[work/the-ten-promised-paradise-mosaad-anwar|'Those Given Glad Tidings of Paradise']] [[person/mosaad-anwar|Mosaad Anwar]] tells the life of Fatima, whom he calls "the sweet basil of the Chosen One", presenting her as raised in the best house in Mecca after the Ka'ba {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:01:00}. He says her mother [[person/khadija|Khadija]] was the first to accept Islam, the first to pray behind the Prophet and the first woman into whose grave the Prophet descended {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:01:30}; that her husband was [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali ibn Abi Talib]], and among her children were [[person/hasan-ibn-ali|al-Hasan]] and [[person/husayn-ibn-ali|al-Husayn]], whom he calls the two masters of the youth of Paradise {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:02:00}; and he gives her the titles mistress of the women of Paradise and mother of al-Hasan and al-Husayn {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:02:30}. In his account she was born in Mecca five years before the mission, while Quraysh were rebuilding the Ka'ba and the Prophet was thirty-five {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:02:30}, and she was the Prophet's youngest daughter and the child who most resembled him {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:03:00}. He holds that she grew up between two noble parents, learning character and magnanimity from them, and was brave and strong-willed {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:04:00}.

For her courage he cites a hadith he relates from Sahih al-Bukhari through [[person/abdullah-ibn-masud|Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud]]: the Prophet was praying at the Ka'ba while [[person/abu-jahl|Abu Jahl]] and his companions sat by; Abu Jahl asked who would bring the afterbirth of a slaughtered camel, and [[person/uqba-ibn-abi-muayt|Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt]] got up and laid it between his shoulders as he prostrated, while Ibn Mas'ud watched helpless, wishing he had protection, and they laughed {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:04:30}; he glosses the word as the afterbirth with its blood and filth {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:05:00}. Fatima came, threw the filth off her father's back and turned on the men reproaching them, which he says is the wording of the hadith; the Prophet then invoked God against Quraysh, which distressed them, since they believed a prayer made in that town was answered {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:06:30}. In his invocation he named Abu Jahl, [[person/utba-ibn-rabia|Utba]] and Shayba the sons of Rabi'a, al-Walid ibn Utba, [[person/umayya-ibn-khalaf|Umayya ibn Khalaf]] and Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt, and Ibn Mas'ud said he saw them lying dead in the well of Badr {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:07:00}. He then recalls the three-year siege of the Muslims in the [[place/shib-abi-talib|ravine of Abu Talib]] {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:08:00}; Khadija came out of it worn down by illness and died soon after, young Fatima grieved, and the Prophet grieved so deeply that some called that year the [[event/year-of-sorrow|Year of Sorrow]] {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:09:00}. The Prophet surrounded her with tenderness to make up for her mother, she cared for him in turn, and he used to say she was "her father's mother" {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:09:30}.

In his account she emigrated to Medina after the Prophet with some of her sisters, glad to be reunited with her father {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:10:00}; she rejoiced at the victory of Badr, and at [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]] she went out carrying a waterskin for the thirsty, dressing the wounded and cooking for the troops {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:10:30}. He describes the Prophet's wounds that day: Utba ibn Abi Waqqas struck his head with a stone, his tooth was broken, the helmet was smashed on his head, [[person/amr-ibn-qamia|Ibn Qami'a]] struck his shoulder with a sword so that he suffered from it for a month, and two rings of the helmet sank into his cheeks {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:11:30}. Ali poured water while Fatima washed his face, and when the bleeding would not stop she burnt a piece of matting and laid its ash on the wound, and the blood stopped {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:12:30}. He says she later took part in the campaigns of [[event/battle-of-the-trench|the Confederates]], [[event/battle-of-khaybar|Khaybar]] and [[event/conquest-of-mecca|the conquest of Mecca]] {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:12:30}.

He relates that the Prophet married her to Ali after Badr {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:13:00}, and that her dowry, as he states it, was a Hutami coat of mail worth no more than two dirhams {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:13:30}; he draws the lesson that the Prophet chose a man, not wealth, for his daughter, and that a woman's blessing lies in an easy engagement and a light dowry {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:14:30}. He describes her house as built of mud and relates Ali saying that they had no bedding but a ram's skin, on which they slept at night and fed the water camel by day, and no servant but her {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:15:00}; the Prophet furnished her with a plush cloth, a pillow stuffed with palm fibre, two hand-mills, a waterskin and two jars, which he values today at three or four hundred Egyptian pounds {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:16:00}. He says Banu Abd al-Muttalib celebrated the marriage and [[person/hamza|Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib]] slaughtered some of his camels for the feast {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:19:00}, and that the Prophet told Ali not to approach her until he came; he then came in, called for a vessel of water, recited over it and wiped Ali's chest and face with it, called Fatima, who rose stumbling from shyness, sprinkled her with it, and told her he had married her to the dearest of his family to him {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:19:30} ([[event/marriage-of-fatima-and-ali|the marriage of Fatima and Ali]]).

He relates from Ali that she ground with the hand-mill until it marked her hand, carried water until the strap marked her chest, swept the house until her clothes were dusty and lit the fire under the pot until her clothes were soiled {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:18:00}. Ali suggested she ask for a servant from captives brought to the Prophet; she went, greeted him and was too shy to ask, and Ali then went and told him of her hardship {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:21:00}. The Prophet refused to give them one while the people of the Suffa writhed with hunger, saying he would sell the captives and spend the price on the poor Muslims, which the preacher reads as his integrity with the community's wealth {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:21:30}. He then came to them at night as they lay under a blanket that left their feet bare when it covered their heads, told them to stay where they were, and taught them something better than a servant, words Gabriel had taught him {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:22:30}: glorifying, praising and magnifying God ten times each after every prayer and thirty-three times each at bedtime; Ali said he never once abandoned them {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:23:30} ([[event/fatima-asks-for-servant-and-tasbih|the request for a servant]]). He advises anyone worn out to try a hundred glorifications {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep07 00:24:00}.

In episode 8 he holds that no household is free of disagreement, Fatima's included, but theirs were few and quickly settled because they referred to revelation {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep08 00:02:00}. He relates a hadith he attributes to Muslim, "if memory serves": the Prophet came to her house and did not find Ali, and she told him Ali had left angry after words between them and had not taken his midday rest there {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep08 00:02:30}; Ali was found asleep in the mosque, his cloak slipped from his side and dust on him, and the Prophet woke him playfully, calling him "Abu Turab" (father of dust), took him by the hand and led him home {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep08 00:03:30}. The lesson he draws is that the Prophet asked for no details and gave no reproach, and he criticises parents who intrude in their married children's homes {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep08 00:04:30}. He stresses that Ali loved her and was protective of her, and relates that finding her cleaning her teeth with a twig of arak he addressed two verses of love poetry to the twig {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep08 00:13:00}.

He says she was given glad tidings of Paradise more than once, among them the report of Ibn Abbas, which he attributes to Ahmad and says al-Hakim graded sound with al-Dhahabi agreeing, that the best women of Paradise are Khadija, Fatima, [[person/maryam|Maryam]] and [[person/asiya|Asiya]] {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep08 00:14:30}. He cites [[person/aisha|Aisha]], in a report he attributes to Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi with al-Hakim's grading, as saying she saw no one closer to the Prophet in speech than Fatima, and that he would rise and kiss her when she came in, and she did the same for him {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep08 00:15:00}. From al-Bukhari, through Aisha, he relates that during his final illness Fatima came walking just as he walked; he seated her on his right and whispered to her, and she wept; he whispered again, and she laughed; and she would not reveal the secret while he lived {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep08 00:16:00}. After his death she said that the first secret was that Gabriel used to review the Qur'an with him once a year and had done so twice that year, which he took as a sign that his end was near, and the second that she would be the leading lady of women and the first of his family to follow him {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep08 00:17:30}. When the illness grew severe she cried out at her father's distress, and he said there would be no distress for her father after that day, which the preacher explains as passing from the narrowness of this world to the breadth of the next {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep08 00:18:30}; after the burial she asked [[person/anas-ibn-malik|Anas]] how they could bear to pour dust on God's Messenger {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep08 00:19:00}.

He says she lived only six months after the Prophet {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep08 00:18:00}, the same span The Sealed Nectar gives {cite:mubarakpuri-raheeq p.61}, and dates her death, by his statement, to Tuesday 3 Ramadan in the year 11 AH {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep08 00:22:00}. Ali washed and shrouded her, from which he infers that a spouse may wash the other; her uncle [[person/abbas-ibn-abd-al-muttalib|al-Abbas ibn Abd al-Muttalib]] led the prayer over her, and her bier went out at night to [[place/baqi-al-gharqad|al-Baqi']], where she was buried beside her sisters [[person/zaynab-bint-muhammad|Zaynab]], [[person/ruqayya-bint-muhammad|Ruqayya]] and [[person/umm-kulthum-bint-muhammad|Umm Kulthum]] {cite:assabile-mosaad-anwar the-ten-promised-paradise/ep08 00:22:30} ([[event/death-of-fatima-bint-muhammad|her death and burial]]). He also discusses Ali's proposal to the daughter of Abu Jahl, and her request to Abu Bakr for her inheritance and what passed between them afterwards, which is not written here.

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "Immortal Women"
Fatima is the main subject of the seventh episode of [[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]]'s programme [[work/nesaa-khalidat-program|"Immortal Women" (Nisa' Khalidat)]]; the archive's seventh file stops mid-lecture, and the ninth file carries the whole recording. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep09 00:01:00} He counts her among the four perfect women and says she was born five years before the mission, the youngest of the Prophet's daughters and the one most like him in looks. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:11:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:12:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:12:30} She emigrated soon after the Prophet; Abu Bakr and then Umar proposed and were gently refused, while Ali, who wished to marry her, had nothing. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep09 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:13:30} The Prophet asked Ali for the armour he had taken at Badr as her dower. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:14:30} Their children were al-Hasan, al-Husayn, Zaynab and Umm Kulthum, and he notes that the Prophet's line continued almost only through her. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep09 00:16:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:16:30}

He dwells on the bond between father and daughter: back from a journey the Prophet went first to the mosque, then to Fatima; each rose and kissed the other; and he carried her sons in his arms, even coming down from the pulpit for them. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:17:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:19:00} He sees in the way she raised al-Hasan and al-Husayn the model of a mother forming leaders. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:20:30} Her life was hard: she worked until her hands were sore and had no servant, and when she went to ask for one the Prophet taught her and Ali to praise, glorify and magnify God thirty-three times each at bedtime instead. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:21:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep09 00:21:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep09 00:21:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep09 00:22:00} He says the Prophet gave her a servant later, when wealth came. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep09 00:22:30}

In the Prophet's last illness he whispered to her twice: she wept at the first, which told her of his coming death, and laughed at the second, which told her she would be the first of his family to join him; she kept the secret until he died. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep09 00:24:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep09 00:25:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep09 00:25:30} He recalls the Prophet gathering Fatima, Ali, al-Hasan and al-Husayn and praying for them, and his words that she was a part of him. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep09 00:26:30} He says she died six months after the Prophet, at twenty-eight, and closes with lines in her praise by the poet Muhammad Iqbal, recited in an Arabic verse translation. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep09 00:27:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep09 00:27:30}
