## Summary
Abd al-Rahman ibn Yusuf, known as Ibn al-Sa'igh, was a well-known calligrapher of the Mamluk period who died in 845/1441–2. He wrote a treatise on calligraphy and copied several Qur'an manuscripts in different scripts, among them one in naskh dated Shawwal 813/January–February 1411 for the library of Faris al-Saqi al-Khazindar, an official of Sultan Faraj ibn Barquq, and another in Cairo dated 814/1411–12. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 363} {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 365}

## The great Qur'an
His best-known work is a single-volume Qur'an transcribed in muhaqqaq in 801/1398–9 for Muhammad ibn Batut al-Salihi al-Dimishqi, whose colophon says it was written "with one pen in sixty days". It is the largest Qur'an known to have been made for the Mamluks, on sheets over a metre high, slightly larger than full-baghdadi size, with eleven lines of large [[concept/muhaqqaq-script|muhaqqaq]] per page and sura titles in [[concept/thuluth-script|thuluth]] rather than the stylised kufic usual at the time. Like its Timurid contemporary it attests to the triumph of calligraphy over illumination: the main ornaments are gold circles between the verses. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 363} {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 364} {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 365}

## Judging his hand
[[person/sheila-blair|Blair]] compares it with the even larger contemporary Timurid Qur'an and finds the anonymous Timurid hand smoother and more fluid, Ibn al-Sa'igh's bumpier and looser: his verticals sometimes wiggle or taper, some strokes do not connect, his vocalisation is larger and heavier, his serifs rounder. She explains the difference in size by paper technology: full-baghdadi sheets were the limit of traditional dipped moulds, which give paper smooth enough to write on both sides, whereas the Timurids probably used floating moulds for their enormous sheets. {cite:blair-islamic-calligraphy-2006 p. 365}
