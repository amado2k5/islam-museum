## Summary
Malalai of Maiwand (1861 – 27 July 1880), also called Malala and Malalai Anna, is the national folk heroine of Afghanistan, said to have rallied the Afghan fighters at the Battle of Maiwand during the Second Anglo-Afghan War and to have been killed there at the age of eighteen or nineteen. {cite:wikipedia-malalai-of-maiwand-en}

This museum presents her as the sources present her: her story is Afghan national tradition, told in school textbooks, and many scholars doubt that she was a historical person at all. Her dates in the data file therefore carry legend status. {cite:wikipedia-malalai-of-maiwand-en}

## The story as told
She was born in 1861 at Khig, a village a few miles south-west of Maiwand in Kandahar province, the daughter of a shepherd; her father and her fiancé were among the thousands who went to fight the British. At Maiwand she came onto the field with the other women to tend the wounded and carry water. When the Afghan line wavered and the standard-bearer fell, she raised her white veil — in other tellings, the fallen standard itself — and sang the landai couplets for which she is remembered, shaming the fighters back into the battle; she was killed in the fighting, and the Afghan army under Mohammad Ayub Khan won the day. {cite:wikipedia-malalai-of-maiwand-en} {cite:wikipedia-malalai-of-maiwand-ar}

Her grave at Khig, at Karez, is venerated locally as a shrine, and schools, hospitals and institutions across Afghanistan bear her name; the Pakistani activist Malala Yousafzai was named after her. In the West she is called the Afghan Joan of Arc. {cite:wikipedia-malalai-of-maiwand-en}

## The historicity question
The English article states the problem plainly: her authenticity is questioned by many scholars, and she seems to be more a figure of folklore than of historical reality. No contemporary British historian or private record of the battle mentions her; local records of the battle are missing; her name first appears in print only in the 1920s to 1940s, and some hold that the writer Abdul Hai Habibi coined the story in those decades; the standard history of Afghanistan by Farhang does not mention her. The couplet she is said to have sung may itself be older or younger than the battle. {cite:wikipedia-malalai-of-maiwand-en}

The museum accordingly records the tradition without asserting the person: what is historically certain is the battle, its date and its Afghan victory — and the power her story has held for Afghanistan ever since. {cite:wikipedia-malalai-of-maiwand-en}
