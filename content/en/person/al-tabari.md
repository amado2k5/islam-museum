## Overview
Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Jarir al-Tabari al-Amuli, born in Amul in 224 AH and died in Baghdad in 310 AH. Ibn al-Nadim counts him among those skilled in the sciences of the Qur'an, grammar, poetry, language and jurisprudence. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.326-327}

## His learning
He took hadith from shaykhs including Muhammad ibn Humayd al-Razi, Abu Kurayb and Hannad ibn al-Sari. He read jurisprudence under Dawud ibn Ali, took the jurisprudence of al-Shafi'i from al-Rabi' ibn Sulayman in Egypt, and the jurisprudence of Malik from Yunus ibn Abd al-A'la. {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.326-327}

## His works
He had a jurisprudential school that he chose for himself. Among his books are "al-Tarikh", which he dictated up to 302 AH, "al-Tafsir", "al-Qira'at", "Tahdhib al-Athar", and "Ikhtilaf al-Fuqaha'". {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p.326-327}

## His death and the dispute about it
Ibn al-Athir says that he was buried at night in his house, because the common people gathered and prevented his burial by day. He says that some Hanbalis were fanatical against him, and the cause was his book on the disagreements of the jurists, in which he did not mention Ahmad ibn Hanbal, and his saying that he was a hadith scholar and not a jurist. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p.99-100}

Ibn Khuzayma, on reading his commentary, said: "I know of no one on the face of the earth more learned than Abu Ja'far." Al-Khatib said he was one of the imams of the scholars, and that rulings were referred to his word. {cite:kamil-ibn-al-athir p.99-100}

## In the 'Al-Mubdi'un' programme (part 1 of 2)
Tareq al-Suwaidan dedicates two episodes to al-Tabari, presenting him as Persian-born yet master of Arabic until he wrote the greatest of exegeses, living 224–310 AH in an age of active codification when the six books were gathered {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:02:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:10:00}.
He pauses on a wider observation: our history was written politically and militarily while civilizational history lies fragmented; political stability usually pairs with civilizational output though not always — and the great mosques like the Umayyad and Baghdad's served as universities through their corner circles {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:03:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:06:30}.
Of his life: he memorised the Qur'an at seven and began writing hadith at nine; his father, seeing his brilliance, freed him entirely from seeking livelihood {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:11:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:12:00}.
The surname Abu Ja'far was agreed by the historians though he never married and had no son — he took it in observance of the sunna of kunyah {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:09:30}.
His travels: beginning in Tabaristan's cities, then Rayy, then Baghdad — seeking Imam Ahmad, whom he missed by his death in 241 AH — he stayed taking hadith and the fiqh of every school, then Basra, Wasit and Kufa, and returned to Baghdad with accumulated questions, focusing on Qur'an and history, taking Shafi'i fiqh from al-Za'farani and teaching {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:15:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:15:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:16:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:17:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:18:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:18:30}.
Then to Syria for the Syrian readings; to Beirut, al-Awza'i's centre that many overlook; to Egypt — then on the Maliki school, so he studied under Abdallah ibn Wahb, Malik's direct student — and back to Egypt in 256 AH learning the readings of Hamza and Warsh and prosody {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:19:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:20:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:20:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:21:00}.
Returning to Tabaristan he could not settle, and came back to Baghdad for the rest of his life, teaching and writing — the rare creator who excelled in more than one field: exegesis, history and fiqh, with complete character, worship and balance {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:21:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:22:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:22:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep13 00:23:00}.

## In the 'Al-Mubdi'un' programme (part 2)
Al-Suwaidan continues al-Tabari's life, presenting the general law of a scholar's life: a taking stage (memorising, learning, travelling) then a giving stage (teaching, authorship), usually twenty to thirty years of taking {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:01:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:04:00}.
He describes his distinctive gift: organisation — an engineering mind for ordering and classification that made him among the greatest teachers at conveying information {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:04:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:05:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:05:30}.
Of his works: Jami' al-Bayan fi Ta'wil Ay al-Qur'an, known as Tafsir al-Tabari — the foundation of exegesis books to this day; Tarikh al-Umam wa-l-Muluk, known as Tarikh al-Tabari; Tahdhib al-Athar, unfinished at his death; Ikhtilaf Ulama' al-Amsar; and al-Latif, a concise fiqh work written for the vizier al-Abbas ibn al-Hasan {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:05:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:08:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:08:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:24:00}.
Of the scholars' testimony: Ibn Khuzayma's 'I know none under heaven's canopy more learned than Muhammad ibn Jarir', and al-Dhahabi's 'trustworthy, truthful, memoriser, firmly rooted in exegesis, imam in fiqh and consensus and difference, authority in history and the days of men, versed in the readings and language' {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:07:30}.
Of his output: al-Khatib al-Baghdadi reports he spent forty years writing forty leaves daily; Yaqut al-Hamawi recounts his students dividing his writings by his days from puberty to death at eighty-six — fourteen leaves every day — adding 'this is granted to no creature except by the Creator's good care' {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:10:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:10:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:11:00}.
Of his brilliance: in Egypt, tested on prosody he had not mastered, he spent one night on al-Khalil's book until 'he retired no prosodist and rose a prosodist'; and at Abu Kurayb's door he recited back what he had heard from him with the days of each hearing, so the shaykh admitted him alone — he says he took over a hundred thousand hadiths from that one man {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:13:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:13:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:14:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:16:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:17:30}.
His ordering of time: a siesta before noon, then authorship until the afternoon prayer, teaching Qur'an in the mosque until sunset, debating fiqh until the evening prayer, then seclusion for writing where none entered — with a nightly Qur'an portion that sometimes reached a quarter of the book {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:18:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:18:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:19:00}.
Of his character toward opponents: when a student of Dawud al-Zahiri wronged him he composed a hundred-leaf rebuttal, then tore it up on hearing of Dawud's death; conversely he praised Muhammad ibn Dawud to his family though the man had written against him — turning enmity to affection {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:21:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:21:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:22:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:22:30}.
He concludes: in him the creativity equation was almost complete — gift, nurture, journey, students, the taking and giving halves, and balance — among the rare few in whom it all met {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:11:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:12:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan al-mubdioune/ep14 00:23:00}.

## In al-Dhahabi's Mizan al-I'tidal
In [[work/mizan-al-itidal|Mizan al-I'tidal]], [[person/al-dhahabi|al-Dhahabi]] gives an entry to Muhammad ibn Jarir ibn Yazid al-Tabari, calling him the great imam and exegete, author of brilliant works, and records that he died in the year 310. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 498} He judges him trustworthy and truthful, with a slight Shi'ism and a loyalty to the Prophet's family that do no harm. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 499}

He reports that the hafiz Ahmad ibn Ali al-Sulaymani spoke harshly of him, saying that he forged reports for the Rafida. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 499} Al-Dhahabi rejects this as "a stone thrown on false suspicion": Ibn Jarir is one of the great relied-on imams of Islam; al-Dhahabi does not claim he was free of error, but it is not lawful to wrong him out of falsehood and caprice. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 499} He states a general rule: what scholars say about one another should be weighed slowly, above all about a great imam. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 499} Then, in an addition from one manuscript, he suggests that al-Sulaymani perhaps meant his namesake [[person/muhammad-ibn-jarir-ibn-rustam|Muhammad ibn Jarir ibn Rustam]], whom al-Dhahabi lists immediately afterwards as a Rafidi. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 499}

:::perspectives
- **Sunni: al-Dhahabi**: trustworthy and truthful, one of the great imams of Islam, with a slight Shi'ism that does no harm; the charge of forgery is a stone thrown on suspicion. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 499}
- **Sunni: Ahmad ibn Ali al-Sulaymani as reported by al-Dhahabi**: he forged reports for the Rafida — possibly meaning Ibn Rustam, not the exegete. {cite:mizan-itidal-bijawi-v3 p. 499}
:::

## As a scholar of language and the readings
Ahmad Khalid Babakr sets out in his thesis to present al-Tabari "as a scholar of language, grammar and the readings, besides being an exegete, a hadith scholar and a historian", and concludes that his [[work/jami-al-bayan|Jami' al-Bayan]] holds many sciences and that in grammar he was independent between the Basrans and the Kufans, relying most on al-Farra'. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.8} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.1443} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.1444}

## His teachers and students as gathered in the thesis on his readings
Ahmad Khalid Babakr arranges [[person/al-tabari|al-Tabari]]'s journeys thus: he made for Baghdad to meet Ahmad ibn Hanbal, who died before he arrived, so he turned to Basra in 241 AH and stayed a year, hearing Abu al-Ash'ath Ahmad ibn al-Miqdam al-Ijli, Muhammad ibn Bashshar (Bundar) and Muhammad ibn al-Muthanna; he moved to Kufa in 242 AH, hearing teachers at Wasit on the way, and in Kufa learned from [[person/hunnad-ibn-al-sari|Hannad ibn al-Sari]], Abu Hammam al-Walid ibn Shuja' al-Sakuni and Abu Kurayb Muhammad ibn al-Ala, from whom, Yaqut says, he took more than a hundred thousand hadith. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.26} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.27} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.28} Back in Baghdad he read the poets with [[person/thaalab|Tha'lab]], learned the readings from [[person/ahmad-ibn-yusuf-al-taghlibi|Ahmad ibn Yusuf al-Taghlibi]], hadith from Ahmad ibn Mani' al-Baghawi, Arabic from [[person/abu-hatim-al-sijistani|Abu Hatim al-Sijistani]], al-Shafi'i's earlier school from [[person/al-hasan-ibn-muhammad-al-zafarani|al-Hasan ibn Muhammad al-Za'farani]], studied with [[person/dawud-ibn-ali-al-isbahani|Dawud ibn Ali al-Isbahani]], and studied Shafi'i law with Abu Sa'id al-Istakhri after his return from Egypt. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.28} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.29} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.30} In Syria he read the Qur'an in Beirut with [[person/al-abbas-ibn-al-walid-al-bayruti|al-Abbas ibn al-Walid al-Bayruti]]; he entered Egypt in 253 AH and took Maliki law from Abd al-Rahman, Muhammad and Sa'd, the sons of Abd Allah ibn Abd al-Hakam, and from Ahmad ibn Abd al-Rahman, nephew of Ibn Wahb; from [[person/yunus-ibn-abd-al-ala|Yunus ibn Abd al-A'la]] he took Warsh's transmission of Nafi's reading and Maliki law, from [[person/al-muzani|al-Muzani]] law, and from [[person/al-rabi-ibn-sulayman-al-muradi|al-Rabi' ibn Sulayman al-Muradi]] al-Shafi'i's later school. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.30} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.31} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.32} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.33}

Among his students the thesis introduces [[person/ibn-mujahid|Abu Bakr ibn Mujahid]], master of the reciters; Ibn al-Buhlul Ahmad ibn Yusuf (d. 376 AH, so in the thesis); Qadi [[person/ahmad-ibn-kamil|Abu Bakr ibn Kamil]] (d. 350 AH), whose biography of al-Tabari Yaqut largely reproduced; Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Sarraj of Egypt, who heard al-Tirimmah's poetry from him; Abu al-Faraj al-Isfahani, author of al-Aghani; [[person/al-tabarani|Abu al-Qasim al-Tabarani]]; and Abu Muhammad Abd Allah ibn Ahmad al-Farghani. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.33} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.34} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.35} {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.36} His biographers counted some twenty-odd works by him, most of them lost, among them his book on the readings. {cite:qiraat-inda-al-tabari-thesis-umm-al-qura p.36}
