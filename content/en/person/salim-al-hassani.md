## Summary
Professor Salim al-Hassani (born 1940) is an Iraqi-British mechanical engineer and historian of science, emeritus professor at the University of Manchester — where he spent his academic career at the University of Manchester Institute of Science and Technology (UMIST) — and founder and chairman of the Foundation for Science, Technology and Civilisation (FSTC). He is best known publicly as the chief editor and driving force of 1001 Inventions: Muslim Heritage in Our World, the book and international exhibition that carried the history of science in Islamic civilisation to a mass audience. {cite:wikipedia-salim-al-hassani-en} {cite:wikipedia-salim-al-hassani-ar} {cite:themuslim500-salim-al-hassani}

## Engineering career
Al-Hassani was born in Baghdad in 1940 and moved to Britain for higher education, specialising in mechanical engineering. He joined UMIST in Manchester, rising to a professorship and chairing departments and research groups; his technical field was the dynamics and design of machinery. He remained at UMIST through its merger into the University of Manchester, retiring as emeritus professor. Alongside technical research he developed a parallel career in the history of engineering, becoming a fellow of engineering and heritage institutions and serving in roles connecting engineering education with the humanities. {cite:wikipedia-salim-al-hassani-en}

## Foundation for Science, Technology and Civilisation
In 1999 he founded FSTC in Manchester to study and publicise the history of science and technology in Islamic and related civilisations. Under his chairmanship the foundation sponsored research, school materials, lectures and exhibitions, building a scholarly network around manuscripts and instruments of the pre-modern Islamic world. Its flagship products — the touring exhibition 1001 Inventions and the associated book, which he edited — opened in Manchester in 2006 and travelled to museums on several continents, claiming audiences in the millions and patronage from heads of state and international bodies. {cite:wikipedia-salim-al-hassani-en} {cite:themuslim500-salim-al-hassani}

## 1001 Inventions and public history
The book 1001 Inventions: Muslim Heritage in Our World surveys medicine, mechanics, optics, architecture and everyday technology across a millennium, presenting figures from Ibn Sina and al-Zahrawi to engineers of the Ottoman and Mughal worlds for general readers. The exhibition of the same name — with working models and interactive displays — toured internationally, from London and Istanbul to venues in the Middle East, North America and Asia. {cite:wikipedia-salim-al-hassani-en}

## Reception and debate
Supporters credit al-Hassani with restoring the Islamic contribution to the history of science to public view and giving diaspora youth a usable heritage; academic reviewers have at times faulted the popular format for celebratory compression and for stretching the word "invention". The museum reports these assessments attributed, without verdict. His Muslim faith is mentioned in biographical coverage as context for his heritage work; no source read names a school for him, and this record records none. {cite:wikipedia-salim-al-hassani-en} {cite:themuslim500-salim-al-hassani}

## Honours and later work
Al-Hassani received honorary fellowships and national recognition for his public-history work, and continued to lecture internationally on Muslim scientific heritage into his eighties, appearing at schools, museums and interfaith gatherings. He has also served in advisory roles connecting engineering institutions with heritage education, and through FSTC's successor projects he promoted films, curricula and digital resources extending the 1001 Inventions approach. For the museum his significance lies less in any single claim about a medieval device than in the popularisation model he built — one that took manuscript scholarship into travelling exhibitions, classrooms and mass media in a dozen languages. {cite:wikipedia-salim-al-hassani-en} {cite:themuslim500-salim-al-hassani}
