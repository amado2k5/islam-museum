## Summary

Ömer, known by the pen name Nef'i, was an Ottoman divan poet born about 1572 at Hasankale, today Pasinler, in the Erzurum province, and executed at Istanbul on 27 January 1635.{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-tr}{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-en} He was the grandson of Mirza Ali, governor of Pasinler, and the son of Mehmed Bey, governor of Sarıkamış.{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-en} His fame rests on his qasidas and above all on his satires, gathered in the Sihâm-ı Kazâ, the "Arrows of Fate".{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-en}{cite:dia-nefi} On his faith, two independent texts quote his own words: answering Tahir Efendi, who had called him a dog, he wrote "Maliki mezhebim benim zira, itikadımca kelp tahirdir" — my madhhab is Maliki, for in my belief the dog is pure — a pun on Tahir, "pure", and the English and Turkish articles both carry the verse, the Turkish glossing its first sense as his membership of the Maliki madhhab; on that self-identification his faith is recorded as sunni-maliki.{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-en}{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-tr}

## Upbringing and education

His father Mehmed Bey was himself a poet, and the TDV entry describes the hardship the family fell into when the father withdrew to Crimea and lived at ease there, leaving his household poor and unprotected.{cite:dia-nefi} Nef'i began his education at Pasinler and continued it at Erzurum, learning Persian there.{cite:dia-nefi} His first pen name was Darrî, "harmful" — the Turkish article writes it Zarrî — and the name Nef'i, "beneficial", was given him by the historian Gelibolulu Âlî, who had seen and admired the young man's poems while serving as finance director at Erzurum.{cite:dia-nefi}{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-tr}

## Istanbul and the court

He came to Istanbul in the first years of Ahmed I's reign and was presented to the sultan, winning a place among his intimates and a first post as comptroller of mines in the Imperial Divan's finance service.{cite:dia-nefi}{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-en} He afterwards served as a tithe clerk, as trustee of the Muradiye endowments at Edirne during an exile there, and as poll-tax accountant at Istanbul.{cite:dia-nefi} He failed to gain the favour of Ahmed I at first, and of Osman II, but Murad IV recognised his skill and granted him a stipend, and under Murad his art and fame reached their peak.{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-en}{cite:dia-nefi} Three times, by his poems' own evidence, he was removed from office.{cite:dia-nefi}

## Satire and the famous exchanges

Strongly influenced by classical Persian poetry, he developed the Ottoman Turkish qasida form, and alongside his odes he wrote sarcastic and often vitriolic verse about the failings of named officials.{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-en} Naîmâ reports that as Murad IV read the Sihâm-ı Kazâ in the palace, lightning struck near the throne; the sultan took it as an ill omen, forbade him satire and dismissed him, and his last years before his recall were spent in the Edirne exile.{cite:dia-nefi}{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-en} When Şeyhülislam Yahya, himself a poet, addressed him a quatrain that praised his verse while calling him a kâfir, an unbeliever, he answered in kind — "The mufti efendi has called us an unbeliever; let us call him a Muslim; but when we reach the Day of Judgement, both of us will come out liars there."{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-tr} His answer to Tahir Efendi's "dog" insult, the quatrain declaring his Maliki madhhab, became his most famous comeback of all, and he defended himself with its first sense when he was tried for it and escaped punishment.{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-en}{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-tr}

## Works

His works are the Turkish Divan, a Persian Divan — the Arabic article notes that the İzah al-Maknun ascribes a Persian divan to him — and the Sihâm-ı Kazâ, the collection of his satires, in which the exchanges with Tâhir Efendi and Şeyhülislâm Yahyâ stand, on the TDV entry's judgement, as elegant examples of the genre.{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-ar}{cite:dia-nefi}

## Death

Recalled to Istanbul in 1634 after qasidas regained him the sultan's favour, he returned to his satires and prepared his own end.{cite:dia-nefi} For a satire on the grand vizier Bayram Pasha he was condemned; Kâtib Çelebi's Fezleke and Naîmâ report that Bayram Pasha had him handed to Boynueğri Mehmed Ağa, strangled in the palace woodshed and his body thrown into the sea.{cite:dia-nefi}{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-en} The Turkish and Persian articles date the execution to 27 January 1635, and the TDV entry holds that the chronogram verses confirm Sha'ban 1044, January 1635, though the sources give differing dates; the Arabic article gives 1636, and both readings are preserved in his record.{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-tr}{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-fa}{cite:dia-nefi}{cite:wikipedia-nef-i-ar}
