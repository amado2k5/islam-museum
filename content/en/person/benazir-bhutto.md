## Summary
Benazir Bhutto (21 June 1953 – 27 December 2007) was a Pakistani politician who served two terms as Prime Minister of Pakistan, in 1988–1990 and 1993–1996, and was the first woman elected to head the government of a Muslim-majority country. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en} {cite:britannica-benazir-bhutto}

She chaired the Pakistan People's Party (PPP), the party founded by her father Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, from 1982 until her death. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

Her career ran through her father's overthrow and execution, years of imprisonment and exile, two governments dismissed by presidents on corruption and law-and-order grounds, and a long legal war fought in Pakistani, Swiss and British courts. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

She returned from exile in October 2007 under an amnesty and was assassinated at an election rally in Rawalpindi on 27 December 2007; who ordered the killing remains contested, and the competing attributions are set out below as the sources give them. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

## Early life and education
Bhutto was born on 21 June 1953 at Pinto's Nursing Home in Karachi, the eldest child of Zulfikar Ali Bhutto and his wife Nusrat Ispahani. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

The English article describes the Bhutto family as Sunni Muslims of Sindhi and Kurdish origin, and records that her mother was born into a Shia family in Isfahan and converted to Sunnism on her marriage — family background, recorded here as the article gives it. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

She was educated at schools in Karachi and Murree — the Urdu article adds her early schooling at the Presentation Convent in Rawalpindi — before reading comparative government at Radcliffe College, Harvard, where she graduated cum laude in 1973. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en} {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-ur}

At Lady Margaret Hall, Oxford, she read philosophy, politics and economics and then studied international law and diplomacy, and in 1977 she became the first Asian woman elected President of the Oxford Union. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

She returned to Pakistan in June 1977, shortly before her father's government was overthrown in General Zia-ul-Haq's military coup. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en} {cite:britannica-benazir-bhutto}

## Prison, exile and the road to power
After Zulfikar Ali Bhutto was executed in April 1979, Benazir and her mother led the Movement for the Restoration of Democracy against the Zia regime. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

She spent much of the following years in prison or under house arrest, including eighteen months in solitary confinement at Sukkur, before being allowed to leave for medical treatment in January 1984 and settling in London, where she led the PPP in exile. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

On 10 April 1986 she returned to Pakistan to an enormous welcome in Lahore, and in December 1987 she married Asif Ali Zardari in Karachi, in a ceremony the article calls the "People's Wedding". {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

## First premiership, 1988–1990
Zia died in a plane crash in August 1988, and in the November elections the PPP won the largest share of seats; Bhutto was sworn in as prime minister on 2 December 1988, at thirty-five the youngest elected head of government in the Islamic world. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

Britannica dates her becoming prime minister to 1 December 1988, the day before the swearing-in date given by the English article; the difference is recorded rather than resolved. {cite:britannica-benazir-bhutto}

Her first government was obstructed by the military–intelligence establishment — the article names President Ghulam Ishaq Khan, army chief Mirza Aslam Beg and ISI chief Hamid Gul — including the "Operation Midnight Jackal" attempt to buy PPP legislators' votes against her. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

In August 1990 President Khan dismissed her government, citing corruption and a failure to maintain law and order, and the PPP lost the elections that followed; Bhutto led the opposition to Nawaz Sharif from 1990 to 1993. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

## Second premiership, 1993–1996
After the 1993 elections Bhutto returned to office at the head of a coalition; the English article dates her swearing-in to 19 October 1993, while the Arabic article dates her election by the National Assembly to 20 October, by 121 votes to 72. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en} {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-ar}

Her second government pursued economic privatisation and a set of women's measures the article lists — signing the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women, a women's bank, women-only police stations and the first women judges of the superior courts. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

In 1996 her government became one of only three to recognise the Taliban regime in Afghanistan, a decision the article's critics count among its foreign-policy failures. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

In September 1996 her brother Murtaza was killed in a police shootout in Karachi; Bhutto blamed a conspiracy reaching into President Farooq Leghari's circle, the Scotland Yard inquiry she commissioned remained unsolved, and the killing is unsolved still. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

On 5 November 1996 Leghari dismissed her government amid the corruption allegations set out in the next section. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

## Exile and the corruption cases
Bhutto left Pakistan in 1998–99 and spent the following years between Dubai and London, fighting proceedings at home and abroad; the outcomes, as the sources give them, were mixed rather than a single verdict. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

In April 1999 the Ehtesab Bench of the Lahore High Court convicted her in absentia, sentencing her to five years' imprisonment and an $8.6 million fine and disqualifying her from public office; two years later a retrial was ordered after it was ascertained that undue political bias had been exerted on the judges. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

In Switzerland, magistrate Daniel Devaud's investigation of the commissions paid by the inspection companies SGS and Cotecna led a Geneva court to order her to surrender $11.9 million to the Pakistani state and serve 180 days in prison; she challenged the ruling and secured a retrial, which overturned it. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

A New York Times investigation by John Burns estimated the couple's takings by 1996 at some $1.5 billion, while Pakistan's Accountability Bureau noted that the family had declared assets totalling only $1.2 million; Bhutto and her husband maintained throughout that the charges were purely political. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

In 2006 the National Accountability Bureau accused the couple of holding $1.5 billion in assets acquired through corrupt practices, and at Pakistan's request Interpol issued notices for them — expressly not arrest warrants. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

The Arabic article also records an earlier outcome in her favour: her 1994 acquittal in the special-courts cases over alleged misuse of authority. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-ar}

In October 2007 President Pervez Musharraf promulgated the National Reconciliation Ordinance, an amnesty brokered with American and British involvement, which cleared the way for her return. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

## Return and assassination
Bhutto returned to Karachi on 18 October 2007; two bombs struck her cavalcade that day, killing 149 people and injuring 402 by the English article's count — the Arabic article gives more than 125 killed — while she herself was unharmed. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en} {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-ar}

On 27 December 2007, leaving an election rally at Liaquat Bagh in Rawalpindi, she stood up through her armoured vehicle's escape hatch to wave to the crowds. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

A man standing within two to three metres of the car fired three shots at her and detonated a suicide vest packed with ball bearings; Bhutto was fatally injured — reports differ as to whether she was hit by bullets or by shrapnel — and twenty-two others also died. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

She was clinically dead on arrival at Rawalpindi General Hospital, no autopsy was conducted, and the following day she was buried beside her father in the family mausoleum at Garhi Khuda Bakhsh, near Larkana. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

Responsibility was attributed in several directions, and the museum records the attributions without adopting any of them. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

The Pakistani authorities stated that the attack had been masterminded by Baitullah Mehsud, leader of the Pakistani Taliban, and the CIA concurred that this was probable; Mehsud denied the accusation. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

Al-Qaeda commander Mustafa Abu al-Yazid claimed responsibility for the assassination on al-Qaeda's behalf. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

The PPP accused the government of a cover-up, with several PPP figures claiming that Bhutto had been killed by a sniper linked to the intelligence agencies. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

A three-person UN commission established in 2009 by Secretary-General Ban Ki-moon — Heraldo Muñoz, Peter FitzGerald and Marzuki Darusman — was not within its remit to identify a culprit; Muñoz later expressed the view that the assassination was likely carried out by the Pakistani Taliban, perhaps with Mehsud's support and possibly assisted by rogue elements in the intelligence agencies, and that the original police investigation had been deliberately botched. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

Pakistan's official enquiry issued its final report in February 2012, placing responsibility with 27 different militant groups, and in February 2011 a Rawalpindi court had subpoenaed Musharraf on the grounds that he had not acted on known threats to Bhutto and had provided insufficient security to protect her. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

Early reports and her party said she had been shot in the neck, chest and head, while the government's official account, as the Arabic article records it, was that she died when her head struck the armoured car's sunroof lever in the blast. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-ar}

## Positions and criticism
The positions and judgements in this section are those of the cited sources, reported as theirs; they are not the museum's verdict.

Critics quoted in the article accused Bhutto of political inexperience and corruption, while Islamist and conservative forces opposed her secularist, modernising agenda; the same article records that she was domestically popular early in her career. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

Her economic liberalisation and privatisation, her governments' human-rights record and the dynasty politics of the PPP — which passed after her death to her husband and then her son — are all criticised in the sources, as her democratic symbolism is praised in them. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

## Writings
Her autobiography appeared in Britain as Daughter of the East and in the United States as Daughter of Destiny, and her Reconciliation: Islam, Democracy, and the West was published posthumously in 2008, co-written with Mark Siegel. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

The Arabic article records that in 1995 she donated $300,000 to Harvard Law School's Islamic studies centre. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-ar}

## Belief and affiliation
The sources do not give Benazir Bhutto a school, and the museum does not assign her one. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

The English article describes her family as Sunni, records her mother's conversion from a Shia family to Sunnism on marriage, says that Benazir, like her father, professed an adherence to Sufism, and in another section says her Islamist opponents opposed her "because she was a Shia Muslim"; Britannica names no sect and Wikidata gives Islam generically. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en} {cite:britannica-benazir-bhutto} {cite:wikidata-benazir-bhutto}

Because these are one article pointing in several directions — a family's affiliation, a professed Sufi adherence, and an opponents' description — rather than two independent attestations of a school, her faith is recorded as unknown in the data, with the article's own statements carried here exactly as it gives them. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

## Personal life
Bhutto married Asif Ali Zardari in Karachi in December 1987, in an arranged marriage the article describes as the "People's Wedding". {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

They had three children: Bilawal, born in 1988; Bakhtawar, born in 1990, which the article notes made Bhutto the first elected head of government to give birth in office; and Aseefa, born in 1993. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}

The Urdu article recounts the naming of her son as Bilawal Bhutto Zardari, joining her family's name to his father's. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-ur}

Zardari spent long stretches of these years in prison and was released in November 2004, in what Musharraf presented as a gesture of goodwill. {cite:wikipedia-benazir-bhutto-en}
