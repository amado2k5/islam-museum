## Overview
Abu al-Qasim al-Husayn ibn Muhammad ibn al-Mufaddal, known as al-Raghib al-Isfahani, was a man of letters and religious scholar of [[place/isfahan|Isfahan]]. Despite the considerable popularity of his works — at least a dozen survive — and his demonstrable influence on al-Ghazali and later figures, his name is missing from almost all the standard biographical collections, and information about his life is extremely scanty {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 389}.

## His age
Late sources place al-Raghib in the 6th/12th century, but recent scholarship has confirmed al-Suyuti's statement in the Bughya that he died early in the 5th/11th. In his literary anthologies he alludes to contemporaries identifiable as members of the circle of the Buyid vizier [[person/sahib-ibn-abbad|al-Sahib Ibn Abbad]] (d. 385/995), and he refers to Ibn Abbad's successor Abu al-Abbas al-Dabbi (d. 399/1008) by his full title, suggesting he was writing during that vizierate. There is no sound evidence that al-Raghib ever visited Baghdad or left his native Isfahan {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 pp. 389-390}.

## His works
His best-known work is the Muhadarat al-udaba', a comprehensive adab encyclopaedia in twenty-five chapters on intellect, rulership, crafts, food, courage, love, death and animals, particularly rich in the poetry of [[person/al-mutanabbi|al-Mutanabbi]] and al-Sharif al-Radi and the prose and verse of Ibn Abbad. Similar in scope is the Mahima al-balagha, a thesaurus of elegant expressions for the aspiring littérateur, probably identical with the Kitab al-Ma'ani al-akbar named by Hajji Khalifa {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 pp. 389-390}.

On the Qur'an he wrote a series of monographs, foremost the [[work/mufradat-gharib-al-quran|Mufradat alfaz al-Qur'an]], an alphabetical lexicon whose influence can be traced in later tafsir and lexicography; its introduction cites two earlier, apparently lost risalas and promises a study of apparent synonyms, probably the Durrat al-ta'wil. His tafsir survives only in its opening sections and is quoted explicitly by Fakhr al-Din al-Razi and anonymously by al-Baydawi {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 pp. 389-390}.

His best-known ethical work, al-Dhari'a ila makarim al-shari'a, is structured on Platonic-Aristotelian psychology and recalls Miskawayh, buried at Isfahan in 421/1030; it was al-Ghazali's direct source for a good half of his Mizan al-'amal and for significant sections of the Ihya' 'ulum al-din and Ma'arij al-quds. Its companion piece, Tafsil al-nash'atayn, stresses the complementarity of reason and revelation, and his kalam treatise argues by philosophical means {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 pp. 389-390}.

## Creed and death
His theological stance seems close to the Ash'aris, though he criticises them once for denying a rational moral order; his omission of Abu Hanifa from a list of formative jurists adds to the evidence for Shafi'i allegiance, and he explicitly supports Sufism in places. His repeated attacks on the Mu'tazila — and occasional ones on the Shi'a — make his alleged adherence to either groundless {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 pp. 389-390}.

He died most probably in Rabi' II 412, as Muhammad Adnan Jawahirji's study in the Damascus Academy journal concluded and the editor of Kashf al-Zunun endorsed {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 197-198}; among the late datings are 402 in the margin of al-Bayhaqi's Tarikh hukama' al-Islam, 452 argued by As'ad Talas, and the sixth century in Hajji Khalifa, followed by al-Zirikli's al-A'lam at 1108 CE {cite:al-aalam 2/261-262} {cite:kashf-zunun-furqan p. 197-198}.
