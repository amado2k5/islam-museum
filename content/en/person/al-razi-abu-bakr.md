## Overview
Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi, philosopher and physician, author of the "al-Mansuri" on medicine. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 547}

## Life and career
The article on al-Razi in the Encyclopaedia of Islam, written by L. E. Goodman, introduces him as a physician, philosopher and alchemist, known to the Latins as Rhazes, who lived from about 250/854 to 313/925, or by another reckoning to 323/935. The author calls him the most free-thinking of the major philosophers of Islam. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 516} He was born in [[place/rayy|Rayy]] and trained there in the Greek sciences, and he is reputed to have been versed in musical theory and performance before he became a physician. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 516}

He headed the hospital of Rayy before taking the corresponding post in [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]]. His property near Rayy seems to have drawn him back there often, and he died there, somewhat embittered and alienated, partly through the loss of his eyesight. Like many of the great physicians of Islam, the article says, he was a courtier as well as a scholar, clinician and teacher. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 516} His medical handbook the Mansuri, translated into Latin by Gerard of Cremona in the twelfth century, was dedicated to Mansur ibn Ishaq, the governor of Rayy for the [[polity/samanid-dynasty|Samanids]]. His Muluki, or Regius, was dedicated to Ali ibn Wahsudhan of [[place/tabaristan|Tabaristan]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 516}

The article reports that al-Razi wrote some two hundred books. In his apologia, the Philosophical Way of Life, he claims a life of moderation, excessive only in devotion to learning, and says that he associated with princes only as a physician and a friend, never as a soldier or an officer of state. He was constantly writing. Others, the article adds, remark on his generosity and compassion: the poor among his patients were properly fed and nursed, and arriving patients first saw an outer circle of disciples, then an inner circle, leaving al-Razi himself to treat the hardest cases. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 516}

## Medical writings and method
His clinical research was methodical, as his notebooks show. They were edited in some twenty-five volumes as the [[work/al-hawi-fi-al-tibb|Kitab al-Hawi fi al-tibb]], at the instance of Ibn al-Amid, the vizier of [[person/rukn-al-dawla|Rukn al-Dawla]]. It was translated into Latin as the Continens in 1279 by the Jewish physician Faraj ibn Salim, known as Farraguth, for King Charles of Anjou. The Hyderabad edition of 1955 contains al-Razi's extensive notes from a wide range of sources, organised anatomically from head to toe, and his own clinical observations, often at variance with received opinion, typically close the sections. He drew on these files for his many medical works, and several unfinished works can be discerned in the Hawi in embryo. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 516} His large Kitab al-Jami al-kabir, or Great Medical Compendium, which is often confused with the Hawi, was a work that he published, not the corpus of his private files. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 516}

Among his most famous medical writings, the article names the book on stones in the kidney and bladder and the book on [[invention/al-razi-smallpox-measles|smallpox and measles]]. The latter, it says, was the first book on smallpox and was translated more than a dozen times into Latin and other European languages; its lack of dogmatism and its Hippocratic reliance on clinical observation are typical of his medical methods. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 516}

His independent mind is shown, according to the article, in his Doubts about Galen. There he rejects claims of [[person/jalinus|Galen]], from the alleged superiority of the Greek language to many of Galen's cosmological and medical views. He places medicine within philosophy and infers that sound practice demands independent thinking. He reports that his own clinical records do not confirm Galen's descriptions of the course of a fever, and that in some cases his experience exceeds Galen's. He also rejects the notion, central to the theory of [[concept/humours|humours]], that a body is warmed or cooled only by warmer or cooler bodies, since a warm drink may heat the body to a degree much hotter than its own; the drink, he argued, must trigger a response and not merely pass on its own warmth or coldness. The article judges that this line of criticism had the potential, in time, to bring down the whole theory of humours and the scheme of four elements on which it rested. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 516}

He also wrote on the profession of medicine, in essays on why people prefer inferior physicians, on mistaken views of the physician's function, on why an intelligent physician cannot heal all diseases, and on why ignorant physicians, common folk and women in the cities sometimes succeed better than scientists with certain diseases. Sharing Galen's interest in philosophy, he heeded Galen's treatise arguing that the outstanding physician must also be a philosopher. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 517}

## Alchemy
The article says that his work in alchemy takes a newer, more empirical and naturalistic approach than that of the Greeks or of [[person/jabir-ibn-hayyan|Jabir ibn Hayyan]], and that he brought the same empirical spirit to medicine. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 516} His alchemy dismisses the idea of potions and dispenses with an appeal to magic, if magic means reliance on symbols as causes, yet he does not reject wonders in the sense of unexplained phenomena in nature. His stockroom was enriched with the products of Persian mining and manufacture and with sal ammoniac, a Chinese discovery. The article adds that he still relied on dominant forms or essences, and that he ascribed inflammability to oiliness and sulphuriousness, properties that the traditional scheme of fire, water, earth and air did not readily explain, as [[person/al-ghazali-abu-hamid|al-Ghazali]] and others were quick to note. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 516} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 517}

## Philosophical writings
[[person/al-biruni-muhammad-ibn-ahmad|Al-Biruni]] lists some eighty philosophical titles in his bibliography of al-Razi, and [[person/ibn-al-nadim|Ibn al-Nadim]] lists dozens of works on logic, cosmology, theology, mathematics and alchemy. Because of repugnance toward his philosophical views among his contemporaries and medieval successors, few of these works were copied, but fragments survive in quotations by later authors. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 517}

The works of which the article records a mention include a commentary on Plato's Timaeus, an epitome of Galen, a response to Porphyry's Letter to Anebo, an appraisal of the Qur'an, a critique of the [[group/mutazila|Mu'tazila]], another on the infallible Ismaili imam, a work on how to measure intelligence, an introduction to and vindication of algebra, a defence of the incorporeality of the soul, a debate with a Manichaean, and an explanation of why people untrained in rigorous demonstration find it hard to accept that the earth is a sphere. Other works deal with love, coitus, nudity and clothing, the effects of poisons, the seasons, the wisdom of the Creator, and the reason for the creation of savage beasts and reptiles. One defends the proposition that God does not interfere with the actions of other agents, and another rebuts the claim that the earth revolves. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 517}

On natural philosophy, the article says that he discussed the intrinsic character of motion and wrote several treatments of the nature of matter. It suggests that his book on the diagonal of the square may have defended his own atomism against an ancient objection, and notes that his acceptance of the void and his rejection of Aristotle's relativity of space gave him an absolute space that is a Euclidean continuum. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 517}

## The Spiritual Physic and ethics
The [[work/al-tibb-al-ruhani|Spiritual Physic]] was written for Mansur as a companion to the Mansuri. The article describes it as developing a moderately ascetic ideal of life from the premise that every pleasure presupposes a prior pain, so that peace of mind is the optimum of pleasure. Pleasures cannot be hoarded, and feeding an appetite only enlarges it, so a life spent serving the passions defeats itself. The treatise follows [[person/al-kindi|al-Kindi]] in treating ethics as a kind of psychic medicine. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 517}

Expressing grave doubts about whether immortality can be demonstrated, al-Razi falls back, in the article's reading, on the position that if death is the end of our existence it is nothing to be feared, only an end to pains and troubles. Wisdom then springs not from the thought of death but from overcoming it, because the fear of death goads the passions that hamper reason. The article calls the argument Epicurean, observes that ethics here becomes entirely prudential, as his critics noted, and says that al-Razi regards settling the question of the afterlife as requiring an examination of all faiths and rites, a task he had no immediate intention of attempting. It reads him as treating what scripture says on the subject as another undemonstrated report. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 517} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 518}

## Prophecy, reason and the five eternals
This section reports the article's account of positions attributed to al-Razi; the museum gives no verdict on them, and other readings of his religious identity are set out further down this page. The article describes his debates with the Ismaili adversary [[person/abu-hatim-ahmad-al-razi|Abu Hatim al-Razi]], who died in 322/934 and served as chief lieutenant and later chief of the Ismaili missionary organisation in Rayy. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 518} When Abu Hatim argued that God's mercy would not deny humanity the guidance of leaders inspired with revealed knowledge, the article says that al-Razi answered that God has given what we need to know in reason, which belongs equally to all, and not in special revelation, which he held to foment bloodshed and contention. In the article's account he called prophets impostors, at best misled by the demonic shades of restless and envious spirits, and held that ordinary people can think for themselves. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 518}

Asked whether a philosopher can follow a prophetically revealed religion, he is reported to have retorted that no one can think philosophically while committed to traditions he saw as founded on contradiction, ignorance and dogmatism. He also took issue with ritualism for its obsession with unseen sources of impurity. He combated the tendency of his contemporaries to think of philosophy as a dogmatic school and held that a philosopher does not slavishly follow a master: one learns from predecessors, but hopes to surpass them. He admitted that he would never be a Socrates, yet affirmed a belief in progress, at least for individuals. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 518}

In works such as the Book of Divine Science and On the Five Eternals, both now lost but known from fragments, paraphrases, descriptions and refutations, the article says that he taught that the soul was one of five eternal things that precede the cosmos, the others being God, matter, time and space. Space is the void and may or may not contain atoms. Time, like space, is absolute and eternal, while motion is not, since matter is inert and moves through the activity of soul. The world soul first stood apart from matter and yearned to be embodied. God, like a wise father, understanding that she learns only by experience, allowed her to embroil herself in matter. Chaos was the first result, and God then imparted intelligence to the world, giving it order and allowing the soul to recognise her exile and seek a return. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 518}

The article adds that he offered this scheme of a formation of the world, not creation from nothing, as the one workable hypothesis for a temporal origin of the world, and that he adopted the idea of a temporal origin from scripture and from the concurring authority of Plato. Because he held that in this life evils outweigh goods, he treated creation as a qualified evil and life in the body as a fall for the Soul. The fall, as the article presents it, is broken by the gift of intelligence. It is ascribed to the Soul's spontaneity, not to God's will, but was foreseen and tolerated by an all-seeing wisdom, and the loss it brought about will be overcome. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 518} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 8 p. 519}

## In al-Mas'udi
Al-Mas'udi saw a book of his which mentions the doctrines of the Harranian Sabians, and distinguishes them from other Sabians, the Kaymariyun. Al-Mas'udi says that it contains matters too long to mention and which are ugly in the view of many people. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 547}

He says that al-Razi refuted al-Kindi's treatise on refuting the craft of gold, and wrote books on the mineral stones and the manner of their work. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 620}

## In Ibn al-Nadim
Ibn al-Nadim relates from him that he held that a philosopher's knowledge is not sound unless the science of alchemy is sound, and that the ancient philosophers used to practise it, and he called Jabir ibn Hayyan "our master". {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 494,500}

Ibn al-Nadim describes him, in the art of physicians, as unique in his age, combining the knowledge of the ancients especially medicine, and he wrote "al-Mansuri", and had a friendship with Mansur ibn Isma'il. He was generous and kind to the poor, and became blind at the end of his life. He mentions from the index of his books "al-Hawi" and "The Doubts on Galen". {cite:fihrist-ibn-al-nadim p. 415-417}

Modern sources give different dates: Britannica has birthc854 and death925 or935; Kaya gives865/251AH for birth. These reports are not collapsed into one certain year {cite:britannica-al-razi biography} {cite:tdv-razi-ebu-bekir pp.479–485}.

## The medical treatise and identity limits

In his [[invention/al-razi-smallpox-measles|comparison of smallpox and measles]], al-Razi arranges shared symptoms and relative differences with an exception and a humoral explanation. ChapterIII establishes comparison, not invention of all diagnosis or vaccination; the1848 translator says its composition date is unknown {cite:razi-greenhill-smallpox-1848 pp.3,34–35}. Nor is the *Hawi* simply a modern personal casebook: Adamson explains its predominantly source-excerpt character {cite:sep-abu-bakr-al-razi sec.1}.

**Sunni frame:** The museum's frame is declared, not a certificate of personal school. Britannica explicitly calls al-Razi a Muslim philosopher, a named individual basis rather than inference from name or country, while disagreement remains {cite:britannica-al-razi biography}.

**Academic reading:** Adamson discusses hostile testimony through Abu Hatim alongside surviving works and contrary reports, including limits on attribution of anti-prophecy positions {cite:sep-abu-bakr-al-razi sec.5}.

**Kaya's reading:** Kaya defends a deist interpretation; this is his reading, not the museum's verdict {cite:tdv-razi-ebu-bekir pp.479–485}. The programme account below remains attributed to its speaker. This additional documentation does not authenticate every priority claim or anecdote in it.

## Classifying materials and arranging a handbook

In the [[invention/razi-substance-classification|substance classification and structured Book of Secrets]], the text orders materials, instruments and operations; six earthy classes sit within a wider division, not a periodic table or invention of all chemistry {cite:razi-asrar-danishpazhuh-1964 p.2}. Ferrario explains the operation-centred organization, warns against treating the1964 edition as a definitive critical text and distinguishes *Secrets* from spurious *On Alums and Salts* {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 pp.25–29}. Sal ammoniac changes class even between two genuine works, so placement alone cannot settle authorship {cite:ferrario-razi-pseudorazi-2024 p.28}. ## Patient groups and uncertainty

The [[invention/razi-patient-group-comparison|reported patient-group comparison]] in a translated *Hawi* excerpt describes intentional comparison of a group receiving an intervention with another not receiving it. Tibi reads this as evidence of comparison's principle, not sufficient grounds for recommending treatment. Pormann identifies a control group while noting the absence of a separate theoretical account of the group concept {cite:tibi-razi-comparison-jll comparison-and-conclusion} {cite:razi-pormann-uncertainty-2013 p.371}.

The approximate300 of2000 in a *Doubts about Galen* passage concerns courses contrary to expectation, not automatically the comparison's patients or a cure rate. The15% ratio is modern arithmetic on an approximate count, not efficacy or mortality. A difficult text's three cases and another outcome do not become a verified four-person trial {cite:razi-pormann-uncertainty-2013 pp.371–372}.
