## Overview
Hammad ibn Salama ibn Dinar, Abu Salama of Basra, was one of the most prolific memorisers of hadith and a teacher of Shu'ba. He recited the Qur'anic readings from 'Asim and Ibn Kathir, and died in 167 AH while praying in the mosque. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.1555-1556}

## Memory and transmission
Wuhayb ibn Khalid called him "our master and the most learned of us"; Ahmad ibn Hanbal said he was the most knowledgeable of Thabit al-Bunani's reports and the firmest in Humayd al-Tawil's. Yahya ibn Ma'in accredited him and Ibn al-Madini counted him a proof among narrators, while 'Affan wrote some thirteen thousand reports from him. Al-Dhahabi nonetheless relays al-Hakim's criticism of his memory and of combining several transmitters under one wording, and summarises him as "truthful, an authority", though short of Hammad ibn Zayd's precision. {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.1555}

## Scrupulosity
When he earned a small profit on a garment in the market he would tie up his robe and sell nothing more that day. He never narrated until he had read a hundred verses in the codex, and he said: "If the ruler summons you to recite 'Say: He is God, One' for him, do not go." {cite:siyar-alam-nubala-ideas-house p.1555-1556}

## Ibn Hibban's defence of him
[[person/ibn-hibban|Ibn Hibban]] relied on Hammad in his [[work/sahih-ibn-hibban|*Sahih*]] and made him, in his preface, the example of a narrator some imams had criticised. He describes him as one who travelled, wrote, gathered, compiled, memorised and discussed, devoted to religion, hidden piety, constant worship, firmness in the Sunna and severity towards innovators; the common people of Basra had no doubt that his prayers were answered, and no one in Basra in his day among those counted as learned was reckoned one of the abdal except him. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 114}

He then answers the objections one by one. If it is said he differed from his peers: there is no trustworthy narrator who never differed from his peers in something. If it is said he erred: no one after the Prophet is free of error. If it is said his errors were many: a narrator is abandoned only when his errors outweigh his accuracy; one who errs often without that is accepted where he did not err, like Sharik, Hushaym and Abu Bakr ibn 'Ayyash. If it is said he concealed sources: Qatada, Abu Ishaq al-Sabi'i, al-A'mash, al-Thawri and [[person/hushaym-ibn-bashir|Hushaym]] did so and are relied on. If it is said he combined several transmitters under one wording: the Companions and Successors passed on reports by their meaning. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 114-117}

[[person/ahmad-muhammad-shakir|Ahmad Muhammad Shakir]] reads this as a veiled reproach of al-Bukhari, who, as Ibn Hajar notes in the introduction to *Fath al-Bari*, used Hammad neither as proof nor as corroboration except once, in the Book of Softening Hearts, with the formula "Abu al-Walid said to us"; and he quotes from *al-Tahdhib* Ibn Hibban's remark elsewhere that whoever turned from relying on Hammad to relying on Fulayh and 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Abd Allah ibn Dinar did not act fairly. {cite:sahih-ibn-hibban-shakir-maarif-v1 p. 115-116}
