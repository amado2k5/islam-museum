## Summary
Sultan Abu al-Mujahid Muhammad Shah, son of Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq Shah, king of India and Sind, whom [[person/ibn-battuta-muhammad|Ibn Battuta]] came to and served. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.354} Ibn Battuta says his name had been "Juna"; when his father Tughluq died he took the throne unopposed, named himself Muhammad and took the kunya Abu al-Mujahid; he tells how the pavilion built to receive Tughluq collapsed on him, that some claimed he was brought out alive and finished off, and that its builder, the vizier Khwaja Jahan, became the most favoured man at Muhammad Shah's court. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.354}

## His portrait by Ibn Battuta
Ibn Battuta says he learned most of what he tells of the kings of India from the chief judge Kamal al-Din ibn al-Burhan al-Ghaznawi, whereas most of what he tells of this king he witnessed. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.354} He describes him as "of all men the most fond of giving gifts and shedding blood": his gate was never without a poor man made rich or a living man put to death; yet he was the humblest of men and the most given to showing justice, strict about prayer and punishing its neglect, and above all generous; Ibn Battuta swears that what he relates of that generosity is true. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.354} {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.355} He describes the palace at Delhi, "Dar Sara", with its three gates, and the executioners sitting outside the first, since anyone the sultan ordered killed was killed at the gate of the audience hall and left there three days. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.355}

## What was held against him
Ibn Battuta gives a section the heading "Account of this sultan's killings and what was held against his deeds": for all his humility, fairness and generosity he was very ready to shed blood, punished small and great offences alike, respected no one of learning, piety or noble descent, and Ibn Battuta himself saw the dead lying at his gate. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.378} Among these he relates the killing of his brother Mas'ud Khan after he confessed to rebellion for fear of torture. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.378} He calls "the greatest thing held against him" the removal of the people of [[place/delhi|Delhi]] to Dawlatabad, forty days away, after insulting notes were thrown into the audience hall: he bought their houses, ordered them to move, and dealt with those who stayed behind, so the city emptied; Ibn Battuta says that when he entered it later it was empty but for a little habitation. {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.385} All of this is Ibn Battuta's own account, as he saw or heard it; no other source is added here.

## Ibn Battuta in his service
The sultan made Ibn Battuta judge of Delhi on twelve thousand dinars a year, telling him that the judgeship of Delhi was "the greatest of tasks with us"; when he excused himself as a Maliki who did not know the language, the sultan gave him two deputies and told him in Arabic, out of courtesy, "rather, you are our master". {cite:rihla-ibn-battuta-khayriyya-1904 p.412} On the reception of these stories of his generosity, see [[question/credibility-of-ibn-battuta|the question of believing Ibn Battuta]].
