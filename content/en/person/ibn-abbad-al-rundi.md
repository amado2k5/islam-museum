## Summary
Ibn 'Abbad al-Nafzi al-Rundi was an Andalusian Sufi of the eighth century AH whom Muhammad 'Abd al-Maqsud Haykal, summarising Abu al-Wafa al-Taftazani's study, describes as the representative of the Shadhili school in al-Andalus, whose teaching influenced the Sufism of East and West. {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 27} His biographers give his name as Muhammad ibn Ibrahim ibn Abi Bakr ibn 'Abd Allah ibn Malik ibn Ibrahim ibn Muhammad ibn Ibrahim ibn Yahya, known as Ibn 'Abbad, of Himyarite lineage and from Ronda. {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 28}

## Upbringing and studies
Ibn 'Abbad was born in [[place/ronda|Ronda]] in southern al-Andalus in 733 AH into an old family; he memorised the Qur'an with his father by the age of seven, learned the language sciences from his maternal uncle, studied at Ronda with the jurist and preacher Abu al-Hasan 'Ali ibn al-Hasan al-Rundi, and then travelled in the Maghrib, staying in Tlemcen and Fez. {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 28} He belonged to the Maliki school, reading al-Tahdhib and the Mukhtasar of Ibn al-Hajib in law, the Muwatta' and Sahih Muslim in hadith, and al-Juwayni's al-Irshad and Ibn al-Hajib's creed in theology. {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 29}

## Taking the path
He then turned to Sufism; of his going to Shaykh Ibn 'Ashir and his companions at Salé he is reported to have said, "I sought them to find safety with them". {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 29} He studied with Ahmad ibn 'Umar ibn Muhammad ibn 'Ashir (d. 763 AH); Haykal thinks his attachment to the Shadhili way came late, when companions asked him to comment on Ibn 'Ata' Allah's Hikam, so that it came not through initiation and chain but through studying them. {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 30} Haykal credits the preservation of the Shadhili heritage to [[person/ibn-ata-allah-al-iskandari|Ibn 'Ata' Allah]], who collected the sayings of al-Shadhili and al-Mursi, and to Ibn 'Abbad, whose commentary spread their views in the Maghrib and al-Andalus. {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 31}

## Private life and office
His biographers say he never married and served himself; Haykal quotes al-Taftazani that this was a personal choice and that many Shadhili shaykhs married. {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 31} {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 32} He was no secluded recluse: he was imam and preacher of the [[building/qarawiyyin|Qarawiyyin]] mosque in Fez for fifteen years until his death, esteemed by the sultan. {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 33}

## Death and pupils
His biographers agree that he died in Rajab 792 AH; Abu Zakariyya al-Sarraj specifies Friday 3 Rajab after the afternoon prayer. {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 33} His tomb in [[place/fez|Fez]] is still visited. {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 34} Among his pupils were [[person/abu-zakariya-al-sarraj-yahya|Yahya al-Sarraj]] (d. 805 AH) and Ibn al-Sakkak (d. 818 AH), both buried with him. {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 34}

## Works
First among them is [[work/ghayth-al-mawahib-al-aliyya|Ghayth al-Mawahib al-'Aliyya bi-Sharh al-Hikam al-'Ata'iyya]]; he is also said to have versified the Hikam; he wrote the Greater Epistles, dedicated to Yahya al-Sarraj, the Lesser Epistles, Tahqiq al-'Alama fi Ahkam al-Imama, a collection of sermons, prayers arranged on the Beautiful Names, answers on questions of the sciences, epistles on Qut al-Qulub, and Fath al-Tuhfa wa-Ida'at al-Sharfa on hadith. {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 35} {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 36} {cite:hikam-ibn-abbad-haykal-ahram-1988 p. 37}
