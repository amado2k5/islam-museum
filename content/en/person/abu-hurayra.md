## Brief
Abu Hurayra knew the Prophet for only about three years — and narrated more hadith than anyone else in the Sunni collections. How one man produced thousands of reports is the question scholars still ask.

## Life
A man of the tribe of Daws in the Yemeni highlands, he came to Medina and embraced Islam about 629 CE, around the time of [[event/battle-of-khaybar|Khaybar]] {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}. For the remaining years of the Prophet's life, the tradition pictures him permanently at the mosque's edge — poor, often hungry, memorizing {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

After 632 he became the flood: reports in his name run into the thousands in the Sunni collections, on law, ritual, the afterlife, and the Prophet's sayings. He served briefly as governor of Bahrayn under [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar]], and died in Medina about 678–681 {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.

:::perspectives
- **Sunni**: Sunni tradition venerates him as the foremost narrator — a man who gave up everything to memorize the Prophet's words {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}.
- **Academic**: Historians and hadith critics note that many reports in his name are later attributions; the sheer volume, in three years of companionship, is treated as a phenomenon of the tradition's growth {cite:watt-1956-medina}.
- **Twelver Shia**: Twelver Shia tradition is cautious about Abu Hurayra, accepting his narrations selectively and seeing some as serving later Umayyad interests {cite:momen-1985}.
:::

## Legacy
However the reports are weighed, a large part of what Sunni Islam knows the Prophet said reaches us through this one man's memory.

## For young visitors
Abu Hurayra spent three years with the Prophet and remembered so much that his name sits under thousands of stories and sayings — more than anyone else's.
