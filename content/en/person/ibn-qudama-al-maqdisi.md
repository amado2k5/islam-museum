## Overview
Muwaffaq al-Din Abu Muhammad Abdallah ibn Ahmad ibn Qudama al-Maqdisi al-Hanbali, a jurist from Jerusalem. {cite:lumat-al-itiqad}

## Works
He wrote [[work/lumat-al-itiqad|Lum'at al-I'tiqad]] (The Gleam of Belief), on the creed of the people of the Sunna in the manner of the early generations. The title page of the edition calls him a Hanbali. {cite:lumat-al-itiqad}

He also wrote [[work/al-mughni|al-Mughni]]. {cite:lumat-al-itiqad-ibn-uraiyir-2022}

## Birth and death
He was born in Jammaʿil in 541 AH, and died in [[place/damascus|Damascus]] in 620 AH, according to the editor's introduction to the second edition of the *Lum'a*. It is also said that he died on the day of Eid al-Fitr and was buried on the slope of Qasiyun. Ibn Rajab confirms both dates in detail, as below. {cite:lumat-al-itiqad-ibn-uraiyir-2022 p.40}

## Ibn Taymiyya's praise
The editor relays from [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] that after al-Awza'i no jurist more learned than al-Muwaffaq had entered Syria. {cite:lumat-al-itiqad-ibn-uraiyir-2022}

## In al-Dhayl ala Tabaqat al-Hanabila
[[person/ibn-rajab|Ibn Rajab]]'s [[work/dhayl-tabaqat-al-hanabila|al-Dhayl]] calls him the jurist, ascetic and imam, Shaykh al-Islam and one of the eminent, brother of [[person/abu-umar-al-maqdisi|Shaykh Abu Umar]]. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 133} He was born in Sha'ban 541 at [[place/jammail|Jamma'il]] and came to [[place/damascus|Damascus]] with his family at the age of ten; he read the Qur'an, memorised the Mukhtasar of al-Khiraqi, and heard hadith from his father [[person/ahmad-ibn-muhammad-ibn-qudama|Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn Qudama]], Abu al-Makarim ibn Hilal, Abu al-Ma'ali ibn Sabir and others. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 133}

## The journey to Baghdad
In 561 he travelled to [[place/baghdad|Baghdad]] with his cousin [[person/abd-al-ghani-al-maqdisi|the hafiz Abd al-Ghani]]; the two heard much from Hibat Allah al-Daqqaq, Ibn al-Batti, Sa'd Allah al-Dajaji, [[person/abd-al-qadir-al-jilani|Shaykh Abd al-Qadir]] and many others, and he also heard in Mecca from al-Mubarak ibn al-Tabbakh and in Mosul from its preacher. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 133} He stayed briefly at Shaykh Abd al-Qadir's school and read part of the Khiraqi with him; when the shaykh died he attached himself to Abu al-Fath [[person/ibn-al-mani|Ibn al-Manni]], studying the school's law, disputation and legal theory with him until he excelled, and stayed in Baghdad about four years, as al-Diya' reports from his mother, the shaykh's sister. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 134} Sibt ibn al-Jawzi mentions a return to Baghdad in 567, and al-Nasih Ibn al-Hanbali says he made the pilgrimage in 574 and then spent a year in Baghdad attending Ibn al-Manni's lessons. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 134}

## Al-Mughni and the other works
Back in Damascus he set to work on [[work/al-mughni|al-Mughni]], his commentary on al-Khiraqi; al-Nasih Ibn al-Hanbali calls it an eloquent book on the school in ten volumes, on which he laboured and excelled and with which he adorned the school. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 134} Ibn Rajab sorts his writings: on the principles of religion, al-Burhan on the question of the Qur'an, al-I'tiqad, the Question of Elevation, Dhamm al-Ta'wil, the Book of Decree, the Virtues of the Companions and an epistle forbidding the study of the theologians' books; on hadith, an abridgement of al-Khallal's al-Ilal; on law, al-Mughni in ten volumes, [[work/al-kafi-ibn-qudama|al-Kafi]] in four, [[work/al-muqni-ibn-qudama|al-Muqni']], an abridgement of al-Hidaya, al-Umda, the Rites of Pilgrimage and Dhamm al-Waswas; on legal theory, [[work/rawdat-al-nazir|al-Rawda]]. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 139} On language and genealogy he wrote Qun'at al-Arib on rare words, al-Tabyin on the lineages of Quraysh and al-Istibsar on the lineages of the Ansar, and among works of piety the Book of the Penitents, the Book of Those Who Love One Another in God and the Book of Tenderness and Weeping. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 140}

Ibn Rajab describes his method in creed as that of the hadith imams, packed with hadiths, reports and chains of transmission in the manner of Imam Ahmad; he did not hold with plunging into the fine points of theology alongside the theologians, even to refute them, and he enjoined passing over the attributes found in the Book and the Sunna as they came, without explanation, asking how, likening, distortion, figurative interpretation or denial. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 139} He quotes [[person/izz-al-din-ibn-abd-al-salam|al-Izz ibn Abd al-Salam]] as saying he had seen nothing in the books of Islam to equal Ibn Hazm's al-Muhalla and al-Mughni in quality, and that he did not feel at ease giving fatwas until he owned a copy of al-Mughni. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 140}

## Praise from the scholars
Ibn Rajab gathers many testimonies. [[person/sibt-ibn-al-jawzi|Sibt ibn al-Jawzi]] said that after his brother Abu Umar and al-Imad no one of his time was more ascetic or scrupulous; he was very modest, turned away from the world, recited a seventh of the Qur'an every day and night, and usually prayed the sunna prayers only at home. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 134} [[person/ibn-al-najjar|Ibn al-Najjar]] said: he was the imam of the Hanbalis at the congregational mosque, trustworthy, a proof, noble, of abundant merit, very careful, often silent, and wrote fine works on the school and on disputed questions. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 134} {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 135} Umar ibn al-Hajib's dictionary calls him imam of the imams and mufti of the community, and [[person/abu-shama-al-dimashqi|Abu Shama]] says that after his brother's death he led the prayer at the Muzaffari mosque and preached on Fridays when present. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 135} Ibn Rajab reports through more than one channel that [[person/ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] said no one more learned in law than al-Muwaffaq had entered Syria since al-Awza'i, and notes that al-Diya' and al-Dhahabi each wrote a separate biography of him. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 136} Al-Diya' called him an imam in the Qur'an and its exegesis, in hadith and its difficulties, unique in his time in law, and an imam in disputation, inheritance law, legal theory, grammar and arithmetic. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 136} Abu Bakr ibn Ghanima said he knew no one in his time who had reached the rank of independent reasoning (*ijtihad*) except al-Muwaffaq, and [[person/ibn-al-salah|Ibn al-Salah]] said: I have not seen the like of Shaykh al-Muwaffaq. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 136} {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 137} [[person/abdallah-al-yunini|Abd Allah al-Yunini]] said he never debated anyone without smiling, so that people said: this shaykh kills his opponent with his smile. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 137}

## Fatwas and teaching
For a time he held a debating circle on Fridays after the prayer in the Damascus mosque, giving it up late in life; students read with him from early morning until the sun was high, and again from noon to sunset. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 137} Ibn Rajab collects a selection of his fatwas outside his famous books, among them his answer that Abu Bakr's caliphate was established by the Prophet's explicit designation in many reports, against the view that it rested on the Companions' consensus. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 144} {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 146} He also records his readiness to withdraw his own reasoning: on a question of agency he wrote that he had given his fatwa by his own ijtihad, and that once the view of the school's authorities was shown to differ, theirs was preferable and returning to it was required. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 147}

## Wonders reported of him
Ibn Rajab relays from Sibt ibn al-Jawzi and others reports told of him as marvels: a man cured after al-Muwaffaq recited verses over him and passed his hand over his back, and accounts, transmitted with chains, of his walking on water. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 137} {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 138} These are reports attributed in the book to their narrators and are given here as told; the edition's corrector adds in a footnote that the Companions of the Messenger of God were more deserving of such things in the conquests of the lands. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 138}

## Death and children
He died on Saturday, the day of Eid al-Fitr 620, at his home in Damascus; the funeral prayer was held the next day and he was carried to the slope of Qasyun and buried there, with a vast crowd filling the mountain paths. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 142} Sibt ibn al-Jawzi names his sons Abu al-Fadl Muhammad, Abu al-Izz Yahya and Abu al-Majd Isa, and says only Isa left descendants, whose line then died out; Ibn Rajab adds that Muhammad died young at Hamadan in 599, and that Isa held the preaching and imamate of the Muzaffari mosque and died in 615. {cite:dhayl-tabaqat-hanabila-faqi-v2 p. 143}
