## Overview
Abd al-Qadir dan Tafa (1804 – 1863/4), born Abd al-Qadir ibn al-Mustafa, was a historian, theologian, philosopher, poet and jurist of the Sokoto Caliphate, considered "the most learned" scholar of his time. A grandson of Usman dan Fodio through his mother, he inherited and ran his father's teaching institute at Salame, advised caliphs, and left at least seventy-two works, above all on history and philosophy. {cite:wikipedia-abd-al-qadir-dan-tafa-en} {cite:wikipedia-abd-al-qadir-dan-tafa-ar}

## Name and family
"Dan Tafa" is Hausa for "son of Tafa": his father, Mustafa ibn Muhammad, a respected scholar, was called Mallam Tafa, "Mallam" signifying a learned man. Mallam Tafa was a trusted servant of Shehu Usman dan Fodio — chief of the Fodiawa clan's scribes (Amir al-kuttab) and its librarian, a companion of the Shehu's preaching tours before the jihad, and afterwards the Caliphate's chief secretary, with a voice in the succession of 1842. His mother, Khadijah, was the eldest daughter of Usman dan Fodio, herself a scholar and prolific writer who authored more than six works, translated Khalil's Mukhtasar into Fulfulde, and educated the women of the Fodiawa — her sister Nana Asma'u among her students. Dan Tafa was born in 1804 at Fankaaji, a village in Gobir, just before the hijra that opened the Sokoto revolution; the delay of the hijra is itself remembered around his birth. {cite:wikipedia-abd-al-qadir-dan-tafa-en} {cite:wikipedia-abd-al-qadir-dan-tafa-ur}

## Education
Both parents were his first teachers. His father taught him fiqh (jurisprudence), lughat (linguistics), tafsir (Qur'an commentary) and tarikh (history) — learning Mallam Tafa had himself received from his mentor Abdullahi dan Fodio. His uncle Muhammad Sanbu taught him Sufism, gnosis and the esoteric sciences, and another uncle, Muhammad Mudi dan Laima, taught him the elemental sciences: medicine, arithmetic, astronomy, philosophy, the science of magic squares (al-awfaq) and political science. {cite:wikipedia-abd-al-qadir-dan-tafa-en}

## Salame and the caliphs
Abdullahi dan Fodio had encouraged Mallam Tafa to found an institute, and Sultan Muhammad Bello gave him land near Sokoto, at Dama by Salame, where the school drew students from across the caliphate. Mallam Tafa ran it until his death in 1856; Dan Tafa then took charge, and scholars travelled from as far as Timbuktu to it throughout the nineteenth century. Building on the works of the three dead founders — Usman, Abdullahi and Bello — he became the court generation's sought-after adviser on history, Islam and leadership; when Heinrich Barth visited Katsina, a scholar from Tuat described Dan Tafa to him as "the most learned of the present generation of the inhabitants of Sokoto". He was the close friend of Ahmad ibn Sultan Abi Bakr Atiku, commander of the Chimola ribat in the Rima valley, who promised him the Wazirate — the caliphate's second office — should he ever become caliph. In the succession after Caliph Ali ibn Bello, the Waziri Abd al-Qadir dan Gidado at first refused to nominate Ahmad because of that promise, and relented only when Ahmad undertook to appoint Dan Gidado's son Ibrahim Khalilu instead; Ahmad, elected, kept his word, and Dan Tafa served his friend as before. He retired to his ribat at Salame, died there not long after — at sixty, in 1863/4 — and was buried beside his mother behind his father's school; his aunt Nana Asma'u composed his elegy, Tilfin Bawa. His wife Fatima, a daughter of Muhammad Bello, died a year after him, in 1865; their sons Muallayidi and Abd al-Raazqid were scholars, and Abd al-Raazqid's line carried the family's learning to Maiurno on the Blue Nile after the fall of Sokoto in 1903. {cite:wikipedia-abd-al-qadir-dan-tafa-en}

## Writings
John Hunwick's catalogue of the Arabic literature of Africa lists at least seventy-two of his titles. His best-known historical work is the Rawdat al-afkar (The Sweet Meadows of Contemplation), written in 1824; the Mawsufat al-sudan (Description of the Black Lands), completed in 1864, describes the history of West Africa, and the Qataif al-jinan explores the topography, history and culture of the Sudan and the Maghreb. Much was lost when the French Voulet-Chanoine expedition devastated Salame in 1898 and his institute's books were burnt or pillaged; forty-four of the works survive with his grandson, Sheikh Bello ibn Abd'r Raazqid, Waziri of Maiurno. {cite:wikipedia-abd-al-qadir-dan-tafa-en}
