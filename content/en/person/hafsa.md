## Overview
Hafsa bint Umar was a wife of the [[person/muhammad|Prophet]] and a daughter of the second caliph. Sunni tradition remembers her above all as the keeper of the collected Qur'anic codex — the copy from which the standard text was made. The Encyclopaedia of Islam article on her adds a fuller account of her family, her marriage, the household episodes in which her name appears, her conduct after the Prophet's death and the fate of the sheets in her keeping. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 72} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 73} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 74}

## Family and marriage
The article reports that Hafsa, the daughter of [[person/umar-ibn-al-khattab|Umar ibn al-Khattab]], is said to have been born five years before Muhammad's mission, while Quraysh were rebuilding the Kaba. Her mother was Zaynab bint Maz'un, a sister of [[person/uthman-ibn-mazun|Uthman ibn Maz'un]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 72} Her first husband, whom the OCR of the article gives as Khumays ibn Hudhafa al-Sahmi, a man of Quraysh, died at Medina on the return from Badr, a veteran of the battle, and left her a widow without children. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 72} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 73}

Her father offered her in marriage to [[person/abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]] and to [[person/uthman-ibn-affan|Uthman ibn Affan]]. Uthman refused, saying he did not wish to marry at that time. Abu Bakr said nothing and later excused himself to Umar, explaining that he had understood that Muhammad himself meant to marry Hafsa. Muhammad then proposed and Umar accepted with enthusiasm. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 73} The article judges it very likely that the Prophet was led by policy, wishing to bind to himself so valuable a supporter as Umar, all the more because shortly before he had asked for the hand of Abu Bakr's daughter [[person/aisha|Aisha]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 73}

According to the article, Hafsa emigrated to Medina with her father. Most sources place her marriage to Muhammad in Shaban of 3 AH, February 625 CE, after his marriage to Aisha and before the battle of [[event/battle-of-uhud|Uhud]], which made her his fourth wife. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 73}

## Episodes in the Prophet's household
The article lists several occasions on which Hafsa's name appears in the sources.

### The secret and the honey
It describes the opening verses of the sixty-sixth sura as alluding to a domestic event or events, in which God reproaches the Prophet about his wives, notes that one of them divulged a secret he had entrusted to her, mentions two wives acting together against him, and ends with the threat of a general repudiation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 73} Commentators, authors on the occasions of revelation, biographers and hadith scholars explain this as follows, the article says. While Hafsa was away, Muhammad had [[person/maria-al-qibtiyya|Mary the Copt]] in her room. Hafsa returned, found them and made a scene. He calmed her by swearing to have no more relations with his slave woman, while asking her to keep the matter secret. She told her friend Aisha, and the news spread. The Prophet, annoyed, divorced Hafsa but soon took her back, because Gabriel came down and told him to do so, since she was very devout and was to be his wife in Paradise, a report the article ties to Ibn Sad. The article adds that Umar's distress over his daughter's treatment probably also weighed on the decision. The Prophet released himself from his oath by an expiation and kept away from his wives for twenty-nine days. The article records that Noldeke dated the episode to 7 AH, 628 to 629 CE, and Caetani to 9 AH, 630 to 631 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 73}

The article notes that some hadith reporters were uneasy with this story as dishonourable to the Prophet. They said that the secret told to Hafsa, and by her to Aisha, was that Abu Bakr would succeed Muhammad and Umar would follow Abu Bakr, a version it traces to al-Baladhuri. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 73}

For the first verse of the same sura the sources give another occasion. Muhammad stayed with one of his wives, usually named as [[person/umm-salama|Umm Salama]] but sometimes as Hafsa, longer than usual because she had offered him a honey drink he liked. Aisha, Hafsa and some other wives agreed that each would tell him, when he visited, that he smelled unpleasant, as though he had eaten a sweet resin. He had not, and the smell could only come from the honey. He then denied himself honey, though God had permitted it, and he later lifted the prohibition. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 73} The article adds that, according to one hadith in al-Bukhari, the secret and the oath mentioned in the Qur'an belong to this prohibition of honey and not to the story of Mary the Copt. In its judgement, some early transmitters, following a different ethical outlook from that of the Prophet's circle, tried to soften the accounts handed down by others, and the same tendency to draw a veil over Muhammad's domestic life shows in modern commentaries, notably in the notes of Muhammad Ali's Ahmadiyya translation. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 73}

### The bride from the Jawn
Another episode, unconnected with these, concerns Aisha and Hafsa acting together against Asma bint al-Numan al-Jawniyya, a woman of noble family whom Muhammad had sent for from her tribe in order to marry her. After adorning her for the meeting, they advised her to say, when he first approached, that she sought refuge with God from him, telling her this pleased him. Muhammad covered his face with his sleeve, said three times that he was the one who took refuge, and left. He then sent the bride home with gifts, and the article says she never married again and died in grief. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 73}

### The four privileged wives
The article notes that a verse of the thirty-third sura authorises the Prophet to call to him whichever wife he chose, without observing an order. Commentators name Aisha, Zaynab and Umm Salama among those so favoured, but al-Baladhuri and al-Yaqubi add Hafsa, so that she appears as one of four privileged wives. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 73}

## The Prophet's last illness
Some hadiths, the article says, report attempts by Aisha and Hafsa to arrange private talks between the dying Prophet and their fathers, to the exclusion of the other Companions and above all of [[person/ali-ibn-abi-talib|Ali]]. The author finds this possible but notes that other hadiths contradict these about who was summoned to his bedside, so it cannot be told which are closer to the truth. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 73} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 74} Some hadiths also bring Hafsa into the question of who should lead the public prayer. On Aisha's advice, or Abu Bakr's through her, Hafsa is said to have suggested that the Prophet give the task to Umar rather than Abu Bakr, as Abu Bakr was too weak and his voice would be choked by tears. The article finds it strange that such advice should come from Aisha, but points out that the one chosen might have suffered rather than gained by taking the Prophet's place at a time when the duty carried no political meaning, and it notes that other hadiths differ completely. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 73} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 74}

## After the Prophet's death
Like the other wives, Hafsa received an annual allowance and the respect of the Muslims, but the article says she played no political role, even under her father's caliphate. What is reported of her then is minor. Some Companions asked her to persuade Umar to allocate more to himself from the public treasury, but he, taking the Prophet's example as his guide, would not. Seeing him dressed too meanly, she urged him to spend more on himself. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 74}

After [[person/abu-lulu-firuz|Abu Lulu]] killed her father, she was, in the article's account, among those who urged [[person/ubaydallah-ibn-umar|Ubaydallah ibn Umar]] to take vengeance on al-Hurmuzan. Her brother [[person/abdullah-ibn-umar|Abdallah]] is reported to have cried "God have pity on Hafsa!" because of this interference. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 74} When Aisha rose with [[person/talha-ibn-ubaydullah|Talha]] and al-Zubayr against Ali, Hafsa wished to join her, but Abdallah dissuaded her. The article also says that at the conference at [[place/adhruh|Adhruh]] she urged Abdallah to take part in order to prevent a split among the Muslims, while adding that nothing proves she took any part in events during the civil wars. The OCR of this passage is partly broken, so its details are reported with caution. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 74}

She died, by most sources, at Medina in Shaban 45 AH, October to November 665 CE, in the caliphate of [[person/muawiya-ibn-abi-sufyan|Muawiya]]. Some give 41 AH, 661 to 662 CE, soon after his accession, and the article records that Ibn al-Athir gives 27 AH, 647 to 648 CE, marking that date with an exclamation mark. [[person/marwan-ibn-al-hakam|Marwan ibn al-Hakam]], then governor of Medina, followed her bier and led the funeral prayer. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 74}

## The sheets of the Qur'an
The article, following Noldeke and Schwally, says the first collection of Qur'anic fragments made by [[person/zayd-ibn-thabit|Zayd ibn Thabit]] on Abu Bakr's orders was treated as private property and stayed with Abu Bakr. After his death it passed to Umar and then to Hafsa. The committee appointed by Uthman to produce the official text used these sheets and returned them to their owner when the collation was finished. According to a report from al-Baladhuri that the article cites, Marwan after her death had them handed over to him and destroyed them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 74}

## Character and standing
The article judges that Hafsa had no outstanding personality: she was neither lively, witty and attractive like Aisha, nor discreet, serious and helpful like Umm Salama. Her character, it says, emerges unclearly from the accounts, and although some hadiths say she took after her father, it is hard to see in what; perhaps they mean how quickly she flared up or her ability to get her own way. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 74} When Muhammad once threatened to divorce all his wives, the article reports, her father advised her to make no excessive demands, not to contradict Muhammad or speak behind his back, and not to be jealous of Aisha, who was more beautiful and more loved. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 74}

In the article's reading Hafsa was not jealous of Abu Bakr's daughter. The two were great friends, and several hadiths show them eating together, fasting together and helping one another to hold their place in the household, perhaps also to further their fathers' affairs. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 74} It adds that in the first years Hafsa had the upper hand over Aisha, still a child, but Aisha soon took the lead and dominated one of the two groups of wives in the harem, that of Aisha, Hafsa, Sawda and Safiyya. If Hafsa had any influence with Muhammad and later with her father, the article says, she used it so discreetly that the sources do not mention it. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 74} It also states that Hafsa could read and write, while other wives of the Prophet could only read. The sentence is cut off in the OCR and what it says of the remaining wives is not reported here. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 74}

## Life in the traditional account
Born about 605 CE, she married young, lost her husband to wounds after [[event/battle-of-badr]], and married the Prophet about 625 {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}. She lived in the Prophet's household in Medina until his death in 632.

Her lasting role came later. When the Qur'an was gathered under [[person/abu-bakr]] after the deaths of many reciters, the sheets were entrusted — in the traditional account — to Hafsa's keeping; when [[person/uthman-ibn-affan]] later ordered a standard recension, her copy was its base {cite:ibn-sad-tabaqat-bewley}. She died in Medina in 665 CE.

## Legacy
Between memory and manuscript, the tradition says the text passed through her house. That makes Hafsa a quiet but central figure in the history of the Qur'an as a written book.

## For young visitors
Before printing, books existed as a few handwritten pages. Hafsa kept the pages of the Qur'an safe in her home — and the copies we have today go back to hers.

## In Tareq al-Suwaidan's programme "Immortal Women"
[[person/tariq-al-suwaidan|Tareq al-Suwaidan]] gives the first half of the tenth episode of his programme [[work/nesaa-khalidat-program|"Immortal Women" (Nisa' Khalidat)]] to Hafsa bint Umar. He says her first husband, Khunays ibn Hudhafa, fought at Badr and was killed at Uhud, leaving her a widow of eighteen. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:01:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:01:30} Umar offered her to Abu Bakr, who said nothing, and to Uthman, who declined, and the Prophet told Umar that someone better than Uthman would marry Hafsa and Uthman would marry someone better than her: the Prophet married Hafsa and Uthman married Umm Kulthum. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:02:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:02:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:09:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep07 00:10:00} He takes from it that seeking a fine husband for a daughter is an old practice of the Companions. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:01:30}

In the household, he says, she and Aisha, close in age and the daughters of Abu Bakr and Umar, usually stood together. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:03:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:04:00} He tells how Umar warned her after learning that the Prophet's wives answered him back, and how the Prophet withdrew from his wives for a month and then offered them the choice between God and His Messenger and a kind release, and all chose God and His Messenger. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:06:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:07:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:09:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:09:30} He sees in these stories proof that they were human, with ordinary quarrels. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:05:30} He describes her as much given to fasting and night prayer, a learned woman who taught after the Prophet's death and took part in public life under the Rightly Guided Caliphs. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:04:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:11:00}

Her lasting role, in his account, was keeping the Qur'an: the sheets gathered under Abu Bakr by Zayd ibn Thabit passed to Umar and then stayed with her. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:12:00} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:14:00} When Uthman feared errors in people's copies, he relied on the copy kept by Hafsa, borrowed it on his oath to return it, sent copies to the provinces and gave the original back to her. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:14:30} {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:15:00} He says she died early in Mu'awiya's reign. {cite:assabile-tareq-al-suwaidan nesaa-khalidat/ep10 00:11:00}
