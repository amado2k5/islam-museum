## Overview
Kulthum al-Attabi settled in Raqqa, and his origin is in the land of the Jund of Qinnasrin and the Awasim. Al-Mas'udi describes him as learned, cultured and eloquent, and as having a skill in correspondence that few of his contemporaries had. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.408-410}

## With al-Ma'mun
He persuaded Yahya ibn Aktham to let al-Ma'mun know where he was. Al-Mas'udi relates from his words that the alms of prestige is to spend it on al-Musta'in. At al-Ma'mun's assembly a joke about his name passed between him and [[person/ishaq-al-mawsili|Ishaq al-Mawsili]], and according to the report Kulthum answered him with a reply that delighted those present. One of his sayings: "A man's secretary is his tongue, his chamberlain is his face, and his companion is all of him." {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.408-410}

## Encyclopaedia of Islam: letter-writer, poet and courtier
Abu 'Amr Kulthum ibn 'Amr ibn Ayyub al-'Attabi, letter-writer and poet, died at the beginning of the third/ninth century. He was a descendant of the pre-Islamic poet 'Amr ibn Kulthum, belonged to the Taghlib, and came from the neighbourhood of Kinnasrin in northern Syria. The dates of his birth and of his arrival in Baghdad are unknown. Ibn Tayfur reports that he stayed for a while at Marw and Nishapur to consult Persian manuscripts, which would suggest a dual Arab and Iranian culture. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 801}

He held an office in the administration, and was attached to the Barmakids. Their fall was almost fatal to him; accused of zandaqa, he had to flee to the Yemen to escape the punishment of Harun al-Rashid, but by his cleverness he regained the caliph's favour. He was also well regarded by Tahir ibn al-Husayn and by al-Ma'mun, and was protected by his patron, the general Malik ibn Tawq (died 259 AH, 873). In his last years he is said to have done penance. He is thought to have died about 220 AH (835), a date given by al-Kutubi; the Fihrist lists six of his works, which were probably on philology and adab. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 801-802}

His poetry, which the Fihrist says filled a collection of 100 folios, survives only in quotations by al-Jahiz, Ibn Qutayba, Ibn 'Abd Rabbih and al-Isfahani; a selection by Ibn Abi Tahir Tayfur (died 280 AH, 893) is lost, and the fragments were collected by F. Rifa'i. His work is that of a court poet, free in style, and shows the influence of Abu '1-'Atahiya and Abu Nuwas, whom he admired. A panegyric on al-Rashid enjoyed great fame. Apart from al-Marzubani, the medieval Muslims greatly esteemed him, and in literary history he represents the start of the neo-classical current that began in northern Syria and was carried on by Abu Tammam and al-Buhturi. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 1 p. 802}
