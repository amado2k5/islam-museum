## Summary
Al-Samawal ibn Yahya al-Maghribi was a mathematician and physician whose youthful [[work/al-bahir-fi-al-jabr|al-Bahir in algebra]] preserves information about lost work of [[person/al-karaji|Karaji]], including [[invention/karaji-binomial-coefficient-recurrence|binomial-coefficient generation]] {cite:samawal-mactutor Biography} {cite:samawal-dsb algebra}. His role in that record is transmitter of an attribution, not an additional inventor assigned a verified Sunni identity at composition {cite:samawal-mactutor Biography}.

## Family and learning
The biography describes his father as a Jewish scholar of religion and literature who moved from Fez to Baghdad, and his mother as originating in Basra; he grew up in a family valuing learning {cite:samawal-mactutor Biography}. He studied medicine and mathematics while young, learning Indian calculation, astronomical tables, surveying, algebra and early Euclidean books, then continued independently after surpassing his teachers' instruction {cite:samawal-mactutor Biography} {cite:samawal-dsb biography}. He read Abu Kamil and Karaji, particularly admiring the latter without abandoning criticism or the search for improvements {cite:samawal-mactutor Biography}. No certain birth year is extracted from approximate biographical headings.

## Algebra and testimony
Al-Bahir has four parts dealing with operations and roots, unknown quantities, irrational magnitudes and problem classification {cite:samawal-mactutor Biography}. The biography describes polynomial operations, negative quantities, division and root extraction, alongside retained geometrical demonstrations for quadratic equations {cite:samawal-mactutor Biography}. Book2 preserves the coefficient computation attributed to Karaji, whose original calculation the sources describe as lost {cite:samawal-mactutor Biography} {cite:samawal-dsb algebra}. Anbouba suggests that the work may have been repeatedly revised, so not every surviving passage necessarily belongs to the same youthful moment {cite:samawal-dsb algebra}.

## Religious identity over time
The biography reports a Jewish upbringing and conversion to Islam at Maragha on8 November1163, with a delay connected to avoiding distress to his father {cite:samawal-mactutor Biography}. Anbouba recounts the conversion through the autobiography; this is modern scholarly mediation, not our own reading of the original religious text {cite:samawal-dsb biography}. **Sunni museum frame:** this account neither makes the whole al-Bahir a post-conversion composition nor independently establishes a personal Sunni affiliation {cite:samawal-mactutor Biography} {cite:samawal-dsb algebra}. We retain the person and scientific role without erasing an earlier phase or making chronology a judgement on mathematical validity.

## Travel and medicine
The biography records journeys in Iraq, Syria, Kohistan and Azerbaijan and medical activity around Maragha, without linking every journey to a particular copy of the coefficient table {cite:samawal-mactutor Biography}. The dictionary entry describes princely patients and medical writings, including Nuzhat al-ashab, together with reports of medicines for which it supplies no independent surviving account {cite:samawal-dsb biography}. This is historical information about the author, not a therapeutic recipe or a recommendation to reuse a medicine.
