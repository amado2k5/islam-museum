# Cafer Seydamet Kırımer

Cafer Seydamet (1 September 1889 – 3 April 1960), who took the surname Kırımer in 1934, was a Crimean Tatar politician and writer and a leading ideologist of the Crimean Tatar national movement. He was born at Kızıltaş village in the Yalta district of Crimea into a well-off peasant family, took his first schooling in Crimea and read law in Istanbul, where in 1908 he co-founded the Crimean Tatar Students' Association with Noman Çelebicihan and other students.{cite:wikipedia-cafer-seydamet-kirimer-en}{cite:wikipedia-cafer-seydamet-kirimer-uk}

His 1911 essay on the oppressed Crimean Tatar nation, published under a pen name, brought a Russian extradition demand, and he continued his law studies in Paris at the Sorbonne. Returning to Russia in 1914, he studied at St Petersburg University, then entered a cadet school and served in a reserve regiment at Izmail during the First World War.{cite:wikipedia-cafer-seydamet-kirimer-en}{cite:wikipedia-cafer-seydamet-kirimer-ru}

In December 1917 the Qurultay appointed him minister of war and foreign affairs of the proclaimed Crimean People's Republic. After the Bolshevik seizure of Crimea in January 1918 he fled to Kyiv, returned in May, and served as foreign minister in the Crimean Regional Government of Sulkiewicz, travelling to Berlin to argue against the loss of Crimean independence before resigning and going into lasting exile.{cite:wikipedia-cafer-seydamet-kirimer-uk}{cite:wikipedia-cafer-seydamet-kirimer-ru}

In exile he lived in Istanbul and Lausanne, supported the Promethean movement, and wrote on Crimea, the Russian Revolution and Bolshevism, including a 1934 book on Ismail Gasprinsky; a large body of memoirs and stories appeared after his death. He died in Istanbul on 3 April 1960 and was buried in the city's Feriköy Cemetery.{cite:wikipedia-cafer-seydamet-kirimer-tr}{cite:wikidata-cafer-seydamet-kirimer}

The English, Russian, Turkish and Ukrainian accounts and Wikidata agree on his dates and career. None of them contains any statement of his religion or school — his writings on Turkism and the national cause are political ideology — so his faith is recorded as unknown.
