## Summary
Syed Ahmad Khan (17 October 1817 – 27 March 1898) was an Indian Muslim reformer, educator and judge, founder of the Aligarh educational movement that reshaped the course of modern Indian Islam. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

He served in the East India Company's judiciary, wrote The Causes of the Indian Mutiny after the 1857 rebellion, founded the Scientific Society in 1864 and then the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College at Aligarh in 1875 — the seed of Aligarh Muslim University — and was among the first to argue the two-nation theory: that Hindus and Muslims in India were two separate nations. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

## Early life and education
He was born in Delhi on 17 October 1817, in the last years of the Mughal Empire. His father, Syed Muhammad Muttaqi, was an adviser to the emperor Akbar Shah II, and his mother Aziz-un-Nisa was the formative influence on his strict upbringing. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

His education began in 1822 under Shah Ghulam Ali, his father's spiritual mentor in the Naqshbandi tradition; a female tutor taught him the Qur'an, and at Moulvi Hamiduddin's maktab he studied Persian, Arabic, mathematics and astronomy, then medicine under Hakim Ghulam Haider Khan. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en} {cite:wikidata-syed-ahmad-khan}

As a young man he was influenced by Shah Waliullah and by Syed Ahmad Barelvi's Mujahidin movement, while opposing the Wahhabi movement, and he read Ghalib, Zauq, Mill, Addison and Steele, and the Tunisian reformer Hayreddin Pasha. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

## Career
He entered East India Company service in 1838 as a clerk in Delhi, moved to Agra in 1839, became a munsif (judge) at Fatehpur Sikri in 1841 and in Delhi in 1846, and a sadr amin at Bijnor in 1855. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

During the 1857 rebellion he was posted at Bijnor and saved the lives of many British officers and residents, while his mother died at Meerut amid the privations of those months. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

At Moradabad in 1858 he began The Causes of the Indian Mutiny (Asbab-e-Baghawat-e-Hind), analysing the rebellion as the fruit of the Company's policies and its estrangement from Indians. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

Posted to Ghazipur in 1862, he founded the Victoria School in 1863 and the Scientific Society in 1864, to translate Western knowledge into Urdu; that year he was posted to Aligarh, which became the seat of his project. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

In April 1869 he travelled to England with his sons Syed Hamid and Syed Mahmood, studied its educational and social order, and returned determined to build a university for India's Muslims on the model of Oxford and Cambridge. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

He retired from the judiciary in 1876 and settled in Aligarh, sat in the Imperial Legislative Council between 1878 and 1883, and received the KCSI and an honorary LLD from the University of Edinburgh in 1889. He died at Aligarh on 27 March 1898 and was buried at the Sir Syed Mosque on the college campus. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en} {cite:wikidata-syed-ahmad-khan}

## Works and achievements
In 1875 he founded the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College (MAO College) at Aligarh — described in the article as the first Muslim university institution in South Asia — which later became Aligarh Muslim University, the heart of the Aligarh movement. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

He published the journal Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq (Refinement of Morals) as the platform of his social and religious reform, urging Muslims towards modern education and a critical re-reading of their heritage. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

His books include Athar-ul-Sanadid, on the monuments and archaeology of Delhi; Tabyin al-Kalam, a comparative commentary; the Khutbat-e-Ahmadiya, written in reply to William Muir's Life of Mahomet; and a Qur'an commentary (Tafsir-ul-Quran) offering a rationalist reading that stirred wide controversy. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

He is regarded as a pioneer of the two-nation theory — the argument that India's Hindus and Muslims were two distinct nations that could not share one rule — which later became an ideological foundation for the creation of Pakistan. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

## Relationships
His first teacher Shah Ghulam Ali, the Naqshbandi mentor of his father; his maternal grandfather Khwaja Fariduddin, a wazir at the Mughal court; and his elder brother Syed Muhammad, founder of the Urdu weekly Syedul Akhbar, are all named in the sources; none yet has a record in this museum, so they appear here in prose. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en} {cite:wikidata-syed-ahmad-khan}

His son Syed Mahmood became the first Muslim judge of an Indian High Court; he and his brother Syed Hamid accompanied their father to England in 1869. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

His formal place relations recorded in this museum are his birth and residence in Delhi and his residence at Agra during his judicial service. Aligarh — the city of his project, death and burial — has no place record in the museum yet. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

## Positions and criticism
This section sets out positions as the sources report them. It is not a verdict by the museum.

His supporters regard him as the saviour of Indian Muslims after the catastrophe of 1857, who turned them towards modern education and an accommodation with colonial reality, and in Pakistan he is honoured as one of its founding fathers in thought. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}

Many scholars of his own age opposed him: they criticised his declared loyalty to Britain, rejected his rationalist reading of the Qur'an and his denial of some miracles and unseen matters, and counted his project a Westernisation of Islam. The argument over his legacy continues among historians — father of a Muslim renaissance in India to some, bearer of the seed of partition to others. {cite:wikipedia-syed-ahmad-khan-en}
