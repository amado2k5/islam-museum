## Overview
Al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf al-Thaqafi, of the Thaqif clan of [[place/taif|al-Ta'if]], was a governor for [[person/abd-al-malik-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan]], as al-Tabari relates. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.6 p.338} The Encyclopaedia of Islam calls him the most famous and most able governor of the [[polity/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyads]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 49} He was the commander of the army at the battle of [[event/battle-of-dayr-al-jamajim|Dayr al-Jamajim]] with [[person/abd-al-rahman-ibn-muhammad-ibn-al-ashath|Ibn al-Ash'ath]], and Ibn al-Ash'ath was defeated there. {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.6 p.346} {cite:tabari-tarikh-abu-al-fadl j.6 p.357} This page sets out the sources it draws on, and the traditional judgements of him are given as those of the sources, not as the museum's own.

## Origins and early career
The Encyclopaedia of Islam places his birth at al-Ta'if about 41 AH, 661 CE. It says his forebears were poor and of lowly origin, said to have worked as stone carriers and builders, and that his mother al-Fari'a, also of Thaqif, was the divorced wife of [[person/al-mughira-ibn-shuba|al-Mughira ibn Shu'ba]]. As a child he was nicknamed Kulayb, "little dog", which poets used in satire, and as a young man he was a schoolmaster in al-Ta'if. The article adds that little is known of his youth and early public life. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 49}

The change came in the first years of Abd al-Malik's reign, when he went from al-Ta'if to [[place/damascus|Damascus]] to serve in the police under the caliph's vizier. He drew the caliph's notice by restoring discipline in a short time among mutinous troops about to march on Iraq against [[person/musab-ibn-al-zubayr|Mus'ab ibn al-Zubayr]]. The article sees in the harsh means he used the method that later made him famous and notorious, and says he seems to have led the rearguard on that campaign. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 49}

## The siege of Mecca and the Hijaz
After the victory over Mus'ab at [[event/battle-of-maskin-mus-ab|Maskin]] in 72 AH, 691 CE, the caliph sent him from [[place/kufa|Kufa]] with about two thousand Syrians against [[person/abdullah-ibn-al-zubayr|Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr]], the anti-caliph at [[place/mecca|Mecca]]. He took al-Ta'if without a fight and used it as a base. His orders were first to negotiate and offer Ibn al-Zubayr immunity, then to starve him out, and in no case to shed blood in the Holy City. When talks failed he asked for reinforcements and for leave to take Mecca by force, received both, and bombarded the city with stones. The article says the bombardment continued through the pilgrimage and struck the [[building/kaaba|Kaaba]] and the pilgrims, and that a sudden thunderstorm which unsettled the soldiers was read by him to them as a promise of victory. After about seven months, with ten thousand men, among them two of Ibn al-Zubayr's sons, having gone over to him, Ibn al-Zubayr was killed in the fighting around the Kaaba in Jumada I 73 AH, October 692 CE. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 49} The article notes that the year 73 is sometimes called the Year of Unity. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 49}

Abd al-Malik rewarded him with the governorship of the [[place/hijaz|Hijaz]], [[place/yemen|Yemen]] and the Yamama. He led the pilgrimage in 73 and 74 AH and saw to the rebuilding of the Kaaba on its earlier foundations and dimensions. The article says he restored peace in the Hijaz with a severity that often made the caliph step in, and thinks the complaints of the people of the Hijaz may have helped bring about his transfer to Iraq. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 49} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 50}

## Governor of Iraq
He took over [[place/iraq|Iraq]] at the age of thirty-three in early 75 AH, 694 CE, on the death of the caliph's brother [[person/bishr-ibn-marwan|Bishr ibn Marwan]], at first without [[place/khurasan|Khurasan]] and [[place/sijistan|Sijistan]]. The article calls the post the most important and responsible in the state because of the constant Kharijite intrigues, and says the sermon with which he installed himself in Kufa is as famous as that of [[person/ziyad-ibn-abi-sufyan|Ziyad ibn Abihi]] at Basra. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 50} His first task was the troops of Kufa and Basra, camped under [[person/al-muhallab-ibn-abi-sufra|al-Muhallab ibn Abi Sufra]] but drifting back to the towns. He threatened death and plunder for anyone not back within three days, and the soldiers returned. A second mutiny, over reduced pay, followed and was put down. The article also reports a violent quarrel with [[person/anas-ibn-malik|Anas ibn Malik]] which, through Abd al-Malik's intervention, ended as a moral victory for the old Companion. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 50}

The troops were then used against the [[group/azariqa|Azariqa]], who had chosen the poet [[person/qatari-ibn-al-fujaa|Qatari ibn al-Fuja'a]] as caliph; al-Muhallab defeated them in 77 AH, 696 CE. The Kharijite leader [[person/shabib-al-kharijite|Shabib]] was beaten the same year with Syrian troops that al-Hajjaj requested, and the governor of al-Mada'in, [[person/mutarrif-ibn-al-mughira|al-Mutarrif ibn al-Mughira]], who had rebelled in alliance with the Kharijites, was also defeated. In 78 AH he was given Khurasan and Sijistan as well. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 50}

## In Iraq
Al-Mas'udi relates his famous sermon on arriving in Iraq, and his address to the people of Iraq in it. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.119} He says that he wrote to Abd al-Malik about the report of Ibn al-Ash'ath, and Abd al-Malik sent him armies. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.123} Then he preached in Kufa when the head of Ibn al-Ash'ath was brought to him. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.123}

He imprisoned [[person/al-ghadban-ibn-al-qabathari|al-Ghadban ibn al-Qabathari]] for three years and then released him after the affair of the green dome at Wasit. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.139}

## The revolt of Ibn al-Ash'ath
The article describes the revolt as far more dangerous than any earlier one, aimed not only at al-Hajjaj but at the dominant role of the Syrians and so at Umayyad rule itself. Al-Hajjaj sent Ibn al-Ash'ath to Sijistan with a splendidly equipped force, the Peacock Army. Ibn al-Ash'ath advanced carefully, and al-Hajjaj, impatient, urged him on in sharp letters and threatened to give the command to his brother. Ibn al-Ash'ath left the decision to his officers, who pledged allegiance to him, and with an army that grew to a hundred thousand he occupied Kufa and Basra and besieged the governor near Basra. Syrian troops under two sons of Abd al-Malik were told to negotiate first and to hold out the prospect of the governor's recall. When Ibn al-Ash'ath refused, they defeated him decisively in 82 AH, 701 CE, at Dayr al-Jamajim and at Maskin. He took his own life three years later; the article notes that the chronology is not fully certain. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 49} {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 50} For the revolt see also [[event/revolt-of-ibn-al-ashath|the revolt of Ibn al-Ash'ath]].

## Building Wasit and his death
The Umayyad state of al-Salabi says that al-Hajjaj built Wasit as a military base between Ahwaz, Basra and Kufa, and that Abd al-Malik on his deathbed advised al-Walid to honour him. {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 p.116} The Encyclopaedia of Islam dates the founding of [[place/wasit|Wasit]] to 83 AH, 702 CE, and says he made it his residence and moved most of the Syrian troops there, officially to protect the inhabitants from Syrian encroachment but in the article's judgement to isolate the Syrians from the Iraqis and keep them under his authority. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 50}

Al-Mas'udi says he died in Wasit in 95 AH at the age of fifty-four, and that his governorship lasted twenty years. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.157} The Encyclopaedia of Islam puts his death in Ramadan 95 AH, June 714 CE, at only fifty-two, one year before al-Walid, and says he was buried in Wasit and the traces of his grave were wiped out to protect it from profanation. It mentions stomach cancer as the reported cause, and notes that Barhebraeus gives consumption. It adds that few mourned him: the caliph, the poet [[person/jarir-ibn-atiya|Jarir]], [[person/khalid-ibn-abdallah-al-qasri|Khalid al-Qasri]] and above all his freedman [[person/yazid-ibn-abi-muslim|Yazid ibn Abi Muslim]], who dared to remind Sulayman of the dead man's merits. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 51}

## Under al-Walid
After the suppression of Ibn al-Ash'ath he secured, in 85 AH, 704 CE, the dismissal of [[person/yazid-ibn-al-muhallab|Yazid ibn al-Muhallab]] from Khurasan after Yazid ignored repeated summonses to Wasit. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 50} The article says [[person/al-walid-ibn-abd-al-malik|al-Walid]] left him a free hand and relied on him all the more because he owed him the succession, which al-Hajjaj had urged on Abd al-Malik against the claims of [[person/abd-al-aziz-ibn-marwan|Abd al-Aziz ibn Marwan]]. It credits him with preparing, with great care and at great cost, the eastern campaigns of the reign, and names three generals he had chosen: [[person/qutayba-ibn-muslim|Qutayba ibn Muslim]] for Transoxania, [[person/muhammad-ibn-al-qasim|Muhammad ibn al-Qasim al-Thaqafi]] for India, and Mudjdja'a ibn Si'r for Oman. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 50} In the article's account his relations with al-Walid were correct but formal, and he dreaded the accession of [[person/sulayman-ibn-abd-al-malik|Sulayman]], whom he had made an implacable opponent by interfering in the succession and by his measures against Yazid ibn al-Muhallab. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 51}

## Administration
Wishing to restore the prosperity of a country worn down by twenty years of war, he is credited in the article with several measures. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 50}

- Text of the Qur'an: the article says he worked for a uniform reading of the text, to end the theologians' disputes and, in the article's view, to purge it of anti-Umayyad allusions. It reports that the division into parts may go back to him, that new vowel points may have been introduced on his orders, and that he declared his sponsored text the only valid one and forbade the reading of [[person/abdullah-ibn-masud|Ibn Mas'ud]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 50}
- Coinage: in connection with Abd al-Malik's reform of 76 AH, 695 CE, he struck purely Arabic coins, first at Kufa and then at Wasit, which gradually displaced Byzantine and Sasanian money. Some theologians disapproved of the name of God being stamped on them, though the coins became legal tender. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 50}
- Tax records: he had the tax register, kept until then in Persian, translated into Arabic. See [[event/arabization-of-iraq-diwan|the Arabization of Iraq's kharaj diwan]]. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 50}
- Agriculture: the article describes efforts to drain marshes by canals and repair embankments, grants of uncultivated land, measures against the drift of country people into the towns, and an order that newly converted Muslims return to the land and keep paying the land tax. It also reports a story that he forbade the slaughter of cattle in the Sawad to preserve ploughing animals. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 50}

## Relations with the caliphs
The article calls him the most loyal servant a dynasty could wish for. Abd al-Malik often urged restraint, when the governor seemed extortionate in taxes, too free with public money or too ready to shed blood, but al-Hajjaj answered with practical reasons, sometimes in verse, and the caliph's trust was not shaken. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 50} The tie was also personal: a niece of his married the later caliph [[person/yazid-ibn-abd-al-malik|Yazid II]], and their first son was named al-Hajjaj after him. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 51} The article quotes a verse reply to Abd al-Malik, who had asked for less generosity with public funds, in which he defends his record, and says the caliph could only answer that he should act as he thought fit. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 51}

## Further reports in al-Mas'udi
Al-Mas'udi preserves other reports about him. Asked by Abd al-Malik to describe strife, he replied that it is kindled by whispering, reaped by complaint and brought forth by sermons. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 111} In his address on taking over Iraq, as al-Mas'udi gives it, he spoke of heads ripe for picking and rebuked the Iraqis as people of discord and hypocrisy, and when they did not return the caliph's greeting he called it the discipline of the son of Sumayya. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 119-121} Al-Mas'udi says he had Umayr ibn Dabi' killed so that the despatch of al-Muhallab against the Azariqa could proceed, and ordered two bridges over the Euphrates for their exit. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 122} He says Ibn al-Qarya was killed in 82 AH for going out with Ibn al-Ash'ath and writing letters for him. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 130-131} Before leaving for the pilgrimage he preached that he had set Muhammad over Iraq with the opposite charge to the one given about the Ansar, not to accept the good of its people nor overlook their wrongdoing. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 136} Al-Mas'udi also tells how he took Iraqi men into the desert to show Syrians that Iraqis were the more capable observers when a caravan came into view. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p. 705-706}

## Accounts of the sources
Al-Mas'udi relates that he counted those he had killed by the sword, apart from those killed in his wars, and found them to be a hundred and twenty thousand. He relates that at his death there were fifty thousand men and thirty thousand women in his prison. {cite:muruj-al-dhahab p.157} This is an account from al-Mas'udi, and the museum does not adopt the figures. Verifying them needs an additional source.

Al-Salabi says that al-Walid made him commander of the pilgrimage in 92 AH, and that [[person/umar-ibn-abd-al-aziz|Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz]] asked to be excused from passing through [[place/medina|Medina]], and al-Walid excused him. {cite:salabi-umayyad-state-2020 p.176}

## Assessment in the Encyclopaedia of Islam
The article says few figures of early Islam have been so much the subject of Arabic literature, with innumerable stories and verses for and against him. It reports that the Abbasids remembered him with hatred while envying the Umayyads such a governor. The author, A. Dietrich, judges that he could be stern and pitiless in the state's interest, but holds that the mass executions and other atrocities attributed to him are inventions of his enemies. This is the author's view; the traditional accounts above, such as al-Mas'udi's figures, are reported by their own authors, and the museum gives no verdict between them. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 51}

The article compares him with Ziyad ibn Abihi, quoting Wellhausen that both saw themselves as representatives of public order. It describes his chief faults as impatience and lack of self-control, notes that he punished bribery and illicit enrichment and so was hated by the officials, and calls him a cultivated man of unsurpassed eloquence who valued pure Arabic and kept company with poets such as Jarir, [[person/farazdaq|al-Farazdaq]] and [[person/layla-al-akhyaliyya|Layla al-Akhyaliyya]] while persecuting satirists. It calls him a devout Muslim, neither bigoted nor superstitious, and its author concludes that an unprejudiced judge sees in him one of the greatest statesmen of the Umayyads and of the whole Islamic world. {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 3 p. 51}

Modern historians' and others' wider assessments of him are not set out here and still need to be added.

## For Sprouts
Al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf al-Thaqafi, governor for Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan; he led the army against Ibn al-Ash'ath at Dayr al-Jamajim, built Wasit, and died in 95 AH.

## For Explorers
Al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf al-Thaqafi, governor for Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan per al-Tabari; he led the army at Dayr al-Jamajim where Ibn al-Ash'ath was beaten. Al-Mas'udi relates his famous sermon on coming to Iraq and his address to its people; that he wrote Abd al-Malik about Ibn al-Ash'ath's affair and was reinforced with armies, then preached in Kufa when Ibn al-Ash'ath's head reached him; and that he imprisoned al-Ghadban ibn al-Qaba'thari three years before freeing him after the Green Dome affair at Wasit. ## From Omar Abdelkafy's programme Bustan al-Amal
The Sheikh relates that when al-Hajjaj seized a young man to kill him, his mother wept and said: will you be killed wronged, my son? He said: mother, would you rather I were killed as a wrongdoer?
