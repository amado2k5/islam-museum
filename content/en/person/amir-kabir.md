# Amir Kabir

## Summary
Amir Kabir — Mirza Taghi Khan-e Farahani (9 January 1807 – 10 January 1852) — was the chief minister of Naser al-Din Shah from 1848 to 1851 and the most capable and innovative figure of the Qajar period. In three years he rebuilt the state's finances, army and administration, founded the Dar ol-Fonun college and the official newspaper Vaqaye'-ye Ettefaqiyeh, and made enemies at court and among the clergy whose privileges he cut; dismissed and exiled to Kashan, he was murdered in the Fin Garden on the young Shah's order. {cite:wikipedia-amir-kabir-en}

The day of his death is split across the sources: the English article gives 10 January 1852, while Wikidata carries 9 January 1852 on a Britannica reference. This record follows the article, with the variant stated. {cite:wikidata-amir-kabir} {cite:wikipedia-amir-kabir-en}

## Rise
He was born at Hazaveh, in the Farahan district near Arak, the son of Karbalayi Mohammad Qorban, a cook who rose to steward in the household of the minister Qa'em-Maqam Farahani; the boy was educated in that household alongside the minister's children. He served on missions to Russia and to the Ottoman lands, and as tutor to the crown prince Naser al-Din in Azerbaijan. When the prince became Shah in 1848, Mirza Taghi Khan was made chief minister — shakhs-e avval, the first person of the state — with the titles Amir-e Nezam and, in time, Amir Kabir, the Great Commander. {cite:wikipedia-amir-kabir-en}

## The reforms
His ministry balanced the budget by cutting court pensions and reordering taxation, reformed the army and founded military industries, and regularised the administration and the judiciary. His two foundations outlived him: the Dar ol-Fonun, opened in Tehran to teach medicine, engineering, military science and languages with European and Persian instructors, and the newspaper Vaqaye'-ye Ettefaqiyeh, among the first Persian newspapers. He also issued an edict against ornate, formula-laden official prose, from which a plainer modern Persian administrative style is dated. {cite:wikipedia-amir-kabir-en}

## Religion as policy
His government suppressed the Babi upheavals of 1848–51, and in 1850 he ordered the execution of the Bab; at the same time he sought to curb the political power of the clergy and moved to prohibit ta'ziyeh performances and ritual self-flagellation, which brought him the ulama's enmity. These were acts of state by a minister who treated religious movements and clerical privilege alike as questions of order; the sources record the policy and make no statement of a school of his own. {cite:wikipedia-amir-kabir-en}

## Fall and death
The coalition of notables he had excluded, in which the queen mother Mahd-e Olia was active, persuaded the Shah that Amir Kabir aimed at the throne. In November 1851 he was dismissed and exiled to Kashan, whose governorship was his last post; on 10 January 1852 he was murdered in the Fin Garden bathhouse on the Shah's order. Through his marriage to Ezzat ed-Dowleh, the Shah's sister, he had been the Shah's brother-in-law; his daughter Taj ol-Molouk married Naser al-Din's son Mozaffar ad-Din, making Amir Kabir the grandfather of Mohammad Ali Shah. {cite:wikipedia-amir-kabir-en} {cite:wikidata-amir-kabir}
