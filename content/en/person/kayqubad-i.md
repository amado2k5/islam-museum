## Overview
Kayqubad I, Ala al-Din, was the most distinguished of the Seljuk sultans of Rum, according to the Encyclopaedia of Islam, and many later rulers would connect themselves to him. His brother and predecessor Kayka'us I had removed him from power, and he succeeded him in 618 AH, 1220 CE {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 855}. The record also links him to [[polity/rum-seljuk-sultanate|the sultanate of Rum]], which reached its greatest extent under him.

## Foreign policy
The article says his policy made his dynasty one of the most powerful of its time. From the start he extended his rule over much of the Cilician Taurus, where he settled Turkmens, and he took Alaiyya, which became his winter residence and the place where he kept his treasury {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 855}. On the Black Sea he disputed the lordship of Trebizond and sent a naval expedition under the emir Chupan that put the Crimean harbour of Sughdaq under Seljuk protection {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 855}. In the east, with the help of the Ayyubid al-Ashraf, he annexed the Artuqid holdings on the right bank of the middle Euphrates, and he annexed the principality of the Mangujakids at Erzincan, which he made over to his eldest son Kaykhusraw {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 855}.

## The Khwarazmians
The article says the Khwarazmians took Akhlat from al-Ashraf, so Kaykubad called in both his former enemies and his friends. Al-Ashraf brought the main force, made up of Syrian troops, and the Khwarazmians were crushed at Yasi-Cimen, west of Erzincan. He then took the principality of Erzurum from its prince Jahan-Shah, who had sided with Jalal al-Din, took some frontier points from the Georgians and occupied Akhlat {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 855}. When the Ayyubid al-Kamil invaded Asia Minor in 631 AH, 1233 CE, he was defeated, and Kaykubad could press on into the upper Tigris region {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 856}.

## Death and buildings
He died in 634 AH, 1237 CE, before the Mongols had sent more than reconnaissance raids into Asia Minor. The article says the disaster that overtook his successor made him in the eyes of later generations the last great sovereign of his dynasty, and in practice the most powerful {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 856}. His reign was marked by many building projects, including ramparts, caravanserais, mosques and madrasas; excavations are gradually revealing his palaces of Kubadabad and Kaykubadiyya, and the article says he was a gifted man, strong in body and mind {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 856}. Kaykubadiyya was a palace he built between 1224 and 1226, about four miles northwest of Kayseri {cite:encyclopaedia-of-islam-2 vol. 4 p. 856}.
