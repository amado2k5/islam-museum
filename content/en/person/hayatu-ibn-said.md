## Overview
Shehu Hayatu ibn Sa'id (c. 1840 – 1898), also known as Hayatu Balda, was a nineteenth-century Sokoto scholar and the leading Mahdist leader of the Central Sudan. Great-grandson of Usman dan Fodio, he broke with the Sokoto Caliphate his ancestor had founded to follow the Sudanese Mahdi, built a Mahdist state at Balda in Adamawa, defeated the emirate's ruler in 1893, and ended as Imam of Rabih az-Zubayr's Bornu. {cite:wikipedia-hayatu-ibn-said-en} {cite:wikipedia-hayatu-ibn-said-fr}

## Early life
Hayatu was born around 1840 in Sokoto, likely in Kofar Rini, where his father resided. He was the eldest son of Sa'id ibn Bello — a son of the second caliph, Muhammad Bello — and so the great-grandson of Usman dan Fodio; his mother Khadijah was a daughter of Sarkin Rafi Idrisu of the family of Gidado dan Laima, the second Waziri of Sokoto. Known from a young age for his memory and intellect, he was educated by his father, a scholar of repute, and later devoted himself to study, teaching, writing and poetry, while also taking part in administering Gandi, a ribat (fortified frontier garrison) his father commanded. {cite:wikipedia-hayatu-ibn-said-en}

## In Sokoto
In the succession dispute of 1867, after the death of Caliph Ali Karami, Hayatu backed Ahmad al-Rufa'i, a son of Usman, over Abubakar Atiku na Rabah, arguing the son of the founder was the better qualified — and Rufa'i was appointed, with Hayatu among his closest advisers until 1873. In the late 1860s he succeeded his father in command of the Gandi ribat, but after some years gave it up — why is unclear: the large non-Muslim slave population of the garrison, some of it politically weighty, made Sharia administration hard for a scholar-commander. Returning to Sokoto under Caliph Abubakar Atiku (r. 1873–1877), he left the city for good in 1878 and settled in Adamawa, the caliphate's easternmost emirate. Contemporaries and historians have explained the move variously: a pilgrimage begun, disillusion with Sokoto after his father's failure to become caliph, a judgement that the caliphate's moral and intellectual standards were declining, and — in the belief of his later followers — a revelation that the Mahdi whose coming Usman had foretold was near. {cite:wikipedia-hayatu-ibn-said-en}

## Adamawa and the Mahdi
Received with honour at Yola as a descendant of Usman and a scholar, Hayatu arrived with thirty-three students and a large retinue, and spent his time teaching and travelling the emirate. In 1882 he journeyed east, a year after Muhammad Ahmad had proclaimed himself the Mahdi in the Sudan, and settled at Balda, near Bogo in the north of Adamawa, whence he launched his jihad against the non-Muslim peoples of the area — Musgum, Masa and Semaya — whom the Fulani of Bogo had failed for decades to subdue. Balda grew rapidly into a town of fighters, a school, a market and a mosque, and the Mahdist community spread across the Marua-Mandara region as far south as Mubi. In 1883 Hayatu sent a mission to Muhammad Ahmad pledging complete submission; the Mahdi replied appointing him his deputy (amil) over the Sokoto Caliphate, with instructions to "declare the jihad on anyone who disobeys God and His messenger and rejects our Mahdism". Sokoto itself rejected the Mahdi's claim and never answered Hayatu's calls to submit. {cite:wikipedia-hayatu-ibn-said-en} {cite:wikipedia-hayatu-ibn-said-ha}

## War and the Rabih alliance
Lamido Sanda of Adamawa, who admired learned men and would not fight a descendant of Usman, let Hayatu be; his half-brother and successor Zubeiru (from October 1890) did not. In 1893 Zubeiru marched on Balda with at least forty divisions and over 10,000 men — and the campaign ended in disaster for him, many of his soldiers refusing to fight, though his men burnt Balda as they withdrew. Victory made Hayatu's name, and Rabih az-Zubayr, the Sudanese warlord and Mahdist sympathiser then moving on Bornu, proposed an alliance: first Bornu, then Mandara and the Sokoto Caliphate. Rabih sealed it by giving Hayatu his daughter Hauwa in marriage. {cite:wikipedia-hayatu-ibn-said-en}

## Bornu and death
In October 1893 the allies defeated Mai Hashim and conquered Bornu; early in 1894 Hayatu moved to Rabih's new capital, Dikwa, as Imam of Bornu, the spiritual leader of the army. The French officer Émile Gentil, who met him there, described a pious man, "a sort of righter of wrongs", who spoke out vehemently against crimes and can hardly have approved Rabih's tyranny. Overshadowed by Rabih, and with the Mahdist cause in the Sudan itself collapsing, Hayatu planned his escape: in January 1898, while Rabih campaigned against the French, some fifty horsemen sent by Jibril Gaini of Gombe rode to lift him out of Dikwa — but Hauwa betrayed the plan to her brother Fadl-Allah, Rabih's second-in-command, and in the ensuing battle Hayatu was killed, his escort dying around his body rather than abandon it. {cite:wikipedia-hayatu-ibn-said-en} {cite:wikidata-hayatu-ibn-said}
